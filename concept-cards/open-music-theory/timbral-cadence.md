# Quick Definition
A timbral cadence uses the addition or change of instrumental timbre to emphasize cadential moments or structural boundaries, either marking a point of closure (adding winds at a cadence) or finessing a transition between sections (adding an instrument to connect them).

# Core Definition
Composers change timbral distribution at cadences to emphasize structural moments. Classical-era composers used wind additions at cadences (Mozart K453/iii). Shostakovich 11/iii adds unison violins at the end of a long viola solo to formalize closure. The same device can work in reverse: adding a timbre to finesse structural boundaries and connect sections (Brahms 2/iii uses the return of the oboe to smooth the A-section return). Not unique to orchestral music -- Blink 182 uses a second vocalist as a timbral cadence.

# Prerequisites
- Orchestral blend

# Key Properties
1. Timbre addition marks cadential moments
2. Can emphasize closure or connect sections
3. Works in both orchestral and non-orchestral contexts
4. Related to holding back a timbre for effect

# Context & Application
Timbral cadences are a subtle orchestration technique that uses color change rather than (or in addition to) harmonic change to articulate form.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Mozart, K453/iii): Strings begin; winds added at cadential moments.
**Example 2** (Shostakovich 11/iii): Violins added to viola solo at the closing cadence.
**Example 3** (Brahms 2/iii): Oboe return finesses the A-section recapitulation.
