# Syncopation (Jazz)

## Quick Definition

The disruption of the expected metric hierarchy by accenting weak beats or off-beats, creating rhythmic tension and interest.

## Formal Definition

**Syncopation** occurs when the normal hierarchical structure of meter is subverted. In 4/4 time, beat 1 is typically most important, beat 3 less so, and beats 2 and 4 least important. Syncopation obscures or reverses this hierarchy through various techniques:

Methods of creating syncopation:
- **Accents** on weak beats (the backbeat is one example)
- **Accents on off-beats**, particularly the second eighth note of a pair
- **Rests** that avoid downbeats
- **Ties** that connect notes across beat boundaries
- **Starting melodic lines** on off-beats rather than downbeats

## Musical Context

Syncopation is essential to jazz style and gives the music its characteristic rhythmic vitality. While classical music uses syncopation as an occasional effect, jazz employs it constantly as a fundamental element of the idiom.

Jazz melodies frequently begin on off-beats, ties obscure downbeats, and the rhythm section's backbeat provides continuous syncopation. The interplay between syncopated melodies and the underlying pulse creates the rhythmic complexity listeners associate with jazz.

## Examples

### Basic

- Accenting the "and" of beat 2 instead of beat 3
- Beginning a melodic phrase on the "and" of beat 4
- Tying a note from beat 2 through beat 3, obscuring beat 3

### From Repertoire

Carla Bley's "Donkey" (1976) demonstrates syncopation created through rests and ties, where the melody consistently avoids landing on strong beats, creating rhythmic tension against the underlying pulse.

## Related Concepts

- **Prerequisite**: beat, meter, tie, rests
- **Leads to**: anticipation
- **See also**: backbeat, swing-eighths

## Common Confusions

- Syncopation does NOT mean "playing off the beat randomly"; it is a deliberate subversion of metric expectations
- The backbeat itself IS a form of syncopation, though it has become so standard in jazz that it functions as the new "norm"
- Syncopation requires an underlying pulse to work against; without a clear beat, there is nothing to syncopate
- Not all off-beat notes are syncopated; context determines whether a note truly disrupts metric expectations
