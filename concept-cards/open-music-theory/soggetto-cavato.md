# Soggetto Cavato

## Quick Definition

A compositional technique in Renaissance music where a musical subject or melody is derived from the vowels of a text (usually a name, motto, or dedication) using solmization syllables, with each vowel mapped to its corresponding pitch through the hexachord system (a=la, e=re or mi, i=mi, o=sol, u=ut), creating personalized or symbolic melodic material as the basis for imitative polyphonic works.

## Formal Definition

**Soggetto Cavato** (Italian: "carved-out subject" or "hollowed-out subject," full term: *soggetto cavato dalle vocali*, "subject carved from the vowels") is a technique where a melodic subject is generated by converting the vowels of a textual phrase into musical pitches using the solmization system.

**Key characteristics**:
- **Text-to-pitch mapping**: Vowels of words converted to solmization syllables
- **Hexachord solmization**: Uses ut-re-mi-fa-sol-la system
- **Symbolic composition**: Encodes names, dedications, or mottoes in music
- **Imitative foundation**: Resulting subject used for points of imitation
- **Renaissance technique**: Most prominent in 15th-16th centuries
- **Intellectual display**: Demonstrates compositional ingenuity and learning
- **Hidden meaning**: Musical surface conceals textual reference

## Vowel-to-Pitch Mapping

**Solmization syllables**:
- **ut** (do) = C (or tonic of hexachord)
- **re** = D (or second degree)
- **mi** = E (or third degree)
- **fa** = F (or fourth degree)
- **sol** = G (or fifth degree)
- **la** = A (or sixth degree)

**Vowel correspondences**:
- **a** → **la** (sixth degree)
- **e** → **re** or **mi** (second or third degree, composer's choice)
- **i** → **mi** (third degree, from Latin "mi")
- **o** → **sol** (fifth degree, from Latin "sol")
- **u** → **ut** (first degree, from Latin "ut")

**Consonants ignored**: Only vowels extracted from text

## Process

**Steps to create soggetto cavato**:
1. **Select text**: Name, motto, dedication, or phrase
2. **Extract vowels**: Identify vowels in sequence (ignore consonants)
3. **Map to solmization**: Convert each vowel to corresponding syllable
4. **Determine pitches**: Choose hexachord transposition and specific pitches
5. **Add rhythm**: Assign note values to create melodic subject
6. **Compose work**: Use subject for imitative polyphony (motet, mass, ricercar)

## Musical Context

Soggetto cavato serves specific purposes:
- **Dedications**: Honor patrons, friends, or institutions through encoded names
- **Symbolic meaning**: Embed hidden messages or references
- **Compositional constraint**: Self-imposed limitation generates creative material
- **Intellectual engagement**: Engages learned audiences who recognize technique
- **Personal significance**: Creates intimate connection between text and music
- **Pedagogical example**: Demonstrates solmization system mastery

Most famous example: Josquin's *Missa Hercules Dux Ferrariae* based on Duke's name.

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple name: "David"**:
```
Text: DAVID
Vowels: A - I - I (consonants D, V, D ignored)

Vowel mapping:
A → la
I → mi
I → mi

Soggetto: la - mi - mi
Pitches (C hexachord): A - E - E
Pitches (G hexachord): E - B - B

With rhythm: A (half) - E (quarter) - E (quarter)
Subject created from "David"
```

**Patron's name: "MARIA"**:
```
Text: MARIA
Vowels: A - I - A

Vowel mapping:
A → la
I → mi
A → la

Soggetto: la - mi - la
Pitches (C hexachord): A - E - A (ascending fourth, descending fourth)

With rhythm: 𝅗𝅥 A - ♩ E - 𝅗𝅥 A
Melodic subject derived from name
```

**Famous example: "Hercules Dux Ferrariae"** (Josquin):
```
Text: HERCULES DUX FERRARIAE
Vowels: E - U - E - U - E - A - I - A - E

Vowel mapping:
E → re
U → ut
E → re (or mi, Josquin chose re)
U → ut
E → re
A → la
I → mi (fa in Josquin's version)
A → la
E → re

Soggetto: re - ut - re - ut - re - la - fa - la - re
Pitches: D - C - D - C - D - A - F - A - D

This becomes the cantus firmus for the entire mass
```

**Motto: "AVE"** (common Marian dedication):
```
Text: AVE
Vowels: A - E

Vowel mapping:
A → la
E → re

Soggetto: la - re
Pitches (C hexachord): A - D

Brief but meaningful subject from Marian salutation
```

**Multiple hexachords**:
```
Text: "FAUSTO"
Vowels: A - U - O

Using different hexachords:
A → la (A in C hexachord)
U → ut (G in G hexachord, transposed)
O → sol (D in G hexachord)

Pitches: A - G - D

Hexachord mutation creates more varied melody
```

### From Repertoire

**Josquin des Prez, Missa Hercules Dux Ferrariae**: Most famous soggetto cavato example, entire mass based on subject derived from Duke Ercole d'Este's name and title, subject appears in all movements.

**Josquin des Prez, Missa La sol fa re mi**: Title itself reveals the soggetto cavato (though exact textual source debated), demonstrates technique prominently in title and musical material.

**Orlando di Lasso, Various Works**: Used soggetto cavato for dedications and personal references in motets and masses.

**Cipriano de Rore, Motets**: Employed technique for patron dedications and symbolic references in sacred works.

**Costanzo Festa, Missa L'homme armé**: Some scholars argue includes soggetto cavato elements alongside cantus firmus technique.

## Common Confusions

- Soggetto cavato uses vowels only (consonants ignored)
- Not every recurring subject is soggetto cavato (must derive from text vowels)
- Solmization system essential—modern note names (A-G) not used
- "E" can map to either "re" or "mi" (composer chooses based on hexachord)
- Technique primarily Renaissance (15th-16th centuries), not Baroque/Classical
- Hidden/symbolic (not immediately obvious to listeners unfamiliar with source text)
- Rhythm added by composer (not determined by vowel mapping)
- Hexachord transposition affects actual pitches (same syllable = different pitch)
- "Cavato dalle vocali" = carved from the vowels (technical term)
- Subject becomes cantus firmus or imitative motive for entire work
- Josquin's Hercules mass most famous example (standard textbook reference)
- Not same as word-painting (visual/semantic text depiction in music)
- Latin vowels used (not vernacular languages typically)
- Some theorists debate exact mappings (historical variations exist)
- Intellectual/symbolic technique (less about audible expression, more about hidden meaning)
- Composer may manipulate resulting melody for musical purposes
