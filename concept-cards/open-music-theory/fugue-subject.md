# Fugue Subject

## Quick Definition

The main melodic theme of a fugue that recurs throughout the composition, initially stated by each voice in turn during the exposition.

## Formal Definition

The **subject** (or **fugue subject**) is a short, distinctive melodic line that serves as the primary thematic material of a fugue. It is first stated alone in a single voice at the beginning of the fugue and then systematically presented in all voices during the exposition, recurring throughout the composition in various keys, transformations, and combinations.

**Characteristics**:
- **Length**: Typically 1-4 measures (though longer subjects exist)
- **Tonality**: Clearly establishes the tonic key
- **Character**: Distinctive, memorable melodic profile
- **Structure**: Often follows a three-part design
- **Function**: Serves as the unifying melodic idea for the entire fugue

**Typical structure** (three parts):
1. **Kopfmotiv** ("head motive"): Distinctive opening gesture that makes the subject immediately recognizable
2. **Fortspinnung** ("spinning out"): Prolongation/development, may include sequences or motor rhythm
3. **Cadence**: Generic cadential formula, often to the dominant or tonic

**Types of subject character**:
- **Toccata style**: Motor-rhythm feel, often chromatic, irregular rhythmic placement
- **Ricercar style**: Slow-moving, antiquated feel, extensive use of suspensions, derives from plainsong/motet tradition
- **Dance-based**: Based on dance rhythms (gigue, sarabande, etc.)
- **Chorale-based**: Derived from hymn tunes

## Musical Context

The subject serves crucial structural and expressive functions:

**Structural functions**:
- Provides thematic unity throughout the fugue
- Establishes the key and mode
- Determines the contrapuntal possibilities (what countersubjects will work)
- Shapes the overall character and affect of the fugue
- Serves as the foundation for fugal devices (stretto, inversion, augmentation, etc.)

**Compositional considerations**:
- Must be suitable for imitation (clear, memorable)
- Should work at different pitch levels (subject and answer)
- Needs to allow for effective countersubjects
- Should have clear harmonic implications
- Must be appropriate for the number of voices and instrument/voice type

**Expressive functions**:
- Establishes the emotional character of the fugue
- Can suggest specific affects (joy, sorrow, grandeur, playfulness)
- May incorporate symbolic or rhetorical figures
- Often connected to text in vocal fugues

**Development throughout fugue**:
- Initial presentation: Subject alone in one voice
- Exposition: Subject and answer alternate in entering voices
- Episodes: Fragments of subject used sequentially
- Later entries: Subject in various keys, possibly inverted, augmented, diminished, or in stretto

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple subject** (hypothetical):
- C–D–E–F–G–A–G–F–E–D–C
  (Scalar ascent and descent, clear tonic orientation)

**Subject with distinctive opening**:
- C (eighth) - G-F (sixteenths) - E (quarter) - ...
  (Kopfmotiv: leap + stepwise descent creates distinctive profile)

### From Repertoire

- **Bach, WTC I, Fugue in C major (BWV 846)**:
  Subject: Ascending scalar line with running sixteenth notes, establishing motor rhythm

- **Bach, WTC I, Fugue in C minor (BWV 847)**:
  Subject: Chromatic descent with distinctive dotted rhythm, toccata-style character

- **Bach, WTC I, Fugue in C♯ major (BWV 848)**:
  Subject: Slow-moving with suspensions, ricercar style

- **Bach, WTC I, Fugue in D minor (BWV 851)**:
  Subject: Based on a simple stepwise descent, very singable

- **Bach, Art of Fugue, Contrapunctus I**:
  Subject: D–A–F–D–C♯–D–E–F–G–E–C♯–D
  (Clear harmonic implications, suitable for multiple contrapuntal combinations)

- **Handel, Messiah, "And with His stripes"**:
  Subject: Text-driven, with expressive leaps and chromatic inflection

- **Beethoven, String Quartet Op. 131**:
  Subject: Slow, chromatic, intensely expressive (ricercar-style)

## Common Confusions

- Subject vs. answer: The subject is the original theme; the answer is its transposition (usually at the fifth)
- Subject vs. countersubject: The subject is the main theme; the countersubject is a complementary melodic line
- Not every fugue subject has all three parts (kopfmotiv, fortspinnung, cadence)—some are simpler
- The subject doesn't have to be especially long or complex—some great fugue subjects are very simple
- Multiple subjects: A double fugue has two subjects; a triple fugue has three
- The subject is not necessarily the most beautiful melody—it needs to be functional for contrapuntal development
- Subject entries after the exposition may be modified, fragmented, or transformed
- The character of the subject determines the character of the entire fugue
- In vocal fugues, the subject often closely follows the text rhythm and meaning
