# Picardy Third

## Quick Definition

A major third used in the final chord of a piece that is otherwise in a minor mode, creating a final major triad instead of the expected minor triad.

## Formal Definition

A **Picardy third** (also called **tierce de Picardie**) is the practice of ending a composition in a minor mode with a major tonic triad, achieved by raising the third of the final chord. Instead of the expected minor third, the final chord contains a major third, converting what would be a minor triad into a major triad.

**Characteristics**:
- Occurs only at the final cadence of a piece or major section
- The third is raised by a half step (e.g., E♭ → E♮ in C minor)
- Converts the final minor triad to a major triad
- Does not change the overall mode of the piece—only the final chord
- Often accompanied by a bare fifth (without third) in inner voices in earlier usage

**Historical context**:
- Originated around 1500 when the third was first admitted in final chords
- Became "fairly common" in the second half of the 16th century
- By Palestrina's time, major triads accounted for around 85% of final chords
- Standard practice in Baroque music (Bach, Handel)
- Less common after 1750, though still used for specific effects

**Origin of term**: "Picardy third" likely derives from the Picardy region of France, though the exact etymology is uncertain.

## Musical Context

The Picardy third serves specific harmonic and expressive functions:

**Harmonic functions**:
- Provides a more stable, conclusive ending
- Major triads were considered more consonant and final than minor triads
- Avoids the perceived incompleteness of a minor triad at the end
- In Renaissance/Baroque theory, perfect consonances (octave, fifth) and major thirds were preferred for final sonorities

**Expressive functions**:
- Creates brightness and affirmation at the end of a minor-mode work
- Provides contrast with the prevailing minor sonority
- Adds a sense of resolution and completion
- Can suggest hope, transcendence, or divine light (especially in sacred music)

**Stylistic uses**:
- **Renaissance (16th century)**: Increasingly common, eventually dominant
- **Baroque**: Standard practice—most minor-mode pieces end with Picardy third
- **Classical/Romantic**: Less common, reserved for specific effects
- **20th/21st century**: Rare, used for historical or ironic effect

**Theoretical background**: In the acoustics of just intonation, the major third is more consonant (simpler frequency ratio) than the minor third, making major triads more stable final sonorities.

## Examples

### Basic

**Minor piece ending with Picardy third**:
- Key: A minor
- Penultimate chord: V (E major: E–G♯–B)
- Expected final chord: i (A minor: A–C–E)
- Actual final chord with Picardy third: I (A major: A–C♯–E)
  (C raised to C♯)

**D minor example**:
- Expected ending: D–F–A (D minor triad)
- With Picardy third: D–F♯–A (D major triad)
  (F raised to F♯)

### From Repertoire

- **Bach, Mass in B minor, "Crucifixus"**: Ends in E major despite E minor tonality of the movement
- **Bach, Well-Tempered Clavier**: Many minor-mode fugues end with Picardy thirds (e.g., Fugue in C minor, Book I)
- **Handel, Messiah**: Frequent use of Picardy thirds at cadences
- **Vivaldi, "Winter" from *Four Seasons***: F minor movement ends with F major
- **Palestrina, Masses**: Approximately 85% of final chords are major triads, including many Picardy thirds in minor modes

## Common Confusions

- Picardy third occurs only at the final cadence—raising the third elsewhere is modal mixture or tonicization, not a Picardy third
- It doesn't change the mode of the piece—the piece is still in minor; only the final chord is major
- Not all minor-mode pieces use Picardy thirds—after the Baroque era, many pieces end with minor tonic triads
- The Picardy third is not the same as modulation to the parallel major—the piece remains in the minor mode until the very end
- It's specifically about the third of the final tonic chord—other scale degrees are not involved
- The practice is historical, not universal—it reflects Renaissance/Baroque aesthetics about consonance and finality
- "Picardy" spelling (not "Picardie" in English usage, though "tierce de Picardie" in French)
- The Picardy third is optional—composers chose whether to use it based on expressive intent
