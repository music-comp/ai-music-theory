# Pitch vs Pitch Class

## Quick Definition

A pitch is a discrete tone at a specific frequency and octave (e.g., C4), while a pitch class (pc) is a group of all pitches related by octave equivalence and enharmonic equivalence (e.g., all Cs, B#s, and Dbb across all octaves).

## Formal Definition

**Pitch**: A discrete tone with a specific frequency corresponding to a particular octave placement. C4 is a different pitch than C3, even though they share the same letter name.

**Pitch class (pc)**: A group of pitches related by:
- **Octave equivalence**: C4, C3, C9, etc. are all members of the same pitch class
- **Enharmonic equivalence**: Ab4, G#4, and Fxx4 are all members of the same pitch class

In set theory, "class" means "group." A pitch class is therefore a group of pitches that share the same position within the chromatic scale, regardless of octave or spelling.

**Key distinction**:
- Pitch = specific frequency, specific octave
- Pitch class = abstract category, octave-independent

This distinction is fundamental to post-tonal analysis, where octave placement and enharmonic spelling are often irrelevant to the musical structure.

## Musical Context

In tonal music, enharmonic spelling matters: Ab as le (b6) leads to G, while G# as si (#5) leads to A. The distinction carries functional meaning.

In post-tonal music, without a tonal center, such distinctions often become irrelevant. Composers no longer constrained by tonal syntax may treat Ab and G# as functionally identical--hence the utility of pitch class as an analytical concept.

The pitch/pitch class distinction parallels the interval/interval class distinction: moving from concrete to abstract, from specific to general.

## Examples

### Basic

**Pitch examples**:
- C4 (middle C, approximately 261.63 Hz)
- C5 (one octave higher, approximately 523.25 Hz)
- These are different pitches

**Pitch class examples**:
- C4, C3, C5, B#2, Dbb7 all belong to pitch class C (pc 0)
- G#4, Ab3, G#6 all belong to pitch class G#/Ab (pc 8)

**The twelve pitch classes in integer notation**:
```
pc 0:  C, B#, Dbb
pc 1:  C#, Db
pc 2:  D, Cx, Ebb
pc 3:  D#, Eb
pc 4:  E, Fb, Dx
pc 5:  F, E#, Gbb
pc 6:  F#, Gb
pc 7:  G, Fx, Abb
pc 8:  G#, Ab
pc 9:  A, Gx, Bbb
pc 10: A#, Bb
pc 11: B, Cb, Ax
```

### From Repertoire

**Schoenberg, Pierrot lunaire**: The vocal line may present the same pitch class (e.g., pc 4 = E) at various octaves. The analytical significance lies in which pitch classes are used, not their specific octave placement.

**Webern, Symphony Op. 21**: The intricate twelve-tone structure is understood through pitch classes, as the rows are designed to create symmetries and relationships at the pitch-class level, regardless of register.

**Debussy's music**: Often benefits from considering both perspectives--sometimes pitch class analysis reveals hidden structures, while pitch-specific analysis honors the quasi-tonal voice leading.

## Common Confusions

- **Pitch vs. pitch class**: C4 is a pitch; "C" (including all octaves and enharmonic spellings) is a pitch class
- **Enharmonic equivalence is assumed** in pitch-class theory: G# and Ab are the same pc (8), even though they differ in tonal contexts
- **Integer 0 = C**, not A (different from some MIDI conventions)
- **There are 12 pitch classes** (0-11), regardless of how many pitches exist
- **Post-tonal analysis often uses pitch class**, but some 20th-century music (especially quasi-tonal works like Debussy) may require considering both pitch and pitch class perspectives
- **The clock face diagram** represents pitch classes, not pitches--there's no octave dimension on a clock
