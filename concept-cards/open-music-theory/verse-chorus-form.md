## Quick Definition

A song form where the primary content is split between lyric-variant verses (advancing the narrative) and lyric-invariant choruses (containing the hook and title), organized into repeating cycles.

## Formal Definition

Verse-chorus form organizes songs into cycles containing:
- **Verse (V)**: Lyric-variant, advances narrative, typically begins on-tonic
- **Prechorus (P)**: Optional; builds energy and tension toward chorus
- **Chorus (C)**: Lyric-invariant, contains title/hook, heightened musical intensity
- **Postchorus (Z)**: Optional; closes the cycle after the chorus
- **Bridge (B)**: Optional; appears once, typically before final chorus(es)

Sections always progress in this order (though not all need be present): V - P - C - Z

## Musical Context

Verse-chorus form rapidly dominated rock music from the 1960s onward. The form's power lies in the contrast between verses that tell the story and choruses that deliver the emotional/thematic core.

### Section Characteristics

| Section | Lyrics | Harmony | Function |
|---------|--------|---------|----------|
| Verse | Variant | Often open (post-1960s) | Narrative |
| Prechorus | Variable | Off-tonic, accelerating | Energy gain |
| Chorus | Invariant | Often on-tonic | Hook/climax |
| Postchorus | Variable | Closing | Cycle closure |
| Bridge | Variable | Contrasting | Variety |

## Common Confusions

- **Chorus vs. refrain**: A chorus is a complete section; a refrain is a repeated lyric within another section
- **Prechorus vs. bridge**: Prechorus builds toward the chorus within a cycle; bridge typically appears once and replaces verse/prechorus
- **Verse openness**: Pre-1960s verses tended to be harmonically closed; since the 1960s, they increasingly end open to propel into the chorus
