# Nota Cambiata

## Quick Definition

A special five-note melodic figure in third-species counterpoint that outlines a stepwise progression from downbeat to downbeat and uniquely permits a leap away from a dissonance.

## Formal Definition

The **nota cambiata** (Italian: "changing tone" or "changed note"; plural: note cambiate) is a specific five-note melodic pattern used in species counterpoint that spans from one downbeat to the next. It is the only figure in strict counterpoint that allows a leap *from* a dissonance.

**Two forms** (both outlining a stepwise descent or ascent from downbeat to downbeat):

1. **Descending form** (step down from downbeat to downbeat):
   - Beat 1 (downbeat): Consonant chord tone
   - Beat 2: Step down (dissonant)
   - Beat 3: Leap down a third (consonant)
   - Beat 4: Step up (consonant, same pitch as next downbeat)
   - Beat 1 (next bar): Step (same pitch as beat 4)

   Example: C–B–G–A–A (filling in C→B by step overall)

2. **Ascending form** (step up from downbeat to downbeat):
   - Beat 1 (downbeat): Consonant chord tone
   - Beat 2: Step up (dissonant)
   - Beat 3: Leap up a third (consonant)
   - Beat 4: Step down (consonant, same pitch as next downbeat)
   - Beat 1 (next bar): Step (same pitch as beat 4)

   Example: C–D–F–E–E (filling in C→D by step overall)

**Key characteristics**:
- The first, third, and fifth notes must be consonant with the cantus firmus
- The second note is dissonant and is followed by a leap (unusual!)
- Beat 4 and the following downbeat are the same pitch
- The overall progression from downbeat to downbeat is a single step
- The leap from the dissonance (beat 2 to beat 3) is a third
- The figure is surrounded by stepwise motion, minimizing the jarring effect of the dissonant leap

## Musical Context

The nota cambiata has specific functions in counterpoint pedagogy:

**Melodic functions**:
- Provides melodic variety in third species
- Allows a leap from a dissonance in a controlled, specific context
- Creates interesting melodic contour with a characteristic shape
- Outlines stepwise motion (the overall "meaning" of the figure) while adding inner melodic detail

**Pedagogical value**:
- Teaches that rules can have specific, controlled exceptions
- Demonstrates that overall stepwise motion can justify local irregularities
- Shows how repetition (beat 4 = downbeat) can smooth over unusual motion
- Introduces a historically important melodic figure

**Historical context**:
- Codified in Fux's *Gradus ad Parnassum* (1725)
- Based on actual usage in Renaissance polyphony
- Found in Palestrina and other 16th-century composers
- The name "changing tone" reflects the pitch change on beat 3

**Why the leap from dissonance is acceptable**:
- The overall progression is a simple step (downbeat to downbeat)
- The dissonance is surrounded by stepwise motion (beat 1→2 is a step)
- Beat 4 repeats, creating stability before the next downbeat
- The leap is small (only a third)
- The figure follows a specific, recognizable pattern

### From Repertoire

The nota cambiata is primarily a pedagogical figure from species counterpoint:
- Palestrina: Actual usage of cambiata-like figures in 16th-century polyphony
- Renaissance polyphony: The source of Fux's codification
- Bach: Occasionally uses similar figures in contrapuntal works
- Modern usage: Rare outside of species counterpoint exercises, but the principle (controlled leap from dissonance in a stepwise context) appears occasionally

## Common Confusions

- Nota cambiata is the ONLY figure in strict species counterpoint where you can leap from a dissonance
- It's a specific five-note pattern, not just any leap from a dissonance
- The term "changing tone" (singular) refers to nota cambiata; "changing tones" (plural) sometimes refers to double neighbors
- Beat 4 and the following downbeat must be the same pitch (this repetition is crucial)
- The overall motion from downbeat to downbeat is a simple step, even though the inner motion is more complex
- Not commonly used in actual composition—it's primarily a pedagogical figure
- The leap is always a third (from the dissonant beat 2 to the consonant beat 3)
- Must distinguish from other similar-sounding figures like échappée (escape tone) or neighbor tones
