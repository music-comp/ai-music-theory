# Invertible Counterpoint

## Quick Definition

A contrapuntal technique where two or more melodic lines are composed so they can exchange vertical positions (the upper voice becoming the lower voice and vice versa) while maintaining proper consonance and dissonance treatment, with the most common type being invertible counterpoint at the octave where voices swap positions by octave transposition, allowing thematic material to be reused in varied textural arrangements throughout a composition.

## Formal Definition

**Invertible Counterpoint** (also called "double counterpoint") is the composition of two or more independent melodic lines designed to function correctly when their vertical positions are exchanged—what was the upper voice becomes the lower voice, and vice versa.

**Key characteristics**:
- **Vertical exchange**: Voices trade positions (upper ↔ lower)
- **Maintained correctness**: Consonance/dissonance relationships remain acceptable after inversion
- **Compositional planning**: Requires forethought in line construction
- **Developmental resource**: Provides varied presentations of same material
- **Interval transformation**: Intervals invert according to mathematical complement
- **Most common**: Invertible counterpoint at the octave (voices exchange by octave)

## Types of Invertible Counterpoint

**By interval of inversion**:
1. **At the octave** (most common): Voices exchange positions by octave transposition
2. **At the tenth**: Voices exchange positions by tenth (octave + third)
3. **At the twelfth**: Voices exchange positions by twelfth (octave + fifth)
4. **Other intervals**: Fifteenth (double octave), etc. (less common)

**By number of voices**:
- **Double counterpoint**: Two voices invertible (most common)
- **Triple counterpoint**: Three voices can exchange positions in multiple combinations
- **Quadruple counterpoint**: Four voices fully invertible (rare, highly complex)

## Interval Transformation

**Invertible at the octave** (interval numbers sum to 9):
- Unison (1) → Octave (8)
- Second (2) → Seventh (7)
- Third (3) → Sixth (6)
- Fourth (4) → Fifth (5)
- Fifth (5) → Fourth (4)
- Sixth (6) → Third (3)
- Seventh (7) → Second (2)
- Octave (8) → Unison (1)

**Quality changes**:
- Perfect intervals remain perfect (P1↔P8, P4↔P5)
- Major intervals become minor and vice versa (M3↔m6, M2↔m7, M6↔m3, M7↔m2)
- Augmented intervals become diminished and vice versa (A4↔d5)

**Critical constraint**:
- **Perfect fourths (P4) become perfect fifths (P5)** when inverted
- P4 dissonant in two-voice texture, P5 consonant
- Therefore: **Avoid perfect fourths in original** (they become exposed P5s in inversion)
- Use sixths (→thirds), thirds (→sixths), fifths (→fourths below), octaves (→unisons) instead

## Compositional Process

**Writing invertible counterpoint**:
1. Compose Voice 1 (upper voice)
2. Compose Voice 2 (lower voice) considering interval constraints
3. Test inversion: Exchange voices by transposition (usually octave)
4. Check that inverted version has acceptable consonance/dissonance treatment
5. Revise if necessary to ensure both versions work

**Interval guidelines for invertible at the octave**:
- **Safe consonances**: 3rds, 6ths, 8ves (become 6ths, 3rds, unisons—all consonant)
- **Avoid**: Perfect 4ths (become P5s, potentially problematic)
- **Dissonances**: Use passing/neighbor tones carefully (ensure they remain proper embellishments after inversion)

## Musical Context

Invertible counterpoint serves specific purposes:
- **Developmental variety**: Reuse material with varied texture
- **Fugue episodes**: Exchange voice positions during sequential passages
- **Textural interest**: Create varied presentations of same counterpoint
- **Formal balance**: Parallel sections with inverted voice positions
- **Compositional efficiency**: One contrapuntal pair provides two textures
- **Intellectual display**: Demonstrates contrapuntal mastery

Extensively used in Baroque fugues, inventions, and canons.

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple invertible counterpoint at the octave**:
```
Original version:
Voice 1 (upper): C—D—E—F—E—D—C (ascending-descending scale)
Voice 2 (lower): C———————E———G———C (tonic triad outline)

Intervals: 1-2-3-4-3-2-1 (unison, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 3rd, 2nd, unison)

Inverted version (voices exchange by octave):
Voice 1 (lower): C—D—E—F—E—D—C (now in bass)
Voice 2 (upper): C———————E———G———C (now in soprano)

Intervals after inversion: 8-7-6-5-6-7-8 (octave, 7th, 6th, 5th, 6th, 7th, octave)

Note: Original P4 at F becomes P5 in inversion—must ensure this is acceptable
```

**Avoiding perfect fourths**:
```
INCORRECT (has P4):
Voice 1: C
Voice 2: G (below C, creates P4)

Inverted:
Voice 1: G (now in bass)
Voice 2: C (creates P5 above G, potentially problematic as exposed interval)

CORRECT (use 6th instead):
Voice 1: C
Voice 2: E (below C, creates M6)

Inverted:
Voice 1: E (now in bass)
Voice 2: C (creates m3 above E, perfectly acceptable)
```

**Fugue episode with invertible counterpoint**:
```
Measures 10-12 (original):
Soprano: [ascending sequence motive]
Alto: [descending counterpoint]

Measures 13-15 (inverted):
Soprano: [descending counterpoint, swapped]
Alto: [ascending sequence motive, swapped]

Same contrapuntal pair, vertically exchanged
```

**Triple counterpoint** (three voices, three positions):
```
Original:
Voice 1 (top): Motive A
Voice 2 (middle): Motive B
Voice 3 (bottom): Motive C

Permutation 1:
Voice 1: Motive B (swapped)
Voice 2: Motive C (swapped)
Voice 3: Motive A (swapped)

Permutation 2:
Voice 1: Motive C
Voice 2: Motive A
Voice 3: Motive B

All six permutations possible if designed as triple counterpoint
```

### From Repertoire

**J.S. Bach, Two-Part Invention No. 6 in E Major (BWV 777)**: Opening subject and countersubject designed as invertible counterpoint, voices exchange positions throughout piece.

**Bach, Well-Tempered Clavier, Book I, Fugue in C Minor (BWV 847)**: Fugue subject and countersubject invertible at the octave, appearing in both configurations throughout.

**Bach, Art of Fugue, Contrapunctus V**: Demonstrates invertible counterpoint at multiple intervals (octave and twelfth), with complete exposition followed by inverted restatement.

**Bach, Goldberg Variations, Variation 18**: Canon at the sixth with free bass, employing invertible counterpoint principles.

**Mozart, Jupiter Symphony, Finale**: Famous combination of five themes in invertible counterpoint at the coda, demonstrating quintuple counterpoint.

## Common Confusions

- Invertible counterpoint = vertical exchange (not melodic inversion)
- "Inversion" here means trading voice positions, not interval inversion
- "Double counterpoint" = two-voice invertible counterpoint (not two fugue subjects)
- Most common type: invertible at the octave (voices exchange by octave transposition)
- Perfect fourths problematic (become fifths when inverted at octave)
- Interval numbers sum to 9 when inverted at octave (3→6, 4→5, etc.)
- Major intervals become minor and vice versa (M3→m6, M6→m3)
- Requires compositional planning (can't add invertibility as afterthought)
- Both original and inverted versions must be correct contrapuntally
- Invertible at the tenth: intervals sum to 11 (different transformation)
- Invertible at the twelfth: intervals sum to 13 (different transformation)
- Triple counterpoint = three voices all invertible (six possible permutations)
- Not same as melodic inversion (up→down transformation of single line)
- Not every counterpoint designed to invert (specific compositional choice)
- Fugue countersubject often designed as invertible counterpoint with subject
