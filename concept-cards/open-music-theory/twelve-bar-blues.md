## Quick Definition

A foundational chord progression spanning 12 bars with a characteristic I-IV-I-V-IV-I structure, typically organized as three 4-bar phrases (aa'b).

## Formal Definition

The 12-bar blues is a harmonic schema that spans 12 measures, traditionally organized into three 4-bar phrases in an aa'b pattern:
- Bars 1-4 (a): I - I - I - I (or I - IV - I - I)
- Bars 5-8 (a'): IV - IV - I - I
- Bars 9-12 (b): V - IV - I - I (or V - V - I - I)

The progression emphasizes plagal motion (IV-I) and often uses dominant seventh chords throughout. It serves as both a harmonic framework and a formal structure for blues and blues-derived music.

## Musical Context

The 12-bar blues is one of the most influential progressions in American popular music, forming the basis for rock and roll, rhythm and blues, and countless other styles. The three-phrase aa'b structure typically corresponds to the lyric pattern of call-call-response.

### Variations

- Quick change: IV appears in bar 2
- Turnaround: V7 in bar 12 leads back to the repeat
- Substitute chords (ii for IV, vi for I, etc.)
- Extended versions (16-bar blues)

## Examples

### Basic Example

In C major (standard 12-bar):

| G | F | C | C/G7 |

## Common Confusions

- **12-bar blues as form vs. schema**: The 12-bar blues functions both as a harmonic progression and as a formal structure (three-phrase strophe)
- **Chord quality**: While the schema is typically analyzed with major/minor chord symbols, traditional blues practice often uses dominant seventh chords on I, IV, and V
- **Strict vs. flexible**: The 12-bar framework allows significant variation while remaining recognizable
