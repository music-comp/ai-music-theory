# Rule of the Octave

## Quick Definition

A standard 18th-century formula for harmonizing an ascending or descending scale in the bass, providing one specific chord for each scale degree.

## Formal Definition

The **Rule of the Octave** (also called **Regola dell'Ottava** in Italian) is a conventional harmonization pattern for diatonic bass scales that assigns specific chords to each scale degree. It served as a fundamental pedagogical tool and "cheat sheet" for 18th-century musicians learning to harmonize bass lines.

**Key characteristics**:
- **One chord per scale degree**: Each note of the scale (^1 through ^8) receives a specific chord
- **Ascending and descending forms**: Different harmonizations for rising vs. falling scales
- **Multiple versions**: Various 18th-century theorists provided slightly different versions
- **Four-voice texture**: Typically realized in four parts (bass plus three upper voices)
- **Figured bass notation**: Usually notated with figured bass symbols

**Common version** (based on Fedele Fenaroli, Naples, 1775):
- Strategic use of root position (5/3) chords on tonic and dominant
- First inversion (6/3) chords on other scale degrees
- Seventh chords preceding important harmonies (tonic and dominant)
- Chromatic alterations for stronger tonicization where appropriate

**Building the Rule from simple principles**:
1. Start with parallel 6/3 chords throughout the scale
2. Add 5/3 chords on tonic (^1 and ^8) for closure
3. Add 5/3 chords on dominant (^5) for hierarchical importance
4. Add seventh chords before tonic and dominant chords for stronger voice leading

**Positions**: The Rule can be realized in different "positions" depending on which chord tone appears in the soprano:
- Position 1: Tonic in soprano
- Position 2: Mediant in soprano
- Position 3: Dominant in soprano

## Musical Context

The Rule of the Octave served crucial pedagogical and practical functions:

**Pedagogical importance**:
- Central to 18th-century harmony instruction
- Part of the partimento tradition
- Taught improvisation and realization skills
- Foundation for understanding harmonic patterns
- Memorized by keyboard players and composers

**Historical context**:
- Codified by Italian theorists (Gasparini, Heinichen, Fenaroli, and others)
- Standard throughout Europe in the 18th century
- Used for teaching figured bass realization
- Foundation for partimento exercises
- Decline in the 19th century as harmonic practice changed

**Practical applications**:
- Harmonizing simple bass lines
- Improvising accompaniments
- Realizing figured bass at sight
- Composing bass lines with appropriate harmonies
- Understanding voice-leading principles

**Relationship to schemas**:
- The Rule provides harmonic framework for many Galant schemas
- Schemas often elaborate or decorate the Rule's basic harmonizations
- Both are part of the formulaic approach to 18th-century composition

## Examples

### Basic

**Ascending Rule of the Octave** (simplified, in C major):
- Bass: C–D–E–F–G–A–B–C
- Chords: I (5/3) – V6 – I6 – IV (5/3) – V (5/3) – vi (or IV6) – V6 – I (5/3)

**Descending Rule of the Octave** (simplified, in C major):
- Bass: C–B–A–G–F–E–D–C
- Chords: I (5/3) – V6 – vi (or IV6) – V (5/3) – IV (5/3) – I6 – V6 – I (5/3)

**With seventh chords** (more elaborate):
- Seventh chords typically precede the important 5/3 chords (tonic and dominant)
- Adds V7 or vii° chords before tonic chords
- Adds secondary dominants before dominant chords

### From Repertoire

The Rule of the Octave appears implicitly throughout 18th-century music:
- **Mozart, Piano Sonatas**: Bass lines often follow Rule of the Octave patterns
- **Haydn, String Quartets**: Harmonizations reflect the Rule's conventions
- **J.S. Bach, Continuo realizations**: Bach's figured bass follows similar principles
- **Handel, Keyboard music**: Demonstrates the Rule in action
- **Partimento realizations**: Student exercises directly apply the Rule

The Rule was so fundamental that composers internalized it, making its influence pervasive but often subtle in actual compositions.

## Common Confusions

- The Rule of the Octave is not a compositional rule in the modern sense—it's a pedagogical formula
- Different theorists provided different versions—there's no single "correct" Rule
- The Rule doesn't apply to every scale in every piece—it's a guideline, not a law
- Ascending and descending forms differ—memorize both
- The Rule applies to diatonic scales, not chromatic ones
- "Octave" refers to the span of the scale (^1 to ^8), not to octave doublings
- The Rule is a starting point for harmonization, not an end point—embellishment and variation are expected
- The figured bass numbers (5/3, 6/3, etc.) indicate chord inversions, not scale degrees
- Learning the Rule was about developing fluency and intuition, not mechanical application
- The Rule's "positions" refer to soprano voice placement, not chord inversions
- Modern functional harmony evolved from but differs from the Rule's approach
- The Rule was memorized through practice at the keyboard, not just theoretical study
