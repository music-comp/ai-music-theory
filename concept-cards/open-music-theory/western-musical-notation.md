# Quick Definition

A system of written symbols used to visually represent musical sounds, privileging pitch (vertical axis) and rhythm (horizontal axis).

# Core Definition

**Western musical notation** is one of many systems of musical notation that appear across time spans, places, and cultures. It uses written symbols to communicate musical sounds, translating an auditory art form into a visual representation. The system privileges two musical features above all others: pitch (represented on the y-axis) and rhythm (represented on the x-axis). No notation system can capture every aspect of sound in full detail, so Western notation selects the most essential elements for practical readability.

# Prerequisites

None -- this is the foundational concept for all of Western music theory notation.

# Key Properties

- Pitch is represented vertically (y-axis): higher positions = higher pitches
- Rhythm is represented horizontally (x-axis): read left to right, top to bottom
- The system is one of many worldwide; it is not the only way to notate music
- It involves choices about what to include and what to leave out
- Social context (milieu) influences the choices a notation system makes

# Construction / Recognition

Western musical notation is recognized by the presence of a staff (five horizontal lines), clefs, noteheads, stems, time signatures, and other standard symbols. Music is read left-to-right within a staff and top-to-bottom across systems.

# Context & Application

Written notation has several key advantages: works do not need to be memorized, details can be recorded that might otherwise be forgotten, and notation makes music accessible to anyone who knows the system without requiring a teacher. Western notation developed over centuries and remains the dominant system for classical, jazz, and much popular music worldwide.

# Examples

- A page of sheet music for piano with treble and bass clefs, notes, rests, and dynamic markings
- An orchestral score with many staves, one per instrument or section

# Relationships

# Common Errors

- Assuming Western notation captures all musical information (it does not -- timbre, exact timing nuance, and many other features are left to the performer)
- Assuming Western notation is the only or "correct" system (many cultures have different notation systems or rely on oral tradition)

# Common Confusions

- Western musical notation is a translation tool, not the music itself; the map is not the territory
