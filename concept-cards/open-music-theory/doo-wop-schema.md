## Quick Definition

The chord progression I-vi-IV-V, named for its prevalence in 1950s-60s rock ballads, distinguished by approaching the major tonic through authentic (V-I) motion.

## Formal Definition

The doo-wop schema is a four-chord cyclical progression: I-vi-IV-V (e.g., C-Am-F-G in C major). It uses the four most common chords in pop music (I, IV, V, vi) in an order that creates authentic cadential motion (V-I) when the progression cycles. The schema can undergo substitution (ii for IV) and rotation (starting on a different chord) while remaining recognizable.

## Musical Context

The doo-wop schema dominated 1950s-60s rock ballads but continues to appear in contemporary music. Its distinctive feature is the authentic approach to tonic (V-I), which sets it apart from other four-chord schemas.

### Common Variations

- **Substitution**: I-vi-ii-V (ii replaces IV due to shared subdominant function)
- **Rotation**: IV-V-I-vi or other starting points

## Examples

### Basic Example

In C major: C - Am - F - G (I-vi-IV-V)
The progression cycles: ...G - C - Am - F - G - C...

## Common Confusions

- **Doo-wop vs. singer/songwriter**: Both use I, IV, V, vi but in different orders. Doo-wop approaches I through V (authentic); singer/songwriter approaches I through IV (plagal)
- **Rotation recognition**: Even when rotated (e.g., IV-V-I-vi), identify the schema by how the major tonic is approached (from V = doo-wop)
- **ii substitution**: When ii replaces IV, the progression is still doo-wop because ii and IV share subdominant function
