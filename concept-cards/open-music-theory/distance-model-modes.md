# Distance Model

## Quick Definition

A distance model generates pitch collections by alternating between two fixed interval sizes. The octatonic (1:2, alternating semitone and whole tone) and hexatonic (1:3, alternating semitone and minor third) are the most common examples. This approach produces symmetrical scales with limited transpositions, strongly associated with Bartok's compositional technique.

## Formal Definition

**Distance model**: A method of generating pitch collections by strictly alternating two interval sizes.

**Notation**: Distance models are expressed as ratios (x:y) indicating the two alternating intervals in semitones.

**Common distance models**:

| Model | Intervals | Collection Name | Transpositions |
|-------|-----------|-----------------|----------------|
| 1:2 | semitone, whole tone | Octatonic | 3 |
| 1:3 | semitone, minor 3rd | Hexatonic | 4 |
| 1:5 | semitone, perfect 4th | (0167) | 6 |
| 2:3 | whole tone, minor 3rd | (related to whole-tone) | varies |

**Properties of distance-model collections**:
- **Symmetrical**: The alternating pattern creates rotational symmetry
- **Limited transposition**: Symmetry reduces unique transpositions
- **Even distribution**: Intervals are uniformly arranged

**Relationship to Messiaen's modes**: Many modes of limited transposition can be understood as distance models or extensions of them.

## Musical Context

Distance models appeal to composers seeking:

1. **Mathematical elegance**: Clear generative principle
2. **Transpositional symmetry**: Limited forms create coherence
3. **Interval consistency**: Uniform color throughout the collection
4. **Non-tonal organization**: Alternative to major-minor

**Bartok's usage**: Strongly associated with Bartok, who often combined distance-model tetrachords (like [0,1,6,7]) to create larger collections. His "From the Island of Bali" features the 1:5 model prominently.

**The 1:5 model** is particularly important:
- Generates set class (0167)
- Two tritone-related semitones
- Bartok's characteristic sound
- Sometimes called the "Z-cell"

## Examples

### Basic

**1:2 model (octatonic)**:
```
Starting on C, alternating 1 and 2:
C - Db - Eb - E - F# - G - A - Bb - (C)
   1    2    1    2    1   2   1   2

8 notes, returns to start after 12 semitones
```

**1:3 model (hexatonic)**:
```
Starting on C, alternating 1 and 3:
C - Db - E - F - Ab - A - (C)
   1    3   1   3    1   3

6 notes, returns to start after 12 semitones
```

**1:5 model (Bartok's tetrachord)**:
```
Starting on C, alternating 1 and 5:
C - Db - F# - G - (C)
   1    5    1   5

4 notes, the (0167) set class
Also expressed as two tritone-related semitones
```

**2:4 model would produce**:
```
C - D - F# - G# - (C)
   2    4    2    4

4 notes, two whole-tone-related major thirds
This is the diminished 7th chord reframed
```

### From Repertoire

**Bartok, "From the Island of Bali"**: The opening features 1:5 tetrachords in each hand. The right hand plays [B, C, F, F#] while the left plays [E, F, Bb, B]. Together they form the octatonic collection, showing how distance-model subsets combine.

**Bartok, Music for Strings, Percussion, and Celesta**: Uses (0167) and related tetrachords throughout, creating the characteristic Bartokian sound.

**Messiaen**: His modes of limited transposition include distance-model constructions--Mode 2 is the 1:2 model (octatonic), Mode 3 involves a 1:1:2 pattern.

**Stravinsky**: Some analyses reveal distance-model organization in works like The Rite of Spring.

## Common Confusions

- **Not all collections are distance models**: Diatonic, pentatonic, and acoustic scales don't have uniform interval alternation
- **The numbers indicate semitones**: 1:2 means alternating 1 semitone and 2 semitones
- **Some models produce familiar collections**: 1:2 = octatonic, 1:3 = hexatonic
- **Others produce tetrachords**: 1:5 produces a 4-note set, not a scale
- **Bartok's (0167) is the 1:5 model**: This tetrachord is characteristic of his style
- **Distance models combine**: Two 1:5 tetrachords a minor second apart yield octatonic
- **Symmetry creates limited transpositions**: This is why these collections have fewer unique forms
