# Direct Fifths and Octaves

## Quick Definition

A voice-leading situation where two voices approach a perfect fifth or perfect octave by similar motion (both moving in the same direction), drawing excessive attention to the stable interval and diminishing melodic independence, generally avoided in strict counterpoint though less problematic than parallel fifths or octaves.

## Formal Definition

**Direct fifths and octaves** (also called **hidden fifths** or **hidden octaves**) occur when:
1. Two voices move in similar motion (same direction)
2. They arrive at a perfect fifth or perfect octave
3. The motion is not parallel (interval before is different from interval after)

**Terminology variants**:
- **Direct fifths/octaves**: Common in British/American theory
- **Hidden fifths/octaves**: Emphasizes that parallelism is "hidden" by the change of interval
- **Exposed fifths/octaves**: Less common variant term

**Why they are problematic**:
- Perfect consonances (P5, P8) are stable and draw attention
- Similar motion reduces independence (both voices move same direction)
- Together, these factors over-emphasize the perfect interval
- Can imply parallel motion even when intervals differ
- Less problematic than true parallel fifths/octaves, but still limits variety

**Severity hierarchy** (most to least problematic):
1. **Parallel fifths/octaves**: Strictly forbidden (equal intervals + same direction)
2. **Direct fifths/octaves**: Avoid in principle, especially in outer voices
3. **Contrary/oblique to perfect consonance**: Generally acceptable

**Context-dependence**:
- **Strict species counterpoint**: Generally avoid
- **First species**: Avoid direct fifths/octaves completely
- **Second species**: More acceptable between downbeats (intervening weak beat mitigates effect)
- **Third species**: Similar to second species
- **Later tonal music**: Common at cadences when mitigated by contrary motion in other voices
- **16th-century style**: Avoid in principle, more common at cadences

**Mitigation strategies**:
- Use contrary motion in one or more additional voices
- Arrive at perfect interval by contrary motion instead
- Ensure stepwise motion in one voice (lessens impact)
- Reserve for cadences where effect is desired

## Musical Context

Direct fifths/octaves represent a middle ground in voice-leading pedagogy:
- More acceptable than parallel fifths/octaves
- Less ideal than contrary motion to perfect consonances
- Avoidance teaches students to prefer contrary motion
- Real music uses them selectively, especially at cadences

In species counterpoint, the guideline "Never move into a perfect consonance by similar motion" helps students develop sensitivity to voice independence and intervallic variety.

## Examples

### Basic

**Direct fifth** (both voices ascend):
```
Measure 1:           Measure 2:
Voice 1:  G4  →      D5 (ascending)
Voice 2:  E4  →      G4 (ascending)
Interval: 3rd →      P5
Analysis: Similar motion to perfect fifth—direct fifth (avoid in strict style)
```

**Direct octave** (both voices descend):
```
Measure 1:           Measure 2:
Voice 1:  E5  →      C5 (descending)
Voice 2:  G4  →      C4 (descending)
Interval: 6th →      P8
Analysis: Similar motion to perfect octave—direct octave (avoid)
```

**Hidden parallelism** (underlying fifths):
```
Measure 1 → 2:
Voice 1: C5 → D5
Voice 2: F4 → G4
Intervals: P5 → P5
If connecting tones filled in: C-D vs. F-G both ascend by step
Implies parallel fifths even though actual motion differs
```

**Acceptable approach** (contrary motion):
```
Measure 1:           Measure 2:
Voice 1:  D5  →      C5 (descending)
Voice 2:  E4  →      C4 (ascending)
Interval: 7th →      P8
Analysis: Contrary motion to octave—perfectly acceptable ✓
```

**Acceptable in second species** (intervening note):
```
Downbeat 1:          Downbeat 2:
Voice 1:  E5         G5 (ascends)
Voice 2:  C4         C4 (same, then:)
Weak beat: A4 (intervening)
Analysis: Weak beat between downbeats mitigates direct fifth effect
```

**Mitigated by stepwise motion**:
```
Voice 1: D5 → E5 (step up)
Voice 2: G4 → A4 (step up)
Interval: 5th → 5th (still similar motion to perfect consonance, but stepwise reduces impact)
```

**Direct fifth with leap** (more problematic):
```
Voice 1: C5 → G5 (leap P5 up)
Voice 2: E4 → B4 (leap P5 up)
Interval: 6th → 6th then to some P5
Analysis: Leaping in same direction heightens negative effect
```

### From Repertoire

Mozart, Haydn: Use direct fifths/octaves at cadences, mitigated by stepwise motion in soprano or strong contrary motion in inner voices. Bach fugues: Occasionally employ direct fifths when melodic integrity demands it, but primarily use contrary motion to perfect intervals. Palestrina: Avoids direct fifths/octaves in principle, but occasionally uses them at cadences when supported by contrary motion in other voices. Fux _Gradus ad Parnassum_: Explicitly teaches avoidance of direct fifths/octaves in first species.

## Common Confusions

- Direct fifths/octaves ≠ parallel fifths/octaves (parallel more problematic)
- "Hidden" doesn't mean invisible—it's clearly audible but less obvious than parallels
- Direct fifths/octaves not absolutely forbidden (context-dependent guideline)
- More acceptable when one voice moves by step (especially soprano)
- Less problematic in inner voices than outer voices (soprano-bass)
- Second species permits them between downbeats (intervening weak beat helps)
- "Direct" = similar motion to perfect interval (both same direction)
- Mitigation by contrary motion in other parts makes them acceptable
- Unison is perfect consonance but "direct unison" is less discussed (usually just avoid unisons)
- Some theorists distinguish "exposed" (outer voices) from "hidden" (inner voices)
- Not every similar motion is direct fifth/octave—only when arriving at P5 or P8
