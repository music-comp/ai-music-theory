# Passing Tone

## Quick Definition

A non-chord tone that fills the space between two chord tones by stepwise motion, connecting them melodically.

## Formal Definition

A **passing tone** (abbreviated PT or p) is a melodic embellishment that moves stepwise between two other tones, typically filling in a melodic third. Passing tones can be consonant or dissonant with the accompanying harmony.

**Dissonant passing tone**:
- Occurs on a metrically weak beat
- Approached by step
- Left by step in the same direction
- Creates temporary dissonance between two consonances
- Most common type in species counterpoint and tonal music

**Consonant passing tone**:
- Follows the same stepwise pattern
- Forms a consonant interval with other voices
- Less tension than dissonant passing tone
- Still functions as melodic embellishment rather than harmonic tone

Pattern: Chord tone → [passing tone] → chord tone
Motion: Step in one direction → step continuing in same direction

In second-species counterpoint, dissonant passing tones fill a melodic third between consonant downbeats (e.g., C → D → E, where D is the passing tone on the weak beat).

## Musical Context

Passing tones serve essential melodic and harmonic functions:

**Melodic functions**:
- Create smooth, stepwise melodic motion
- Fill in melodic skips (usually thirds)
- Add melodic interest and detail
- Connect structural chord tones

**Harmonic functions**:
- Add harmonic color and tension
- Create local dissonance that resolves
- Enhance voice leading between harmonies

**Stylistic uses**:
- **Baroque/Classical**: Primarily on weak beats, carefully controlled
- **Romantic**: Increasingly chromatic passing tones
- **Jazz**: Both diatonic and chromatic passing tones common
- **Pop/Rock**: Often less strict about preparation/resolution

Passing tones are among the most common non-chord tones in all tonal music styles.

## Examples

### Basic

**Dissonant passing tone**:
- Harmony: C major → C major
- Melody: E → D → C
(D is a dissonant passing tone between chord tones E and C)

**Consonant passing tone**:
- Lower voice (cantus firmus): C → C
- Upper voice: E → F → G
(F is a consonant passing tone, making a P4 with the cantus)

**Chromatic passing tone**:
- Melody over C major: C → C♯ → D
(C♯ is a chromatic passing tone filling C-D)

### From Repertoire

- Bach: Chorales and inventions use passing tones extensively for melodic fluency
- Mozart: Melodies filled with elegant passing tones
- Chopin: Chromatic passing tones in nocturnes and waltzes
- Jazz: Bebop scales incorporate passing tones systematically
- The Beatles: "Yesterday" features numerous passing tones in the melody

## Common Confusions

- Passing tone vs. chord tone: Passing tones are melodic embellishments; chord tones are structural
- Direction: Passing tones must continue in the same direction (unlike neighbor tones, which return)
- All passing tones move by step, but not all stepwise motion creates passing tones
- Dissonant passing tones in strict style occur only on weak beats, but freer styles allow more flexibility
- Chromatic passing tones follow the same rules but use chromatic rather than diatonic steps
- "Passing" doesn't mean the note is unimportant—it's crucial for melodic shape and expression
