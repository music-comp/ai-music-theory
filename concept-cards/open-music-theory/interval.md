# Quick Definition

The distance between two pitches, defined by both size (generic interval number) and quality (perfect, major, minor, augmented, diminished).

# Core Definition

An **interval** is defined as the distance between two notes. Every interval has a **size** (generic interval: unison, 2nd, 3rd, etc.) and a **quality** (perfect, major, minor, augmented, diminished). Size is the number of letter names spanned; quality specifies the exact number of half steps. The "major scale method" identifies quality: imagine the major scale of the bottom note; if the top note is in that scale, the interval is perfect (unisons, 4ths, 5ths, octaves) or major (2nds, 3rds, 6ths, 7ths). Augmented intervals are one half step larger than perfect or major; diminished intervals are one half step smaller than perfect or minor.

# Prerequisites

- **Pitch**: the notes forming the interval
- **Generic interval**: the size measurement
- **Major scale**: the reference for the major-scale method

# Key Properties

- Size + quality = complete interval identification (e.g., "perfect fifth," "minor third")
- Perfect intervals: unisons, 4ths, 5ths, octaves
- Major/minor intervals: 2nds, 3rds, 6ths, 7ths
- Augmented = one half step larger than perfect or major
- Diminished = one half step smaller than perfect or minor
- Intervals can be melodic (successive) or harmonic (simultaneous)
- Intervals can be simple (up to octave) or compound (larger than octave)

# Construction / Recognition

1. Determine size (count letter names). 2. Imagine the major scale of the bottom note. 3. Check if the top note is in that scale. 4. Assign quality accordingly.

# Context & Application

Intervals are the fundamental building blocks of melody (successive intervals) and harmony (simultaneous intervals). They are essential for chord construction, voice leading, and ear training.

# Examples

- C to G: generic 5th; G is in C major, so perfect fifth (P5)
- E to C: generic 6th; C is in E major? E major has C#, so C-natural is lowered = minor sixth (mi6)
- F to B: generic 4th; B is NOT in F major (F major has Bb), so augmented fourth (A4)

# Common Errors

- Counting half steps instead of using the major-scale method (time-consuming and error-prone)
- For difficult bottom notes (imaginary key signatures), use intervallic inversion or enharmonic equivalence

# Common Confusions

- Interval size is generic (independent of accidentals); quality makes it specific
- Augmented and diminished intervals are NOT interchangeable with major and minor
