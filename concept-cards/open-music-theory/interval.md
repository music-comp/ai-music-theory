# Interval

## Quick Definition

The distance between two pitches, measured both as written distance on a staff and as aural space between sounding pitches.

## Formal Definition

An **interval** is the relationship between two pitches, measured in two complementary ways:
1. **Written distance**: The number of lines and spaces on a staff between two notes
2. **Aural distance**: The frequency relationship or number of semitones between two pitches

Every interval has two components:
- **Size** (or generic interval): The written distance, counted in letter names (unison, second, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh, octave)
- **Quality**: The specific measurement in semitones (perfect, major, minor, augmented, diminished)

Intervals can be:
- **Melodic**: Notes played sequentially (one after another)
- **Harmonic**: Notes played simultaneously (together)

In pitch-class theory, intervals are measured as ordered pitch-class intervals: i(a,b) = (b - a) mod 12, or as unordered interval classes: ic(a,b) = min(i, 12-i), where interval classes range from ic0 to ic6.

## Musical Context

Intervals are fundamental to understanding:
- **Melody**: The shape and character of melodic lines
- **Harmony**: The sound and function of chords
- **Voice leading**: How parts move relative to each other
- **Consonance and dissonance**: Which intervals sound stable vs. unstable

Small intervals (seconds, thirds) create stepwise or smooth melodies, while large intervals (sixths, sevenths, octaves) create dramatic leaps. Some intervals have strong cultural associations: perfect fifths sound "pure," minor seconds sound "dissonant," major thirds sound "bright."

## Examples

### Basic

Melodic intervals:
- C to D (ascending) = major second (M2)
- E to C (descending) = minor sixth (m6)

Harmonic intervals:
- C and E played together = major third (M3)
- F and B played together = augmented fourth/tritone (A4)

Semitone counts:
- Minor second = 1 semitone
- Major second = 2 semitones
- Perfect fifth = 7 semitones
- Octave = 12 semitones

### From Repertoire

The opening of "Somewhere Over the Rainbow" leaps up an octave (P8). The "Jaws" theme uses repeated minor seconds (m2) for tension. The first two notes of "Twinkle, Twinkle" form a perfect fifth (P5).

## Common Confusions

- Interval vs. distance: An interval is a relationship, not just a number
- Size (generic interval) doesn't account for accidentals; quality does
- Same size, different qualities: C-E and C-E♭ are both thirds (size) but major vs. minor (quality)
- Harmonic vs. melodic intervals sound different even when they have the same name
- Always count the starting note as "one" when determining size
