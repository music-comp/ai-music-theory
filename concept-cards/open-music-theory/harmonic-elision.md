# Quick Definition
Harmonic elision occurs when an expected chord is suppressed and replaced by a functionally distinct chord, creating the effect of two simultaneous harmonic events. Two types: leading-tone elision (leading tone lowered instead of resolving up) and raised-root elision (expected root raised to become an applied leading tone).

# Core Definition
Leading-tone elision: a chord with a leading tone fails to resolve up by half step; instead the leading tone becomes its lowered version (e.g., B becomes Bb). The expected triad is replaced by a dominant seventh with the same root, or by a functionally equivalent fully diminished seventh. The bass stays the same unless the leading tone was in the bass (as with V6, V65, viio7). Raised-root elision: the root of the expected chord is raised to become a leading tone in an applied chord (e.g., expected C becomes C#, producing V/ii or viio7/ii). The progression is pushed higher up the scale. The voice leading changes only minimally — the altered note is the sole difference.

# Prerequisites
- Applied (secondary) dominant chords
- Dominant seventh chord resolutions
- Standard voice-leading conventions

# Key Properties
1. Suppression (not mere substitution) of an expected chord
2. Leading-tone elision: leading tone becomes lowered version; expected triad becomes V7 or viio7 with same root
3. Raised-root elision: expected root raised to applied leading tone
4. Voice leading differs only at the altered note
5. Bass changes when leading tone is in bass (V6, V65, viio7)
6. Common: V6/5 to V4/2/IV (leading-tone elision)
7. Spotted by incongruities in Roman numeral sequences
8. Notation practice: write expected Roman numeral, cross out, place actual chord

# Context & Application
Strauss, "Zueignung," Op. 10, No. 1, mm. 1-10: Three harmonic elisions in one phrase. M. 6: raised-root elision (C raised to C#, applied leading tone). M. 8: quasi-cad.6/4 with raised-root elision (G raised to G#, applied to vi). M. 9: leading-tone elision (leading tone resolves down, creating V7/IV). Elision with cad.6/4 is special: the expected root appears with the 6/4 but transforms into its raised version when 6/4 resolves.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Strauss, "Zueignung"): Three elisions in a single phrase — two raised-root, one leading-tone.
**Example 2** (Common progressions): V6/5 to V4/2/IV; V6/5/V to V4/2.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Elision is the same as chord substitution.
  **Clarification**: Elision creates a sense of overlapping/suppression; substitution is a single unexpected chord. Some use "substitution" broadly to include elision.
- **Confusion**: The voice leading changes significantly.
  **Clarification**: Only the altered note (lowered leading tone or raised root) behaves differently; all other voices follow normal patterns.
