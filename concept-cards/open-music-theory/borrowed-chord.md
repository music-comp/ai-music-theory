# Borrowed Chord

## Quick Definition

A borrowed chord is a chord taken from the parallel mode (parallel major or minor sharing the same tonic) and used within a passage, introducing chromatic alterations that change chord quality while preserving harmonic function.

## Formal Definition

**Borrowed chords** are the specific chords that result from modal mixture. When notes are borrowed from the parallel key, the resulting chord qualities differ from their diatonic counterparts:

**In major keys (borrowing from parallel minor)**:

| Diatonic Chord | Borrowed Chord | Changed Notes |
|----------------|----------------|---------------|
| ii | iio | ^6 becomes lowered-^6 |
| ii7 | ii∅7 | ^6 becomes lowered-^6 |
| IV | iv | ^6 becomes lowered-^6 |
| I | i | ^3 becomes lowered-^3 |
| V | v | ^7 becomes lowered-^7 |
| iii | ♭III | ^3 and ^7 both lowered |
| vi | ♭VI | ^6 and ^3 both lowered |
| viio | ♭VII | ^7 lowered (root changes) |

## Categories of Borrowed Chords

### Predominant Borrowings (Most Common)

These appear frequently in cadential progressions:

**iv (minor subdominant)**:
- In C major: F-A♭-C
- Only requires lowered-^6 (A♭)
- Creates darker, more poignant sound than IV
- Very common in both classical and popular music

**iio6 (diminished supertonic, first inversion)**:
- In C major: D-F-A♭ (with F in bass)
- Only requires lowered-^6 (A♭)
- Functions as predominant like ii

**ii∅7 (half-diminished seventh)**:
- In C major: D-F-A♭-C
- Only requires lowered-^6 (A♭)
- Rich predominant sound

### Chords with Lowered Roots

When the ROOT of the chord is altered, the Roman numeral receives an accidental:

**♭VI (flat submediant)**:
- In C major: A♭-C-E♭
- Requires lowered-^6 (root) and lowered-^3
- Major triad on lowered sixth degree
- Dark, dramatic sound

**♭VII (flat subtonic)**:
- In C major: B♭-D-F
- Requires lowered-^7 (root)
- Major triad on lowered seventh degree
- Common in rock and popular music

**♭III (flat mediant)**:
- In C major: E♭-G-B♭
- Requires lowered-^3 (root) and lowered-^7
- Major triad on lowered third degree

### Tonic Borrowing

**i (minor tonic in major key)**:
- In C major: C-E♭-G
- Changes tonic quality for expressive effect
- Often used for momentary darkening

## Voice Leading with Borrowed Chords

**Key principle**: Borrowed tones typically resolve in the direction of their alteration:
- Lowered notes (♭) tend to resolve downward
- Example: lowered-^6 (A♭ in C) naturally resolves down to ^5 (G)

**Continuity within harmonic areas**:
- If one chord in a functional area uses mixture, subsequent chords in that area often maintain it
- Example: If first predominant uses mixture, second predominant likely will too

## Musical Context

Borrowed chords serve to:
- Add emotional depth and darkness to major-key passages
- Create chromatic color without leaving the key
- Intensify cadential approaches
- Connect to the expressive world of the parallel mode

**Common stylistic associations**:
- Romantic era: Extensive use of iv, ♭VI, and chromatic mediants
- Popular music: ♭VII especially common (rock, pop progressions)
- Film music: Modal mixture for emotional coloring

## Examples

### Basic

**Borrowed iv in C major**:
```
I - IV - iv - I
C - F  - Fm - C

The A natural in F major becomes A♭ in F minor
Creates poignant "chromatic plagal" effect
```

**Borrowed ♭VI in C major**:
```
I - ♭VI - V - I
C - A♭  - G - C

Dramatic chromatic color
Bass motion: C - A♭ - G - C
```

**Borrowed ♭VII in C major**:
```
I - ♭VII - IV - I
C - B♭   - F  - C

Common in rock/pop progressions
Modal, "mixolydian" flavor
```

### From Repertoire

**Saint-Saens, Samson et Dalila, "Mon coeur s'ouvre a ta voix"**:
- Progression borrows lowered-^6 from parallel minor
- Creates ii∅4/3 from what would be ii4/3

**The Beatles, "While My Guitar Gently Weeps"**:
- Uses ♭VII and ♭VI in major-key context
- Demonstrates borrowed chords in popular music

**Chopin, Nocturnes**:
- Frequent use of iv for expressive cadences
- Chromatic descending basslines incorporating borrowed chords

## Common Confusions

- Borrowed chords come from the PARALLEL mode (same tonic, different mode), not the relative mode
- The function of the chord does not change when borrowed (iv is still predominant)
- Accidentals before Roman numerals indicate altered ROOTS (♭VI means the root is lowered)
- Borrowed chords are not modulations—the tonic remains unchanged
- When using borrowed chords, prefer fully borrowed versions over partial borrowing
- Borrowed chords are different from secondary dominants (which tonicize other chords)
