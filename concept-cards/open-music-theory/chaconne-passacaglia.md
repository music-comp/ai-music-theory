# Chaconne and Passacaglia

## Quick Definition

Baroque variation forms based on a repeating bass line (ground bass) in triple meter, with the chaconne and passacaglia being closely related sub-genres characterized by continuous variation over an ostinato pattern, creating extended compositions through harmonic and textural transformation.

## Formal Definition

**Chaconne** and **Passacaglia** are specific types of ground bass composition that flourished in the Baroque era, distinguished by their structural and stylistic characteristics.

**Common features**:
- Both are ground bass compositions (bass line repeats throughout)
- Both typically in triple meter (3/4, 3/2, etc.)
- Both employ continuous variation technique
- Both create unity through bass repetition, variety through upper-voice transformation
- International variant spellings exist (Chacony for Chaconne)

**Relationship to broader categories**:
- **Ostinato**: Any repeating pattern in one voice
- **Ground bass**: Ostinato specifically in the bass line
- **Chaconne/Passacaglia**: Ground bass in triple meter with specific Baroque characteristics
- Every Chaconne/Passacaglia is a ground bass
- Every ground bass is an ostinato
- But reverse is NOT true (not every ostinato is a ground bass, etc.)

**Historical distinction** (often blurred in practice):
- Theorists debate precise difference between Chaconne and Passacaglia
- Some claim Passacaglia = ground in minor, Chaconne = ground in major
- Others claim Passacaglia = stricter bass repetition, Chaconne = more harmonic freedom
- In practice, composers used terms somewhat interchangeably
- Modern analysis focuses more on shared characteristics than distinction

**Compositional technique**:
- **Harmonic variation**: Re-harmonize same bass in different ways
- **Textural variation**: Change number of voices, rhythmic density, register
- **Melodic variation**: Create new melodic material in upper voices
- **Phrase rhythm**: Vary phrase lengths by re-harmonizing bass start/end points
- **Modulation**: Tonicize other keys while maintaining bass pattern
- **Meter/rhythm**: Occasionally shift to compound meter or change rhythmic values

**Bass interval reharmonization**:
- Single bass interval can support multiple harmonic interpretations
- Composers exploit ambiguity to create tonal variety
- Avoidance of perfect cadence at ground boundaries creates seamless flow
- Strategic use of seventh chords, inversions, and tonicizations

**Structural approach**:
- Typically long compositions (many iterations of ground)
- Build trajectory from simple to complex textures
- Often include climactic moments with dense polyphony
- May conclude with return to simpler texture
- Unity through repetition, variety through transformation

## Musical Context

Chaconne and Passacaglia represent pinnacles of Baroque variation technique:
- **Pedagogical value**: Teaches re-harmonization and voice-leading over fixed bass
- **Compositional challenge**: Create extended, coherent work from simple material
- **Performance vehicle**: Showcases both technical virtuosity and musical imagination
- **Enduring form**: Continues in jazz, popular music (repeating chord progressions)

The forms demonstrate how composers balance:
- Repetition (ground bass) vs. Variety (upper voices)
- Unity (tonal center) vs. Diversity (tonicizations)
- Simplicity (bass pattern) vs. Complexity (contrapuntal elaboration)

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple Chaconne structure** (hypothetical):
```
Ground bass (4 measures): C - G - Am - Em - F - C - D - G
Iteration 1: Simple chordal harmonization
Iteration 2: Add melodic line in soprano
Iteration 3: Introduce imitation between soprano and alto
Iteration 4: Re-harmonize to tonicize F major at measure 3
Iteration 5: Increase rhythmic values (eighth notes)
Iteration 6: Change to compound meter feel
...
Iteration 20: Return to simple texture for conclusion
```

**Bass interval reharmonization** (avoiding cadence):
```
Bass: G → C (could be V → I perfect cadence)
Iteration 1: G (V7) → C (I) - cadential
Iteration 2: G (vii°6 in F) → C (IV in G) - non-cadential
Iteration 3: G (I) → C (IV) - subdominant function
Result: Same bass notes, different harmonic meanings blur phrase boundaries
```

**Textural variation**:
```
Variation 1: Two voices (bass + one upper)
Variation 5: Four voices (full SATB texture)
Variation 10: Return to two voices at higher register
Variation 15: Six voices with canon in upper parts
Creates dynamic trajectory through texture
```

### From Repertoire

**Bach "Crucifixus" from Mass in B Minor (BWV 232)**: Passacaglia, chromatic descending bass (4 measures), 13 iterations, profound expression of suffering through descending tetrachord ground.

**Purcell Chacony in G Minor (Z 730)**: Extended chaconne, demonstrates extensive imitation and harmonic variety, multiple tonicizations while maintaining G minor center.

**Purcell Trio Sonata in G Minor (Z 807)**: Ground bass composition with elaborate two-violin imitation above repeating bass, showcases re-harmonization techniques.

**Corelli La Folia**: Variations on Folia bass pattern (I-V-I-VII-III-VII-I-V-I-V-I-VII-I-V-I), demonstrates Baroque variation practice over well-known ground.

**J.S. Bach Chaconne from Partita No. 2 in D Minor (BWV 1004)**: Solo violin, tour-de-force of implied harmony and polyphonic writing over chaconne structure.

**Handel Passacaglia from Harpsichord Suite No. 7 in G Minor (HWV 432)**: Demonstrates typical Baroque ground bass treatment with continuous eighth-note motion.

## Common Confusions

- Chaconne vs. Passacaglia: historical distinction often blurred, focus on shared features
- Not every triple-meter ground bass is Chaconne/Passacaglia (term implies Baroque style)
- Ground bass may be varied slightly in some iterations (not always exact repetition)
- Chaconne/Passacaglia ≠ theme and variations (continuous vs. sectional variation)
- Not limited to minor mode (many in major, despite associations)
- Modern examples (Ravel "Boléro") show ostinato but not same as Baroque Chaconne/Passacaglia
- Bass pattern length varies (typically 4-8 measures, but can differ)
- Upper voices create variety, not bass (bass is "fixed" or "ground")
- Continuous form (no pauses between variations, seamless flow)
- Not same as "changes" in jazz (though conceptually related through repeating harmonic cycle)
