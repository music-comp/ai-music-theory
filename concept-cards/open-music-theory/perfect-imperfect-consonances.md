# Perfect and Imperfect Consonances

## Quick Definition

In species counterpoint, consonances are categorized as perfect (unisons, fifths, and octaves) or imperfect (thirds and sixths), with perfect consonances being more stable but potentially problematic in succession, while imperfect consonances are preferred for most progressions due to their balance of consonance and melodic independence.

## Formal Definition

**Perfect and imperfect consonances** represent a fundamental distinction in species counterpoint (and tonal music generally) between highly stable intervals and moderately stable intervals, each with different voice-leading implications.

**Perfect consonances**:
- **Perfect unison** (P1): Same pitch in both voices
- **Perfect fifth** (P5): Seven semitones
- **Perfect octave** (P8): Twelve semitones (octave equivalence)
- Compound intervals: P12 (octave + fifth), P15 (double octave), etc.

**Imperfect consonances**:
- **Major third** (M3): Four semitones
- **Minor third** (m3): Three semitones
- **Major sixth** (M6): Nine semitones
- **Minor sixth** (m6): Eight semitones
- Compound intervals: M10, m10, M13, m13, etc.

**Acoustic/perceptual basis**:
- **Perfect consonances**: Simple frequency ratios (2:1, 3:2, 1:1)
- **Imperfect consonances**: More complex ratios (5:4, 6:5, etc.)
- Perfect intervals create stronger tonal fusion (voices blend)
- Imperfect intervals maintain more voice independence

**Species counterpoint guidelines**:

**Beginning and ending**:
- **Start**: Use perfect consonance (P1, P5, or P8) for stability
- **End**: Use perfect consonance (P1 or P8, not P5) for finality
- Perfect consonances frame the exercise

**Middle of exercise**:
- **Prefer imperfect consonances** (3rds, 6ths) over perfect (5ths, 8ves)
- Imperfect consonances promote:
  - Voice independence (less tonal fusion)
  - Variety (multiple options: M3, m3, M6, m6)
  - Motion toward goal (less stable = more dynamic)
- Perfect consonances in middle:
  - Use sparingly
  - Never two of same type in succession (no P5-P5 or P8-P8)
  - Different perfect consonances in succession acceptable (P8-P5 allowed)

**Unisons**:
- Perfect unison is perfect consonance
- Only at beginning and end (never in middle)
- Unisons diminish independence (voices merge completely)
- Exception: Second species may use unisons on weak beats when necessary

**Parallel motion restrictions**:
- **Parallel perfect consonances forbidden**: P5-P5, P8-P8, P1-P1
- Destroys voice independence, limits variety
- Parallel imperfect consonances: Limited to three in a row maximum
- Perfect consonances draw attention; parallel motion compounds this

**Direct/hidden fifths and octaves**:
- Similar motion into perfect consonance (both voices same direction)
- Avoided in strict first species
- Less problematic than parallel perfect consonances
- More acceptable in later species (intervening notes mitigate effect)

**Special case: Perfect fourth**:
- Perfect fourth (P4) is dissonant in two-voice counterpoint when involving lowest voice
- Consonant between two upper voices (three+ voice textures)
- In species counterpoint (two voices), all fourths involve bass → dissonant

## Musical Context

The perfect/imperfect distinction serves multiple purposes:
- **Pedagogical**: Teaches hierarchical treatment of consonances
- **Perceptual**: Reflects acoustic properties and hearing psychology
- **Voice-leading**: Guides decisions about interval succession
- **Cadential**: Perfect consonances mark structural boundaries (beginnings, endings)
- **Historical**: Reflects centuries of contrapuntal practice

Understanding this distinction helps composers balance stability (perfect) with variety and independence (imperfect).

## Examples

### Basic

**Proper use at beginning/end**:
```
Beginning: Perfect fifth (C4 and G4) - stable opening ✓
Middle: Major third, minor sixth, major sixth, minor third - variety ✓
End: Perfect octave (C4 and C5) - stable close ✓
```

**Improper: Two perfect fifths in succession**:
```
Measure 1: Perfect fifth (C4-G4)
Measure 2: Perfect fifth (D4-A4)
Error: Parallel fifths - forbidden! ✗
```

**Proper: Varied consonances in middle**:
```
M1: Major third (C4-E4)
M2: Perfect fifth (D4-A4) - okay, just one
M3: Major sixth (E4-C5)
M4: Minor third (F4-Ab4)
Uses perfect fifth once, surrounded by imperfect ✓
```

**Improper: Unison in middle**:
```
M1: Minor sixth (E4-C5)
M2: Perfect unison (G4-G4)
M3: Major third (F4-A4)
Error: Unison only at beginning/end ✗
```

**Perfect vs. imperfect preference**:
```
Option 1: M1 P8, M2 P5, M3 P8, M4 P5 (mostly perfect - lacks variety)
Option 2: M1 P8, M2 m6, M3 M3, M4 P8 (better - imperfect in middle) ✓
```

**Special case: Perfect fourth**:
```
In two-voice counterpoint:
Upper: F4
Lower: C4
Interval: Perfect fourth - DISSONANT in this context ✗

In three-voice texture (upper voices):
Voice 1: F4
Voice 2: C4
(Bass: C3)
Interval: Perfect fourth - consonant between upper voices ✓
```

### From Repertoire

Fux _Gradus ad Parnassum_ exercises: Begin/end with perfect consonances, predominantly use imperfect in middle sections. Palestrina masses: Perfect consonances at cadence points, imperfect consonances predominate in phrase interiors. Bach inventions: Uses thirds and sixths extensively, perfect fifths/octaves structurally significant. Mozart/Haydn string quartets: Balance perfect and imperfect according to contrapuntal principles.

## Common Confusions

- Perfect ≠ "better" (just more stable, not higher quality)
- Perfect fourth is dissonant in two-voice texture (special case!)
- Imperfect consonances preferred in middle (not perfect)
- Parallel perfect consonances forbidden; parallel imperfect limited to three
- Perfect consonances at structural boundaries (beginning, end, cadences)
- Unison is perfect consonance but avoided except at beginning/end
- "Perfect" refers to interval quality (acoustically simple), not aesthetic judgment
- Direct fifths/octaves different from parallel (less severe but still avoid in strict style)
- Compound intervals (10ths, 12ths, 13ths) follow same principles as simple
- Two different perfect consonances in row (P8-P5) acceptable, unlike two same (P5-P5)
- Perfect consonances create strong tonal fusion (voices blend) - good for cadences, limits independence in middle
- Three in a row maximum for imperfect = consecutive bars, not just any three in exercise
