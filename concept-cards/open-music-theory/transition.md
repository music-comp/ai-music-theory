# Quick Definition
A connective auxiliary section that leads away from the main section toward a contrasting section (A→B), often introducing modulation, instability, and energy gain, and ending with attainment of the new key's dominant.

# Core Definition
A **transition** connects two core sections where "the upcoming section is not the initiation of a large-scale return (i.e., between A and B, not between B and A)." Transitions introduce instability and energy gain. They often modulate to prepare a section in a new key. Near their end, transitions often "drive toward attaining the dominant chord of the upcoming key" -- sometimes called "standing on the dominant" (Caplin) or "dominant lock" (Hepokoski/Darcy). Transitions come in large (with complete phrases) and small (without) varieties. In sonata form, transitions may be **dependent** (derived from P) or **independent** (new material) (Ch. 5).

# Key Properties
1. Connects A to B (not B to A; that's a retransition)
2. Introduces instability and energy
3. Often modulates to prepare new key
4. May end with dominant lock / standing on the dominant
5. Large or small (with or without complete phrases)
6. In sonata form: dependent (from P) or independent (new material)
7. May have clear stopping point, caesura fill, or elision with next section

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Look for passage between two core sections going from A to B
2. Check for increased instability (chromaticism, sequences, modulation)
3. Note if it drives toward the dominant of the upcoming key

# Context & Application
Transitions are essential in sonata form (between P and S), rondo form (between refrain and episode), and ternary form (between A and B sections).

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 5): Mozart K. 333, I -- transition between primary and secondary themes (0:20-0:42).
