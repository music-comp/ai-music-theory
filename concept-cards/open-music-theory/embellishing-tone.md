# Quick Definition

An embellishing tone is a note that does not belong to the underlying chord, typically the middle note of a three-note gesture where the first and last notes are consonant with the bass. They are classified into three categories: stepwise (passing tone, neighbor tone), involving a leap (appoggiatura, escape tone), and involving static notes (suspension, retardation, pedal, anticipation).

# Core Definition

**Embellishing tones** (also called non-chord tones) embellish notes that belong to the chord (OMT, Ch. IV.4). In nearly all cases, an embellishing tone is the middle note of a three-note gesture in which the first and last notes are consonant with the bass. The embellishing tone itself may be consonant or dissonant with the bass but is a note that does not belong to the underlying chord.

Three categories:
1. **Involving only stepwise motion**: passing tone (PT), neighbor tone (NT)
2. **Involving a leap**: appoggiatura (APP), escape tone (ET)
3. **Involving static notes**: suspension (SUS), retardation (RET), pedal (PED), anticipation (ANT)

# Prerequisites

- **Voice leading** — Embellishing tones are understood within the context of voice-leading principles
- **Consonance** — Distinguishing chord tones from non-chord tones requires understanding consonance and dissonance

# Key Properties

1. Middle note of a three-note gesture (except anticipation, which is two-note)
2. First and last notes are consonant with the bass
3. The embellishing tone itself does not belong to the underlying chord
4. May be consonant or dissonant with the bass
5. Eight types across three categories
6. Each type defined by its approach (step, leap, or static) and departure pattern

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify an Embellishing Tone:
1. Determine the chord tones for the current harmony
2. Any note not belonging to the chord is a potential embellishing tone
3. Examine the approach (how the voice arrives at the non-chord tone): step, leap, or static
4. Examine the departure (how the voice leaves): step in same/opposite direction, leap, or step down/up
5. Match the pattern to the type (see summary table in source, Example 13)

# Context & Application

- **Typical contexts**: All voice parts in tonal music, from simple two-voice textures to full orchestral writing
- **Common applications**: Adding melodic interest, creating local dissonance and resolution, filling in gaps between chord tones
- **Historical/stylistic notes**: The source uses examples from Szymanowska, Boulogne, Lang, and Casson to demonstrate embellishing tones in common-practice music.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Labeling a chord tone as an embellishing tone
  **Correction**: Verify the note does not belong to the underlying harmony before labeling it

- **Error**: Confusing the categories (e.g., calling an appoggiatura a suspension)
  **Correction**: Check approach and departure patterns systematically

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking embellishing tones must be dissonant
  **Clarification**: An embellishing tone may be consonant with the bass; the key criterion is that it does not belong to the underlying chord

- **Confusion**: Believing anticipations follow the three-note pattern
  **Clarification**: Anticipations are a two-note gesture (the only exception to the three-note pattern)
