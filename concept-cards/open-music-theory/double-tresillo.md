## Quick Definition

An expanded tresillo pattern that doubles the 3+3+2 grouping across four beats, resulting in a 3+3+3+3+2+2 subdivision pattern.

## Formal Definition

The double tresillo (term from Nicole Biamonte) extends the basic tresillo pattern by doubling its length. Where the standard tresillo divides two beats as 3+3+2 sixteenth notes, the double tresillo divides four beats as 3+3+3+3+2+2 sixteenth notes. This creates an extended syncopated pattern that maintains the tresillo's characteristic near-triplet feel over a longer span.

## Musical Context

The double tresillo creates a more complex rhythmic layer than the basic tresillo, often appearing in guitar or keyboard parts against a steady beat in the drums. The pattern emphasizes the tension between the irregular groupings (3+3+3+3) and the underlying pulse (2+2+2+2+2+2+2+2), only resolving with the final 2+2 grouping.

### Pattern Structure

Over four beats (16 sixteenths):
- Attacks at: 1, 4, 7, 10, 13, 15 (counting sixteenths 1-16)
- Groupings: 3+3+3+3+2+2

## Examples

### Basic Example

In 4/4 time over one measure:
- Attack 1 (beat 1)
- Attack 2 (last sixteenth of beat 1)
- Attack 3 (second half of beat 2)
- Attack 4 ("a" of beat 2/first sixteenth of beat 3)
- Attack 5 (beat 4)
- Attack 6 (second half of beat 4)

## Common Confusions

- **Double tresillo vs. two tresillos**: The double tresillo is a single expanded pattern (3+3+3+3+2+2), not simply two tresillos in sequence (which would be 3+3+2+3+3+2)
- **Resolution point**: The pattern resolves on the final two groups (2+2), re-aligning with the underlying beat structure
