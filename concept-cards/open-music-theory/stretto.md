# Stretto

## Quick Definition

A contrapuntal technique in fugue where complete subject entries overlap before the previous statement finishes, with the second voice entering before the first voice completes the subject, creating increased density and intensification typically used in development sections or climactic passages to build excitement toward structural goals.

## Formal Definition

**Stretto** (Italian: "tightening" or "compression") is an imitative technique where two or more voices present the fugue subject (or answer) in overlapping succession, with subsequent entries beginning before the previous entry has concluded.

**Key characteristics**:
- **Overlapping subjects**: Complete subject/answer statements, not just motives
- **Compression**: Temporal spacing between entries reduced from exposition
- **Intensification**: Creates increased contrapuntal density and excitement
- **Development technique**: Typically appears after exposition, not during
- **Climactic function**: Often used to build toward important structural moments
- **Voice independence maintained**: Each voice presents complete or near-complete subject
- **Intervallic consistency**: Subject interval structure preserved (unlike sequence)

**Types of stretto**:
1. **Simple stretto**: Two voices overlap
2. **Multiple stretto**: Three or more voices overlap simultaneously
3. **Stretto maestrale** (master stretto): All voices participate in overlapping entries
4. **Partial stretto**: Later entry begins partway through previous entry
5. **Continuous stretto**: Chain of overlapping entries

**Temporal relationships**:
- **Strict stretto**: Fixed time interval between entries (e.g., always 2 beats apart)
- **Free stretto**: Varying time intervals between entries
- **Close stretto**: Minimal spacing (1-2 beats)
- **Wide stretto**: Larger spacing (but still overlapping)

**Intervallic relationships**:
- **Stretto at the octave**: Second entry octave above/below first
- **Stretto at the fifth**: Second entry fifth above/below first
- **Stretto at other intervals**: Second, fourth, sixth, etc. (less common)

## Stretto vs. Related Techniques

**Stretto vs. Canon**:
- **Stretto**: Temporary overlapping, doesn't continue throughout piece
- **Canon**: Continuous imitation throughout entire section/piece
- **Stretto**: Used for climactic effect in fugue development
- **Canon**: Formal structural principle of entire work

**Stretto vs. Episode**:
- **Stretto**: Complete subject/answer entries overlapping
- **Episode**: Sequential treatment of subject motives (fragmentation)
- **Stretto**: Presents full thematic material
- **Episode**: Uses derived motivic material transitionally

**Stretto vs. Exposition**:
- **Stretto**: Overlapping entries (compression)
- **Exposition**: Sequential entries with complete statements before next entry
- **Stretto**: Development/climactic function
- **Exposition**: Presentational function

## Musical Context

Stretto serves specific purposes in fugue composition:
- **Developmental intensity**: Increases contrapuntal activity and excitement
- **Climactic build-up**: Creates momentum toward cadences or structural goals
- **Textural density**: Adds complexity through overlapping complete subjects
- **Formal articulation**: Marks important structural moments (climax, coda, final section)
- **Compositional display**: Demonstrates contrapuntal mastery and planning
- **Harmonic compression**: Multiple tonal centers active simultaneously

Composers often design fugue subjects specifically to allow stretto treatment.

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple stretto (two voices)**:
```
Subject: C-D-E-F-G (5-note subject, 4 beats total)

Voice 1: C—D—E—F—G  (beats 1-4)
Voice 2:     C—D—E—F—G  (beats 2-5, enters at beat 2)

Voice 2 enters after 2 beats, while Voice 1 still on F-G
Overlap: 2 beats (Voice 1: F-G concurrent with Voice 2: C-D)
```

**Stretto at the fifth**:
```
Voice 1 (tonic):     C-D-E-F-G
Voice 2 (dominant):  G-A-B-C-D (enters before Voice 1 finishes)

Intervallic relationship: Fifth above
Temporal relationship: Voice 2 enters partway through Voice 1
```

**Multiple stretto (three voices)**:
```
Voice 1: [Subject begins]___________
Voice 2:    [Subject begins]________
Voice 3:       [Subject begins]_____

All three voices present subject simultaneously, overlapping
Creates dense contrapuntal texture
```

**Stretto maestrale**:
```
Four-voice fugue:
Voice 1 (soprano):  [Subject]_________________
Voice 2 (alto):        [Subject]______________
Voice 3 (tenor):          [Subject]___________
Voice 4 (bass):              [Subject]________

All four voices participate in stretto
Typically used for final climax or coda
```

**Close vs. wide stretto**:
```
Close stretto:
Voice 1: C-D-E-F-G
Voice 2:  C-D-E-F-G (1-beat separation)

Wide stretto:
Voice 1: C-D-E-F-G
Voice 2:       C-D-E-F-G (3-beat separation, but still overlaps)
```

### From Repertoire

**J.S. Bach, Well-Tempered Clavier, Book I, Fugue in C# Minor (BWV 849)**: Multiple stretto passages build intensity, with close stretto at measure 31 creating climactic density before final cadence.

**Bach, Fugue in E-flat Major, BWV 552 ("St. Anne")**: Famous stretto maestrale in final section, all voices entering in close succession to create triumphant conclusion.

**Bach, Art of Fugue, Contrapunctus VII**: Entire fugue demonstrates various stretto techniques at different intervals and temporal spacings.

**Handel, Messiah, "And with His Stripes"**: Choral fugue employs stretto to intensify text expression at climactic moments.

**Mozart, Requiem, Kyrie fugue**: Uses stretto in development to build dramatic intensity before return and final cadence.

## Common Confusions

- Stretto = overlapping complete subjects, not just any imitation
- Stretto ≠ canon (stretto temporary, canon continuous throughout)
- Stretto ≠ episode (stretto uses complete subjects, episode fragments motives)
- "Stretto" (tightening) describes compression of entry timing, not tempo
- Stretto typically in development sections, rarely in initial exposition
- Subject must be designed to allow stretto (not all subjects work in stretto)
- Stretto can occur at various intervals (octave, fifth, etc.)
- Multiple stretto = more than two overlapping voices simultaneously
- Stretto maestrale involves all fugue voices, not just some
- Partial stretto = later entry begins partway through earlier one (doesn't wait for end)
- Stretto adds intensity through compression, not through speed increase
- Close stretto ≠ fast tempo—refers to short time between entries
- Free vs. strict stretto: varying vs. fixed intervals between entries
