# Turnaround

## Quick Definition

A chord progression at the end of a phrase or section that leads back to the beginning, typically using a I-vi-ii-V pattern or its variations.

## Formal Definition

A **turnaround** is a progression that creates harmonic momentum to "turn around" and return to the tonic. The basic diatonic turnaround is:

**I - vi - ii - V - I**

Common variations include:
- **Tonicized turnaround**: Replace vi with V7/ii, then precede that with ii/ii:
  - I - iii7 - VI7 - ii - V - I (where iii7-VI7 is ii-V/ii)
- **With tritone substitutions**: Replace any dominant with its tritone sub
- **All dominants**: I7 - VI7 - II7 - V7 (each chord becomes applied V7)
- **Chromatic bass**: Use tritone subs to create stepwise bass motion

The turnaround typically occupies the last two or four bars of a form before it repeats.

## Musical Context

Turnarounds are essential in jazz because most jazz performance involves repeating forms (head-solos-head). Without a turnaround, the return to the top would feel abrupt. The turnaround creates forward motion and signals to the band that a new chorus is beginning.

Turnarounds are also a common site for substitutions and embellishments, allowing performers to personalize their harmonic approach.

## Examples

### Basic

In C major:
- **Diatonic**: Cmaj7 - Am7 - Dm7 - G7 - Cmaj7
- **With applied chords**: Cmaj7 - A7 - D7 - G7 - Cmaj7
- **Tonicized ii**: Cmaj7 - Em7 - A7 - Dm7 - G7 - Cmaj7

### From Repertoire

Turnarounds appear at the end of virtually every jazz standard's form. "Rhythm Changes" (based on "I Got Rhythm") features a famous turnaround (I-VI7-ii-V) that has become a standard pattern. Blues progressions often end with a V7 or more elaborate turnaround.

## Common Confusions

- A turnaround is NOT just any ending; it specifically leads BACK to the beginning
- The vi chord can be replaced with V7/ii because they share the same root
- Turnarounds can be as simple as V7 or as complex as multiple chord substitutions
- In notation, turnarounds may or may not be written out; performers often improvise them
- The final chord of a turnaround overlaps with the first chord of the new chorus
