# Quick Definition
A seamless orchestral crescendo achieves a continuous, gradual intensification by subtly adding instruments one at a time, often on a sustained pitch, progressing from spectrally simple instruments (muted horn) to spectrally rich ones (heavy brass), creating a crescendo of both volume and timbral brilliance.

# Core Definition
Some composers transform the step-by-step orchestral crescendo into an extremely subtle, nearly imperceptible process. Wagner's Parsifal adds a higher, brighter instrument at the peak of each crescendo. Berg's Wozzeck murder scene (Act III) builds a crescendo on a single pitch (B3) from pppp muted horn (nearly pure fundamental, minimal upper partials) through muted violin, bass clarinet, and progressively more instruments, culminating in heavy brass with maximum spectral content. Cogan described the beginning as "almost entirely fundamental" -- a near-pure sine wave. The crescendo operates not just in volume but in spectral richness.

# Key Properties
1. Gradual, nearly imperceptible instrument additions
2. Progression from spectrally simple to spectrally rich
3. Crescendo of timbral brilliance, not just volume
4. Muted horn = least spectrally active orchestral instrument
5. Heavy brass = most spectrally brilliant
6. Can operate on a single sustained pitch

# Context & Application
The iconic example is Berg's Wozzeck B3 crescendo, but Wagner and other composers used similar techniques more subtly.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Berg, Wozzeck, murder scene): B3 crescendo from pppp muted horn to fff full orchestra, representing a spectral progression from pure fundamental to full harmonic series.
**Example 2** (Wagner, Parsifal): Higher, brighter instrument added at crescendo peaks.
