# Theme and Variations

## Quick Definition

A formal structure where an initial theme (typically a simple, complete melody with clear phrase structure) is followed by a series of modified versions (variations) that preserve the theme's essential harmonic and/or melodic structure while altering surface features like rhythm, melody, texture, harmony, mode, tempo, or character—creating a unified movement through transformation of a single idea.

## Formal Definition

**Theme and Variations** (also "air and variations" or "double" in Baroque) is a form based on the principle of presenting a theme followed by a succession of variations that elaborate, transform, or reimagine the original material while maintaining recognizable connections to it.

**Essential components**:

**Theme**:
- **Character**: Usually simple, clear, memorable melody
- **Structure**: Complete form (often period, sentence, or binary)
- **Harmony**: Clear harmonic progression (often I-IV-V-I or similar)
- **Length**: Typically 8-32 measures
- **Source**: Original composition or borrowed tune (folk song, aria, etc.)

**Variations** (each typically same length as theme):
- **Melodic variation**: Ornament, embellish, or transform melody
- **Harmonic variation**: Alter chords, add chromaticism, change mode
- **Rhythmic variation**: Change note values, meter, or rhythmic patterns
- **Textural variation**: Change accompaniment, voicing, or instrumentation
- **Character variation**: Change mood, tempo, or dynamic level
- **Structural variations**: Maintain phrase structure and harmonic framework

**Number of variations**: Variable (commonly 3-10, sometimes many more)

## Types of Variation

**Continuous variations** (Baroque):
- Ground bass or ostinato as foundation
- Variations unfold continuously over repeated bass
- Examples: Chaconne, Passacaglia
- No clear breaks between variations

**Sectional variations** (Classical/Romantic):
- Each variation is self-contained section
- Clear articulation between variations
- Theme stated fully, then each variation
- Most common in Classical repertoire

**Character variations**:
- Each variation has distinct character or mood
- May include mode changes (major → minor)
- Tempo changes between variations
- Dynamic and textural contrasts

**Double variations**:
- Two themes alternate with variations
- Pattern: Theme A, Var. A1, Theme B, Var. B1, Var. A2, Var. B2, etc.
- Haydn frequently used this approach

**Free variations** (Romantic):
- Looser connection to theme
- Harmonic structure may be freely altered
- Theme fragmented or transformed beyond recognition
- More developmental than decorative

## Common Variation Techniques

**Melodic variation**:
- **Ornamentation**: Add passing tones, neighbor tones, turns, trills
- **Figuration**: Arpeggiate melody, add scale runs
- **Contour change**: Alter melodic contour while keeping harmony
- **Inversion**: Flip melodic intervals

**Harmonic variation**:
- **Chromaticism**: Add chromatic chords, secondary dominants
- **Mode change**: Major → minor or vice versa ("mode variation")
- **Reharmonization**: Different chords under same melody
- **Harmonic rhythm**: Change frequency of chord changes

**Rhythmic variation**:
- **Diminution**: Faster note values (quarters → eighths → sixteenths)
- **Augmentation**: Slower note values (rare in variations)
- **Meter change**: 4/4 → 3/4 or 6/8
- **Rhythmic patterns**: Add characteristic rhythms (march, waltz, etc.)

**Textural variation**:
- **Accompaniment**: Change accompaniment figure (Alberti bass, arpeggios, etc.)
- **Voice**: Move melody to different voice (soprano → bass)
- **Counterpoint**: Add contrapuntal lines
- **Instrumentation**: Change instruments (in orchestral variations)

**Character variation**:
- **Tempo**: Allegro → Adagio
- **Dynamics**: Piano → forte, or vice versa
- **Style**: March, waltz, fugue, chorale, etc.
- **Mood**: Playful → serious → triumphant

## Typical Variation Set Structure

**Classical variation set**:
```
Theme (simple statement)
Variation 1 (melodic ornamentation, maintaining character)
Variation 2 (rhythmic activation, faster notes)
Variation 3 (minor mode, contrasting character)
Variation 4 (contrapuntal, perhaps fugal)
Variation 5 (slow, expressive)
Variation 6 (finale: fast, brilliant, possibly longest/most elaborate)
```

**Common patterns**:
- Early variations stay close to theme
- Middle variations explore contrasts (mode, tempo, character)
- Final variation often most virtuosic or returns to original character
- Slow variation often placed near end for emotional contrast
- Final variation may include coda

## Musical Context

Theme and variations serves specific purposes:
- **Unity through transformation**: Explores single idea thoroughly
- **Compositional display**: Shows imagination and technical skill
- **Pedagogical value**: Teaches variation techniques
- **Accessibility**: Clear theme helps listeners follow variations
- **Structural flexibility**: Can be standalone movement or part of larger work
- **Historical continuity**: Used from Baroque through present

Common in slow movements, standalone keyboard works, and orchestral movements.

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple theme** (8-measure period):
```
Theme (C major, 4/4):
Measures 1-4 (antecedent): I - IV - V - HC (half cadence)
Measures 5-8 (consequent): I - IV - V - PAC (authentic cadence)

Clear, simple harmonic structure forms basis for variations
```

**Variation 1** (melodic ornamentation):
```
Same harmonic progression (I-IV-V-HC, I-IV-V-PAC)
Melody decorated with neighbor tones, passing tones
Eighth notes replace quarter notes
Character: more elaborate but still recognizable
```

**Variation 2** (rhythmic activation):
```
Same harmonic progression
Melody in sixteenth notes with Alberti bass accompaniment
Character: more animated, virtuosic
```

**Variation 3** (minor mode):
```
Harmonic progression: i-iv-V-HC, i-iv-V-PAC (parallel minor)
Melody adapted to minor mode (^3, ^6, ^7 lowered)
Character: darker, more serious
```

**Variation 4** (contrapuntal):
```
Canon between soprano and bass
Same harmonic foundation
Character: learned, fugal style
```

**Variation 5** (Adagio):
```
Tempo slows, longer note values
Expressive ornamentation, rubato
Character: lyrical, contemplative
```

**Variation 6** (Finale):
```
Fast tempo (Presto), virtuosic figuration
Brilliant passagework, dramatic
May include coda extending the variation
Character: triumphant conclusion
```

### From Repertoire

**Mozart, Piano Sonata in A Major, K. 331, I**: Famous theme and variations opening, six variations on a simple theme, includes minor-mode variation, various character changes, graceful conclusion.

**Beethoven, "Diabelli Variations," Op. 120**: 33 variations on a waltz theme by Diabelli, monumental work exploring every conceivable variation technique, some variations barely recognizable as related to theme.

**Brahms, Variations on a Theme by Haydn, Op. 56a**: Orchestral variations, uses "St. Anthony Chorale," demonstrates orchestral variation techniques, includes a finale based on ground bass (synthesis of two variation types).

**Haydn, Symphony No. 103 ("Drumroll"), II**: Slow movement in double variation form, two themes alternating with variations, shows Haydn's formal inventiveness.

**Bach, Goldberg Variations, BWV 988**: 30 variations on a bass line (ground bass variation), every third variation is canon, culminates in quodlibet, pinnacle of Baroque variation technique.

**Mozart, Twelve Variations on "Ah vous dirai-je, Maman," K. 265**: Variations on "Twinkle, Twinkle Little Star," demonstrates various keyboard figuration patterns, pedagogical value.

## Related Concepts

- **Prerequisite**: phrase, period, sentence, binary-form, harmonic-progression, fragmentation, texture, mode
- **Leads to**: ground-bass, chaconne-passacaglia, transformation
- **See also**: double-variations

## Common Confusions

- Theme should be simple and clear (elaborate themes difficult to vary effectively)
- Variations preserve harmonic/structural framework (free variations may not)
- Each variation typically same length as theme (structural parallelism)
- Not every ornamented repeat is a variation (must be structural principle of movement)
- Continuous vs. sectional variations: continuous flows without breaks (Baroque); sectional has clear divisions (Classical)
- Ground bass variation = bass line repeated, melody varies above
- Chaconne/Passacaglia = specific types of continuous variation
- Double variations = two alternating themes, each with variations
- Mode variation (major → minor) common but not required
- Final variation often most elaborate or returns to simplicity
- Free variations (Romantic) may depart significantly from theme
- Variations can change tempo between sections (not just within)
- Number of variations variable (no standard count)
- Borrowed themes common (folk songs, popular tunes, other composers)
- Variation form used as standalone work or movement in larger work
- Theme need not be composer's original (Mozart varied many folk songs)
