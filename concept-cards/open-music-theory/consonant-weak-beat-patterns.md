# Consonant Weak-Beat Patterns (Second Species)

## Quick Definition

Seven standard melodic patterns for consonant weak beats in second-species counterpoint, providing variety in the 2:1 rhythmic texture while maintaining smooth voice leading between consonant downbeats, including consonant passing tone, substitution, skipped passing tone, interval subdivision, change of register, delay of melodic progression, and consonant neighbor tone.

## Formal Definition

In second-species counterpoint, weak beats may be either consonant or dissonant. **Consonant weak-beat patterns** are standardized melodic formulas that connect two consonant downbeats with consonant intervals throughout, offering alternatives to the dissonant passing tone and promoting variety in the counterpoint line.

**Seven standard patterns**:

### 1. Consonant Passing Tone
- Outlines a third from downbeat to downbeat
- Same pattern as dissonant passing tone, but all three notes consonant with cantus
- Fills third with stepwise motion
- Always forms sixth or perfect fifth above/below cantus
- Pattern: step-step in same direction

**Example**: Downbeat C5 (8ve) → weak beat D5 (6th) → downbeat E5 (3rd)
All intervals consonant, melodic third filled by step

### 2. Substitution
- Outlines a third from downbeat to downbeat
- Leaps a fourth, then steps in opposite direction
- Substitutes for passing tone when leap or direction change needed
- All three notes consonant with cantus
- Adds variety through leap instead of stepwise motion

**Example**: Downbeat G4 (5th) → weak beat C5 (leap P4 up) → downbeat B4 (step down, 6th)
Outlines third G-B but uses leap instead of stepwise passing

### 3. Skipped Passing Tone
- Outlines a fourth from downbeat to downbeat
- Weak beat divides fourth into third + step
- Leap third, step in same direction (or step, leap third)
- All three intervals consonant (note-to-note and downbeat-to-downbeat)

**Example**: Downbeat C5 (8ve) → weak beat E5 (leap 3rd) → downbeat F5 (step, 3rd)
Outlines fourth C-F with intervening third + step

### 4. Interval Subdivision
- Outlines fifth or sixth between successive downbeats
- Weak beat divides large interval into two smaller consonant leaps
- Fifth divided into two thirds
- Sixth divided into third + fourth (or fourth + third)
- All melodic intervals consonant, all notes consonant with cantus

**Example**: Downbeat C5 (8ve) → weak beat E5 (leap 3rd) → downbeat G5 (leap 3rd, makes 5th total)
Divides fifth C-G into two thirds

### 5. Change of Register
- Large consonant leap (P5, 6th, or 8ve) from strong to weak beat
- Step in opposite direction into following downbeat
- Used to achieve melodic variety after stepwise motion
- Maintains independence by moving away from cantus range
- All notes consonant with cantus

**Example**: Downbeat E5 (3rd) → weak beat C6 (leap 6th up) → downbeat B5 (step down, 6th)
Dramatic leap followed by stepwise recovery

### 6. Delay of Melodic Progression
- Outlines a step from downbeat to downbeat
- Leap third from strong to weak beat, step opposite direction to next downbeat
- "Delays" what would otherwise be simple stepwise motion
- Embellishes slower first-species progression
- All notes consonant

**Example**: Downbeat G4 (5th) → weak beat E5 (leap 6th up) → downbeat F5 (step down, 3rd)
Delays simple G-F stepwise motion with intervening leap

### 7. Consonant Neighbor Tone
- Downbeat and following downbeat same pitch
- Weak beat steps away and returns
- If first downbeat = fifth, neighbor = sixth (and vice versa)
- Stepwise departure and return
- All notes consonant

**Example**: Downbeat G4 (5th) → weak beat A4 (step up, 6th) → downbeat G4 (step back, 5th)
Neighbor embellishment around G4

## General Principles

**All patterns share**:
- Three consonances: first downbeat, weak beat, second downbeat
- Smooth voice leading (primarily stepwise or small leaps)
- Melodic coherence from downbeat to downbeat
- Variety in approach to connecting consonant downbeats

**Choosing patterns**:
- Start with downbeat note, choose following downbeat, select pattern to connect them
- Consider melodic shape and variety needed
- Balance stepwise motion (patterns 1, 7) with leaps (patterns 2-6)
- Use variety of patterns across exercise (not just one type)

**Relationship to dissonant passing tone**:
- Dissonant passing tone (only dissonant weak-beat pattern) fills third by step with dissonance
- Consonant patterns provide alternatives when dissonance not desired/possible
- Mix consonant and dissonant weak beats for variety

## Musical Context

These patterns serve pedagogical and compositional purposes:
- **Second-species pedagogy**: Teaches variety in connecting consonant downbeats
- **Melodic fluency**: Develops multiple approaches to voice leading
- **Real music application**: Patterns appear in faster-moving voices in actual compositions
- **Improvisation foundation**: Provides vocabulary for embellishing harmonic progressions

Understanding these patterns helps composers/students move beyond simple stepwise motion while maintaining consonance and smoothness.

## Examples

### Basic (all in C major, counterpoint above cantus)

**1. Consonant passing tone**:
```
Downbeat 1: C5 over C4 (P8)
Weak beat:  D5 over F4 (6th) - consonant passing tone
Downbeat 2: E5 over C5 (3rd)
Pattern: Fills third C-E with consonant D
```

**2. Substitution**:
```
Downbeat 1: G4 over C4 (5th)
Weak beat:  C5 over F4 (leap P4) - substitution
Downbeat 2: B4 over G4 (step down, 3rd)
Pattern: Substitutes leap for passing tone, outlines third G-B
```

**3. Skipped passing tone**:
```
Downbeat 1: C5 over C4 (8ve)
Weak beat:  E5 over F4 (leap 3rd, makes 7th or adjust cantus - actually 6th if F4)
Downbeat 2: F5 over D4 (step, makes 3rd)
Pattern: Skips passing D, leaps to E, steps to F
```

**4. Interval subdivision**:
```
Downbeat 1: C5 over C4 (8ve)
Weak beat:  E5 over D4 (leap 3rd)
Downbeat 2: G5 over C4 (leap 3rd, total = 5th C-G)
Pattern: Divides fifth into two thirds
```

**5. Change of register**:
```
Downbeat 1: E5 over C4 (3rd)
Weak beat:  C6 over F4 (leap 6th up)
Downbeat 2: B5 over G4 (step down)
Pattern: Large leap up, step back down
```

**6. Delay of melodic progression**:
```
Downbeat 1: G4 over C4 (5th)
Weak beat:  E5 over F4 (leap 6th up)
Downbeat 2: F5 over D4 (step down)
Pattern: Delays simple G-F step with E detour
```

**7. Consonant neighbor tone**:
```
Downbeat 1: G4 over C4 (5th)
Weak beat:  A4 over F4 (step up, 6th)
Downbeat 2: G4 over C4 (step back, 5th)
Pattern: Neighbor A embellishes G
```

### From Repertoire

Fux's _Gradus ad Parnassum_ second-species exercises demonstrate all seven patterns. Mozart/Haydn student counterpoint exercises employ these patterns. Bach's faster-moving contrapuntal lines (eighth notes in inventions) use these patterns at quicker tempo. Palestrina's semibreve motion against breve cantus shows historical precedent.

## Common Confusions

- These are consonant patterns (vs. dissonant passing tone which creates dissonance)
- Not all patterns fill same interval (some thirds, some fourths, some fifths/sixths)
- Consonant passing tone ≠ dissonant passing tone (same motion, different consonance)
- Substitution doesn't "replace" passing tone—it's alternative when leap needed
- Patterns are prototypes, not rigid rules (composers vary them)
- All seven patterns acceptable in second species (mix for variety)
- Not every weak beat must fit one pattern (these are common solutions)
- Skipped passing tone "skips" the passing note with leap
- Change of register used sparingly (dramatic effect)
- Delay of melodic progression outlines step (not third like passing/substitution)
- Consonant neighbor returns to same pitch (not step progression)
- Knowing pattern names helps analyze/compose, but music is more than labels
