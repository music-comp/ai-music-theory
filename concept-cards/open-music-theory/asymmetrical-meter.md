# Quick Definition
Asymmetrical meters contain measures divided into unequal groupings of beats or divisions (e.g., 5/8 as 3+2 or 2+3, 7/8 as 3+2+2 or 2+2+3), creating an uneven metrical pulse that resists regular patterns and can suggest anxiety, playfulness, or folk character.

# Core Definition
In asymmetrical meters, the beats within a measure are not all the same length. Common examples include 5/8, 7/8, 11/8, and 13/8, with beat-division groupings typically indicated by beaming or explicit notation (e.g., (2+2+3)/8). The beat unit can be the eighth note, quarter note, sixteenth note, or other values. Asymmetrical meters are common in folk music traditions (Greek, Balkan, etc.) and were adopted by 20th-century classical and musical theater composers. Sondheim's "Ladies in Their Sensitivities" uses 5/8 to create an "off-kilter" feeling; Brubeck's "Unsquare Dance" uses 7/4.

# Key Properties
1. Unequal beat groupings within the measure
2. Groupings indicated by beaming or explicit notation
3. Common signatures: 5/8, 7/8, 11/8, 13/8
4. Can use various beat units (8th, 4th, 16th)
5. Common in folk music traditions
6. Creates uneven, often energetic pulse

# Context & Application
Asymmetrical meters became common in 20th-century concert music, musical theater, jazz, and film scoring, often evoking folk traditions or creating rhythmic tension.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Sondheim, "Ladies in Their Sensitivities"): 5/8 with 3+2 grouping creates anxious character.
**Example 2** (Dave Brubeck, "Unsquare Dance"): 7/4 (or 7/8) asymmetrical dance.
**Example 3** (Andrew Lloyd Webber, "Skimbleshanks"): 13/8 (3+3+3+2+2) for lively character.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: 5/4 is always asymmetrical
  **Clarification**: It depends on the internal grouping; 5/4 as 3+2 is asymmetrical, but the term "asymmetrical" specifically refers to unequal groupings
