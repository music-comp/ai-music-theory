# Paraphrase Technique

## Quick Definition

A Renaissance compositional method where a pre-existing melody (typically plainchant) is embellished and elaborated with ornamental figures, rhythmic variation, and melodic extensions while preserving its essential melodic contour and identity, then woven into polyphonic texture rather than presented as a strict cantus firmus, creating a flexible integration of borrowed and original material in masses, motets, and other sacred works.

## Formal Definition

**Paraphrase Technique** (also called "paraphrase mass" when applied to mass composition) is a compositional approach where a pre-existing melody—most commonly a plainchant, hymn, or sacred song—is freely embellished and integrated into all voices of a polyphonic texture, rather than being presented literally as a single cantus firmus voice.

**Key characteristics**:
- **Melodic elaboration**: Original melody decorated with passing tones, neighbor tones, turns, and other ornaments
- **Rhythmic transformation**: Chant's free rhythm converted to measured mensurations
- **Melodic extension**: Original phrases expanded through interpolations and sequences
- **Motivic fragmentation**: Melody broken into segments used imitatively
- **Migrating cantus**: Borrowed melody appears in different voices throughout work
- **Contrapuntal integration**: Paraphrased melody integrated with free counterpoint
- **Flexibility**: Freer than strict cantus firmus technique, less elaborate than parody

**Comparison to other techniques**:
- **vs. Cantus firmus**: Paraphrase more flexible, embellished; cantus firmus literal quotation
- **vs. Parody**: Paraphrase uses single melody; parody uses multiple voices from model
- **vs. Imitation**: Paraphrase based on pre-existing material; free imitation creates new themes

## Paraphrase Process

**Methods of elaboration**:
1. **Ornamentation**: Add passing tones, neighbor tones, turns, mordents
2. **Rhythmic variation**: Convert unmeasured chant to rhythmic patterns
3. **Melodic extension**: Add notes between structural pitches
4. **Phrase expansion**: Lengthen phrases through repetition or sequence
5. **Registral variation**: Shift octave registers
6. **Motivic extraction**: Use fragments imitatively across voices
7. **Contrapuntal treatment**: Surround with free counterpoint in other voices

**Structural approaches**:
- **Head motive**: Paraphrased opening becomes motto for all sections
- **Through-paraphrase**: Entire original melody paraphrased systematically
- **Sectional paraphrase**: Different movements or sections paraphrase different portions
- **Migrating paraphrase**: Paraphrase moves between voices (soprano, alto, tenor, bass)

## Musical Context

Paraphrase technique serves specific purposes in Renaissance music:
- **Liturgical connection**: Links mass/motet to specific feast or occasion through chant reference
- **Unification**: Provides thematic coherence across multi-movement work
- **Flexibility**: Allows more compositional freedom than strict cantus firmus
- **Tradition**: Maintains connection to sacred plainchant repertory
- **Artistry**: Demonstrates skill in embellishment and elaboration
- **Accessibility**: Makes chant melodies more recognizable than highly abstract treatments

Particularly prominent in 15th-16th century mass composition.

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple plainchant paraphrase**:
```
Original chant (Kyrie): C - D - E - F - E - D - C

Paraphrased version:
C - D - C - D - E - F - E - E - F - E - D - C - D - C

Additions:
- Neighbor tone C after first D
- Repeated E before F
- Repeated F-E figure
- Repeated D-C at end

Structural pitches (C-D-E-F-E-D-C) preserved
Embellishments added for measured rhythm and ornament
```

**Rhythmic transformation**:
```
Chant (unmeasured, approximate):
C—————D———E—F———E—D———C

Paraphrased (measured 4/4):
♩C ♪D ♩E ♪F ♪E ♩D 𝅗𝅥C

Converts free rhythm to metric pattern
Maintains melodic contour
```

**Motivic fragmentation** (imitative entries):
```
Original chant opening: C - D - E - F

Soprano (m. 1):  C - D - E - F [continues with paraphrase]
Alto (m. 3):     C - D - E - F [imitates opening]
Tenor (m. 5):    C - D - E - F [imitates opening]

Chant head motive used as point of imitation
Each voice paraphrases differently after entry
```

**Registral displacement**:
```
Original chant: C - D - E - F - G (ascending)

Paraphrase: C - D - E - F - G - c (continues octave higher)
            c - d - e (continues in higher register)

Extends range beyond original chant compass
```

**Comparison: Cantus firmus vs. Paraphrase**:
```
Cantus firmus approach:
Tenor (long notes): C————————D————————E————————F————————
Other voices: [free counterpoint, no chant reference]

Paraphrase approach:
All voices: [various paraphrases of C-D-E-F with embellishments]
            [chant melody woven throughout texture]

Paraphrase integrates melody into all voices
```

### From Repertoire

**Guillaume Dufay, Missa Ave Regina Caelorum**: Paraphrases the antiphon *Ave Regina Caelorum* throughout all movements, with elaborate embellishments and migrating cantus among voices.

**Johannes Ockeghem, Missa Ecce Ancilla Domini**: Uses paraphrase technique on the antiphon, distributing embellished chant material across all four voices flexibly.

**Josquin des Prez, Missa Pange Lingua**: Perhaps the most famous paraphrase mass, elaborates the hymn *Pange lingua* with free rhythmic and melodic treatment throughout all movements and voices.

**Palestrina, Missa Aeterna Christi Munera**: Paraphrases the hymn *Aeterna Christi munera* in all voices, demonstrating mature Renaissance paraphrase style.

**William Byrd, Mass for Four Voices**: English example incorporating paraphrase technique in portions of the ordinary.

- **Prerequisite**: cantus-firmus, imitation, fragmentation

## Common Confusions

- Paraphrase technique ≠ exact quotation (melody is embellished and varied)
- Paraphrase uses single pre-existing melody (parody uses multiple voices)
- Freer than cantus firmus (which presents melody literally, usually in long notes)
- All voices may participate in paraphrase (not restricted to one cantus voice)
- "Paraphrase mass" = mass based on paraphrased pre-existing melody
- Borrowed melody maintains recognizable identity despite elaboration
- Not improvisation (carefully composed embellishment)
- Paraphrase can migrate between voices (soprano, alto, tenor, bass)
- Structural pitches of original melody preserved (embellishments added)
- Rhythmic values flexible (chant's unmeasured rhythm becomes measured)
- Common with plainchant sources (hymns, antiphons, sequences)
- Renaissance technique (15th-16th centuries) especially for mass composition
- Less abstract than cantus firmus, more recognizable to listeners
- More flexible than strict cantus firmus, less complex than parody
- Head motive often paraphrased opening of source melody
- May combine with other techniques (cantus firmus, imitation, canon)
