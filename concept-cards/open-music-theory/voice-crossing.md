# Voice Crossing

## Quick Definition

When a lower voice temporarily moves above a higher voice (or vice versa) in multi-voice texture, creating a momentary reversal of normal voice relationships, generally avoided in traditional SATB part-writing.

## Formal Definition

**Voice crossing** occurs in polyphonic music when the relative vertical positions of two voices exchange, with a lower voice moving above a higher voice or a higher voice moving below a lower voice.

**Common crossings**:
- Alto crosses above Soprano
- Tenor crosses above Alto (or Soprano)
- Bass crosses above Tenor (rare)
- Any combination where normal order is reversed

**Traditional part-writing guideline**: Voice crossing is generally avoided in strict SATB style because:
- Makes individual lines harder to hear/follow
- Complicates score reading
- Can obscure harmonic clarity
- Creates potential range issues

**When voice crossing occurs**:
- Special expressive effect desired
- Linear voice leading demands it
- Imitative counterpoint (each voice has melodic integrity)
- Contemporary or experimental textures

**Contrast with voice overlap**: Voice overlap is when a voice leaps past where another voice just was (not simultaneous crossing).

## Musical Context

Voice crossing considerations vary by style:
- **Strict SATB chorale style**: Avoided except for compelling reasons
- **Renaissance polyphony**: More common due to imitative counterpoint
- **Baroque fugues**: Occurs when melodic lines demand independence
- **Romantic music**: Used for expressive/dramatic effect
- **Contemporary music**: Often intentional for textural variety

The guideline to avoid crossing is pedagogical (teaches clear part-writing) rather than absolute musical law.

## Examples

### Basic

**No voice crossing** (C major, typical SATB):
- Soprano: G4 (highest)
- Alto: E4 (second highest)
- Tenor: C4 (second lowest)
- Bass: C3 (lowest)
Normal order maintained: S > A > T > B

**Voice crossing** (Alto crosses above Soprano):
- Soprano: E4
- Alto: G4 (higher than Soprano—crossing!)
- Tenor: C4
- Bass: C3
Altered order: A > S > T > B

**Valid musical reason for crossing**:
- Soprano has long sustained note
- Alto has important melodic line passing above it
- Creates special effect or maintains line integrity

### From Repertoire

Bach's fugues occasionally feature voice crossings when subjects demand independence. Renaissance motets (Palestrina, Lassus) allow more crossing due to imitative counterpoint. Romantic choral music (Brahms) uses crossing for expressive coloring. Strict chorale harmonizations (Bach's 371 chorales) generally avoid crossing.

## Common Confusions

- Voice crossing vs. voice overlap: crossing = simultaneous reversal; overlap = leap past previous pitch
- Not "wrong" per se—context dependent (strict style vs. free counterpoint)
- Voice crossing ≠ poor voice leading (can be intentional and effective)
- More acceptable in instrumental music than vocal SATB style
- Crossing within a chord vs. crossing between chords (both count as crossing)
- One brief crossing is less problematic than sustained crossing
- Voice crossing more acceptable in inner voices (AT) than outer voices (SB)
