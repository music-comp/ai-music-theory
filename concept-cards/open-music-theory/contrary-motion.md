# Contrary Motion

## Quick Definition

When two voices move in opposite directions—one ascending while the other descends.

## Formal Definition

**Contrary motion** is a type of motion between two voices in which the voices move in opposite directions. When one voice ascends, the other descends, and vice versa.

Example:
- Voice 1: C → E (ascending)
- Voice 2: G → D (descending)

Contrary motion is independent of interval size—what matters is the direction of motion, not the distance traveled. The voices can move by step, by leap, or by different interval sizes, as long as they move in opposite directions.

Types of motion between voices:
1. **Contrary**: Opposite directions (this concept)
2. **Parallel**: Same direction, same interval type maintained
3. **Similar**: Same direction, different intervals
4. **Oblique**: One voice moves, the other stays on the same pitch

## Musical Context

Contrary motion is the most valuable type of motion in counterpoint and voice leading because it:
- **Maximizes independence**: The voices are clearly distinct
- **Promotes variety**: Creates diverse contours and harmonic changes
- **Avoids voice-leading problems**: Prevents parallel fifths and octaves
- **Balances range**: Prevents voices from getting too high or too low
- **Creates natural convergence/divergence**: Voices meeting or separating

Pedagogical importance:
- Species counterpoint prefers contrary motion where possible
- Contrary motion is safer than parallel or similar motion
- Beginning and ending phrases often use contrary motion
- The *clausula vera* (standard cadential formula) uses contrary motion

Contrary motion provides the most freedom for composers while maintaining good voice leading.

## Examples

### Basic

Two voices in contrary motion:
- Upper: C → D → E → F
- Lower: C → B → A → G
(Upper ascends, lower descends)

Approaching a perfect octave:
- Upper: B → C (ascending step)
- Lower: D → C (descending step)
(Contrary motion into perfect consonance is good)

### From Repertoire

- Bach chorales: Frequently use contrary motion between soprano and bass
- Mozart: String quartets often feature contrary motion between outer voices
- The "wedge" technique: Voices start together and move outward in contrary motion, or vice versa
- Final cadences: Soprano descends (^2–^1), bass ascends (^2–^1) in contrary motion

## Common Confusions

- Contrary motion doesn't require the same interval size in both voices—only opposite direction
- Both voices can move by step, both can leap, or one can step while the other leaps—all are contrary motion
- Contrary motion into a perfect consonance is allowed (even encouraged); similar motion into perfects is forbidden
- Contrary motion ≠ mirror/inversion: Inversion mirrors interval sizes; contrary motion just requires opposite direction
- Two voices can cross in contrary motion—that's voice crossing, which is generally avoided but doesn't negate the contrary motion
