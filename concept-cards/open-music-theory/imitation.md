# Quick Definition
A contrapuntal technique where two or more parts enter separately with the same melody or a version of it, common at beginnings of movements and new text entries, and the precursor to Baroque fugal writing.

# Core Definition
**Imitation** involves two or more parts entering separately with the same melody or versions of it. The source describes it as "a common practice in 16th-century contrapuntal music, particularly for beginning whole movements and large sections" and "a precursor to the later fugue." Guidelines: pitch intervals between parts usually perfect consonances at section openings; imitate at metrically comparable positions; ensure meaningful overlap between entries; consider how closely to imitate; preserve rhythm and distinctive intervals of the point's opening (Ch. 8).

# Prerequisites
- **Counterpoint** -- Imitation is a contrapuntal technique

# Key Properties
1. Parts enter separately with the same or similar melody
2. Entry intervals usually perfect consonances at movement beginnings
3. Metrically comparable entry points (strong→strong, weak→weak)
4. Meaningful overlap between consecutive entries required
5. Longer melodies transition from strict imitation to free counterpoint
6. Phrases and sections can overlap at cadences

# Construction / Recognition
## To Write:
1. Identify points of imitation (text changes, section beginnings)
2. Choose entry interval (usually perfect consonance)
3. Enter at metrically comparable positions
4. Preserve rhythm and distinctive intervals of opening
## To Identify:
1. Listen for successive entries of similar melodic material
2. Note time gap and pitch interval between entries

# Context & Application
Imitation is central to Renaissance polyphony and Baroque fugal writing. The source provides Lusitano's "Ave spes nostra" as a five-voice example.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 8): Lusitano, "Ave spes nostra" -- five-voice opening with two points of imitation overlapping.
