# Imitation

## Quick Definition

A compositional technique where a melodic idea presented in one voice is restated in another voice, either exactly or with modifications, while the first voice continues with different material.

## Formal Definition

**Imitation** is a contrapuntal technique in which two or more voices enter separately with the same melody, or versions of the same melody. The imitating voice repeats the melodic material introduced by the first voice, often at a different pitch level, while the original voice proceeds with new contrapuntal material.

**Key characteristics**:
- **Successive entries**: Voices enter one after another, not simultaneously
- **Melodic correspondence**: The imitating voice repeats the melodic idea from the first voice
- **Temporal offset**: The second voice begins after the first voice has started
- **Pitch interval**: Often at a perfect consonance (unison, fourth, fifth, or octave), though other intervals are possible
- **Metrical correspondence**: Imitation typically occurs at metrically comparable positions (strong to strong, weak to weak)
- **Variable exactness**: Imitation can be exact or modified (rhythm, intervals, or contour may be adjusted)

**Related concepts**:
- **Point of imitation**: A melodic idea designed to be imitated, often associated with a line of text
- **Tonal answer**: An imitation that adjusts certain intervals (typically fifth ↔ fourth) to maintain tonal coherence
- **Canon**: Strict, continuous imitation where the imitating voice follows the original exactly
- **Fugue**: A later development featuring systematic imitation with specific formal conventions

## Musical Context

Imitation serves crucial structural and expressive functions:

**Structural functions**:
- Opens movements and major sections
- Marks new text entries in vocal music
- Creates coherence through melodic unity
- Builds texture gradually as voices enter
- Provides formal organization

**Expressive functions**:
- Creates dialogue between voices
- Generates forward momentum through overlapping entries
- Adds variety through repetition at different pitch levels
- Unifies texture while maintaining independence

**Historical development**:
- **Renaissance (15th-16th century)**: Imitation becomes the dominant contrapuntal technique in sacred and secular polyphony (Josquin, Palestrina, Lassus)
- **Baroque**: Develops into fugue and other systematized imitative forms (Bach, Handel)
- **Classical/Romantic**: Used in development sections and contrapuntal passages (Beethoven, Brahms)
- **20th/21st century**: Continues in various styles, from neoclassical to minimalism (Stravinsky, Reich)

**Stylistic considerations**:
- **16th century**: Multiple points of imitation per movement, often overlapping
- **Baroque fugue**: Systematic entries following specific tonal plan
- **Canon**: Perpetual strict imitation throughout
- **Free imitation**: Looser adaptation of the melodic idea

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple imitation at the fifth**:
- Voice 1 (soprano): C–D–E–F–E–D–C
- Voice 2 (alto, entering later): G–A–B–C–B–A–G
  (Alto imitates soprano a fifth below)

**Imitation with rhythmic modification**:
- Voice 1: Quarter notes C–D–E–F
- Voice 2: Half notes C–D (same pitches, slower rhythm)

**Point of imitation** (Renaissance motet):
- Text: "Ave Maria"
- Melodic idea: Ascending scalar figure
- All voices enter successively with the same "Ave Maria" melody

### From Repertoire

- **Palestrina, "Ave spes nostra"**: Multiple points of imitation, each associated with a line of text
- **Josquin des Prez, "Ave Maria"**: Systematic imitation throughout
- **Bach, Fugue in C major (WTC I)**: Subject imitated in all voices at specific intervals
- **Bach, Goldberg Variations, Canons**: Strict canonic imitation
- **Mozart, Jupiter Symphony, finale**: Fugal imitation in symphonic context
- **Brahms, Symphony No. 4, finale**: Passacaglia with imitative counterpoint
- **Steve Reich, "Different Trains"**: Minimalist imitation and phase shifting

- **Prerequisite**: interval, counterpoint, texture

## Common Confusions

- Imitation vs. sequence: Imitation occurs between voices (horizontal/temporal); sequence is repetition within a single voice at different pitch levels
- Imitation vs. canon: Canon is strict, continuous imitation; imitation is more general and can be free or modified
- All voices don't have to imitate—often only two or three voices participate in a given point of imitation
- Imitation doesn't have to be exact—rhythm, intervals, or contour can be modified while still being recognizable as imitation
- The interval of imitation can vary—perfect consonances are common but not required
- Points of imitation often overlap—a new point may begin before the previous one finishes
- "Real" vs. "tonal" imitation: Real = exact transposition; tonal = adjusted to fit the key/mode
- Imitation is a technique, not a form—it's used in many forms (motet, fugue, invention, etc.)
