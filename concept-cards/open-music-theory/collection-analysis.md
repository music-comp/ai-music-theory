# Quick Definition
Collection analysis identifies the pitch collections (modes, scales, or other collections) operating in a passage of 20th/21st-century music, determines the tonic or final, characterizes the mode's expressive quality, and traces how collections change over the course of a work.

# Core Definition
Analyzing with modes and collections involves: identifying the pitch collection and its intervallic relationships; determining the tonic using "first, last, loudest, longest" (Cohn/Harrison); recognizing hierarchical levels of pitch importance; and considering melodic shapes and ranges. The analytical process moves from identification to interpretation: How easy was the tonal reading? What does the mode's character suggest? How do mode changes relate to formal structure? Collections carry extramusical associations (whole-tone = dreamlike; octatonic = exotic/magical; phrygian ra = Hispano-Arabic topic). Bartok's From the Island of Bali demonstrates analysis of modes combining multiple collections.

# Prerequisites
- Diatonic modes in 20th-century context and octatonic collection

# Key Properties
1. Identify collection, tonic, hierarchical levels, melodic shapes
2. "First, last, loudest, longest" for finding the tonic
3. Collections carry extramusical associations
4. Modal "modulations" can be close or remote (based on common tones/finals)
5. Mode changes often align with formal boundaries
6. Chromatic notes can exist within modal music

# Context & Application
Collection analysis is an alternative to set-theory analysis for 20th-century music that is not fully "atonal" but also not common-practice tonal. It is especially suited to music influenced by folk traditions, Impressionism, and neoclassicism.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Bartok, From the Island of Bali): Opening combines hands into octatonic or 1:5 distance model; later passages establish G-flat and E-flat as tonics with more clearly modal material.
**Example 2** (Debussy, "Voiles"): Whole-tone collection exploited for dreamlike, rootless quality.
