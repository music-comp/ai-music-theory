# Collection Analysis

## Quick Definition

Collection analysis identifies the pitch collections (diatonic modes, pentatonic, whole-tone, octatonic, hexatonic, acoustic) organizing a passage of 20th/21st-century music, determining pitch-class centers where applicable, and interpreting relationships between collections as the music unfolds. It offers an alternative to set-theory analysis for music that is centric but not traditionally tonal.

## Formal Definition

**Collection analysis**: An analytical approach that:

1. **Identifies the pitch collection(s)** in use
2. **Determines pitch-class centers** (tonics/finals) where present
3. **Tracks collection changes** through a piece
4. **Interprets relationships** between collections and sections

**Four criteria for mode/collection identification** (from church mode theory):
1. The pitch collection and intervallic relationships
2. A tonic/final as referential point
3. Hierarchical relationships between pitches
4. Melodic shapes and range

**When collection analysis is appropriate**:
- Music using non-diatonic collections (octatonic, whole-tone, etc.)
- Modal music without traditional tonal function
- Music mixing multiple collections
- Works where set-class analysis seems too abstract

**Relationship to set theory**: Collections can be understood as set classes (octatonic = 8-28, whole-tone = 6-35), but collection analysis emphasizes scalar/modal properties rather than abstract set relationships.

## Musical Context

Collection analysis bridges tonal and atonal methods. It's useful when:

**The music is centric but not tonal**: A pitch acts as "home" but without V-I motion
**Collections are audible organizing forces**: Passages clearly "in" octatonic or "in" Lydian
**Multiple collections interact**: The piece moves between different scalar resources

**Key analytical questions**:
- What collection is in use? (Identify the pitch material)
- Is there a pitch-class center? How is it established?
- Where do collections change? Do changes align with formal boundaries?
- How do collections relate? (Common tones, complementary sets)

**Establishing pitch-class centers without tonal function**:
- "First, last, loudest, longest" (Cohn's maxim)
- Pedal points and drones
- Metric accent on the candidate tonic
- Cadential arrival on the pitch
- Registral emphasis (highest/lowest pitch)

## Examples

### Basic

**Identifying a collection**:
```
Given pitches: C, D, Eb, E, F#, G, A, Bb

Test against known collections:
- Diatonic? No (has Eb AND E, has F# AND G)
- Whole-tone? No (has both Eb and E)
- Octatonic? Let's check for 1-2-1-2 pattern:
  C-Db-Eb-E-F#-G-A-Bb... YES, it's OCT0,1

Octatonic collection confirmed.
```

**Finding a pitch center**:
```
Passage uses E-F#-G-A-B-C#-D-E

Test: Is this a mode of E?
- Collection: E-F#-G-A-B-C#-D = E Dorian (raised 6th = C#)
- Evidence for E as tonic:
  - Melody starts on E
  - Phrase cadences on E
  - Bass pedal on E
  - E receives longest durations

Conclusion: E Dorian, with E as pitch-class center
```

**Tracking collection changes**:
```
mm. 1-8:   Db pentatonic (Db-Eb-F-Ab-Bb)
mm. 9-16:  Whole-tone WT1 (contains Db-Eb-F-G-A-B)
mm. 17-24: Return to Db pentatonic

The pentatonic is a SUBSET of WT1, creating smooth transition
The collections share Db-Eb-F as common tones
```

### From Repertoire

**Bartok, "From the Island of Bali"**: Analysis reveals:
- Opening: Ambiguous, (0167) tetrachords in each hand combine to form octatonic
- First cadential passage: Clear arrival on Gb, suggesting Gb as temporary center
- Second cadential passage: Eb as center, with E-major-like clarity
- Return: Back to the opening's ambiguity

The analysis shows trajectory from ambiguity to clarity and back.

**Debussy, "Voiles"**: Primarily WT0, but the B section introduces pentatonic material. The whole-tone A sections lack tonal center (floating), while the pentatonic B section suggests Gb as a momentary anchor.

**Stravinsky, Petrushka**: Analysts identify pandiatonic passages (diatonic without tonic), passages in specific modes, and moments of polymodal combination.

## Common Confusions

- **Collection =/= key**: A collection provides pitch material; a key implies functional relationships
- **Pitch center =/= tonic**: A pitch center is established by emphasis, not by V-I function
- **Multiple collections may coexist**: Polymodal or polytonal passages are possible
- **Collections can be subsets of other collections**: Pentatonic is a subset of diatonic
- **Chromatic notes may appear**: One "outside" note doesn't necessarily change the governing collection
- **Set theory and collection analysis are compatible**: Different lenses for similar material
- **Analysis should illuminate, not categorize**: The goal is insight into the music, not just labeling collections
