# Ground Bass

## Quick Definition

A compositional technique (and resulting musical form) in which a bass line repeats continuously throughout a piece or section, while the upper voices vary.

## Formal Definition

A **ground bass** (also called **basso ostinato**) is both a compositional technique and a musical form characterized by a bass line that repeats continually throughout a composition, usually exactly or nearly so. The repeating bass line serves as the foundation for variation and development in the upper voices.

**Characteristics**:
- **Repeating bass**: A specific melodic pattern in the bass voice that cycles throughout
- **Variation above**: Upper voices change with each repetition, creating variety
- **Harmonic variety**: The same bass notes can be re-harmonized differently on each iteration
- **Textural variety**: Rhythmic values, imitation, and melodic material in upper voices varies
- **Length**: The ground pattern typically ranges from 4-8 measures, though longer and shorter grounds exist

**Relationship to related terms**:
- **Ostinato**: Any pattern that repeats throughout in one voice (broader term)
- **Ground bass**: Specific type of ostinato where a bass line repeats (subset of ostinato)
- **Chaconne**: Sub-genre of Baroque ground bass, usually in triple meter
- **Passacaglia**: Sub-genre of Baroque ground bass, usually in triple meter
- Every ground bass is an ostinato, but not every ostinato is a ground bass
- Every chaconne/passacaglia is a ground bass, but not every ground bass is a chaconne/passacaglia

**Creating variety** (Baroque practice):
- **Re-harmonization**: Same bass notes support different chords, suggesting tonicizations of various keys
- **Textural changes**: Variation in rhythmic values (whole notes → eighth notes → sixteenth notes, etc.)
- **Imitation**: Upper voices engage in imitative counterpoint
- **Rhythmic variation**: Introduction of syncopation, dotted rhythms, different metrical levels
- **Meter changes**: Occasional shift between simple and compound meter
- **Avoiding strong cadences**: Subtly join ground iterations by avoiding perfect cadences at seams

## Musical Context

Ground bass has been an enduringly popular compositional constraint:

**Historical development**:
- **Renaissance**: Early examples in dance music
- **Baroque (17th-18th century)**: Peak of popularity (Purcell, Bach, Handel, Corelli)
- **Classical/Romantic**: Less common but still used (Brahms, Haydn variations)
- **20th century**: Continued use in various styles (Britten, jazz, minimalism)
- **Popular music**: Extremely common—repeating bass lines in rock, pop, hip-hop

**Compositional appeal**:
- Provides structural constraint that spurs creativity
- Creates unity through repetition
- Allows focus on variation and development
- Generates momentum through continuous bass motion
- Offers opportunities for harmonic and textural exploration

**Expressive functions**:
- Provides grounding stability while upper voices vary
- Can suggest relentless motion or inevitability
- Creates cumulative intensity through variations
- Often associated with lament (descending chromatic bass) or dance (triple meter grounds)

**Baroque practice** (focus of this chapter):
- Triple meter common (chaconne, passacaglia)
- Extensive use of imitation between upper voices
- Re-harmonization for tonicization and variety
- Textural and rhythmic variation across iterations
- May feature over 20-30 iterations of the ground

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple four-bar ground in D minor**:
- Bass: D–C–B♭–A (descending tetrachord, repeated)
- Iteration 1: Simple block chords above
- Iteration 2: Upper voice uses eighth-note motion
- Iteration 3: Upper voices in imitation
- Iteration 4: Syncopation and suspensions
(And so on, creating variations)

**Eight-bar ground with re-harmonization**:
- Same bass notes harmonized differently each time
- First time: I–V–vi–iii...
- Second time: I–V/IV–IV–ii... (tonicizing IV)

### From Repertoire

**Baroque ground bass works**:
- **Purcell, "Dido's Lament" from *Dido and Aeneas***: Descending chromatic ground, expresses lament
- **Purcell, Chacony in G minor (Z 730)**: Extensive imitation, re-harmonization, over 100 iterations
- **Purcell, Sonata in G minor (Z 807)**: Triple meter, extensive tonicization and imitation
- **Bach, "Crucifixus" from *Mass in B minor* (BWV 232)**: Chromatic descending ground, lament
- **Corelli, La Folia**: Variations on the famous Folia ground bass pattern
- **Pachelbel, Canon in D**: Famous repeating bass pattern (though primarily known for the canon above)

**Later repertoire**:
- **Brahms, Symphony No. 4, finale**: Passacaglia for orchestra
- **Britten, "Lachrymae"**: Modern passacaglia
- **Jazz**: Walking bass lines often function as grounds
- **Popular music**: Countless songs with repeating bass lines (e.g., "Come Together" by The Beatles, numerous hip-hop tracks)

## Common Confusions

- Ground bass vs. ostinato: Ground bass is a specific type of ostinato (bass ostinato); ostinato can occur in any voice
- Ground bass vs. chaconne/passacaglia: All chaconnes and passacaglias are ground basses, but not all ground basses are chaconnes/passacaglias (which have specific characteristics like triple meter)
- The bass doesn't have to remain absolutely identical—slight variations are acceptable, especially at cadences
- "Ground" refers to the bass line that grounds the piece, not "ground" as in earth
- Ground bass is both a technique (using a repeating bass) and a form (a piece structured around a ground)
- Re-harmonization doesn't mean changing the bass notes—it means putting different chords above the same bass notes
- Not all variations forms use ground bass—theme and variations typically varies the melody, not just the bass
- The number of iterations can vary widely—some have just a few, others have dozens
- Ground bass works can be in any meter, though triple meter is common for Baroque chaconnes/passacaglias
- Modern popular music uses ground bass constantly, though it may not be called by that name
