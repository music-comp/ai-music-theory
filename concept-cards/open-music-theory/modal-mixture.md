# Quick Definition
Modal mixture (or borrowing) is the technique of incorporating notes from the parallel key (same tonic, different mode) into a chord progression, most commonly borrowing from the parallel minor in a major-key piece. It changes chord quality but not harmonic function.

# Core Definition
Modal mixture involves borrowing notes from the parallel key. The three scale-degree differences between parallel major and natural minor are: mi/me (^3/lowered-^3), la/le (^6/lowered-^6), and ti/te (^7/lowered-^7). Borrowing these notes changes chord qualities while preserving harmonic function. When a chord root is lowered, a flat sign precedes the Roman numeral (e.g., bVI, bIII, bVII). The technique ranges from a single melodic inflection to extended passages that facilitate modulation to distantly related keys.

# Prerequisites
- Parallel major and minor keys (same tonic, different mode)
- Chord quality and Roman numeral analysis
- Harmonic function (T/PD/D)

# Key Properties
1. Most common direction: major keys borrowing from parallel minor
2. Three borrowed scale degrees: me (lowered-^3), le (lowered-^6), te (lowered-^7)
3. Changes chord QUALITY but not harmonic FUNCTION
4. Chords borrowing only le: iio6, ii-half-dim-7, iv (common predominants)
5. Chords with altered roots: bVI, bIII, bVII (lowered-^3 and lowered-^7)
6. Once mixture enters, it typically persists within a harmonic area
7. Fully borrowed chords preferred over partial borrowing

# Context & Application
Modal mixture serves to add expressive color and emotional depth, particularly creating darkness or poignancy in major keys. Common predominant mixture chords (iio6, ii-half-dim-7, iv) borrow only le from the parallel minor. Chords bVI, bIII, and bVII involve altered roots. Modal mixture can facilitate modulation to distantly related keys through chromatic pivot chords: e.g., iv in the first key becomes ii in a new key. The Picardy third (ending a minor-key piece with a major tonic chord) is the one common use of mixture in minor keys, prevalent in the 16th-17th centuries.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Saint-Saens, "Mon coeur s'ouvre a ta voix" from Samson et Dalila): Borrows le from parallel minor, changing ii4/3 to ii-half-dim-4/3 quality.
**Example 2** (Catalani, "Ebben? Ne andro lontana" from La Wally): Extended modal mixture starting in measure 14 results in modulation from E major to E minor, demonstrating the dramatic effect of starting a phrase in major and ending in minor with the same tonic.
**Example 3** (Large-scale): Bb major to Db major modulation using iv as a chromatic pivot chord.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Modal mixture changes the key.
  **Clarification**: Mixture changes chord quality, not tonic. The tonic remains the same; only chord qualities are altered.
- **Confusion**: The flat sign in bVI means the note is literally a flat.
  **Clarification**: The flat indicates the root is lowered by a half step from its diatonic position, not necessarily a flat note.
- **Confusion**: Minor-to-major borrowing is equally common.
  **Clarification**: Major-to-minor borrowing is far more common. The Picardy third is the main exception.
