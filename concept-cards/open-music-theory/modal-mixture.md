# Modal Mixture

## Quick Definition

Modal mixture (or borrowing) is the harmonic technique of mixing notes from parallel major and natural-minor modes, resulting in changed chord qualities and melodic color to achieve expressive effects not available in the main scale itself—most commonly occurring in major-key pieces where notes from the parallel minor are borrowed.

## Formal Definition

**Modal mixture** involves borrowing notes from the parallel key (same tonic, different mode). The three scale degree differences between parallel major and minor are:

- **mi/me** (^3/lowered-^3)
- **la/le** (^6/lowered-^6)
- **ti/te** (^7/lowered-^7)

**Key characteristics**:
- Most common: major keys borrowing from parallel minor
- Changes chord quality but NOT harmonic function
- Can be used in melodies, individual chords, extended tonicizations, or modulations
- When a chord root is lowered, a flat sign precedes the Roman numeral (e.g., ♭VI, ♭VII)

## Types of Modal Mixture

### Chords Borrowing Only le (lowered-^6)

Common predominant mixture chords that only borrow lowered-^6:

| Chord | Notes in C major | Description |
|-------|------------------|-------------|
| iio6 | D-F-A♭ | Diminished supertonic, first inversion |
| ii∅7 | D-F-A♭-C | Half-diminished seventh chord |
| iv | F-A♭-C | Minor subdominant |

These appear frequently in cadential progressions.

### Chords with Altered Roots

Borrowing lowered-^3 and lowered-^7 generates chords with lowered roots:

| Chord | Notes in C major | Root Alteration |
|-------|------------------|-----------------|
| ♭VI | A♭-C-E♭ | Root lowered from A to A♭ |
| ♭III | E♭-G-B♭ | Root lowered from E to E♭ |
| ♭VII | B♭-D-F | Root lowered from B to B♭ |

The accidental indicates the root is lowered by a half step from its diatonic position.

### Tonic and Dominant Mixture

- **I to i**: Common technique for expressive color
- **V to v**: Less common; when v appears in major, it often indicates extended tonicization or modulation rather than simple mixture

## Voice Leading Patterns

**Mixture typically continues through a harmonic area**:
- Once mixture enters, it often stays until the harmony moves to a new functional category
- Example: If the first predominant chord uses mixture, the second predominant likely includes it as well
- Mixture typically stops when moving from predominant to dominant

**Best practice**: Use fully borrowed chords rather than partial borrowing (e.g., using only lowered-^6 when the chord also uses ^3 is uncommon)

## Picardy Third

The one common use of modal mixture in minor keys: ending a minor-key piece or movement with a major tonic chord.

- Origin of the name "picardy" is unknown
- Common in 16th and 17th centuries
- Faded in popularity in 18th and 19th centuries
- Creates a bright, conclusive ending

## Large-Scale Modal Mixture

Modal mixture can facilitate modulation to distantly related keys through chromatic pivot chords:

**Example**: B♭ major to D♭ major modulation
- Subdominant uses lowered-^6 instead of diatonic ^6
- Creates iv in the first key but ii in the second key
- Smooths what could otherwise be an abrupt modulation

## Musical Context

Modal mixture serves to:
- Add expressive color and emotional depth
- Create darkness or poignancy in major keys
- Facilitate modulations between distantly related keys
- Connect parallel major and minor worlds within a single tonal framework

## Examples

### Basic

**Diatonic progression in C major**:
```
I - ii - V - I
C - Dm - G - C
```

**Same progression with modal mixture in predominant area**:
```
I - ii∅7 - V - I
C - Dm7♭5 - G - C
```

The A♭ (lowered-^6) borrowed from C minor creates the half-diminished quality.

### From Repertoire

**Saint-Saens, "Mon coeur s'ouvre a ta voix" from Samson et Dalila**:
- Simple progression borrowing lowered-^6 from parallel minor
- Changes ii4/3 to ii∅4/3 quality

**Catalani, "Ebben? Ne andro lontana" from La Wally**:
- Extended modal mixture starting in measure 14
- Results in modulation from E major to E minor (parallel minor)
- Dramatic effect of starting phrase in major and ending in minor with same tonic

## Common Confusions

- Modal mixture changes chord QUALITY, not FUNCTION (iv still functions as subdominant)
- Do not confuse with modulation: mixture keeps the same tonic
- The flat sign (♭) before Roman numerals indicates a lowered ROOT, not necessarily a literal flat note
- Major-to-minor borrowing is far more common than minor-to-major borrowing
- Partial borrowing (mixing diatonic and borrowed notes in the same chord) is uncommon
- In solfege: mi becomes me (lowered-^3), la becomes le (lowered-^6), ti becomes te (lowered-^7)
