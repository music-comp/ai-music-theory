# 16-Bar Blues

## Quick Definition

A blues form variant with four four-bar phrases, typically two phrases of tonic followed by subdominant and dominant phrases.

## Formal Definition

The **16-bar blues** is a less common variant of the blues schema, consisting of four four-bar phrases:

**Basic structure**:
- **Phrase 1 (mm. 1-4)**: I7 (tonic, four bars)
- **Phrase 2 (mm. 5-8)**: I7 (tonic, four bars)
- **Phrase 3 (mm. 9-12)**: IV7 (subdominant, four bars) or IV7 to I7
- **Phrase 4 (mm. 13-16)**: V7 to IV7 to I7, or variations with turnaround

**Characteristics**:
- Like 12-bar blues, all chords are typically dominant seventh quality
- More extended tonic area at the beginning
- May or may not include a turnaround at the end
- More prevalent in blues-based rock than in traditional blues

## Musical Context

The 16-bar blues provides a more spacious framework than the 12-bar form, allowing for longer melodic development over each harmony. While less common than the 12-bar blues, it appears in notable songs across blues and rock repertoire.

The additional four bars (compared to 12-bar) typically extend the opening tonic section, giving the form a more deliberate pacing.

## Examples

### Basic

In C:
| C7 | C7 | C7 | C7 |
| C7 | C7 | C7 | C7 |
| F7 | F7 | C7 | C7 |
| G7 | F7 | C7 | C7 |

### From Repertoire

"Hoochie Coochie Man" (Muddy Waters, 1954): A classic example of the 16-bar blues form.

"Surfin' USA" (The Beach Boys, 1963): A 16-bar blues where the first two phrases each begin with two bars of V before two bars of I.

## Common Confusions

- 16-bar blues is NOT simply a 12-bar blues with 4 extra bars; it has its own structure
- The extra bars typically extend the tonic, not other areas
- Like 12-bar blues, the 16-bar form is a schema with many possible variations
- Not all 16-bar songs are 16-bar blues; the specific harmonic pattern defines the form
- The turnaround is optional but common
