# Retransition

## Quick Definition

The concluding portion of the development section in sonata form that prepares the return of the opening theme in the recapitulation, typically featuring prolonged dominant harmony (often as a pedal point), rhythmic intensification, and dramatic buildup through crescendo—creating maximum anticipation and tension before the triumphant or climactic return of the primary theme in the tonic key, marking the formal boundary between development's instability and recapitulation's resolution.

## Formal Definition

**Retransition** (also called "standing on the dominant" or "dominant preparation") is a formal function at the end of the development section characterized by harmonic stasis on the dominant and dramatic buildup toward the recapitulation.

**Essential characteristics**:
- **Location**: Final portion of development section
- **Harmonic function**: Prolongs dominant (V) of home key
- **Goal**: Prepares return of P theme in tonic
- **Character**: Anticipatory, tension-building, climactic
- **Typical features**: Pedal point, crescendo, rhythmic intensification
- **Duration**: Variable (4 measures to 30+ measures in Beethoven)

**Function**: Creates maximum expectation for recapitulation return

## Typical Features

**Dominant pedal**:
- Sustained or repeated V in bass
- Upper voices move above static bass
- Creates harmonic "standing still"
- Tension accumulates

**Dynamic buildup**:
- Crescendo (piano → forte or fortissimo)
- Builds energy toward return
- Dramatic intensification

**Rhythmic intensification**:
- Increased note values (halves → quarters → eighths)
- Metric acceleration
- Rhythmic fragmentation

**Textural buildup**:
- Addition of instruments (orchestral)
- Thickening texture
- Fuller sonority

**Motivic preparation**:
- Fragments of P theme appear
- Hints at returning material
- "Pre-echoes" of recapitulation

**Fermata** (optional):
- Pause on dominant before release
- Creates maximum suspense
- Highlights formal articulation

## Retransition vs. Transition

**Transition (TR)**:
- Connects P to S (within exposition/recapitulation)
- Modulates (I → V in exposition)
- Forward motion, goal-directed to new key

**Retransition (RT)**:
- Connects development to recapitulation
- Remains on V (prolongs dominant)
- Prepares return to tonic, not departure

**Both**:
- Create motion and energy
- Connect formal sections
- Function as "connective tissue"

## Harmonic Strategies

**Simple dominant prolongation**:
```
Development ends: Unstable harmony, remote key
Retransition: V - - - V - - - V - - - (pedal)
Recapitulation: I (P returns)

Sustained or repeated V creates anticipation
```

**Ascending sequence to dominant**:
```
Development: Chromatic sequence builds
Retransition: Sequential rise to V
V pedal established
Crescendo
Recapitulation: I

Sequence drives toward dominant goal
```

**Dominant preparation with voice leading**:
```
Development: ii - V/V - viio7/V
Retransition: V (arrives and prolongs)
Upper voices: Chromatic approach to ^1 and ^3 of tonic
Recapitulation: I

Voice leading prepares tonic arrival
```

**Diminished seventh approach**:
```
Development: viio7 (diminished)
Retransition: Resolves to V, prolonged
Recapitulation: I

Diminished tension releases to dominant, then tonic
```

## Textural Variations

**Pedal point**:
```
Bass: V (sustained for 8 measures)
Upper voices: Fragments of P, S motives
Harmony: All chords over dominant pedal
Effect: Stasis with surface motion

Typical retransition texture
```

**Scalar buildup**:
```
Measures 1-4: Ascending scales, piano
Measures 5-8: Continuing ascent, crescendo
Measures 9-12: Dominant arrival, forte
Measures 13-16: V pedal sustained, fortissimo
Recapitulation: I bursts in

Scalar motion builds energy
```

**Rhythmic acceleration**:
```
Measure 1: Whole notes (slow)
Measure 2: Half notes
Measure 3: Quarter notes
Measure 4: Eighth notes (faster)
Measure 5: Sixteenth notes (fastest)
Measure 6: V (dominant arrived, sustained)
Recapitulation: P returns

Rhythmic compression creates urgency
```

## Musical Context

Retransitions serve critical functions:
- **Formal articulation**: Clearly marks development-recapitulation boundary
- **Dramatic preparation**: Builds anticipation for thematic return
- **Harmonic clarification**: Re-establishes tonic key after development's wandering
- **Tension-resolution**: Maximum tension (V) releases to tonic (I)
- **Listener orientation**: Prepares psychologically for familiar material
- **Climactic moment**: Often most intense point before resolution

The retransition is the "deep breath" before the recapitulation.

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple retransition** (8 measures):
```
Development concludes (m. 85): f minor, unstable

Retransition (mm. 86-93):
mm. 86-89: V pedal begins (G in bass, C major context)
mm. 90-91: Upper voices fragment P motive
mm. 92-93: Crescendo to forte, dominant sustained

Recapitulation (m. 94): P returns in C major, tonic

Eight-bar dominant pedal with crescendo
```

**Extended retransition with buildup**:
```
Development ends (m. 120): Chromatic, unstable

Retransition (mm. 121-145, 24 bars!):
mm. 121-128: Sequential approach to V
mm. 129-136: V pedal established, piano
mm. 137-140: Fragments of P over pedal, crescendo begins
mm. 141-144: Fortissimo, full orchestra on V
mm. 145: Fermata on V chord

Recapitulation (m. 146): P bursts in, tonic, triumphant

Extended preparation creates maximum anticipation
```

**Beethoven-style retransition**:
```
Development (chromatic, distant keys)

Retransition:
1. Diminished seventh chord sequence
2. Arrives on V
3. V pedal (20 measures!)
4. Crescendo from pianissimo to fortissimo
5. Rhythmic acceleration
6. Timpani roll on V
7. [Fermata]

Recapitulation: Explosive return of P

Dramatic, theatrical preparation
```

**Brief retransition**:
```
Development ends: viio7
Retransition (2 bars): V (dominant, half cadence)
Recapitulation: I (immediate return)

Minimal retransition, direct preparation
```

### From Repertoire

**Mozart, Symphony No. 40, I, mm. 152-163**: Retransition begins with ascending sequence, arrives on V pedal, crescendo buildup, clear preparation for recapitulation return in g minor.

**Beethoven, Symphony No. 5, I, mm. 228-247**: Extended retransition with famous "false recapitulation" (horn enters early with P theme in wrong key), actual retransition continues with dominant buildup to m. 248 return.

**Beethoven, Symphony No. 3 ("Eroica"), I, mm. 338-397**: Monumental retransition (60 measures!), complex harmonic preparation, builds through enormous crescendo, timpani emphasize dominant, triumphant return.

**Haydn, Symphony No. 104, I, mm. 138-150**: Classical retransition, clear dominant preparation, crescendo, witty touches characteristic of Haydn.

**Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 545, I, mm. 37-41**: Pedagogical example, brief but clear retransition, simple V pedal preparation, demonstrates function efficiently.

## Common Confusions

- Retransition = end of development, prepares recapitulation return
- Located within development section (not separate formal division)
- Prolonged dominant (V) typical, not tonic
- "Re-transition" = transition back (to tonic and P theme)
- Not same as transition (which modulates away; retransition prepares return)
- Dominant pedal most common harmonic device
- Can be very brief (2 measures) or extended (30+ measures in Beethoven)
- Crescendo typical but not required (can be piano throughout)
- Often includes fermata on V before recapitulation
- Prepares tonic return but doesn't arrive on I (that's recapitulation's job)
- May include fragments of P theme as "preview"
- Creates anticipation, not arrival (arrival is recapitulation)
- Some developments transition smoothly without clear retransition
- Beethoven retransitions often most dramatic (timpani rolls, fermatas, extreme dynamics)
- Classical retransitions typically shorter than Romantic
- "Standing on the dominant" = colloquial term for retransition
- Textural buildup common (thin → thick, piano → forte)
- Rhythmic intensification typical (slower → faster note values)
