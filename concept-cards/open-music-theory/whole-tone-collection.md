# Whole-Tone Collection

## Quick Definition

The whole-tone collection is a six-note symmetrical scale built entirely of whole steps (2-2-2-2-2-2 in semitones). Because all intervals are identical, it has no leading tones, no perfect fifths, and no sense of tonal gravity--only augmented triads and the augmented chord's dreamlike, floating quality. There are only two unique whole-tone collections: WT0 (even pitch classes) and WT1 (odd pitch classes).

## Formal Definition

**Whole-tone collection**: A six-note pitch collection with the following properties:

**Structure**:
- **Interval pattern**: 2-2-2-2-2-2 (all whole steps)
- **Cardinality**: 6 pitch classes
- **Set class**: (02468T), Forte number 6-35

**Only two unique collections** (due to symmetry):
```
WT0: C - D - E - F# - G# - A# (pc 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10)
WT1: C# - D# - F - G - A - B (pc 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11)
```

**Symmetry properties**:
- **Transpositional symmetry**: T2 maps the collection onto itself
- Only 2 unique forms: 12 transposition levels / 6 (period of symmetry) = 2
- Any note can serve as "tonic" with equal validity

**Harmonic content**:
- Augmented triads only (no major, minor, or diminished)
- No perfect fifths or fourths
- Dominant 7th chords with altered fifths (7#5)
- French augmented sixth chords

**What's missing**:
- Semitones (half steps)
- Perfect fifths/fourths
- Major/minor triads
- Leading-tone function

## Musical Context

The whole-tone collection became prominent in Impressionism (especially Debussy) and remained important throughout the 20th century. Its distinctive qualities:

1. **Ambiguity**: No note has more "gravity" than another
2. **Dreamlike quality**: Film/TV often uses whole-tone for dream sequences
3. **Timbral focus**: Without harmonic function, color and texture become primary
4. **Exotic associations**: Often used for magical or otherworldly effects

**Relationship to dominant harmony**: The whole-tone scale fits over dominant 7#5 chords, making it useful in jazz and late-Romantic harmony as an extension of the dominant function.

**Compositional uses**:
- Transitional passages (lack of gravity aids modulation)
- Depicting water, mist, dreams, magic
- Creating tonal ambiguity
- Contrasting with more grounded pitch collections

## Examples

### Basic

**The two whole-tone collections**:
```
WT0 (starting on C):
C - D - E - F# - G# - A# - (C)
Pitch classes: 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 (all even)

WT1 (starting on C#):
C# - D# - F - G - A - B - (C#)
Pitch classes: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 (all odd)

Together, WT0 + WT1 = all 12 pitch classes
```

**Available harmonies in WT0**:
```
Triads: C+, D+, E+, F#+, G#+, A#+
  (All augmented, all equivalent by transposition)

Seventh chords: C7#5, D7#5, E7#5, etc.
  (French augmented sixths also fit)

No major triads, minor triads, diminished triads, or perfect fifths!
```

**Symmetry demonstration**:
```
WT0 transposed by T2:
Original: {0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10}
T2:       {2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 0}
Same collection, different starting point!
```

### From Repertoire

**Debussy, "Voiles"**: Almost entirely whole-tone (WT0 in the A sections), creating the hazy, static quality that evokes sails (or veils) in the wind.

**Debussy, "L'isle joyeuse"**: Whole-tone passages alternate with more grounded material, creating contrast.

**Ravel, "Jeux d'eau"**: Uses whole-tone for water imagery, exploiting its shimmering quality.

**Berg, Wozzeck**: Whole-tone passages accompany moments of psychological dissolution.

**Film scoring**: The "dream sequence" trope often employs whole-tone scales, as in countless Hollywood scores since the 1930s.

## Common Confusions

- **Only TWO unique whole-tone scales exist**: WT0 and WT1 are complements (together = all 12 pcs)
- **No fifths, no triads (except augmented)**: This is the key difference from other collections
- **Any note can be "tonic"**: The uniform structure prevents any pitch from having more gravity
- **WT0 contains even pitch classes, WT1 contains odd**: An easy way to identify which collection
- **"Whole-tone scale" and "whole-tone collection" mean the same thing**: The terms are interchangeable
- **Not just for "exotic" effects**: Whole-tone has structural uses in 20th-century harmony
- **Debussy didn't invent it**: Predecessors include Liszt, Glinka, and others; Debussy popularized it
