# Point of Imitation

## Quick Definition

A contrapuntal technique characteristic of Renaissance polyphony (particularly motets and madrigals) where a brief melodic idea is presented successively in all voices through imitation, with each voice entering in turn while previous voices continue with free counterpoint, creating overlapping entries that establish a complete polyphonic texture before the next point begins—essentially a short, contained imitative section within a larger work.

## Formal Definition

A **point of imitation** is a structural unit in Renaissance polyphonic composition where all voices present the same melodic motive in succession through imitation, typically at the octave or fifth, creating a brief imitative complex before moving to the next point of imitation or to free counterpoint.

**Key characteristics**:
- **Successive entry**: Each voice enters in turn with the same motive
- **Complete participation**: All voices eventually present the motive
- **Imitative relationship**: Later entries echo earlier ones (usually at octave/fifth)
- **Overlapping texture**: Earlier voices continue with free counterpoint while later voices enter
- **Self-contained unit**: Each point is a complete structural section
- **Text-based**: Often corresponds to a phrase or clause of text
- **Chain structure**: Multiple points of imitation create entire work

**Typical structure**:
1. **Voice 1 enters**: Presents motive alone (optional)
2. **Voice 2 enters**: Imitates motive while Voice 1 continues freely
3. **Voice 3 enters**: Imitates motive while Voices 1-2 continue freely
4. **Voice 4 enters**: Imitates motive while Voices 1-3 continue freely
5. **All voices active**: Complete polyphonic texture established
6. **Cadence or transition**: Point concludes, next point may begin

**Intervallic imitation**:
- **At the octave**: Most common (motive transposed up/down octave)
- **At the fifth**: Second most common (motive transposed to dominant)
- **At the fourth**: Occasional (motive transposed to subdominant)
- **Other intervals**: Rare, but possible

**Temporal spacing**:
- **Flexible entry times**: Not strictly regular (unlike canon)
- **Typically 1-4 measures**: Between successive entries
- **Varied according to text/phrase**: Spacing serves expressive needs

**Cadential articulation**:
- Points typically end with cadence (perfect, imperfect, or plagal)
- Cadence may overlap with next point's beginning
- Creates continuous flow through overlapping sections

## Point of Imitation vs. Related Techniques

**vs. Fugue**:
- **Point of imitation**: Brief, one motive per point, multiple points in succession
- **Fugue**: Extended development, single subject developed throughout

**vs. Canon**:
- **Point of imitation**: Free counterpoint after initial imitation
- **Canon**: Continuous strict imitation throughout

**vs. Motet structure**:
- **Point of imitation**: Building block unit
- **Motet**: Complete work composed of multiple points of imitation

## Musical Context

Points of imitation serve specific purposes in Renaissance polyphony:
- **Textural generation**: Creates full polyphonic fabric through successive entries
- **Text expression**: Each text phrase gets own point of imitation
- **Formal structure**: Chain of points creates sectional form
- **Clarity**: Imitation helps listeners track thematic material
- **Vocal independence**: Each voice has moment of melodic prominence
- **Pedagogical model**: Teaches imitative writing and counterpoint

This technique is the primary structural principle of 16th-century sacred vocal music.

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple point of imitation (4 voices, C major)**:
```
Motive: C-D-E-F-E (5-note melodic idea)

Measure 1:
  Soprano: C—D—E—F—E—[continues freely]
  Alto: [rest]
  Tenor: [rest]
  Bass: [rest]

Measure 3:
  Soprano: [free counterpoint]
  Alto: C—D—E—F—E—[continues freely]
  Tenor: [rest]
  Bass: [rest]

Measure 5:
  Soprano: [free counterpoint]
  Alto: [free counterpoint]
  Tenor: C—D—E—F—E—[continues freely]
  Bass: [rest]

Measure 7:
  Soprano: [free counterpoint]
  Alto: [free counterpoint]
  Tenor: [free counterpoint]
  Bass: C—D—E—F—E—[continues freely]

Measure 9-12:
  All voices: [free counterpoint, approaching cadence]

Measure 12: Cadence on C, point concludes
```

**Imitation at the fifth**:
```
Voice 1 (tonic):    C-D-E-F-E (C major)
Voice 2 (dominant): G-A-B-C-B (transposed up fifth, G major area)
Voice 3 (tonic):    C-D-E-F-E (returns to C major)
Voice 4 (dominant): G-A-B-C-B (fifth relationship again)

Alternating tonic/dominant entries common
```

**Multiple points of imitation (motet excerpt)**:
```
Point 1 (text: "Kyrie"):
  All voices enter imitatively with "Kyrie" motive
  Concludes with cadence (measure 8)

Point 2 (text: "eleison"):
  All voices enter imitatively with "eleison" motive (different from Point 1)
  Begins overlapping with Point 1 cadence (measure 7-8)
  Concludes with cadence (measure 16)

Point 3 (text: "Christe"):
  All voices enter imitatively with new "Christe" motive
  Process continues...

Each point = one text phrase = one imitative complex
```

**Entry spacing**:
```
Voice 1: Enters measure 1
Voice 2: Enters measure 3 (2 measures later)
Voice 3: Enters measure 5 (2 measures later)
Voice 4: Enters measure 8 (3 measures later, varied spacing)

Spacing need not be regular (unlike strict canon)
```

### From Repertoire

**Palestrina, Missa Papae Marcelli, Kyrie**: Opens with point of imitation on "Kyrie," all six voices entering successively, followed by new point on "eleison."

**Josquin des Prez, Ave Maria**: Each phrase of text receives its own point of imitation, creating chain structure throughout motet.

**William Byrd, Ave Verum Corpus**: Four-voice texture built through successive points of imitation, each corresponding to text phrase.

**Lassus, Magnificat**: Alternates points of imitation with homophonic passages, points providing polyphonic richness.

**Victoria, O Magnum Mysterium**: Opening point of imitation establishes contemplative mood through slow, overlapping entries in all voices.

## Related Concepts

- **Prerequisite**: imitation, counterpoint, polyphony
- **Leads to**: fugal-exposition, ricercar, canzona

## Common Confusions

- Point of imitation = structural unit, not entire composition
- Each point uses one melodic idea (motive), then moves to next point
- Free counterpoint follows initial imitation (not strict canon throughout)
- All voices must eventually present the motive (not just some voices)
- Text typically drives structure—one point per text phrase
- Imitation usually at octave or fifth (not necessarily at unison)
- Entry spacing flexible, not strictly regular (unlike fugue exposition)
- Points typically cadence, providing structural articulation
- Multiple points create complete work (motet, madrigal, etc.)
- Not same as fugue exposition (fugue develops one subject, points succeed one another)
- "Point" refers to the imitative complex itself, not a specific voice entry
- Renaissance technique (Palestrina, Josquin), not Baroque (though influences fugue)
- Overlap between points common (new point begins before previous cadences)
- Soggetto = the melodic idea being imitated in each point
- Points of imitation characteristic of motet style (sacred/secular vocal works)
