# Ricercar

## Quick Definition

A learned, imitative instrumental composition (primarily for keyboard or ensemble) emphasizing strict contrapuntal treatment of one or more subjects through systematic imitation, inversion, augmentation, diminution, and other contrapuntal devices, characterized by serious scholarly character and rigorous intellectual construction—essentially a "research piece" exploring contrapuntal possibilities in a more abstract, academic manner than the fugue.

## Formal Definition

**Ricercar** (Italian: "to search," "to seek," or "to research") is an instrumental genre emphasizing systematic contrapuntal exploration, developed in the 16th century and culminating in the Baroque period, serving as a precursor to and learned counterpart of the fugue.

**Key characteristics**:
- **Imitative counterpoint**: Systematic use of imitation throughout
- **Single or multiple subjects**: One theme explored thoroughly, or several combined
- **Contrapuntal devices**: Extensive use of augmentation, diminution, inversion, stretto
- **Learned style**: Scholarly, intellectual, rigorous contrapuntal construction
- **Abstract character**: Less virtuosic than toccata, more cerebral than fugue
- **Long note values**: Often slower-moving, contemplative (especially Renaissance)
- **Systematic exploration**: Thorough investigation of contrapuntal possibilities

**Historical development**:
- **Renaissance ricercar** (c. 1540-1600): Multiple brief points of imitation (similar to motet)
- **Early Baroque** (c. 1600-1650): Fewer subjects, more sustained development
- **High Baroque** (c. 1650-1750): Monothematic, systematic contrapuntal exploration
- **Culmination**: Bach's *Musical Offering* and *Art of Fugue*

## Ricercar vs. Fugue

**Similarities**:
- Both use imitative counterpoint
- Both develop subjects systematically
- Both employ contrapuntal devices (stretto, inversion, etc.)

**Differences**:
- **Ricercar**: More learned, abstract, scholarly character
- **Fugue**: More conventional form, clearer structure, more "popular"
- **Ricercar**: Often explores multiple contrapuntal techniques simultaneously
- **Fugue**: Typically emphasizes musical coherence over technical display
- **Ricercar**: Historical term (Renaissance/Baroque)
- **Fugue**: Continued beyond Baroque into later periods
- **Ricercar**: Slower tempos, longer note values common
- **Fugue**: More varied tempos and characters

**Note**: In late Baroque, terms sometimes overlap; some ricercars essentially fugues

## Types of Ricercar

**By structure**:
1. **Polythematic ricercar**: Multiple subjects presented in succession (Renaissance style)
2. **Monothematic ricercar**: Single subject explored throughout (Baroque style)
3. **Combinatorial ricercar**: Multiple subjects combined simultaneously

**By texture**:
- **Keyboard ricercar**: For organ or harpsichord
- **Ensemble ricercar**: For instrumental consort (viols, winds)

**By character**:
- **Learned ricercar**: Emphasizes contrapuntal complexity and devices
- **Canzona-like ricercar**: Lighter, more dancelike character (transitional type)

## Musical Context

Ricercars serve specific purposes:
- **Contrapuntal study**: Teaches and demonstrates contrapuntal mastery
- **Intellectual engagement**: Appeals to learned audiences and connoisseurs
- **Liturgical use**: Organ ricercars for church services (preludes, interludes)
- **Compositional exploration**: Composer investigates contrapuntal possibilities
- **Pedagogical models**: Examples for study of strict counterpoint
- **Historical development**: Bridges Renaissance motet style to Baroque fugue

The ricercar represents the most learned, scholarly end of contrapuntal composition.

## Examples

### Basic

**Renaissance polythematic ricercar structure**:
```
Section 1 (measures 1-12): Subject A in all voices (point of imitation)
Section 2 (measures 13-24): Subject B in all voices (new point)
Section 3 (measures 25-36): Subject C in all voices (new point)
Section 4 (measures 37-48): Subject D in all voices (new point)
Final cadence

Multiple subjects, each in separate point of imitation
Similar to motet structure
```

**Baroque monothematic ricercar** (single subject explored):
```
Subject: C-D-E-F-G

Exposition: Subject in all voices at tonic/dominant
Episode 1: Sequential development
Entry 2: Subject in inversion (C-B-A-G-F)
Episode 2: Development
Entry 3: Subject in augmentation (longer note values)
Episode 3: Development
Entry 4: Subject in stretto (overlapping entries)
Conclusion: Final entries and cadence

Systematic exploration of one subject through various transformations
```

**Contrapuntal devices in ricercar**:
```
Subject (original): C-D-E-F-G (ascending scale)

Inversion: C-Bb-Ab-G-F (descending intervals)
Augmentation: C——D——E——F——G—— (doubled note values)
Diminution: C-D-E-F-G (halved note values, faster)
Retrograde: G-F-E-D-C (backward)
Stretto: Overlapping entries at 2-beat intervals

All systematically employed throughout ricercar
```

**Combining subjects** (combinatorial ricercar):
```
Subject 1: C-D-E-F-G (soprano)
Subject 2: G-F-E-D-C (alto, presented simultaneously)
Subject 3: E-E-E-D-C (tenor, rhythmically distinct)

All three subjects combined in invertible counterpoint
Demonstrates advanced contrapuntal planning
```

**Learned style characteristics**:
```
Long note values:
𝅝 𝅝 𝅝 𝅗𝅥 𝅗𝅥 𝅗𝅥 𝅗𝅥

Deliberate, measured pace
Emphasizes linear clarity over virtuosity
Contemplative, scholarly character
```

### From Repertoire

**J.S. Bach, Musical Offering (BWV 1079), Ricercar a 3 and Ricercar a 6**: Two monumental ricercars on the "Royal Theme" given by Frederick the Great, demonstrating supreme contrapuntal mastery with extensive use of inversion, augmentation, stretto, and other devices.

**Girolamo Frescobaldi, Ricercari from *Fiori Musicali* (1635)**: Early Baroque organ ricercars, learned style, systematic contrapuntal development, liturgical function.

**Andrea Gabrieli and Giovanni Gabrieli, Ricercari**: Renaissance polythematic ricercars for organ, multiple sections with different subjects, transitional between motet and fugue.

**Adriano Banchieri, Ricercari**: Late Renaissance examples showing evolution toward monothematic structure.

**J.S. Bach, Art of Fugue (BWV 1080)**: Though titled "fugues" (*Contrapunctus*), many movements are ricercar-like in their learned, systematic exploration of contrapuntal devices.

## Related Concepts

- **Prerequisite**: fugue, imitation, counterpoint, augmentation-diminution, inversion, stretto
- **See also**: toccata, fantasia, canzona, point-of-imitation

## Common Confusions

- Ricercar ≠ just another name for fugue (distinct character, though related)
- "Ricercar" from "ricercare" (to seek/search), refers to exploring contrapuntal possibilities
- More learned and abstract than typical fugue
- Renaissance ricercar: multiple subjects in succession (polythematic)
- Baroque ricercar: single subject systematically developed (monothematic)
- Primarily instrumental genre (organ, ensemble; rarely vocal)
- Emphasizes contrapuntal devices more than typical fugue
- Slower, more deliberate character common (not virtuosic like toccata)
- Bach's Musical Offering ricercars archetypal examples (especially 6-voice)
- "Ricercar" sometimes spelled "ricercare" (Italian infinitive)
- Historical term (Renaissance/Baroque), less used after 1750
- Can include multiple contrapuntal transformations of subject simultaneously
- Scholarly, intellectual character (appeals to learned audiences)
- Often liturgical function (organ ricercars for church)
- Precursor to fugue (historical development)
- Some late Baroque ricercars essentially fugues with learned character
