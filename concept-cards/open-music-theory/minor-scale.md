# Minor Scale

## Quick Definition

A seven-note scale characterized by a lowered third scale degree compared to major, creating a darker, more somber sound. Has three forms: natural, harmonic, and melodic.

## Formal Definition

A **minor scale** is a diatonic scale whose third scale degree (^3) is always a half step lower than the third of a major scale with the same tonic. There are three forms of the minor scale:

1. **Natural minor**: W-H-W-W-H-W-W (same ascending and descending)
2. **Harmonic minor**: W-H-W-W-H-3H-H (same ascending and descending)
   - Raises ^7 by a half step compared to natural minor
3. **Melodic minor**: W-H-W-W-W-W-H (ascending), W-W-H-W-W-H-W (descending)
   - Ascending: raises ^6 and ^7 compared to natural minor
   - Descending: same as natural minor

Despite three forms, pieces are simply described as being "in minor" (e.g., "A minor"), not in a specific form. The forms are primarily pedagogical categories for instrumental practice. Minor keys use the natural minor scale for their key signature.

In pitch-class notation, A natural minor = {9, 11, 0, 2, 4, 5, 7} (relative to C=0).

## Musical Context

Minor scales provide the foundation for minor keys, which create:
- Darker, more somber, or serious character compared to major
- Greater variety due to three forms (especially harmonic and melodic)
- Strong melodic tendencies (raised ^7 in harmonic and melodic ascending)

The three forms serve different musical purposes:
- **Natural minor**: Provides the basic key signature, common in modal and folk music
- **Harmonic minor**: Creates a leading tone (^7) for strong harmonic resolution, common in classical harmony
- **Melodic minor**: Smooths ascending melodies by avoiding the augmented second between ^6 and ^7 in harmonic minor

## Examples

### Basic

A natural minor:
A-B-C-D-E-F-G-A
Pattern: W-H-W-W-H-W-W

A harmonic minor:
A-B-C-D-E-F-G♯-A
Raises ^7 (G becomes G♯)

A melodic minor:
A-B-C-D-E-F♯-G♯-A (ascending)
A-G-F-E-D-C-B-A (descending, same as natural)

### From Repertoire

Bach's Toccata and Fugue in D minor, Beethoven's "Moonlight" Sonata (C♯ minor), "Greensleeves" (primarily natural minor), Chopin's Funeral March (B♭ minor). Most classical minor-key works use all three forms depending on musical context.

## Common Confusions

- Minor scale vs. minor key: The scale is the pitch collection; the key is the tonal system
- All three forms are "minor"—pieces aren't "in harmonic minor," they're "in minor" but may use harmonic minor patterns
- The key signature is based on natural minor, even though music may use harmonic or melodic forms
- Melodic minor is different ascending vs. descending (only form that changes)
- The third is ALWAYS lowered in minor (all three forms); it's ^6 and ^7 that vary
