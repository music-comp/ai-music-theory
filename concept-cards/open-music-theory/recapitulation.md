# Recapitulation

## Quick Definition

The third and final main section of sonata form that returns the exposition's thematic material with a crucial modification: both primary and secondary themes now appear in the tonic key, resolving the tonal conflict established in the exposition (where S was in the dominant or relative major) and creating harmonic closure—typically followed by a coda that provides final affirmation of the tonic, representing the dramatic resolution after the developmental exploration.

## Formal Definition

**Recapitulation** (Latin: "return to the beginning" or "summing up") is the third main division of sonata form, characterized by the return of exposition material with tonal resolution—all themes presented in the home key.

**Essential components** (parallel to exposition):

**1. Primary Theme (P)**:
- **Key**: Tonic (I or i) - same as exposition
- **Material**: Returns recognizably, may be varied
- **Function**: Re-establishes tonic and main theme
- **Character**: Often triumphant or conclusive return

**2. Transition (TR)**:
- **Key**: Remains in tonic (I) - CRUCIAL DIFFERENCE from exposition
- **Material**: Often abbreviated, recomposed, or varied
- **Function**: Connects P to S without modulating
- **Problem**: Must stay in tonic while maintaining transition energy

**3. Secondary Theme (S)**:
- **Key**: Tonic (I or i) - RESOLVES TONAL CONFLICT
- **Material**: Same theme as exposition S
- **Function**: Provides resolution of exposition's tonal opposition
- **Cadence**: PAC in tonic (Essential Structural Closure = ESC)

**4. Closing Zone (C)**:
- **Key**: Tonic (I or i)
- **Material**: Closing material from exposition
- **Function**: Confirms final tonic stability
- **Often followed by**: Coda (extended closing section)

**Key structural principle**: **Tonal resolution**
- Exposition: P in I, S in V (conflict)
- Recapitulation: P in I, S in I (resolution)

## Tonal Structure

**Major-key recapitulation**:
```
P          TR         S          C          (Coda)
I    →     I     →    I     →    I     →    I
Tonic      Tonic      Tonic      Tonic      Tonic

All in tonic - RESOLUTION of exposition's I vs. V conflict
```

**Minor-key recapitulation**:
```
P          TR         S          C          (Coda)
i    →     i     →    i     →    i     →    i
Tonic      Tonic      Tonic      Tonic      Tonic

All in tonic minor - RESOLUTION of exposition's i vs. III conflict
(S may be in parallel major I for "brighter" effect)
```

## Transition Problem and Solutions

**The transition problem**: Exposition TR modulates I → V; recapitulation TR must stay in I

**Common solutions**:

**1. Abbreviation**:
- Shorten or compress TR
- Remove modulatory portion
- Jump directly to S

**2. Recomposition**:
- Rewrite TR harmonically
- Maintain character but alter progression
- Stay in tonic instead of modulating

**3. Shift to subdominant**:
- Brief move to IV instead of V
- Returns to I for S
- Provides harmonic variety without leaving tonic

**4. Omission**:
- Skip TR entirely
- Move directly from P to S
- Rare but occurs

## Variations and Modifications

**Exact recapitulation**:
- Literal repetition of exposition (transposed to tonic)
- Common in early Classical (Mozart, early Haydn)

**Varied recapitulation**:
- Themes recognizable but altered
- Ornamentation, extension, compression
- Common in late Classical/Romantic

**Expanded recapitulation**:
- Extended beyond exposition proportions
- Additional development within recap
- Beethoven specialty

**Compressed recapitulation**:
- Shorter than exposition
- Omits repeated material
- Creates drive to conclusion

**False recapitulation**:
- P theme appears in development in wrong key
- "Fools" listener before actual return
- Beethoven famous for this (Symphony No. 5)

## Essential Structural Closure (ESC)

**ESC** = First PAC of S theme in tonic (in recapitulation)

**Function**: Completes the tonal and formal journey
- Parallels EEC (Essential Expositional Closure) in exposition
- EEC in V; ESC in I
- ESC represents formal/tonal goal of entire movement

**Location**: Usually same point in S as EEC was in exposition

## Musical Context

The recapitulation serves critical functions:
- **Tonal resolution**: Resolves I vs. V conflict from exposition
- **Thematic return**: Satisfies listener expectation for familiar material
- **Dramatic conclusion**: Provides sense of "homecoming" and closure
- **Formal balance**: Balances exposition (ABA' large-scale symmetry)
- **Triumphant return**: Often emphatic, especially after dramatic development

The recapitulation is where sonata form's "promise" is fulfilled.

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple recapitulation** (major key):
```
Exposition:
P (mm. 1-8): I
TR (mm. 9-16): I → V
S (mm. 17-24): V (EEC = PAC in V)
C (mm. 25-32): V

Recapitulation:
P (mm. 90-97): I (exact return)
TR (mm. 98-102): I (abbreviated, stays in tonic!)
S (mm. 103-110): I (ESC = PAC in I) ← RESOLUTION
C (mm. 111-118): I

All material now in tonic = tonal resolution
```

**Transition recomposition**:
```
Exposition TR (8 bars): I - ii - iii - IV - V/V - V (modulates)

Recapitulation TR (4 bars): I - IV - I - I (stays in tonic, abbreviated)

Exposition purpose: modulate I → V
Recapitulation purpose: stay in I
Therefore: recomposed
```

**Minor-key resolution**:
```
Exposition:
P: g minor (i)
S: Bb major (III, relative major)

Recapitulation:
P: g minor (i)
S: g minor OR G major (i or I)

Options: Keep S minor (darker) or parallel major (brighter)
```

**False recapitulation** (Beethoven):
```
Development (m. 150): P theme enters in ♭VI (unexpected key)
  → Sounds like recapitulation!
  → But wrong key, development continues...

Actual Recapitulation (m. 200): P enters in I (tonic)
  → Real return, much later

Creates dramatic surprise and extends form
```

**Extended recapitulation** (Beethoven):
```
Exposition (60 bars total)
Recapitulation (80 bars total) - LONGER

P: Extended with additional development
TR: Expanded with new harmonic areas
S: Extended codetta after ESC
C: Leads to massive coda

Beethoven often expands recap beyond exposition
```

### From Repertoire

**Mozart, Symphony No. 40, I, mm. 164-261**: Clear recapitulation, P returns in g minor, TR abbreviated, S now in g minor (ESC, tonal resolution), closing zone in tonic, brief coda.

**Beethoven, Symphony No. 5, I, mm. 248-374**: Famous false recapitulation in horn (m. 195, during development), actual recap at m. 248, expanded proportions, massive coda functions as second development.

**Haydn, Symphony No. 104, I**: Recapitulation with Haydn's characteristic wit, varied repeats, surprising twists even in return, demonstrates Classical flexibility.

**Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 545, I, mm. 42-73**: Pedagogical example, clear recapitulation structure, exact transposition of S to tonic, demonstrates textbook form.

**Beethoven, Piano Sonata Op. 2, No. 1, I**: Recapitulation expands beyond exposition, energetic drive to conclusion, demonstrates Beethoven's expansion tendencies.

## Common Confusions

- Recapitulation = return of exposition material ALL IN TONIC
- Tonal resolution key feature: S moves from V (exposition) to I (recapitulation)
- Not exact repetition (often varied, abbreviated, or expanded)
- Transition must stay in tonic (can't modulate like exposition TR)
- ESC (Essential Structural Closure) = PAC in S zone, in tonic
- False recapitulation = P appears in development in wrong key (not real return)
- Recapitulation typically not repeated (unlike exposition)
- May be longer or shorter than exposition (varies by composer/work)
- Beethoven often expands recapitulation (longer than exposition)
- Classical recaps often compress or abbreviate
- Coda typically follows recapitulation (provides additional closure)
- Recapitulation resolves both tonal and thematic drama
- S in minor-key works: may stay minor or shift to parallel major
- Recapitulation returns P first (after development and retransition)
- Not a literal da capo (not "D.C." back to beginning)
- Thematic return satisfies psychological expectation for familiar material
