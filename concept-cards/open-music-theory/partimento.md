# Partimento

## Quick Definition

An 18th-century pedagogical method involving improvised realization of a given bass line (with or without figures), used to teach harmony, voice leading, and composition.

## Formal Definition

A **partimento** (plural: **partimenti**; Italian: "division" or "partition") is both a pedagogical method and a type of musical exercise used extensively in 18th-century Italy and throughout Europe for teaching composition, improvisation, figured bass, and keyboard skills. A partimento consists of a bass line (sometimes with figured bass symbols) that the student realizes into a complete keyboard piece through improvisation or written elaboration.

**Characteristics**:
- **Bass line foundation**: Student begins with a given bass line (the partimento)
- **Figured bass**: May include figures indicating harmonies, or may be unfigured
- **Realization**: Student adds upper voices, creating a complete texture
- **Improvisation**: Originally performed extemporaneously at the keyboard
- **Written exercises**: Can also be written out as composition exercises
- **Schemas**: Relies heavily on conventional patterns (Galant schemas)
- **Progressive difficulty**: From simple harmonizations to complex fugues

**Types of partimenti**:
- **Simple partimenti**: Basic bass lines for practicing harmonization
- **Rule of the Octave exercises**: Applying the Rule to bass scales
- **Sequential patterns**: Practicing standard harmonic sequences
- **Fugal partimenti**: Bass lines designed for fugal realization
- **Sonata partimenti**: More complex pieces in sonata form or similar structures

**Relationship to other concepts**:
- **Figured bass**: Partimenti often use figured bass notation
- **Schemas**: Partimenti incorporate and teach Galant schemas
- **Rule of the Octave**: Fundamental partimento technique
- **Thoroughbass**: Related practice, though partimento emphasizes composition more

## Musical Context

Partimento was central to musical education in the 18th century:

**Pedagogical functions**:
- **Harmony training**: Learning chord progressions and voice leading
- **Improvisation**: Developing ability to realize music spontaneously
- **Composition**: Teaching compositional techniques and patterns
- **Keyboard skills**: Developing fluency at the instrument
- **Pattern recognition**: Internalizing conventional musical formulas
- **Style acquisition**: Absorbing the Galant style through practice

**Historical context**:
- **Italian conservatories**: Partimento central to Neapolitan school (Naples conservatories)
- **Key figures**: Francesco Durante, Fedele Fenaroli, Giovanni Paisiello, Bernardo Pasquini
- **18th century**: Peak of partimento pedagogy
- **Geographic spread**: From Italy throughout Europe
- **Students**: Mozart, Haydn, and many other major composers likely encountered partimento methods
- **Decline**: 19th century saw shift to more theoretical approaches

**How partimento worked**:
1. **Memorization**: Students memorized the Rule of the Octave and standard schemas
2. **Pattern practice**: Practiced realizing common sequential patterns
3. **Simple exercises**: Began with straightforward bass lines
4. **Progressive complexity**: Advanced to more elaborate partimenti
5. **Improvisation**: Eventually could improvise complete pieces from minimal notation
6. **Composition**: Techniques transferred to original composition

**Modern revival**:
- Robert Gjerdingen's research on Galant schemas
- Renewed interest in historical pedagogy
- Partimento methods reintroduced in some conservatories
- Used for teaching improvisation and 18th-century style

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple partimento** (C major, four measures):
- Given bass: C–G–A–F–G–C
- Student realizes with upper voices following Rule of the Octave and schemas
- Result: Complete four-voice texture

**Figured partimento**:
- Bass with figures: C (5/3) – G (6/4–5/3) – C (5/3)
- Figures guide the harmonization
- Student fills in specific voicing and embellishment

**Unfigured partimento**:
- Bass line only, no figures
- Student determines harmonies based on Rule of the Octave and patterns
- More challenging, requires deeper understanding

## Common Confusions

- Partimento vs. figured bass: Partimento is a pedagogical method; figured bass is a notation system (though partimenti often use figured bass)
- Partimento is not just continuo realization—it's more compositional and creative
- Not all partimenti have figures—unfigured partimenti require the student to determine harmonies
- Partimento is both a noun (the exercise itself) and describes a method (partimento pedagogy)
- The goal was improvisation and composition, not just accurate realization
- Partimenti taught style and patterns, not just "correct" harmony in the abstract
- Modern harmonic analysis doesn't fully capture how partimento students thought about music
- Partimento pedagogy emphasized memorization and pattern recognition over theoretical rules
- The method relied on extensive keyboard practice, not just written exercises
- Partimenti could be simple or extremely complex (even fugues)
- "Partimento" originally meant a keyboard work generally; the pedagogical meaning developed later
- Learning partimento was a multi-year process requiring dedication and practice
