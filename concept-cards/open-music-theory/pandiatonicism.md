# Pandiatonicism

## Quick Definition

Pandiatonicism is the use of the diatonic collection (the seven notes of a major scale) without establishing any particular pitch as tonic and without functional harmonic progressions. All seven notes are treated as equally available, creating sonorities that are consonant (diatonic) yet non-tonal (no tonic-dominant hierarchy).

## Formal Definition

**Pandiatonicism**: A compositional approach characterized by:

1. **Use of diatonic collection**: Only the seven notes of a major scale (or modal rotation)
2. **No tonal hierarchy**: No pitch functions as tonic in the traditional sense
3. **No functional harmony**: Chords do not progress according to tonal voice-leading rules
4. **Free combination**: Any notes from the collection may sound simultaneously

**Terminology**: "Pan" (Greek for "all") + "diatonic" = using all diatonic notes freely.

**Properties**:
- Sounds consonant (diatonic intervals are relatively stable)
- Lacks tonal direction or resolution
- May feature "added-note" chords (e.g., C-E-G-A)
- Often creates a "white-note" sound in its simplest form

**Relationship to modes**: Pandiatonicism uses the same pitch material as diatonic modes but doesn't establish a modal center either.

## Musical Context

Pandiatonicism emerged in early 20th-century music as one way to move beyond common-practice tonality while retaining consonant sound:

**Why pandiatonicism appealed**:
- Familiar pitch material (diatonic) in unfamiliar usage
- Avoids both tonal cliches and atonal harshness
- Creates a "floating" or "static" effect
- Suggests folk or archaic music

**How it differs from**:
- **Tonal music**: No tonic, no functional progressions
- **Modal music**: No established final or modal center
- **Atonality**: Uses only diatonic notes, sounds consonant
- **Polytonality**: Uses one collection, not multiple keys

**Common techniques**:
- Extended tertian chords (9ths, 11ths, 13ths)
- Parallel motion in diatonic intervals
- Ostinatos using diatonic notes
- "Cluster" or "added-note" voicings

## Examples

### Basic

**White-note pandiatonicism**:
```
Collection: C-D-E-F-G-A-B (C major / no sharps or flats)

Possible simultaneous combinations:
- C-E-G-B-D (Cmaj9 with no functional meaning)
- F-A-C-E-G (Fmaj9 blended with C major)
- G-B-D-F-A (G9 without dominant function)

None of these "resolve" - they simply exist within the collection.
```

**Distinguishing pandiatonic from tonal**:
```
Tonal: C - F - G7 - C (functional progression, tonic established)

Pandiatonic: Sustained C-E-G-A-D chord, no progression,
             no voice leading, no sense of "home"
```

**Pandiatonic vs. modal**:
```
Dorian (modal): D emphasized as final, A as secondary pitch,
                sense of D-centered hierarchy

Pandiatonic: D-F-A-C-E-G chord held without any pitch
             functioning as central
```

### From Repertoire

**Stravinsky, Petrushka**: The opening fairground scenes feature extended pandiatonic passages--diatonic notes swirling without tonal orientation.

**Stravinsky, Symphony of Psalms**: The famous "E-G" chord at the opening uses diatonic notes (from the eventual E-minor collection) without establishing E as tonic.

**Copland**: The "American" sound often associated with Copland frequently involves pandiatonic techniques--open fifths and added-note chords that float in diatonic space.

**Debussy**: Some passages in Debussy approach pandiatonicism, though his style often incorporates chromatic notes as well.

## Common Confusions

- **Not the same as modal music**: Modes have a tonic; pandiatonicism doesn't
- **Not polytonal**: Pandiatonicism uses one diatonic collection, not superimposed keys
- **"Consonant" doesn't mean "tonal"**: Pandiatonic music sounds stable but lacks tonal function
- **Can use any diatonic transposition**: Not limited to "white notes"
- **The collection is fixed, the usage is free**: You can't add chromaticism and still be pandiatonic
- **Extended chords don't necessarily resolve**: A Cmaj13 in pandiatonic music just "is," rather than leading somewhere
- **Stravinsky's usage is not Copland's**: Different composers use pandiatonicism in distinct ways
