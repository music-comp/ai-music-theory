# Quick Definition
An analytical method that identifies the grouping units of a passage by marking phrase endings (usually cadences), dividing phrases into smaller idea-level units, and representing the hierarchy with square brackets and arcs above the score.

# Core Definition
A **segmentation analysis** uses square brackets above the staff to identify a phrase's component parts. The smallest level is the **idea level** -- short grouping units (often two measures) containing the motivic material. The source specifies: square brackets for ideas and subphrases (anything that doesn't end with a cadence), and arcs for anything at the phrase level or above (anything that must end with a cadence) (Ch. 1-2).

# Prerequisites
- **Phrase** -- Must understand what constitutes a phrase
- **Cadence** -- Cadences mark phrase endings

# Key Properties
1. Square brackets: ideas and subphrases (no cadence required)
2. Arcs: phrases and above (must end with cadence)
3. Smallest level: idea level (often 2 measures)
4. Procedure: identify phrase endings first, then subdivide

# Construction / Recognition
## To Perform a Segmentation Analysis:
1. Identify potential points of closure (cadences in tonal music)
2. Divide each phrase into smaller units (ideas)
3. Use square brackets above the staff for ideas and subphrases
4. Use arcs for phrase-level and above
5. Verify and label any cadences

# Context & Application
Segmentation analysis is the foundational analytical tool for understanding phrase-level form. It precedes more specific formal analyses (sentence, period, hybrid) by establishing the basic grouping structure.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 1, Examples 6-7): Segmentation analyses of Donizetti's "Me voglio fa 'na casa" and Clara Schumann's Piano Trio Op. 17, I.

**Example 2** (Ch. 2, Examples 1-2): Segmentation of Schubert's "Du bist die Ruh" (4 measures) and Hensel's "Abendbild" (13 measures).

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Placing phrase boundaries at every pause or V-I motion
  **Correction**: Not every pause is a cadence; phrases need beginning, middle, and end

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Segmentation analysis and motivic analysis are the same
  **Clarification**: Segmentation shows grouping structure; motivic analysis tracks recurring patterns
