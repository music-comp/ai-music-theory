# Quick Definition
The historical contrapuntal style of Renaissance vocal polyphony (especially Palestrina), involving imitative entries, predominantly conjunct motion, careful dissonance treatment, and text-driven phrase structure -- related to but not identical with species counterpoint rules.

# Core Definition
The **16th-century contrapuntal style** is the compositional practice of Renaissance imitative choral polyphony, especially in the style of Palestrina. The source notes it "has historically enjoyed a prominent position in the teaching of music theory" and is "sometimes conflated with the later, 18th-century notion of species counterpoint" but is a distinct practice. Key principles include: imitation at points of text change, conjunct motion predominating within parts, contrary motion predominating between parts, careful control of dissonance (suspensions, passing tones, neighbor tones, nota cambiata), final chords as bare fifths or major triads (Picardy third), and text setting that places strong syllables on strong beats (Ch. 8).

# Key Properties
1. Imitative entries at points of text change (points of imitation)
2. Contrary motion predominates between parts
3. Conjunct (stepwise) motion predominates within parts
4. Melodic leaps: seconds through octaves permitted; avoid sevenths and tritones
5. Rising minor sixths freely used; descending sixths extremely rare
6. Suspensions: preparation (weak beat), suspension (strong), resolution (step down)
7. Final chord: bare fifth or major triad (Picardy third, common by Palestrina)
8. Parallel fifths and octaves avoided (including diminished fifths)
9. Arch-shaped melodies common; range per part typically within a twelfth
10. Text setting: one beat minimum per syllable; strong syllables on strong beats

# Construction / Recognition
## To Compose in 16th-Century Style:
1. Identify points of imitation from text changes
2. Enter voices separately with the same melody at consonant intervals
3. Use predominantly stepwise motion within each part
4. Employ suspensions at cadences and within phrases
5. Match textual and musical meters (strong syllables on strong beats)
6. End on bare fifth or major triad
## To Recognize:
1. Listen for successive voice entries with the same or similar melody
2. Note predominantly stepwise melodic motion
3. Identify overlapping phrases at text changes

# Context & Application
Renaissance polyphonic style is particularly associated with sacred music: masses, motets, and magnificats. The source provides texture conventions for Mass settings: Kyrie (imitative), Gloria (homophonic), Credo (homophonic at important moments), Sanctus (imitative), Benedictus (imitative, fewer voices), Agnus Dei (imitative, often with canons). The style influenced later Baroque fugal writing.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating species counterpoint rules as identical to 16th-century style
  **Correction**: Species counterpoint is pedagogical; 16th-century style has its own conventions (e.g., fauxbourdon, different cadential practices)

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: 16th-century style and species counterpoint are the same thing
  **Clarification**: They are "related but not identical" (source); species counterpoint is an 18th-century pedagogical method inspired by Renaissance practice
