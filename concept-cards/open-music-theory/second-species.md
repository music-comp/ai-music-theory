# Quick Definition
The second species exercise, where the counterpoint moves in half notes against whole-note cantus firmus (2:1 ratio), introducing the differentiation of strong and weak beats and the dissonant passing tone.

# Core Definition
In **second-species counterpoint**, the counterpoint line moves in half notes against a cantus firmus in whole notes, creating a 2:1 rhythmic ratio. This introduces two fundamental problems: the differentiation between strong beats (always consonant) and weak beats (may be consonant or dissonant), and the introduction of the **dissonant passing tone** -- the only dissonance type permitted, which fills in a melodic third by stepwise motion between two consonant downbeats (Ch. 3). Seven types of consonant weak-beat patterns are also identified: consonant passing tone, substitution, skipped passing tone, interval subdivision, change of register, delay of melodic progression, and consonant neighbor tone.

# Key Properties
1. Counterpoint in half notes against whole-note cantus (2:1 ratio)
2. Strong beats (downbeats) always consonant
3. Weak beats may be consonant or dissonant
4. Only dissonance type: dissonant passing tone (stepwise, fills a third)
5. Seven consonant weak-beat patterns available
6. Cross-bar-line motion follows first-species rules
7. May begin with half rest followed by half note
8. More stepwise motion than first species due to increased notes
9. Should have one or two secondary climaxes
10. Leaps preferred within the bar rather than across bar lines

# Construction / Recognition
## To Write Second Species:
1. Choose downbeat notes following first-species harmonic rules
2. Fill weak beats using one of the seven consonant patterns or a dissonant passing tone
3. Ensure all downbeats are consonant (no unisons except at beginning/end)
4. Follow first-species rules for motion across bar lines
5. End with clausula vera (penultimate bar may be whole note or two half notes)
## To Identify Weak-Beat Types:
1. Dissonant passing tone: step-step in same direction, filling a third
2. Consonant passing tone: same pattern but all three notes consonant
3. Substitution: leap a fourth, step back (outlines a third)
4. Skipped passing tone: third + step in same direction (outlines a fourth)
5. Interval subdivision: two leaps dividing a fifth or sixth
6. Change of register: large consonant leap, then step opposite
7. Consonant neighbor tone: step away and back to original pitch

# Context & Application
Second species marks the first introduction of dissonance in species counterpoint. The dissonant passing tone is carefully controlled -- it must occur on a weak beat, be approached and left by step in the same direction, and fill in a melodic third. This teaches the fundamental principle that dissonance must be introduced smoothly and resolved predictably, a principle that governs all tonal music.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Placing a dissonance on a strong beat (downbeat)
  **Correction**: All downbeats must be consonant; dissonance only on weak beats
- **Error**: Leaping into or out of a dissonant tone
  **Correction**: Dissonant passing tones must be approached and left by step in the same direction

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Any weak-beat note that's consonant counts as a "consonant passing tone"
  **Clarification**: "Consonant passing tone" is a specific pattern (step-step in same direction, all consonant); other consonant weak beats have their own names
- **Confusion**: Hidden fifths/octaves between successive downbeats are forbidden
  **Clarification**: Direct fifths/octaves between successive downbeats are fine in second species because the intervening note diminishes the effect
