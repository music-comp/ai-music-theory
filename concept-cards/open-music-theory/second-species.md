# Second Species Counterpoint

## Quick Definition

A 2:1 contrapuntal exercise where the counterpoint moves in half notes against a cantus firmus in whole notes, introducing the metric distinction between strong beats (always consonant) and weak beats (may be consonant or dissonant), with passing-tone dissonances allowed on weak beats.

## Formal Definition

**Second-species counterpoint** (also called "two-against-one" or 2:1 counterpoint) is the second species in Fux's pedagogical method. The counterpoint moves twice as fast as the cantus firmus, creating fundamental musical problems of metric hierarchy and controlled dissonance.

**Rhythmic structure**:
- Counterpoint: half notes (two per measure)
- Cantus firmus: whole notes (one per measure)
- Creates strong beats (downbeats) and weak beats

**Strong beats (downbeats)**:
- Always consonant (no unisons except when unavoidable)
- Prefer imperfect consonances (3rds, 6ths) to perfect (5ths, 8ves)
- Follow first-species principles for motion from downbeat to downbeat
- No two perfect intervals of same size on consecutive downbeats
- No more than three bars beginning with same imperfect consonance

**Weak beats**:
- May be consonant or dissonant
- Mixture of consonance/dissonance promotes variety
- Unisons permitted on weak beats (rhythmic difference maintains independence)
- Dissonances must follow passing-tone pattern (see below)

**Dissonant passing tone** (weak beats only):
- Fills melodic third by stepwise motion between two consonant downbeats
- Approached by step, left by step, in same direction
- Never leap into or out of dissonance
- Never change direction on dissonance
- Always on weak beat, never downbeat

**Consonant weak-beat patterns** (see related concepts for full list):
1. Consonant passing tone: step-step in same direction, outlining third
2. Substitution: leap fourth, step back in opposite direction
3. Skipped passing tone: leap third + step in same direction, outlining fourth
4. Interval subdivision: two leaps dividing larger interval (two 3rds = 5th)
5. Change of register: large leap (P5, 6th, 8ve), step back opposite direction
6. Delay of melodic progression: leap 3rd, step opposite direction
7. Consonant neighbor tone: step away, step back to original pitch

**Beginning**:
- Above cantus: begin on ^1 (do) or ^5 (sol)
- Below cantus: begin on ^1 (do)
- May start with half rest + half note, or two half notes
- Half rest beginning establishes rhythmic profile more clearly

**Ending**:
- Clausula vera: final pitch = ^1 (do), penultimate follows first-species pattern
- Penultimate bar may be whole note or two half notes
- Allows clausula vera to begin on strong or weak beat

**Melodic considerations**:
- Dominated by stepwise motion (more than first species)
- Leap from strong to weak beat preferred (diminishes attention to leap)
- One or two secondary climaxes appropriate (more notes than first species)
- Single overall climax, not coinciding with cantus climax

**Motion between voices**:
- Downbeat to downbeat: follow first-species principles
- Hidden/direct fifths or octaves between downbeats acceptable (intervening weak beat diminishes effect)

## Musical Context

Second species introduces two essential compositional elements:
- **Metric hierarchy**: Strong vs. weak beats, foundation for all metric music
- **Controlled dissonance**: Passing tones, the most common dissonance type in tonal music

The 2:1 ratio creates enough rhythmic activity for interesting lines while maintaining focus on passing-tone dissonance treatment.

## Examples

### Basic

**Dissonant passing tone**:
```
Measure 1:
CP: A4 (strong, consonant 6th) - G4 (weak, dissonant 4th passing) | F4 (strong, consonant 3rd)
CF: C4 -------------------- C4 -------------------- | D4
Pattern: Step down - step down (fills third A-F with passing G)
```

**Consonant passing tone**:
```
CP: C5 (consonant 8ve) - D5 (consonant 6th passing) | E5 (consonant 3rd)
CF: C4 ----------------- F4 ----------------- | C5
Pattern: Outlines third C-E with consonant passing D
```

**Substitution**:
```
CP: A4 (6th) - D5 (leap 4th up) | C5 (step down, opposite direction)
CF: C4 -------- F4 ------------- | E4
Pattern: Leap 4th, step back opposite direction
```

**Invalid - leap from dissonance**:
```
CP: C5 (consonant) - B4 (dissonant 7th) | G4 (leaps - WRONG!)
CF: C4 ------------ C4 --------------- | C4
Error: Cannot leap away from dissonance
```

**Hidden fifth (acceptable in second species)**:
```
Downbeat 1: CP G4, CF C4 (P5)
Weak beat:  CP A4, CF D4
Downbeat 2: CP D5, CF G4 (P5) - similar motion
Acceptable: intervening weak beat weakens the effect
```

### From Repertoire

Fux's _Gradus ad Parnassum_ Part I, second-species exercises. Mozart and Haydn notebook exercises in species counterpoint. Second species appears embedded in real music: alberti bass patterns, continuous eighth-note accompaniments, and running passages all employ passing-tone logic from second species.

## Common Confusions

- Second species ≠ "faster first species"—introduces dissonance on weak beats
- Strong beats always consonant; weak beats may be consonant or dissonant
- Dissonant passing tone must fill melodic third (not other intervals)
- Consonant passing tone also fills third but all notes are consonant
- Passing tone always by step in same direction (no leaps, no direction changes)
- Hidden fifths/octaves between downbeats acceptable in second species (unlike first)
- Weak beat dissonance ≠ "unimportant"—creates tension and motion
- Beginning with half rest recommended but not required
- Unisons on weak beats acceptable (unlike in first species where they're generally avoided)
- Don't confuse weak-beat patterns (many types!) with simple passing tones
