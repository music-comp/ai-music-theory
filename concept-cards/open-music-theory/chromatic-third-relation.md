# Chromatic Third Relation

## Quick Definition

A harmonic relationship between two chords or keys whose roots are a third apart (major or minor third) while sharing at least one common tone but differing in quality or mode—creating a colorful, non-functional progression often involving parallel voice motion and used extensively in Romantic music for dramatic tonal shifts without traditional dominant-tonic motion.

## Formal Definition

**Chromatic third relation** (also called chromatic mediant or third relation) describes:

**Definition**: Chords/keys related by major or minor third with:
- Root motion by third (M3 or m3)
- At least one common tone between chords
- Quality change (major/minor shift)
- No dominant-tonic functional relationship

**Types**:
1. **Upper chromatic mediant**: Root a third above tonic (e.g., C to E or E-flat)
2. **Lower chromatic mediant**: Root a third below tonic (e.g., C to A or A-flat)

**Quality patterns**:
```
From C major:
- C → E major (M3 up, both major, common tone E)
- C → E-flat major (m3 up, both major, common tone G)
- C → A major (m3 down, both major, common tone E)
- C → A-flat major (M3 down, both major, common tone C)
```

## Third Relations from Major Keys

**All chromatic third relations from C major**:
```
Upper thirds:
C major → E major   (M3 up, common tone: E)
C major → E-flat major (m3 up, common tone: G)

Lower thirds:
C major → A major   (m3 down, common tone: E)
C major → A-flat major (M3 down, common tone: C)
```

**Comparison with diatonic mediants**:
```
Diatonic: C major → E minor (vi, fully diatonic)
Chromatic: C major → E major (chromatic alteration G# needed)

Diatonic: C major → A minor (vi, fully diatonic)  
Chromatic: C major → A major (chromatic alteration C# needed)
```

## Voice Leading Patterns

**Common tone + chromatic motion**:
```
C major (C-E-G) → E major (E-G#-B)
         ↓           ↓
Common tone: E (retained)
Chromatic: G → G# (half step up)
Chromatic: C → B (half step down)

Smooth voice leading despite harmonic distance
```

**Parallel motion characteristic**:
```
C:  C → E → G    (C major)
    ↓   ↓   ↓
E:  B → E → G#   (E major)

Root descends by half step
Third retained as common tone
Fifth ascends by half step
Creates parallel voice motion
```

**Planing effect**:
```
When moving through chromatic thirds:
All voices move in same direction
Creates "planing" or parallel chord motion
Non-functional, coloristic effect
```

## Chromatic Mediant Modulation

**Direct chromatic mediant shift**:
```
C major: I → flat-III
         C    E-flat major

No pivot chord needed
Direct juxtaposition
Dramatic tonal shift
```

**As modulation technique**:
```
C major: ... I  |
         ... C  
              ↓ (direct shift)
E-flat major: | I    IV    V    I
                E-flat A-flat B-flat E-flat

Key change by chromatic third
Confirmed by cadence in new key
```

**Sequence of thirds**:
```
C → E-flat → G-flat → A
Divides octave by minor thirds
Returns to starting pitch class (A ≈ C enharmonically close)
```

## Musical Context

Chromatic third relations serve specific purposes:
- **Coloristic effect**: Non-functional harmonic color
- **Romantic idiom**: Characteristic of 19th-century harmony
- **Dramatic modulation**: Sudden key shifts by third
- **Parallel voice motion**: Creates planing effect
- **Common tones**: Smooth connection despite distance
- **Tonal ambiguity**: Weakens functional clarity
- **Expressive range**: Expands beyond diatonic mediants
- **Schubert and Brahms**: Particularly associated with these composers
- **Film music**: Continues tradition of third relations

Central to Romantic harmonic vocabulary and tonal expansion.

## Examples

### Basic

**C major to E major** (chromatic upper mediant):
```
C major: C-E-G    →    E major: E-G#-B
         ↓  ↓  ↓              ↓   ↓  ↓
         C→ B             (step down)
         E→ E             (common tone)
         G→ G#            (half step up)

Common tone: E
Chromatic motion: G → G#, C → B
Root relation: M3 up (C to E)
```

**C major to A-flat major** (chromatic lower mediant):
```
C major: C-E-G    →    A-flat major: A-flat-C-E-flat
         ↓  ↓  ↓                     ↓      ↓   ↓
         C→ C                       (common tone)
         E→ E-flat                  (half step down)
         G→ A-flat                  (half step up)

Common tone: C
Chromatic motion: E → E-flat, G → A-flat
Root relation: M3 down (C to A-flat)
```

**Sequence of chromatic thirds**:
```
C major → E-flat major → G-flat major → A major
  I           flat-III        flat-V       VI

Each move: minor third up
Returns close to starting point
Creates omnibus-like chromatic effect
```

**In context**:
```
C: I    IV    V    I  | flat-III |
   C    F     G    C    E-flat   

E-flat: I     IV      V      I
        E-flat A-flat B-flat E-flat

Direct shift to chromatic mediant
No functional preparation
Confirmed by cadence in new key
```

### From Repertoire

**Schubert, Piano Sonatas and Impromptus**: Extensive use of chromatic third relations, characteristic "Schubertian" modulations to mediant keys.

**Brahms, Intermezzo Op. 119 No. 1**: Third relations create coloristic harmonic shifts, expressive Romantic harmony.

**Wagner, "Tristan und Isolde"**: Chromatic third relations contribute to harmonic ambiguity and endless melody.

**Mahler, Symphonies**: Third relations for expressive tonal shifts, continues Romantic tradition.

**Film Scores (John Williams, Hans Zimmer)**: Chromatic third relations for dramatic effect, continues 19th-century practice.

## Common Confusions

- Chromatic third relation = root motion by third with quality change
- Different from diatonic mediant (which shares key signature)
- Chromatic mediant: major-major or minor-minor quality pairing
- Upper chromatic mediants: E and E-flat from C major
- Lower chromatic mediants: A and A-flat from C major
- Common tones provide smooth voice leading
- Chromatic motion in other voices (half steps)
- Creates parallel/planing voice motion
- Non-functional (not dominant-tonic relationship)
- Characteristic of Romantic harmony (Schubert, Brahms)
- Can occur as direct modulation (no pivot)
- Can sequence through chromatic thirds
- Minor third sequence divides octave equally (like diminished seventh)
- Not same as mixture (which borrows from parallel mode)
- Different from secondary dominant relationships
- Quality contrast (both major or both minor) is defining feature
- Creates coloristic rather than functional effect
- Continues in film music tradition
