# Scale Degree Names

## Quick Definition

Traditional names for each scale degree based on their intervallic relationship to the tonic: tonic, supertonic, mediant, subdominant, dominant, submediant, leading tone (or subtonic), providing functional identity to each scale position.

## Formal Definition

**Scale degree names** are standardized labels for each position in a diatonic scale, reflecting the note's intervallic relationship to the tonic and its functional role in tonal harmony.

**The seven scale degree names**:

**1. Tonic** (^1, do):
- First scale degree, tonal center
- Most stable, point of rest and resolution
- Latin: "tone" (the main tone)

**2. Supertonic** (^2, re):
- Second scale degree, a major second above tonic
- Latin prefix "super" = above
- Leads to mediant or back to tonic

**3. Mediant** (^3, mi):
- Third scale degree
- Latin "medius" = middle (midpoint between tonic and dominant)
- Defines major vs. minor quality

**4. Subdominant** (^4, fa):
- Fourth scale degree, a perfect fifth below tonic (or perfect fourth above)
- Latin prefix "sub" = below
- Pre-dominant function in harmony

**5. Dominant** (^5, sol):
- Fifth scale degree, a perfect fifth above tonic
- Most important pitch after tonic
- Creates harmonic tension requiring resolution to tonic
- From medieval theory emphasizing fifth's importance

**6. Submediant** (^6, la):
- Sixth scale degree, a third below tonic (or sixth above)
- Midpoint between tonic and subdominant below
- Latin "sub" = below

**7. Leading Tone** (^7, ti) or **Subtonic** (♭^7, te):
- **Leading tone**: Raised seventh (natural in major, raised in harmonic minor)
  - One half step below tonic
  - Strong tendency to resolve upward to tonic
- **Subtonic**: Lowered seventh (natural in minor, Mixolydian mode)
  - One whole step below tonic
  - Latin "sub" = below
  - Weaker pull to tonic than leading tone

**Derivation**: Names reflect intervals from tonic:
- Dominant = fifth above tonic
- Subdominant = fifth below tonic (= fourth above)
- Mediant = third above tonic (middle between tonic and dominant)
- Submediant = third below tonic (= sixth above)

## Musical Context

Scale degree names convey functional meaning:
- **Harmonic function**: Tonic (stable), Dominant (tension), Subdominant (preparation)
- **Melodic tendency**: Leading tone resolves up, supertonic leads to tonic/mediant
- **Chord construction**: Chords named by root's scale degree (tonic chord, dominant chord)
- **Analysis**: Roman numerals correspond to scale degree names (I = tonic, V = dominant)

Understanding scale degree names clarifies tonal relationships across different keys.

## Examples

### Basic

**C major scale**:
- C = Tonic (^1)
- D = Supertonic (^2)
- E = Mediant (^3)
- F = Subdominant (^4)
- G = Dominant (^5)
- A = Submediant (^6)
- B = Leading tone (^7)
- C = Tonic (^1 or ^8)

**G major scale**:
- G = Tonic
- A = Supertonic
- B = Mediant
- C = Subdominant
- D = Dominant
- E = Submediant
- F♯ = Leading tone

**A natural minor**:
- A = Tonic
- B = Supertonic
- C = Mediant (♭^3)
- D = Subdominant
- E = Dominant
- F = Submediant (♭^6)
- G = Subtonic (♭^7, not leading tone)

### From Repertoire

Cadences emphasize dominant → tonic motion. Pre-dominant chords (subdominant) prepare dominant. Picardy third raises mediant in final minor chord to major. Chromatic mediants exploit mediant relationships. Modal mixture borrows subtonic from parallel minor.

## Common Confusions

- Scale degree names are same in all keys (tonic is always ^1, regardless of key)
- Leading tone (^7) vs. subtonic (♭^7): half step vs. whole step below tonic
- Dominant (noun) = ^5; dominant (adjective) can describe V or V7
- Mediant = ^3, not "median" (different word)
- Supertonic = above tonic; subtonic = below tonic (super vs. sub)
- Subdominant is fifth below tonic, which is same as fourth above tonic
- Scale degree names ≠ chord names (though related: V chord = dominant chord)
