## Quick Definition

Chord progressions featuring a major IV chord with a minor tonic, particularly the dorian shuttle (i-IV), characteristic of funk and disco.

## Formal Definition

Dorian schemas employ the raised ^6 (la instead of le) scale degree with a minor tonic. This produces a major IV chord where minor/aeolian would have minor iv. The major IV chord serves as the primary marker distinguishing dorian from aeolian in pop music.

## Musical Context

Dorian mode is especially associated with funk, disco, and their derivative genres. The major IV with minor i creates a distinctive "bright minor" sound that became a hallmark of 1970s dance music and continues in contemporary electronic dance music.

### The Dorian Shuttle

**Basic form (i-IV)**
- Alternates between minor tonic and major subdominant
- Creates tonic prolongation through neighboring motion
- Upper voices form neighbor motion emphasizing ^6 (la)

**Expanded form (i-IV-i7-IV)**
- Adds seventh to tonic chord
- Creates arching melodic motion in upper voices

## Examples

### Basic Example

In D dorian:
- Dorian shuttle: Dm - G (i-IV)
- Note: G major contains B natural (^6/la), not Bb (flat^6/le)

## Common Confusions

- **Dorian shuttle vs. ii-V**: Listeners unfamiliar with funk/disco may hear the dorian shuttle as ii-V in the relative major. Extensive listening to the genre helps train the ear to hear the minor chord as tonic.
- **Dorian vs. aeolian/minor**: The single distinguishing feature is ^6: dorian has la (natural 6), creating major IV; aeolian has le (flat 6), creating minor iv
- **Modal context**: Using one major IV doesn't make a song dorian; consistent use of major IV with minor tonic indicates the mode
