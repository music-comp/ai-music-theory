# Introduction

## Quick Definition

An opening section that precedes the main formal structure of a movement, typically in a slower tempo and different character than the main body, functioning to prepare the listener, establish the key and mood, build anticipation for the main theme, and provide weight to the movement—common in symphonies, sonatas, and overtures where the introduction creates gravitas before the main Allegro begins.

## Formal Definition

**Introduction** is a formal section that precedes the exposition or main thematic material of a movement, serving preparatory and rhetorical functions rather than being part of the primary formal structure.

**Key characteristics**:
- **Placement**: Before exposition/main form (movement opening)
- **Tempo**: Typically slower than main body (Adagio, Andante before Allegro)
- **Character**: Preparatory, expectant, often serious or grand
- **Tonality**: Establishes tonic or creates ambiguity resolved by main theme
- **Themes**: Usually distinct from main themes (not returned in recapitulation)
- **Function**: Rhetorical preparation, not structural development

**Typical features**:
- Slower tempo marking (Grave, Adagio, Largo)
- Often begins forte or piano mysterioso
- May use dotted rhythms, sustained chords, or dramatic gestures
- Harmonic ambiguity or tonic prolongation
- No stable theme (preparatory materials only)
- Builds expectation for main tempo/theme arrival

## Types of Introduction

**Slow introduction** (Classical symphonies):
- Slow tempo (Adagio, Largo)
- Grand, serious character
- Establishes tonic or creates ambiguity
- Transitions to fast main tempo (Allegro)
- Common in Haydn and Beethoven symphonies

**Dramatic introduction** (opera, concert works):
- Creates mood or suggests drama to come
- May use programmatic elements
- Often includes striking harmonic or rhythmic events
- Prepares theatrical or dramatic content

**Tonic-establishing introduction**:
- Clearly establishes tonic key
- Affirms harmonic center before main theme
- Creates stability

**Ambiguous introduction**:
- Obscures tonic initially
- Uses chromaticism, diminished chords, or remote keys
- Resolution to tonic at main theme creates dramatic arrival

**Transition-like introduction**:
- Brief (4-8 measures)
- Leads directly to main theme
- More functional than rhetorical

## Introduction vs. Other Sections

**vs. Exposition**:
- Introduction: Preparatory, not developed or returned
- Exposition: Presents main themes, returned in recapitulation

**vs. Development**:
- Introduction: Precedes exposition, establishes rather than develops
- Development: Follows exposition, fragments and transforms themes

**vs. Prelude**:
- Introduction: Part of larger movement
- Prelude: Separate piece (though may function similarly)

## Harmonic Strategies

**Tonic prolongation**:
```
Introduction: I - - - I - - - I - V
Main theme: I [begins clearly in tonic]

Introduction establishes tonic, main theme confirms it
```

**Dominant preparation**:
```
Introduction: I - vi - ii - V - - - V
Main theme: I [arrives on downbeat]

Introduction builds on dominant, resolves to tonic at main theme entrance
```

**Harmonic ambiguity**:
```
Introduction: ? - dim7 - ? - V
Main theme: I [clarifies tonality]

Introduction obscures key, main theme reveals it
```

**Modulatory introduction** (rare):
```
Introduction: vi → V/V → V
Main theme: V [begins in dominant, unusual]

Creates tonal drama (e.g., opening in relative minor)
```

## Musical Context

Introductions serve specific purposes:
- **Rhetorical weight**: Adds seriousness and gravitas to movement
- **Preparation**: Readies listener psychologically for main material
- **Contrast**: Slow intro makes fast main theme more exciting by contrast
- **Ambiguity-resolution**: Creates suspense, released by main theme arrival
- **Grandeur**: Imparts symphonic or monumental character
- **Programmatic suggestion**: Hints at extra-musical content

Most common in:
- Symphonies (especially first movements)
- Overtures
- Concerti (especially late Classical/Romantic)
- Sonatas (less common but appears in Beethoven)
- Oratorios and cantatas

## Examples

### Basic

**Slow introduction to sonata form**:
```
Introduction (Adagio, mm. 1-12):
Tempo: Slow (quarter = 60)
Harmony: I - vi - IV - V - V - V
Character: Sustained chords, dotted rhythms, forte
Ends: Dominant prolongation with fermata

Main theme/Exposition (Allegro, mm. 13ff):
Tempo: Fast (quarter = 144)
Harmony: I [clear tonic arrival]
Character: Energetic, melodic, piano
Primary theme begins in bright tonic

Slow-to-fast transition typical of Classical symphony
```

**Ambiguous introduction**:
```
Introduction (mm. 1-8):
m. 1-2: Diminished 7th chord (no clear key)
m. 3-4: Chromatic voice leading
m. 5-6: Half-diminished 7th
m. 7-8: V (dominant finally clear)

Main theme (m. 9):
I in C major (reveals key previously obscured)

Creates mystery resolved by main theme
```

**Brief introduction** (transition-like):
```
Introduction (mm. 1-4):
Ascending octaves in bass: C - C - C - C
Crescendo from piano to forte
Ends: V (dominant)

Main theme (m. 5):
I - begins on tonic

Four-bar lead-in, functional rather than rhetorical
```

**Dramatic introduction** (opera overture):
```
Introduction (Grave, mm. 1-20):
mm. 1-8: Dotted rhythms, full orchestra, forte
mm. 9-16: Solo oboe, piano, lyrical fragment
mm. 17-20: Crescendo, building to...

Main Allegro (m. 21):
Fast, rhythmic theme erupts

Introduction suggests drama and mood of opera
```

### From Repertoire

**Haydn, Symphony No. 104 ("London"), I**: Slow introduction (Adagio, 16 measures) establishes D major with dotted rhythms and grand gestures, transitions to Allegro with energetic primary theme.

**Beethoven, Symphony No. 1, I**: Begins with striking ambiguity (C major movement opens with F major chord), slow introduction establishes C through harmonic drama before Allegro clarifies tonic.

**Mozart, Symphony No. 39, I**: Slow introduction (Adagio, 23 measures) in E♭ major, sustained chords and majestic character, transitions to quick Allegro with main themes.

**Beethoven, Symphony No. 7, I**: Extended introduction (Poco sostenuto, 62 measures!), one of longest proportionally, builds anticipation through harmonic exploration, rhythmic patterns previewed.

**Haydn, "Surprise" Symphony No. 94, I**: Brief Adagio introduction establishes D major simply before famous Andante main theme.

**Beethoven, Piano Sonata Op. 13 ("Pathétique"), I**: Slow introduction returns between sections (not typical), creates cyclic connection, dramatic and integral to formal conception.

## Common Confusions

- Introduction precedes main formal structure (not part of exposition)
- Typically not returned in recapitulation (unlike main themes)
- Introduction material usually distinct from primary/secondary themes
- Slow introduction common in symphonies; less common in solo sonatas
- Not required (many movements begin directly with main theme)
- May end on dominant (half cadence), resolved by main theme tonic arrival
- Classical introductions often in same key as main movement
- Romantic introductions may be modulatory or programmatic
- Length variable (4 measures to 60+ measures in Beethoven)
- Tempo contrast typical but not absolute requirement
- "Slow introduction" most common type (Adagio/Grave before Allegro)
- Haydn and Beethoven symphonies frequently use introductions
- Mozart symphonies occasionally use introductions (less common than Haydn)
- Introduction creates rhetorical weight (movements without feel lighter)
- Can create harmonic ambiguity resolved by main theme
- Fermata often marks end of introduction (pause before main tempo)
- Some works (Beethoven Pathétique) integrate introduction into form cyclically
