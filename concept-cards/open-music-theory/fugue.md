# Fugue

## Quick Definition

A contrapuntal composition featuring strict use of a specific number of independent voices, each entering successively with the same main theme (subject), developed through imitation and other contrapuntal techniques.

## Formal Definition

A **fugue** is a contrapuntal composition or technique characterized by the systematic presentation of a melodic theme (the **subject**) in multiple independent voices. Each voice enters one by one, stating the subject or its transposition (the **answer**), creating a complex polyphonic texture.

**Essential elements**:
- **Fixed number of voices**: Typically 3-4, though 2-6 are possible
- **Subject**: The main melodic idea that recurs throughout
- **Answer**: Transposition of the subject (usually at the fifth or fourth)
- **Exposition**: Opening section where each voice enters with subject or answer
- **Countersubject**: Melodic material that accompanies subsequent subject/answer entries
- **Episodes**: Transitional sections between subject entries, often using fragments of the subject
- **Development**: Later sections with subject entries in various keys, with stretto, inversion, augmentation, diminution, etc.

**Voice entry pattern** (in exposition):
1. Voice 1: Subject
2. Voice 2: Answer (while Voice 1 continues with countersubject)
3. Voice 3: Subject (while Voices 1 and 2 continue)
4. Voice 4: Answer (while other voices continue)

**Types of fugue**:
- **Instrumental fugue**: For keyboard, strings, or other instruments (Bach, Well-Tempered Clavier)
- **Vocal fugue**: For voices, often in sacred music (Bach, Mass in B minor)
- **Double/triple fugue**: Features two or three subjects
- **Ricercar**: Slow, antiquated style with suspensions
- **Toccata-style**: Fast, chromatic, with motor rhythm

## Musical Context

The fugue serves as the pinnacle of Baroque contrapuntal technique:

**Historical development**:
- **Renaissance precursors**: Imitative polyphony, ricercar, canzona
- **Early Baroque (1600-1680)**: Fugal techniques develop from imitative practices
- **High Baroque (1680-1750)**: Fugue reaches maturity (Bach, Handel, Buxtehude)
- **Classical/Romantic**: Used in development sections and finale climaxes (Mozart, Beethoven, Brahms)
- **20th/21st century**: Continues in neoclassical and academic contexts (Shostakovich, Hindemith, Britten)

**Pedagogical importance**:
- Teaches advanced counterpoint and voice independence
- Develops understanding of tonal structure and modulation
- Trains compositional craft and structural thinking
- Demonstrates synthesis of melody, harmony, and form

**Expressive range**:
- Intellectual rigor and mathematical precision
- Emotional depth and expressive power
- Sacred and ceremonial grandeur
- Dance-like energy (some Bach fugues are based on dance rhythms)

**Fugal devices** (used after exposition):
- **Stretto**: Overlapping subject entries
- **Inversion**: Subject presented upside down
- **Augmentation**: Subject in longer note values
- **Diminution**: Subject in shorter note values
- **Retrograde**: Subject backwards
- **Counter-exposition**: Second exposition in different key

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple three-voice fugue structure**:
- Exposition:
  - Voice 1: Subject (C major)
  - Voice 2: Answer (G major) + Voice 1: Countersubject
  - Voice 3: Subject (C major) + Voices 1-2: Free counterpoint
- Episode: Sequences using subject fragments, modulating
- Middle entries: Subject in various keys
- Final entry: Subject returns in tonic

### From Repertoire

- **Bach, Well-Tempered Clavier**: 48 fugues demonstrating every major and minor key
  - BWV 847 (C minor): Three voices, tonal answer, two countersubjects
  - BWV 846 (C major): Four voices, simple real answer
- **Bach, Art of Fugue (BWV 1080)**: Systematic exploration of fugal techniques
- **Bach, Fugue in G minor "Little" (BWV 578)**: Organ fugue, four voices
- **Handel, Messiah, "And with His stripes"**: Vocal fugue in oratorio
- **Mozart, Requiem, "Kyrie"**: Double fugue
- **Beethoven, String Quartet Op. 131**: Opening fugue in C♯ minor
- **Brahms, Variations and Fugue on a Theme by Handel, Op. 24**: Piano fugue
- **Shostakovich, 24 Preludes and Fugues**: 20th-century fugal cycle

## Related Concepts

- **Prerequisite**: counterpoint, imitation, transposition, modulation
- **Leads to**: exposition, countersubject, stretto, inversion, fragmentation, retrograde
- **See also**: canzona, ricercar, toccata, species-counterpoint

## Common Confusions

- Fugue vs. canon: Fugue has free development after exposition; canon is strict continuous imitation throughout
- Fugue vs. sonata form: Both have "expositions," but fugue exposition introduces voices with the subject; sonata exposition presents themes and keys
- The number of voices is not the same as the number of performers—a keyboard fugue may have 3-4 voices played by one person
- Not all fugue subjects are fast—ricercar-style fugues are slow and stately
- A fugue doesn't have to use every possible device (stretto, inversion, etc.)—many Bach fugues are relatively simple
- Subject and answer alternate (usually), but both are versions of the main theme—the answer is not a "response" but a transposition
- Fugue is both a form (with exposition, episodes, etc.) and a technique (fugal writing)
- Not all imitative counterpoint is fugue—fugue requires specific structural elements
- The term "voices" applies to both vocal and instrumental fugues
