# Suspension

## Quick Definition

An accented non-chord tone that occurs when a consonant note is held over (tied) to a strong beat where it becomes dissonant, then resolves downward by step to a consonance.

## Formal Definition

A **suspension** is a rhythmic-harmonic device in which a note is sustained (or repeated) from a weak beat to a strong beat, creating a dissonance on the accented beat that resolves downward by step to a consonance.

**The three-part suspension figure**:
1. **Preparation**: A consonant note on a weak beat (or unaccented position)
2. **Suspension**: The same note sustained or tied to a strong beat (accented position), where it becomes dissonant with the bass or other voices
3. **Resolution**: A note one step below the suspension, consonant with the bass, occurring on a weak beat

**Key characteristics**:
- **Accented dissonance**: Unlike passing tones or neighbor tones, the dissonance falls on a strong beat
- **Tied or sustained**: The preparation and suspension are the same pitch
- **Stepwise downward resolution**: The resolution always moves down by step
- **Metric displacement**: Creates syncopation and delays harmonic resolution

**Suspension types** (labeled by intervals with the bass):
- **Above the bass**: 9–8 (2–1), 7–6, 4–3 are common
- **Below the bass**: 2–3 is the main type
- Numbers indicate: [suspension interval]–[resolution interval]
- Example: 4–3 means a fourth above the bass suspends and resolves to a third

**Special types**:
- **Consonant suspension**: Tied figure where the suspension is consonant (e.g., 5–6)—technically not a dissonant suspension
- **Retardation**: Upward-resolving suspension (rare, opposite of normal suspension)
- **Chain of suspensions**: Multiple suspensions in succession

## Musical Context

Suspensions serve crucial expressive and structural functions in tonal music:

**Expressive functions**:
- Create tension through accented dissonance
- Delay expected harmonic motion
- Add pathos and emotional weight
- Enhance cadential arrivals
- Generate forward momentum through resolution tendency

**Harmonic functions**:
- Embellish and delay chord tones
- Create dissonance on strong beats for expressive effect
- Strengthen harmonic progression through tension-resolution
- Mark important structural moments (especially cadences)

**Rhythmic functions**:
- Create syncopation
- Add rhythmic variety and interest
- Emphasize metric structure through displacement
- Generate forward drive across bar lines

**Stylistic uses**:
- **Baroque**: Elaborate chains of suspensions (Corelli, Bach, Handel)
- **Classical**: Cadential 4–3 suspensions ubiquitous
- **Romantic**: Expressive suspensions for dramatic effect
- **Jazz**: Suspended chords (sus4, sus2) derive from suspension practice
- **Contemporary**: Suspensions in film music for emotional impact

Suspensions are among the most important dissonance types in all tonal music.

## Examples

### Basic

**4–3 suspension** (above bass):
- Bass: G (half) – F (half)
- Upper: C (half, consonant 4th) [tie] – C (suspension, dissonant 4th) – B (resolution, consonant 3rd)
  - Preparation: C (weak beat, P4 with G—consonant in this context)
  - Suspension: C (strong beat, P4 with F—dissonant)
  - Resolution: B♭ (weak beat, M3 with F—consonant)

**7–6 suspension** (above bass):
- Bass: C (half) – D (half)
- Upper: B (half, consonant 7th) [tie] – B (suspension, dissonant 7th) – A (resolution, consonant 6th)
  - Preparation: B (weak beat, M7 with C)
  - Suspension: B (strong beat, m7 with D—dissonant)
  - Resolution: A (weak beat, P5/M6 with D—consonant)

**9–8 suspension** (also called 2–1):
- Bass: D (half) – C (half)
- Upper: E (half) [tie] – E (suspension, 9th/2nd) – D (resolution, octave/unison)

**2–3 suspension** (below bass):
- Bass: C (half) – D (half)
- Lower: D (half, consonant 2nd below) [tie] – D (suspension, 2nd below C) – E (resolution, 3rd below D)

### From Repertoire

- **Bach, Cello Suite No. 1, Prélude**: Chain of suspensions throughout
- **Handel, "And with His stripes" from Messiah**: Famous chain of 4–3 suspensions
- **Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 545, I**: Cadential 4–3 suspensions
- **Beethoven, Piano Sonata Op. 13 "Pathétique"**: Expressive suspensions in slow introduction
- **Chopin, Nocturnes**: Elaborate chromatic suspensions
- **Jazz**: "Sus" chords (Csus4 = C–F–G, derived from 4–3 suspension over C)
- **The Beatles, "A Hard Day's Night"**: Opening chord includes suspensions

## Common Confusions

- Suspension vs. passing tone: Suspensions are accented (strong beat); passing tones are unaccented (weak beat)
- Suspension vs. appoggiatura: Suspensions are tied/sustained from preparation; appoggiaturas are approached by leap
- All suspensions resolve down by step—upward resolutions are called retardations (much rarer)
- The preparation must be consonant—you cannot prepare a suspension with a dissonance
- The numbers (4–3, 7–6, etc.) indicate intervals above (or below) the bass, not scale degrees
- "Suspended chord" (like Csus4) is related but not identical to a suspension—it's derived from suspension practice
- The suspension itself is the dissonant note on the strong beat, not the entire three-part figure (though "suspension" often refers to the whole figure)
- Consonant suspensions (like 5–6) don't create dissonance but still feature the tied, syncopated rhythm
- "Chain of suspensions" means multiple suspensions in a row, creating a sequence of tension and resolution
