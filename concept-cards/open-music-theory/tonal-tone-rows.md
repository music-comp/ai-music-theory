# Quick Definition
Tonal tone rows are twelve-tone rows specifically designed to produce tonal-sounding music, featuring embedded triads, fifths, or other tonal intervals. Examples include Berg's Violin Concerto row (centered on triads and fifths), Hale Smith's Evocation (with jazz-influenced quartal harmony), and Britten's Turn of the Screw (quartal structure organizing both theme and tonal centers).

# Core Definition
Some composers deliberately designed rows to produce tonal associations, demonstrating that twelve-tone technique and tonality are not mutually exclusive. Berg's Violin Concerto row contains alternating major and minor triads plus a whole-tone segment. Smith's Evocation foregrounds set [0,2,7], linked to quartal jazz stylings. Britten's Turn of the Screw uses a quartal row to organize both the theme and the key scheme of the acts. These works challenge the assumption that serial technique necessarily produces "atonal" music. Schoenberg's own early serial works used tonal forms, rhythms, and idioms (waltzes) despite twelve-tone pitch organization.

# Key Properties
1. Rows designed with embedded tonal intervals (triads, fifths, fourths)
2. Demonstrates compatibility of twelve-tone technique with tonal sound
3. Challenges the "serial = atonal" equation
4. Parametric choices (form, rhythm, idiom) can reinforce tonal associations

# Context & Application
Tonal tone rows demonstrate the flexibility of serial technique and its compatibility with various aesthetic goals.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Berg, Violin Concerto): Row based on triads and fifths: G-Bb-D-F#-A-C-E-G#-B-C#-Eb-F.
**Example 2** (Hale Smith, Evocation): Foregrounding set [0,2,7] with jazz-influenced quartal harmony.
**Example 3** (Britten, Turn of the Screw): Quartal row structure organizing theme and tonal centers.
