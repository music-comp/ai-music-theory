## Quick Definition

A schema of at least four chords whose roots descend by perfect fifth, often creating motion from minor tonic toward the relative major.

## Formal Definition

The circle-of-fifths schema consists of a chain of chords with roots descending by perfect fifth (or ascending by perfect fourth). While it can begin on any chord, it frequently starts on i in minor and progresses toward III (the relative major). The schema exhibits tonal ambiguity similar to the singer/songwriter progression, as the VII-III motion can sound like V-I in the relative major.

## Musical Context

Derived from common-practice harmonic sequences, the circle-of-fifths schema maintains its classical associations while appearing in pop/rock contexts. Longer chains can include chromatic alterations, with chords functioning as applied ii-V progressions.

### Tonal Ambiguity

- Starting chord (i) initially sounds like tonic
- VII-III motion can be heard as V-I in relative major
- Schema can facilitate modulation between relative keys

## Examples

### Basic Example

In A minor: Am - Dm - G - C (i-iv-VII-III)
Root motion: A down 5th to D, down 5th to G, down 5th to C

### From Repertoire

- "Barbie Girl" by Aqua (1997) - chorus uses tonally ambiguous circle-of-fifths; relative major/minor both viable
- "Thoughts of a Dying Atheist" by Muse (2004) - four-chord circle-of-fifths follows lament; progression facilitates move to relative major chorus
- "You and Whose Army?" by Radiohead (2004) - chromatic sequence with each chord pair functioning as applied ii-V; tonally ambiguous between E major and C# minor

## Common Confusions

- **Circle-of-fifths vs. random fifth motion**: The schema requires a consistent chain of descending fifths, not just occasional fifth relationships
- **Pop vs. classical usage**: Classical sequences often modulate; pop versions may loop back to the starting chord
- **Tonal center identification**: The ambiguity means both the starting minor chord and the relative major may legitimately be heard as tonic
