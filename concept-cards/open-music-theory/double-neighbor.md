# Double Neighbor

## Quick Definition

A four-note melodic figure that ornaments a chord tone by moving to both the upper and lower neighbor tones before returning to the original pitch.

## Formal Definition

A **double neighbor** (also called **double auxiliary**) is a melodic embellishment that combines both upper and lower neighbor tones in a single four-note figure. The pattern starts and ends on the same chord tone, with the two middle notes being the pitches a step above and a step below the main tone.

**Structure**:
- Beat 1: Consonant chord tone
- Beat 2: Step away (upper or lower neighbor)
- Beat 3: Step to the opposite neighbor (lower or upper)
- Beat 4: Step back to the original tone

**Two orderings**:
1. **Upper-then-lower**: Original → upper neighbor → lower neighbor → original (e.g., C–D–B–C)
2. **Lower-then-upper**: Original → lower neighbor → upper neighbor → original (e.g., C–B–D–C)

**Characteristics in third species**:
- Beats 1 and 4 are the same pitch (consonant with cantus)
- Beats 2 and 3 are typically dissonant
- The leap between beats 2 and 3 is usually a third (between the two neighbors)
- Despite the leap and dissonances, the overall effect is smooth because the figure is entirely stepwise embellishment of a single tone

**Voice-leading guideline**: The direction between beats 3 and 4 should match the direction between beat 4 and the following downbeat, maintaining stepwise motion across the bar line.

## Musical Context

The double neighbor serves specific melodic functions:

**Melodic functions**:
- Ornaments a sustained or repeated chord tone with maximum variety
- Creates melodic interest around a single pitch
- Provides both upper and lower chromatic/diatonic color
- Adds rhythmic motion to a static harmony

**Harmonic functions**:
- Embellishes a chord tone without changing the underlying harmony
- Creates two dissonances (beats 2 and 3) that resolve to the same consonance
- The dissonant leap (beat 2 to beat 3) is smoothed by the stepwise context

**Pedagogical value**:
- Teaches that leaps from dissonances are sometimes acceptable in specific contexts
- Demonstrates how overall stepwise motion can mitigate local irregularities
- Introduced in third species to add variety beyond simple passing and neighbor tones

**Stylistic uses**:
- Common in Baroque ornamentation (related to the "turn")
- Classical melodic embellishment
- Appears as rapid ornamental figures in many styles
- Related to the musical ornament called a "turn"

## Examples

### Basic

**Upper-then-lower double neighbor**:
- Cantus: C (whole note)
- Counterpoint: E–F–D–E (four quarter notes)
  Intervals: 3–4–2–3
  (E is the main tone; F is upper neighbor; D is lower neighbor)

**Lower-then-upper double neighbor**:
- Cantus: C (whole note)
- Counterpoint: G–F–A–G (four quarter notes)
  Intervals: 5–4–6–5
  (G is the main tone; F is lower neighbor; A is upper neighbor)

**Chromatic double neighbor**:
- Main tone: C
- Pattern: C–C♯–B♭–C
  (C♯ is chromatic upper neighbor; B♭ is chromatic lower neighbor)

### From Repertoire

Double neighbors appear in various guises:
- **Turn ornament**: A rapid double-neighbor figure (written as an ornament symbol)
- Bach: Keyboard works use turn-like figures extensively
- Mozart: Ornamental passages with double-neighbor patterns
- Classical ornamentation: The "turn" is essentially a double neighbor
- Jazz: Chromatic enclosures (upper and lower chromatic neighbors) are a common bebop device

## Related Concepts

- **Prerequisite**: neighbor-tone, consonance, dissonance

## Common Confusions

- Double neighbor vs. two separate neighbor tones: A double neighbor is a specific four-note figure with a leap in the middle, not just two neighbors used separately
- The leap between the two neighbors (beat 2 to beat 3) is unusual—most dissonances resolve by step, but the double neighbor is an exception
- The order matters less than the overall pattern: both upper-then-lower and lower-then-upper are valid
- Related to but distinct from a "turn" ornament: turns are typically faster and may include additional notes
- In third-species counterpoint, the smoothness of motion into and out of the double neighbor (beat 4 to following downbeat) is crucial
- Sometimes called "changing tones" (plural), but this risks confusion with the nota cambiata (changing tone)
