# Half Step

## Quick Definition

The smallest interval in Western music notation, equal to the distance between two adjacent keys on a piano.

## Formal Definition

A **half step** (also called a semitone) is the smallest interval (distance between two pitches) in standard Western musical notation. In the equal-tempered tuning system, a half step represents a frequency ratio of approximately 2^(1/12):1.

On a piano keyboard:
- A half step above a white key is typically the black key to its immediate right
- A half step below a white key is typically the black key to its immediate left
- The pairs E-F and B-C are natural half steps (no black key between them)

In pitch-class integer notation, a half step = 1 semitone. The ordered pitch-class interval from pitch class a to pitch class b is i(a,b) = (b - a) mod 12.

## Musical Context

Half steps are the building blocks of scales, melodies, and harmonic progressions in Western music. The pattern of half steps and whole steps determines the character of scales (major, minor, chromatic, etc.).

The half step has a characteristic sound: close, tense, and unstable. This tension creates expectations for resolution in tonal music. Leading tones move by half step to the tonic, and tendency tones in chords often resolve by half step.

## Examples

### Basic

On a piano:
- C to C♯ (or D♭) = half step
- E to F = half step (natural half step, no black key)
- B to C = half step (natural half step, no black key)
- G♯ to A = half step

### From Repertoire

The opening of Beethoven's "Für Elise" features prominent half-step motion (E-D♯-E-D♯-E). The theme from "Jaws" uses repeating half steps (E-F, E-F) to create tension.

## Common Confusions

- Half step vs. whole step: A whole step equals two half steps
- On a keyboard, E-F and B-C look like they might be whole steps (both white keys), but they are half steps
- In pitch-class theory, moving by half step = adding or subtracting 1 (mod 12)
- Enharmonically equivalent notes (like C♯ and D♭) sound the same but may function differently in musical contexts
