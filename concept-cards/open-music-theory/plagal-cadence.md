# Plagal Cadence

## Quick Definition

A cadence featuring the progression IV-I (subdominant to tonic), creating a gentler, more archaic sense of closure than the dominant-to-tonic authentic cadence, commonly known as the "Amen cadence" from its use in hymns where congregations sing "A-men" over IV-I at the end of verses—functioning as a supplement or extension to authentic cadences rather than a primary closing gesture in common-practice music.

## Formal Definition

**Plagal Cadence** (PC) is a cadence characterized by the progression IV → I (subdominant to tonic), offering an alternative conclusive gesture to the dominant-based authentic cadence.

**Essential progression**: IV → I (subdominant to tonic)

**Common names**:
- Plagal cadence (technical term)
- "Amen cadence" (from hymn tradition)
- Subdominant cadence

**Characteristics**:
- Weaker closure than authentic cadence (V-I)
- Gentler, more archaic character
- Often follows authentic cadence as extension
- No leading-tone resolution (unlike V-I)

**In minor keys**: iv → i (both chords minor)

## Historical Context

**Medieval/Renaissance origins**:
- Plagal modes ended with whole-step descent to final
- IV-I reflects plagal mode cadential pattern
- Predates dominant-based tonality

**Common-practice period**:
- Supplementary to authentic cadences
- Often extends or reinforces PAC
- Less common as primary cadence

**Hymn tradition**:
- "Amen" sung over IV-I
- Congregational singing reinforced association
- Hence "Amen cadence" nickname

## Plagal Cadence vs. Authentic Cadence

**Authentic (V-I)**:
- Strong dominant function (leading tone)
- Primary cadence in tonal music
- Creates maximum closure

**Plagal (IV-I)**:
- Subdominant function (no leading tone)
- Secondary/supplementary cadence
- Gentler, less decisive closure

**Usage hierarchy**: AC > PC (authentic stronger/more common)

## Typical Usage

**After authentic cadence** (extension):
```
...IV - V - I [PAC] - IV - I [PC]

AC provides primary closure
PC extends and reinforces
```

**Hymn ending**:
```
...V - I [PAC]
A - men [IV-I, sung by congregation]
```

**Standalone plagal** (less common):
```
...IV - I [PC]

Works but weaker than V-I
```

## Function in Harmony

**Subdominant function**:
- IV belongs to predominant (subdominant) function
- Normally moves TO dominant (IV-V)
- In plagal cadence: moves directly to tonic (IV-I)

**Avoids dominant**:
- Skips V entirely
- No leading-tone resolution
- Creates different color than V-I

**Post-cadential**:
- Often appears after authentic cadence
- Extends tonic arrival
- Adds weight to conclusion

## Voice Leading

**Typical plagal voice leading** (IV-I):
```
Soprano: ^6 → ^5 (or ^1 → ^1)
Alto:    ^4 → ^3
Tenor:   ^1 → ^1 (common tone)
Bass:    ^4 → ^1

Smooth voice leading, common-tone connection
```

**No leading tone**:
- Unlike V-I, no ^7 → ^1 resolution
- Creates different harmonic color
- Gentler, less urgent resolution

## Musical Context

Plagal cadences serve specific purposes:
- **Hymn endings**: "Amen" cadences in sacred music
- **Post-cadential extension**: After PAC for added weight
- **Archaic character**: Evokes medieval/Renaissance style
- **Gentle closure**: Less dramatic than authentic cadence
- **Supplement**: Reinforces rather than replaces authentic cadence

Relatively rare as primary cadence; more common as supplement.

## Examples

### Basic

**Simple plagal cadence** (C major):
```
F   -  C
IV  -  I [PC]

Subdominant to tonic
"Amen" pattern
```

**After authentic cadence**:
```
G  -  C  -  F  -  C
V  -  I  -  IV -  I
   [PAC]    [PC]

AC provides closure, PC extends
```

**In minor** (A minor):
```
Dm  -  Am
iv  -  i [PC]

Minor plagal cadence
Same subdominant-tonic relationship
```

**Hymn pattern**:
```
...IV - V  - I  - IV - I
   PD   D    T    PD   T
        [PAC]      [PC]

Standard hymn ending with "Amen"
```

**IV-I with seventh** (rarer):
```
F-A-C-E  -  C-E-G-C
IV⁷      -  I

Plagal with added seventh
More color, still subdominant function
```

### From Repertoire

**Hymns (generic)**: Countless hymns end with plagal cadence on "Amen" (IV-I), sung by congregation after verse concludes with PAC.

**Handel, Messiah, "Hallelujah Chorus"**: Final "Hallelujah" includes plagal cadence extensions after authentic cadences, adding weight to conclusion.

**Bach, Chorales**: Some phrases end with plagal cadences, though authentic cadences far more common in chorale tradition.

**Beatles, "Let It Be"**: Modern example, final "let it be" uses IV-I plagal cadence, demonstrates continued use in popular music.

**Mozart, Requiem, various movements**: Plagal cadences appear in sacred movements, appropriate to liturgical context.

## Common Confusions

- Plagal cadence = IV-I (subdominant to tonic)
- "Amen cadence" nickname from hymn tradition (IV-I on "A-men")
- Weaker than authentic cadence (V-I stronger)
- Often supplements authentic cadence (comes after, not instead of)
- No leading-tone resolution (unlike V-I which has ^7 → ^1)
- Plagal ≠ plagiarism (unrelated words; plagal from Medieval modes)
- IV-I works as cadence but less common than V-I in tonal music
- Post-cadential function typical (extends PAC)
- "Plagal" from plagal modes (Medieval/Renaissance modal theory)
- Not wrong or weak music (just different harmonic color)
- Minor plagal: iv-i (both chords minor quality)
- Can end pieces (but usually after authentic cadence)
- Hymns preserve tradition (congregational "Amen" over IV-I)
- IV has subdominant/predominant function (normally moves to V)
- Plagal skips dominant entirely (IV directly to I)
- Less urgent than authentic (no dominant tension/resolution)
- Renaissance/Medieval origins (predates dominant-tonic tonality)
