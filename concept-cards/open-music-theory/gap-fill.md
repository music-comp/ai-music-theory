# Gap Fill

## Quick Definition

A melodic principle where a large leap in one direction is followed (and often preceded) by stepwise motion in the opposite direction, filling in the gap created by the leap.

## Formal Definition

**Gap fill** (also called **gap-fill melody** or **post-skip reversal**) is a melodic principle in which a melodic line that makes a large leap subsequently moves by step in the opposite direction, "filling in" the interval that was leaped over. This creates a balance between disjunct and conjunct motion and helps maintain a cohesive melodic line.

**Characteristics**:
- **The gap**: A melodic leap (typically a fourth or larger)
- **The fill**: Stepwise motion in the opposite direction following the leap
- **Direction**: Fill proceeds in the opposite direction from the leap
- **Extent**: The fill may partially or completely fill the gap
- **Function**: Balances leaps with stepwise motion, creating melodic coherence

**Typical pattern**:
1. Melodic line leaps (e.g., C up to G, a fifth)
2. Melodic line moves stepwise in the opposite direction (e.g., G–F–E–D–C, filling the gap)

**Related principle**: Gap fill is related to the concept of **regression to the mean** or **return to tessitura**—after moving away from the melodic center, the line tends to return toward the middle of its range.

## Musical Context

Gap fill serves important melodic functions:

**Melodic functions**:
- Creates balance between leaps and steps
- Maintains melodic coherence and smoothness
- Prevents melodic lines from wandering too far from their center
- Helps define the melodic range without excessive leaps in one direction
- Provides a sense of completion after a leap

**Voice-leading principle**:
- Especially important in 16th-century counterpoint and species counterpoint
- Helps avoid melodic lines that are too disjunct or angular
- Maintains singability by balancing large intervals with stepwise motion
- Prevents excessive outlining of dissonant intervals (sevenths, tritones)

**Perceptual basis**:
- Large leaps create gaps in the melodic space
- Listeners expect these gaps to be filled in
- Stepwise motion in the opposite direction provides melodic closure
- Creates a sense of melodic direction and purpose

**Stylistic application**:
- **Renaissance/Baroque**: Strong convention, especially in vocal writing
- **Classical**: Continues as a general principle, though less rigid
- **Romantic**: More flexible, but still informs melodic writing
- **Contemporary**: Principle still relevant in melodic construction

## Examples

### Basic

**Gap fill after ascending leap**:
- Melody: C–G (leap up a fifth) – F–E–D–C (stepwise descent filling the gap)
- The ascending fifth is followed by stepwise descent back to the starting note

**Gap fill after descending leap**:
- Melody: G–C (leap down a fifth) – D–E–F–G (stepwise ascent filling the gap)
- The descending fifth is followed by stepwise ascent back to the starting note

**Partial gap fill**:
- Melody: C–A (leap up a sixth) – G–F–E (stepwise descent partially filling the gap)
- Not all gaps are completely filled, but the direction is reversed

**Gap fill with preceding stepwise motion**:
- Melody: E–D–C (stepwise descent) – A (leap down a third) – B–C (stepwise ascent filling the leap)
- Gap fill principle applies to both directions

### From Repertoire

- **Palestrina, Masses**: Vocal lines consistently follow gap-fill principle
- **Bach, Cello Suites**: Melodic lines leap and then fill in the gaps stepwise
- **Mozart, Symphony No. 40, first theme**: Leaps followed by stepwise contrary motion
- **Beethoven, "Ode to Joy"**: Primarily stepwise, but leaps are followed by stepwise motion in opposite direction
- **Folk melodies**: Often demonstrate gap-fill principle naturally
- **Jazz improvisation**: Bebop lines frequently use gap-fill patterns

## Common Confusions

- Gap fill doesn't require completely filling the entire leap—partial fills are common
- The fill doesn't have to be immediate—other notes may intervene before the fill
- Not every leap requires gap fill, but it's a strong tendency especially for larger leaps
- Gap fill is a principle, not a rule—composers may intentionally violate it for effect
- The principle applies primarily to leaps of a fourth or larger; thirds may or may not be filled
- "Filling" means stepwise motion in the opposite direction, not just any motion after a leap
- Gap fill is related to but distinct from arch-shaped melodies (which may use leaps in both directions)
- Consecutive leaps in the same direction are less common precisely because they create unfilled gaps
- In 16th-century style, larger leaps (sixth, octave) more strongly require gap fill
