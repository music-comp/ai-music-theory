## Quick Definition

The IV-I harmonic motion that serves as the foundation for blues-based schemas in pop/rock, often enhanced with minor iv or extended through applied IV chords.

## Formal Definition

Plagal motion (IV-I) is fundamental to blues-based and "flat-side" schemas in pop/rock. While IV functions as a predominant chord in classical harmony, it takes on a more prominent role in popular music, often serving as the primary harmonic motion. This prominence may derive from the 5-6 neighboring motion in shuffle-blues guitar accompaniment patterns.

## Musical Context

### Basic Plagal Vamp

The alternation between I and IV creates the simplest plagal schema, extremely common in R&B, soul, and rock.

### The Plagal Sigh (IV-iv-I)

The minor iv chord creates the voice-leading ^6-flat^6-^5 (la-le-sol), producing a particularly expressive cadential gesture. This "plagal sigh" (term from J. Kent Williams and Frank Lehman) often accompanies lyrics of nostalgia and sentimentality.

### Double Plagal (flat-VII-IV-I)

Expands plagal motion by treating flat-VII as IV/IV (the IV of IV), creating a chain of plagal relationships.

### Extended Plagal

Applies the IV relationship in sequence: flat-VI-flat-III-flat-VII-IV-I (each chord is IV of the next).

## Examples

### Basic Example

- I-IV vamp: C-F-C-F...
- Plagal sigh: F-Fm-C (IV-iv-I)
- Double plagal: Bb-F-C (flat-VII-IV-I)

### From Repertoire

- "Soul Man" by Sam and Dave (1967) - I-IV alternation in the verse
- "In the Midnight Hour" by Wilson Pickett (1965) - similar I-IV oscillation
- "Wake Me Up When September Ends" by Green Day (2005) - IV-iv-I with nostalgic lyrics
- "Hey Jude" by The Beatles (1968) - famous double plagal coda (flat-VII-IV-I)
- "Hey Joe" by Jimi Hendrix (1966) - extended plagal: flat-VI-flat-III-flat-VII-IV-I

## Common Confusions

- **Plagal vs. authentic motion**: Plagal motion (IV-I) differs from authentic motion (V-I); both are common in pop but create different effects
- **flat-VII as applied IV**: The flat-VII chord can be understood as IV/IV (the IV of IV), explaining its prevalence in blues-based music
- **Plagal sigh context**: The IV-iv-I progression typically appears at phrase endings as a cadential gesture, not as a looping progression
