# Lowered Scale Degrees in Mixture

## Quick Definition

In modal mixture, three scale degrees can be lowered from their major-key position to create borrowed sounds from the parallel minor: lowered-^3 (me), lowered-^6 (le), and lowered-^7 (te), each producing distinct chord changes and chromatic effects.

## Formal Definition

The differences between parallel major and minor scales occur on three scale degrees:

| Major Scale | Minor Scale | Solfege Change | Interval Change |
|-------------|-------------|----------------|-----------------|
| ^3 (mi) | lowered-^3 (me) | mi → me | Major 3rd → Minor 3rd |
| ^6 (la) | lowered-^6 (le) | la → le | Major 6th → Minor 6th |
| ^7 (ti) | lowered-^7 (te) | ti → te | Major 7th → Minor 7th |

These three scale degrees, when borrowed from parallel minor into major, create the foundation for all modal mixture.

## Lowered-^6 (le) Borrowings

**The most common borrowed scale degree**

### Chords Affected

| Diatonic (with ^6) | Borrowed (with lowered-^6) | Notes in C |
|-------------------|---------------------------|------------|
| ii (D-F-A) | iio (D-F-A♭) | A → A♭ |
| ii7 (D-F-A-C) | ii∅7 (D-F-A♭-C) | A → A♭ |
| IV (F-A-C) | iv (F-A♭-C) | A → A♭ |

### Voice Leading

**lowered-^6 resolves DOWN to ^5**:
```
A♭ → G in C major
lowered-^6 → ^5
Half-step descent
```

This is the most common chromatic voice leading in modal mixture.

### Character

- Creates poignant, dark sound
- Minimal chromatic alteration (one note)
- Very common in Romantic and popular music

## Lowered-^3 (me) Borrowings

**Affects chord quality and creates altered-root chords**

### Chords Affected

| Diatonic (with ^3) | Borrowed (with lowered-^3) | Notes in C |
|-------------------|---------------------------|------------|
| I (C-E-G) | i (C-E♭-G) | E → E♭ |
| vi (A-C-E) | ♭VI (A♭-C-E♭) | Both A and E lowered |
| iii (E-G-B) | ♭III (E♭-G-B♭) | E and B both lowered |

### Voice Leading

**lowered-^3 resolves DOWN to ^2 or UP to ^3**:
```
E♭ → D or E♭ → E natural
lowered-^3 → ^2 or lowered-^3 → ^3
Depends on context
```

### Character

- Creates darker, minor-mode color
- Often combined with lowered-^6 and/or lowered-^7
- Dramatic chromatic effect

## Lowered-^7 (te) Borrowings

**Eliminates leading tone, creates ♭VII**

### Chords Affected

| Diatonic (with ^7) | Borrowed (with lowered-^7) | Notes in C |
|-------------------|---------------------------|------------|
| viio (B-D-F) | ♭VII (B♭-D-F) | B → B♭ |
| V (G-B-D) | v (G-B♭-D) | B → B♭ |
| iii (E-G-B) | ♭III (E♭-G-B♭) | Both E and B lowered |

### Voice Leading

**lowered-^7 resolves UP to ^1 by whole step**:
```
B♭ → C in C major
lowered-^7 → ^1
Whole-step ascent (not half step like ^7 → ^1)
```

### Character

- Modal, less directional than leading tone
- Creates Mixolydian flavor
- Very common in rock and pop music

## Combinations

**Chords may use multiple lowered degrees**:

| Chord | Lowered Degrees | Notes in C |
|-------|-----------------|------------|
| ♭VI | lowered-^6 (root), lowered-^3 (fifth) | A♭-C-E♭ |
| ♭III | lowered-^3 (root), lowered-^7 (fifth) | E♭-G-B♭ |
| ♭VII | lowered-^7 (root only) | B♭-D-F |
| iv | lowered-^6 only | F-A♭-C |
| i | lowered-^3 only | C-E♭-G |

## Chromatic Voice Leading Summary

| Lowered Degree | Resolution | Interval |
|----------------|------------|----------|
| lowered-^6 (le) | → ^5 | Descending half step |
| lowered-^3 (me) | → ^2 or → ^3 | Varies by context |
| lowered-^7 (te) | → ^1 | Ascending whole step |

All lowered degrees tend to continue in the direction of their alteration (downward), though lowered-^7 often ascends to resolve to tonic.

## Musical Context

### Frequency of Use

1. **lowered-^6**: Most common (iv, iio6, ii∅7)
2. **lowered-^7**: Very common in popular music (♭VII)
3. **lowered-^3**: Common in combination with others (♭VI, ♭III, i)

### Stylistic Associations

- **Romantic era**: All three, especially lowered-^6
- **Rock/Pop**: lowered-^7 (♭VII) especially common
- **Film music**: All three for emotional coloring
- **Gospel**: lowered-^6 (iv chord) foundational

## Examples

### Basic

**Each lowered degree in C major**:

```
lowered-^6 only:
I - iv - V - I
C - Fm - G - C
(A♭ = lowered-^6)

lowered-^7 only:
I - ♭VII - IV - I
C - B♭  - F  - C
(B♭ = lowered-^7)

lowered-^3 (with others):
I - ♭VI - V - I
C - A♭  - G - C
(A♭ = lowered-^6 as root, E♭ = lowered-^3 as fifth)
```

**Multiple lowered degrees**:
```
I - ♭VI - ♭VII - I
C - A♭  - B♭   - C

lowered-^6, lowered-^3, and lowered-^7 all appear
Rich chromatic color
```

### From Repertoire

**Brahms (lowered-^6)**:
- Extensive use of iv for characteristic warmth
- lowered-^6 most prominent borrowed degree

**The Beatles (lowered-^7)**:
- ♭VII in countless songs
- "Hey Jude" coda uses it prominently

**Schubert (all three)**:
- Modulation between parallel modes
- All lowered degrees used for expressive depth

## Common Confusions

- Only THREE scale degrees differ between parallel major and minor: ^3, ^6, ^7
- When borrowed, these become lowered-^3, lowered-^6, lowered-^7
- The "lowered" indicates direction from major-key position
- lowered-^6 is most common and simplest (appears in iv, iio, ii∅7)
- lowered-^7 creates ♭VII (no longer a diminished chord, now major)
- lowered-^3 changes tonic quality (I → i)
- Some chords combine multiple lowered degrees (♭VI has both lowered-^6 and lowered-^3)
- Borrowed notes resolve in the direction of their alteration (lowered → downward)
- Exception: lowered-^7 often rises to ^1 (like a whole-step approach to tonic)
