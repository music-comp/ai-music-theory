# Suspended Chord

## Quick Definition

A chord in which the third is replaced by a fourth (sus4) or second (sus2) above the root, creating harmonic tension that often resolves.

## Formal Definition

A **suspended chord** (abbreviated "sus") replaces the third of a triad or seventh chord with another interval:

- **Sus4** (or simply "sus"): Third replaced by perfect fourth
  - Csus = C-F-G (instead of C-E-G)
- **Sus2**: Third replaced by major second
  - Csus2 = C-D-G (instead of C-E-G)

The term "suspended" comes from the classical concept of suspension, where a non-chord tone resolves by step to a chord tone. In practice, a sus chord often resolves to the corresponding regular chord (Csus to C), though this resolution is not required.

For seventh chords, "sus" follows the extension number:
- **C7sus** = C-F-G-Bb (not Csus7, which could be misread)

## Musical Context

Suspended chords create a sense of tension and openness because they lack the defining third that determines major or minor quality. They are common in jazz, rock, and pop music for:
- Creating harmonic ambiguity
- Adding variety to repeated chords
- Voice-leading purposes
- Modal and contemporary sounds

In jazz, sus chords appear frequently in modal compositions and as color chords in more traditional contexts.

## Examples

### Basic

- **Csus** or **Csus4** = C-F-G
- **Csus2** = C-D-G
- **C7sus** = C-F-G-Bb
- **Gsus** = G-C-D
- **Dsus2** = D-E-A

### From Repertoire

Sus chords are common in modal jazz (Miles Davis's "So What" era), contemporary pop, and rock music. In jazz standards, they often appear as passing harmonies or substitutes for dominant chords.

## Common Confusions

- "Sus" alone means sus4, NOT sus2
- Sus chords are NOT inversions; they have a different set of pitches entirely
- A sus chord does NOT have to resolve, despite the name suggesting suspension
- C7sus is different from Csus7; the "sus" goes after the extension number
- Sus chords omit the third entirely; they don't just move it
