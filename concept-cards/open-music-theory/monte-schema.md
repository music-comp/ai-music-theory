# Monte Schema

## Quick Definition

A four-stage ascending sequential galant schema that rises by whole step through two tonicizations (V/IV-IV, V/V-V), with each module featuring a characteristic sol-fa-mi or do-te-la melodic pattern, providing upward sequential motion and harmonic intensification in 18th-century music.

## Formal Definition

The **Monte** (Italian: "mountain" or "upward"; named by Joseph Riepel in 18th century) is an ascending sequential schema that creates harmonic ascent through two parallel tonicizations rising by whole step. The name reflects the climbing, ascending character of the sequence.

**Four-stage structure**:

| Stage | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|-------|---|---|---|---|
| Meter | W | S | W | S |
| Melody | do-te (^1-♭^7) | la (^6) | re-do (^2-^1) | ti (^7) |
| Bass | mi (^3) | fa (^4) | fi (♯^4) | sol (^5) |
| Figures | 6/5 | 5 | 6/5 | 5 |
| Roman numerals | V/IV | IV | V/V | V |

**Key characteristics**:
- **Sequential structure**: Two-stage module (stages 1-2) repeated a step higher (stages 3-4)
- **Ascending by step**: Entire pattern ascends one scale degree
- **Tonicization pattern**: V-I in one key, then V-I a step higher
- **Modular repetition**: Stages 3-4 are transposition of stages 1-2 up a whole step
- **Sol-fa-mi pattern**: Each module features descending melodic gesture within ascending sequence
- **Metric pattern**: Weak-strong-weak-strong (W-S-W-S)
- **Chromatic alteration**: ♯^4 (fi) in bass at stage 3 signals V/V tonicization

**Modularity**:
The core module (one tonicization):

| Stage | 1 | 2 |
|-------|---|---|
| Meter | W | S |
| Melody | sol-fa (^5-^4) | mi (^3) |
| Bass | ti (^7) | do (^1) |
| Figures | 6/5 | 5 |
| Roman numerals | V | I |

This module appears twice: first in subdominant context, then in dominant context (one step higher).

**Relationship to cycle of fifths**:
- Monte connects to ascending fifths in root motion
- Bass: ^3 → ^4 → ♯^4 → ^5 (chromatic ascent to dominant)
- Tonicizes IV, then V (pre-dominant → dominant trajectory)
- Each module = V-I resolution in successively higher keys

**Melodic variants**:
The soprano line may vary:
- **do-te-la, re-do-ti**: Chromatic neighbor figures
- **sol-fa-mi** pattern (related to Meyer/Fonte)
- Flexibility in upper voice while bass remains more fixed

**Function**:
- **Ascending continuation**: Provides upward sequential motion
- **Harmonic intensification**: Builds tension through rising tonicizations
- **Pre-dominant preparation**: Often leads to dominant arrival or half cadence
- **Sequential development**: Teaches ascending sequential technique

**Contrast with Fonte**:
- **Fonte**: Descends (V/ii-ii, V-I), releases tension
- **Monte**: Ascends (V/IV-IV, V/V-V), builds tension
- **Both**: Sequential, modular, use tonicization technique
- **Fonte**: Relaxation function
- **Monte**: Intensification function

## Musical Context

The Monte serves specific purposes in galant music:
- **Sequential ascent**: Creates rising harmonic trajectory
- **Tension building**: Ascending tonicizations increase harmonic energy
- **Pre-dominant function**: Prepares dominant arrival or half cadence
- **Developmental material**: Used in development sections or transitions
- **Improvisation pattern**: Provides ascending sequential framework

The ascending motion contrasts with descending schemas (Fonte, Prinner), providing variety and directional energy.

## Examples

### Basic

**Monte in C major**:
```
Stage 1 (W): Soprano C-Bb (do-te), Bass E4 (mi/^3), V6/5/IV (= V6/5 in F)
Stage 2 (S): Soprano A4 (la/^6), Bass F4 (fa/^4), IV (= I in F)
Stage 3 (W): Soprano D-C (re-do), Bass F#4 (fi/♯^4), V6/5/V (= V6/5 in G)
Stage 4 (S): Soprano B4 (ti/^7), Bass G4 (sol/^5), V (= I in G)

Module 1 (stages 1-2): Tonicizes F major (IV)
Module 2 (stages 3-4): Tonicizes G major (V), one step higher
Bass ascends: E-F-F#-G (chromatic approach to dominant)
```

**Monte modular structure** (showing repetition):
```
Module A (in F major context):
  sol-fa → mi over ti → do (in F: C-Bb-A over E-F)
  Creates V-I in F major

Module B (in G major context, one step higher):
  sol-fa → mi over ti → do (in G: D-C-B over F#-G)
  Creates V-I in G major

Same pattern, transposed up a step
```

**Monte bass line** (chromatic ascent):
```
Bass: E - F - F# - G
           ↑ (raised ^4 for V/V tonicization)
Chromatic ascent from mediant to dominant
F# signals shift to dominant tonicization
```

**Monte vs. Fonte trajectory**:
```
Monte (ascending): V/IV - IV - V/V - V (climbs toward V)
Fonte (descending): V/ii - ii - V - I (descends to I)

Opposite directional motion
Complementary functions (build vs. release tension)
```

### From Repertoire

**Mozart Piano Sonata K. 284, III (variations)**: Monte appears in developmental variations, demonstrating ascending sequential technique.

**Haydn String Quartet Op. 20, No. 5, I**: Uses Monte in development section to build tension before returning to main theme.

**Mozart Symphony No. 40, K. 550, I, development**: Monte provides ascending sequential motion in developmental passage.

**Galant keyboard sonatas** (J.C. Bach, Galuppi): Monte as standard continuation pattern for building harmonic intensity.

**C.P.E. Bach sonatas**: Features Monte in transitions and developmental sections, often with chromatic embellishments.

## Common Confusions

- Monte ≠ any ascending sequence—specific V/IV-IV, V/V-V pattern
- Ascending direction distinguishes from Fonte (descending)
- Chromatic bass (♯^4) essential for V/V tonicization
- Monte vs. ascending scale: Monte has specific harmonic structure
- Name "Monte" (mountain) describes ascent—functional name
- Soprano melody can vary (do-te-la or sol-fa-mi patterns)
- Two tonicizations ≠ true modulations (temporary tonal coloring)
- Monte typically leads to dominant (V), not tonic resolution
- Stages 3-4 are transposed copy of stages 1-2 up one step
- Monte builds tension; Fonte releases it (complementary functions)
- Named by Riepel, part of historical pedagogical tradition
- Can appear in minor mode with adjusted scale degrees
