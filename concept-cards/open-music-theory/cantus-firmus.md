# Cantus Firmus

## Quick Definition

A pre-existing or newly composed fixed melodic line that serves as the foundation for a species counterpoint exercise.

## Formal Definition

A **cantus firmus** (Latin for "fixed voice" or "fixed melody"; plural: *cantus firmi*) is a single, well-formed melodic line that serves as the basis for contrapuntal composition. In species counterpoint pedagogy, the cantus firmus is given first, and students compose one or more additional voices (counterpoints) against it.

Characteristics of a well-formed cantus firmus:
- **Length**: 8–16 notes
- **Rhythm**: Arhythmic (all whole notes—no rhythmic variety)
- **Range**: No more than a tenth, usually less than an octave
- **Starting/ending**: Begins and ends on the tonic (^1, *do*)
- **Approach to final**: Steps to final tonic (usually ^2–^1, sometimes ^7–^1)
- **Melodic intervals**: All note-to-note progressions are consonant
- **Climax**: Single high point, usually appearing once
- **Motion**: Mostly stepwise with some small leaps
- **Shape**: Clear logical connection from beginning to climax to end
- **Leaps**: Large leaps (fourth or larger) followed by step in opposite direction
- **Consecutive leaps**: No more than two in a row; no consecutive leaps in same direction
- **Leading tone**: Progresses to tonic (raised in minor modes)
- **No repetition**: Avoids motivic repetition or melodic "licks"

## Musical Context

The cantus firmus concept has two primary uses:

**Pedagogical** (species counterpoint):
- Provides a stable foundation for learning to compose counterpoint
- Fux's *Gradus ad Parnassum* (1725) established the standard pedagogical approach
- Students first learn to write good cantus firmi, then add counterpoint to them
- Allows focus on combining lines without having to compose both simultaneously

**Historical** (Renaissance polyphony):
- Pre-existing chant melodies served as cantus firmi for polyphonic compositions
- Composers added elaborate polyphonic voices around the chant
- Common in masses and motets based on Gregorian chant
- The cantus firmus was often placed in the tenor voice

The term emphasizes that this melody is "fixed"—it doesn't change while other voices are added around it.

## Examples

### Basic

A simple cantus firmus in C major (in whole notes):
C–D–F–E–D–C–E–G–F–E–D–C

This exhibits: stepwise motion, small leaps, single climax (G), ^2–^1 cadence, limited range.

### From Repertoire

**Historical cantus firmi**:
- Gregorian chant melodies used in Renaissance masses
- Lutheran chorales as cantus firmi in Bach's chorale preludes
- *L'homme armé* tune (secular melody used in many Renaissance masses)

**Pedagogical examples**:
- Fux's cantus firmi in *Gradus ad Parnassum* (one for each mode)
- Standard cantus firmi in theory textbooks

## Common Confusions

- Cantus firmus vs. theme: A theme can be rhythmically varied and motivic; a cantus firmus is rhythmically uniform and non-motivic
- Cantus firmus vs. melody: All cantus firmi are melodies, but not all melodies are cantus firmi—cantus firmi have specific compositional constraints
- The cantus firmus in species counterpoint is "given" or composed first, but it's not necessarily a famous or pre-existing tune
- Not all Renaissance polyphony uses cantus firmi—some is freely composed
- "Fixed" doesn't mean the cantus firmus is unchangeable historically, just that it doesn't change during the exercise
