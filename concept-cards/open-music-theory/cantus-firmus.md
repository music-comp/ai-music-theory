# Quick Definition
A pre-existing or newly composed fixed melodic line of 8-16 whole notes that serves as the foundation for species counterpoint exercises, exhibiting smoothness, clear shape, and goal-directed motion.

# Core Definition
A **cantus firmus** (Latin for "fixed voice" or "fixed melody"; plural *cantus firmi*) is a single, well-formed melodic line that serves as the basis for contrapuntal composition. In species counterpoint pedagogy, the cantus firmus is given first, and students compose one or more additional voices against it. The source lists specific characteristics of well-formed cantus firmi: length of 8-16 notes, all whole notes (arhythmic), beginning and ending on do (scale degree 1), approach to final by step (usually re-do or ti-do), all note-to-note progressions melodically consonant, range no more than a tenth, single climax, mostly stepwise motion, no motivic repetition, large leaps followed by step in opposite direction, and leading tone resolving to tonic (Ch. 1, "Composing a Cantus Firmus").

# Prerequisites
- **Scale degree** -- Cantus firmus rules are expressed in terms of scale degrees
- **Consonance** -- All melodic intervals must be consonant

# Key Properties
1. Length: 8-16 notes
2. Rhythm: Arhythmic (all whole notes)
3. Begins and ends on do (scale degree 1)
4. Approaches final by step: usually re-do (2-1), sometimes ti-do (7-1)
5. Range: no more than a tenth, usually less than an octave
6. Single climax (high point), usually appearing only once
7. Mostly stepwise motion with some small leaps
8. No motivic repetition or "licks"
9. Large leaps (fourth or larger) followed by step in opposite direction
10. No more than two leaps in a row; no consecutive leaps in same direction
11. Leading tone progresses to tonic (raised in minor modes)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Compose a Cantus Firmus:
1. Begin on do (scale degree 1)
2. Move mostly by step, incorporating occasional small leaps for variety
3. Create a single climax point, preferably reached by leap
4. Avoid motivic repetition -- each note-to-note progression should feel fresh
5. Follow any large leap with stepwise motion in the opposite direction
6. Approach the final note by step (re-do or ti-do)
7. End on do (scale degree 1)
8. Check range does not exceed a tenth
## To Evaluate a Cantus Firmus:
1. Verify beginning and ending on do
2. Check for single climax
3. Ensure mostly stepwise motion with controlled leaps
4. Confirm no motivic repetition
5. Verify all melodic intervals are consonant

# Context & Application
The cantus firmus concept serves two purposes. **Pedagogically**, it provides a stable foundation for learning counterpoint -- students first learn to write good cantus firmi, then add counterpoint against them. **Historically**, pre-existing chant melodies served as cantus firmi for Renaissance polyphonic masses and motets, often placed in the tenor voice. Fux's *Gradus ad Parnassum* (1725) established the standard pedagogical approach, providing one cantus firmus for each mode.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Writing a cantus firmus with repeated melodic patterns or "licks"
  **Correction**: Each passage should feel fresh; avoid motivic repetition
- **Error**: Including large leaps without compensating stepwise motion in the opposite direction
  **Correction**: Every leap of a fourth or larger must be followed by step in the opposite direction

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Cantus firmus is the same as a melody or theme
  **Clarification**: All cantus firmi are melodies, but cantus firmi have specific constraints (uniform rhythm, no motivic repetition) that ordinary melodies do not
- **Confusion**: The cantus firmus must be a pre-existing tune
  **Clarification**: In species counterpoint, it is often newly composed as an exercise; historically, pre-existing chants served this role
- **Confusion**: "Fixed" means the cantus firmus can never be changed
  **Clarification**: "Fixed" means it doesn't change during the exercise while other voices are added
