# Third Species

## Quick Definition

A species counterpoint exercise in which the counterpoint moves four notes (quarter notes) against each cantus firmus note (whole note), in a 4:1 rhythmic ratio.

## Formal Definition

**Third species** (or **third-species counterpoint**) is the third type of species counterpoint exercise, characterized by a 4:1 rhythmic ratio: four quarter notes in the counterpoint line against each whole note in the cantus firmus.

Third species introduces:
- **Three metrical levels**: strong (beat 1), moderately strong (beat 3), and weak (beats 2 and 4)
- **New dissonance types**: neighbor tone, double neighbor, nota cambiata
- **More elaborate melodic patterns**: greater stepwise motion and embellishment opportunities

**Dissonance types in third species**:
1. **Dissonant passing tone**: fills in a melodic third via stepwise motion (from second species)
2. **Dissonant neighbor tone**: steps away from and back to the same consonant tone
3. **Double neighbor**: both upper and lower neighbor tones together (e.g., C–D–B–C)
4. **Nota cambiata**: a special five-note figure that includes a leap from a dissonance

**Key rules**:
- Begin with do (^1) or sol (^5) above the cantus; do (^1) below
- End with clausula vera (whole note on do, ^1)
- Strong beats (downbeats) must be consonant, preferably imperfect consonances
- Dissonances occur on beats 2, 3, or 4 (not downbeats)
- Dissonances must be approached and left by step (except in double neighbor and nota cambiata)
- Motion from beat 4 to downbeat follows first-species rules

## Musical Context

**Pedagogical purpose**:
- Develops fluency in rapid stepwise motion
- Teaches treatment of neighbor-tone dissonances
- Introduces more complex metrical hierarchy (strong, medium, weak beats)
- Builds melodic embellishment skills
- Prepares for florid (fifth species) counterpoint

**Complexity level**:
- More elaborate than first and second species
- Requires balancing consonance and dissonance within each bar
- Demands greater melodic fluency and variety
- Four notes per bar create more opportunities for melodic interest

**Relationship to composition**:
- Teaches embellishment techniques applicable to real composition
- Neighbor tones and passing tones are fundamental melodic ornaments
- The 4:1 ratio approximates common note values in many styles
- Exercises smooth, flowing melodic writing

Third species bridges the gap between second species and the free mixture of rhythms in fifth species.

## Examples

### Basic

**Third-species line above cantus**:
- Cantus: C (whole note)
- Counterpoint: E–F–E–D (four quarter notes)
  Intervals: 3–4–3–2 (F is consonant passing tone, E returns as neighbor)

**Double neighbor figure**:
- Cantus: C (whole note)
- Counterpoint: E–F–D–E (four quarter notes)
  Intervals: 3–4–2–3 (beats 1 and 4 are the same; beats 2 and 3 are neighbors)

**Nota cambiata**:
- Cantus: C–D (two whole notes)
- Counterpoint: E–F–D–D (bar 1) – C (downbeat bar 2)
  Five-note figure outlining stepwise descent E→D across the bar line

### From Repertoire

Third species is pedagogical, but the techniques appear in:
- Bach: Rapid embellishment in keyboard works and chorales
- Mozart: Alberti bass and melodic ornamentation use neighbor and passing tones
- Chopin: Elaborate melodic figures with neighbor-tone embellishments
- All tonal music: Neighbor tones and passing tones are ubiquitous melodic devices

## Related Concepts

- **Prerequisite**: species-counterpoint, second-species, passing-tone, consonance, dissonance
- **Leads to**: fourth-species, fifth-species, neighbor-tone, double-neighbor, nota-cambiata

## Common Confusions

- Third species uses four quarter notes per bar, not three—the "third" refers to the exercise type, not the number of notes
- Dissonances can occur on beats 2, 3, or 4, but the treatment differs: neighbor tones return to the starting consonance; passing tones continue in the same direction
- The moderately strong beat (beat 3) is weaker than the downbeat but stronger than beats 2 and 4
- Not all dissonances require stepwise approach and departure—the double neighbor and nota cambiata are exceptions
- Third species is more about melodic fluency than harmonic interest—the focus is on smooth, singable lines
