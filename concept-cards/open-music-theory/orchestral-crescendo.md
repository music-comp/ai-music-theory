# Quick Definition
An orchestral crescendo uses three techniques beyond mere dynamic markings: adding instruments progressively (lightest to heaviest: strings-winds-brass-percussion), expanding the overall register (upward or outward), and diversifying note values (adding sustained notes, tremolo, or repeated-note patterns).

# Core Definition
Effective orchestral crescendos combine dynamic markings with progressive addition of instruments, register expansion, and rhythmic diversification. Instruments are typically added from lightest (strings) to heaviest (brass, percussion). Register expands upward or outward from the center. Note values diversify with longer sustained notes and shorter repeated or tremolo figures. Reserving a specific timbre (piccolo, triangle) for the climactic moment amplifies the arrival. The strings-to-brass progression also relates to practical playing time: strings play the most, percussion the least.

# Key Properties
1. Add instruments lightest to heaviest
2. Expand register upward or outward
3. Diversify note values (sustained + short/tremolo)
4. Reserve special timbres for climax
5. Strings play the most; percussion the least (pragmatic + musical)

# Context & Application
Grieg's "In the Hall of the Mountain King" demonstrates all three techniques: progressive instrument addition, upward register expansion, and rhythmic diversification.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Grieg, "In the Hall of the Mountain King"): Strings to winds to brass to percussion; register expands; note values diversify.
**Example 2** (Mahler 4/iii): Triangle enters at climax (reh. 12), cutting through the full texture.
**Example 3** (Debussy, Prelude a l'apres-midi d'un faune): Antique cymbal reserved until near the end for entirely new sonority.
