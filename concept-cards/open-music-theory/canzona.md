# Canzona

## Quick Definition

An instrumental composition (originally for keyboard or ensemble) featuring lively, dance-like character derived from French chansons, characterized by multiple contrasting sections with different subjects in imitation, often beginning with the distinctive "canzona rhythm" (long-short-short: dotted quarter, eighth, eighth), serving as an important precursor to the Baroque fugue through its imitative counterpoint and sectional structure with recurring subject material.

## Formal Definition

**Canzona** (Italian: "song"; plural *canzoni* or *canzone*; also *canzon*, *canzona da sonar*) is an instrumental genre that evolved from vocal chansons in the 16th century, developing into a multi-sectional imitative work that influenced the development of the fugue and sonata.

**Key characteristics**:
- **Instrumental origin**: "Canzona da sonar" (canzona to be played), distinguishing from vocal models
- **Multiple sections**: Contrasting sections with different subjects and characters
- **Imitative texture**: Each section begins with imitative entries of its subject
- **"Canzona rhythm"**: Characteristic dotted rhythm (long-short-short) often at opening
- **Lively character**: Dance-like energy and rhythmic vitality
- **Sectional contrast**: Varied meters, tempos, and textures across sections
- **Thematic relationships**: Later sections may reference earlier material

**Historical development**:
- **Early canzona** (c. 1580-1620): Keyboard transcriptions of French chansons, simple imitation
- **Mature canzona** (c. 1600-1650): Original instrumental compositions, multiple sections
- **Late canzona** (c. 1650-1700): Evolves toward fugue and sonata da chiesa
- **Influence**: Direct precursor to Baroque fugue and trio sonata

**Typical "canzona rhythm"**:
```
♩. ♪ ♪ or 𝅗𝅥 ♩ ♩ (long - short - short pattern)
```
Often at the beginning, establishes energetic character

## Canzona Structure

**Multi-sectional form**:
```
Section 1: Subject A, imitative entries, development
Section 2: Subject B (contrasting character), imitative entries
Section 3: Subject C (further contrast), imitative entries
Section 4: Subject A returns (optional), varied or transformed
Coda: Final entries, conclusive cadence
```

**Sectional contrasts**:
- **Meter changes**: Duple to triple, or vice versa
- **Tempo changes**: Allegro to Adagio to Allegro
- **Textural changes**: Imitative to homophonic to soloistic
- **Rhythmic character**: Dotted rhythms to smooth eighth notes to syncopation

**Thematic transformation**:
- Later sections may use augmentation or diminution of earlier subjects
- Subjects may be combined in later sections
- Final section often recalls opening material (proto-ABA or cyclic form)

## Canzona vs. Related Genres

**vs. Ricercar**:
- **Canzona**: Livelier, dance-like, sectional with contrasts
- **Ricercar**: More learned, slower, sustained imitative exploration
- Both use imitation, but different characters and structures

**vs. Fugue**:
- **Canzona**: Multiple subjects in different sections
- **Fugue**: Single subject developed throughout (typically)
- Canzona is precursor; fugue evolved from canzona tradition

**vs. French Chanson**:
- **Canzona**: Instrumental (derived from vocal chanson)
- **Chanson**: Vocal original with text
- Canzona originally transcription, later independent instrumental genre

**vs. Sonata**:
- **Canzona**: Multi-sectional, imitative, contrapuntal
- **Sonata da chiesa**: Multi-movement, evolved from canzona structure
- Canzona evolves into sonata form in late Baroque

## Musical Context

Canzoni serve specific purposes:
- **Instrumental development**: Establishes independent instrumental idiom
- **Formal evolution**: Bridge between Renaissance chanson and Baroque fugue
- **Contrapuntal practice**: Teaches and demonstrates imitative writing
- **Chamber music**: For instrumental consorts (brass, strings, mixed ensembles)
- **Keyboard repertoire**: Solo organ or harpsichord canzoni
- **Liturgical use**: Church music (especially organ canzoni for Mass)

The canzona represents a crucial step in the development of instrumental music.

## Examples

### Basic

**Characteristic "canzona rhythm" opening**:
```
Subject (C major):
♩. ♪ ♪ | ♩ ♩ | 𝅗𝅥
C   D E | F E | D

Long-short-short pattern (dotted quarter - eighth - eighth)
Energetic, dance-like character
Immediately recognizable canzona style
```

**Multi-sectional structure**:
```
Section 1 (measures 1-20): Subject A in duple meter (♩. ♪ ♪ rhythm), C major
  Imitative entries in all voices, development

Section 2 (measures 21-35): Subject B in triple meter (3/4), smoother rhythm, G major
  New imitative subject, contrasting character

Section 3 (measures 36-50): Subject C in duple meter, faster note values, C major
  Third contrasting section, increased activity

Section 4 (measures 51-60): Subject A returns, transformed, C major
  Recalls opening material, brings closure
```

**Sectional contrast (meter change)**:
```
Section 1: 4/4 meter, dotted rhythms
Section 2: 3/2 meter, smooth half notes and quarters
Section 3: 2/2 meter, faster eighth-note motion

Meter changes create sectional distinction
```

**Imitative entries** (typical section opening):
```
Voice 1 (soprano): Subject begins, measure 1
Voice 2 (alto): Subject imitates at fifth, measure 3
Voice 3 (tenor): Subject imitates at octave, measure 5
Voice 4 (bass): Subject imitates at fifth, measure 7

Each section begins with systematic imitative entries
Similar to fugal exposition
```

**Thematic recall**:
```
Section 1 (opening): Subject A (♩. ♪ ♪ C-D-E-F)
Section 2: Subject B (contrasting)
Section 3: Subject C (contrasting)
Section 4: Subject A returns in augmentation (𝅗𝅥. ♩ ♩ C-D-E-F)

Final section recalls opening, provides unity
```

### From Repertoire

**Giovanni Gabrieli, Canzon septimi toni a 8**: Venetian polychoral canzona for double brass choir, dramatic antiphonal effects, characteristic canzona rhythms, sectional structure.

**Girolamo Frescobaldi, Canzoni from *Fiori Musicali***: Organ canzoni, multiple contrasting sections, imitative texture, characteristic dotted rhythms, liturgical function.

**Samuel Scheidt, Canzon Bergamasca**: Keyboard canzona, sectional structure with meter changes, imitative writing, demonstrates North German organ tradition.

**Tarquinio Merula, Canzona La Strada**: Instrumental ensemble canzona, lively character, dance-like energy, multiple subjects in contrasting sections.

**J.S. Bach, early works**: Bach's early fugues show canzona influence (multi-sectional structure, contrasting subjects), transitional between canzona and mature fugue.

## Common Confusions

- Canzona = instrumental genre (not vocal, despite derivation from chanson)
- "Canzona da sonar" = canzona to be played (distinguishes from vocal canzone)
- Characteristic dotted rhythm (♩. ♪ ♪) typical but not required in every canzona
- Multi-sectional with contrasting subjects (not monothematic like typical fugue)
- Precursor to fugue (historical development, not identical genres)
- Lively, dance-like character (unlike serious ricercar)
- Evolved from French chanson transcriptions to original instrumental works
- Sectional contrasts typical (meter, tempo, texture changes)
- Both keyboard and ensemble versions exist
- Later sections may recall earlier material (thematic unity)
- Not same as canzona (Italian vocal song with poetry)—instrumental genre
- Polychoral canzoni (Gabrieli) exploit spatial effects in performance
- Influenced development of sonata da chiesa (church sonata)
- "Canzon" and "canzona" variant spellings (same genre)
- Baroque period gradually replaced by fugue and sonata forms
- Sectional form with imitation distinguishes from continuous fugue
