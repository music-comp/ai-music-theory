# AAB Lyric Form

## Quick Definition

The standard blues lyric structure where a line is stated, repeated (with possible variation), then followed by a contrasting line.

## Formal Definition

**AAB form** describes the typical lyric and melodic structure of a 12-bar blues:

**Structure**:
- **A (mm. 1-4)**: First statement of a lyric line
- **A (mm. 5-8)**: Repetition of the same lyric line (may have slight variation)
- **B (mm. 9-12)**: Contrasting lyric line that responds to or completes the A lines

**Melodic correspondence**:
The melody often follows the same AAB pattern, with the A phrases set to similar or identical melodies and the B phrase introducing contrasting melodic material.

**Function of repetition**:
The repeated A line:
- Emphasizes the text for listeners
- Creates expectation and momentum toward the B line
- Allows for variation in delivery on the repetition
- Historically may have allowed time for improvised verse creation

## Musical Context

The AAB form is deeply rooted in blues tradition and reflects the emotional arc of many blues texts: a statement of a situation (A), its reinforcement (A repeated), and a response, resolution, or consequence (B).

This structure creates a satisfying dramatic shape within each 12-bar chorus and has influenced lyric writing in rock, R&B, and other blues-derived genres.

## Examples

### Basic

From "Gulf Coast Blues" (Clarence Williams):
- A: "The man I love he has done left this town"
- A: "The man I love he has done left this town"
- B: "And if it keeps on raining, I will be Gulf Coast bound"

### From Repertoire

"Cross Road Blues" (Robert Johnson):
- A: "I went to the crossroad, fell down on my knees"
- A: "I went to the crossroad, fell down on my knees"
- B: "Asked the Lord above, have mercy now, save poor Bob if you please"

Most traditional blues lyrics follow this pattern, though variations exist (ABC, AAA, etc.).

## Common Confusions

- The repeated A line is NOT necessarily identical; subtle variations in text or delivery are common
- AAB refers to LYRICS/MELODY, not harmony (the harmony follows the 12-bar blues structure)
- Not all blues use AAB, but it is the most traditional and common structure
- The B line provides CONTRAST, not just continuation; it often has a punchline quality
- AAB within each verse can combine with larger verse structures across a song
