# Quick Definition
The prime ordering (P) is the designated original or reference form of a twelve-tone series, typically the first statement in the piece, from which all other orderings are calculated.

# Core Definition
The series is traditionally used in four different orderings: prime, retrograde, inversion, and retrograde-inversion. "Some statement of the series, usually the very first one in the piece, is designated the *prime*, and the rest are calculated in relation to it" (Straus, p. 312). The prime ordering is labeled P_n, where n indicates the starting pitch class. There are twelve different prime forms: P0, P1, P2, ..., P11, all sharing the same interval succession.

# Prerequisites
- **Twelve-tone series** -- the series whose ordering is being designated
- **Content and order** -- understanding that transposition changes order, not content

# Key Properties
1. Subscript n indicates the starting pitch class (P2 begins on pc 2 = D)
2. All twelve P-forms share the same interval succession
3. Notes are numbered 1--12 according to order position
4. The ordered pitch-class intervals define the prime ordering
5. Usually designated from the first clear statement of the series in the piece

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the first explicit presentation of the series (typically the opening melody)
- Label it P_n where n is the first pitch class
- Number each note by its order position (1--12)
- Calculate the ordered pitch-class intervals between successive notes
- These intervals define all P-forms

# Context & Application
The choice of which statement to designate as prime is somewhat conventional -- what matters is consistency. Positive integers 1--11 represent the ordered pitch-class intervals, though negative numbers are sometimes used for intervals 7 and above (7 = -5; 8 = -4; 9 = -3; 10 = -2; 11 = -1).

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 312, Ex. 6-1): Schoenberg, String Quartet No. 4, first movement -- the first violin melody presents P2: D-C#-A-Bb-F-Eb-E-C-Ab-G-F#-B. Ordered pitch-class intervals: 11, 8, 1, 7, 10, 1, 8, 8, 11, 11, 5.

**Example 2** (p. 319, Ex. 6-11): Webern, "Wie bin ich froh!" -- P7 begins on G; each note is numbered by its order position in the series.

- **Retrograde-inversion** -- RI is the retrograde of I
- **Series class** -- the family of 48 forms derived from P

# Common Errors
- Assuming P0 is always "the" prime form; the subscript merely indicates starting pitch class
- Confusing twelve-tone prime ordering (P_n) with set-theory prime form notation

# Common Confusions
- **Prime ordering vs. prime form (set theory)**: These are different concepts; prime ordering is an ordered series form, prime form is an unordered set-class representative
- **P subscript**: Different analysts may label the same series differently depending on which statement they call prime
