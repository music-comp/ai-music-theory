# Quick Definition
Invariant trichords are three-note collections that remain intact (though possibly reordered) when the series is transformed, often mapping onto each other or onto themselves under inversion.

# Formal Definition
The same principles that apply to invariant dyads extend to larger collections. Trichords can be held invariant through transformations in two ways:
1. Two equivalent trichords (related by transposition or inversion) map onto each other
2. An inversionally symmetrical trichord maps onto itself at some level of inversion

If two trichords are related by I_n, inverting the series by the same I_n will cause the two trichords to map onto each other. If a trichord is inversionally symmetrical, at some level of inversion it will map onto itself.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
To find invariant trichords:
1. Identify trichords in the series (especially segmental subsets)
2. Determine if two trichords are related by inversion (calculate index number)
3. For self-mapping: check if the trichord is inversionally symmetrical (maps onto itself at some I_n)
4. Index calculation: sum corresponding pitch classes; must be consistent for all pairs

Example (Berg, Violin Concerto):
- Two (048) augmented triads in the series
- They map onto each other at I_2
- A (024) trichord maps onto itself at I_2
- P_7 and I_7 are related by I_2 (since 7+7=14→2)

# Musical Context/Application
- Trichordal invariance creates motivic continuity across series forms
- Augmented triads (048) always map onto themselves or equivalent trichords
- Composers exploit trichordal invariance for associative paths
- Invariant trichords link melodic statements related by inversion
- Creates audible connections between seemingly different musical passages

# Examples
- Berg, Violin Concerto: Series contains two (048) augmented triads and one (024)
- Under I_2, the two augmented triads exchange positions; the (024) maps onto itself
- P_7 and I_7 share these trichords: the melodic lines are associated not only by inversion but by shared segments
- The (024) stays in the same position; the two (048)s change places

# Common Confusions
- Expecting trichords to remain in the same order position—they may swap
- Forgetting that inversionally symmetrical sets can map onto themselves
- Confusing set-class invariance (always preserved) with pitch-class invariance (requires specific transformations)
- Not calculating the correct index number for the transformation
