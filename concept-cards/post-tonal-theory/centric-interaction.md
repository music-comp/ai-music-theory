# Quick Definition
The competition or interaction between two or more centric tones within a passage, where different pitches or pitch classes vie for centricity without clear functional resolution.

# Formal Definition
Centric interaction describes passages where two or more pitch classes compete for centric status, held in tension without unequivocal resolution. Unlike traditional tonal modulation, which moves definitively from one key to another, centric interaction in post-tonal music may leave the competing centers unresolved. This creates structural tension and ambiguity as a compositional resource rather than a problem to be solved.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
Types of centric interaction:
1. Sequential competition: Different centers emphasized in succession
2. Simultaneous competition: Different voices/layers emphasize different centers
3. Shared pitch-class in contrasting triads/collections
4. Unresolved tension: Neither center achieves clear priority

Common patterns:
- Centers related by P5/P4 (traditional dominant-tonic relationship subverted)
- Centers related by m3/M3 (triadic transformation relationships)
- Centers related by tritone (maximum distance)

# Musical Context/Application
Centric interaction provides:
1. Structural tension without functional resolution
2. Ambiguity as an expressive resource
3. Alternative to tonal modulation
4. Layered organization where different elements suggest different centers

# Examples
Example 5-2: Stravinsky's Symphony in C - The final chord contains both C and E as potentially centric. Bn moves to C (suggesting C as tonic) while Bb reinstates Bn (negating resolution). E in the bass and C in the harmony remain in "tense, unresolved relationship."

Example 5-32: Stravinsky's Serenade in A - A Aeolian and F Ionian interact. The sonority on each downbeat has A in outer voices but F in inner voice. The final sonority combines A-minor and F-major triads.

Example 5-33: Bartók's "Harvest Song" - Instead of a single centric tone, the music projects "framing perfect fourths" in tension with each other.

# Common Confusions
- Centric interaction differs from tonal ambiguity (which implies eventual resolution)
- The competing centers may remain unresolved throughout
- Different analytical listeners may hear different centers as primary
- Competition can occur within a single harmony or between voices
