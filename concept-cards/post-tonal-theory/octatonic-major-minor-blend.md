# Quick Definition
The characteristic property of octatonic music where major and minor thirds coexist on the same root, reflecting the collection's containment of both the third and fourth scale degrees simultaneously.

# Core Definition
Because the octatonic collection contains both the major third and minor third above any of its constituent pitch classes that serve as roots, it inherently blends major and minor qualities. This produces a characteristic ambiguity where "E major and E minor seem to be joined within a single gesture" and "the clash between G# and Gn (the third and fourth degrees of the octatonic scale on E) and the seeming blend of major and minor are typical of octatonic music" (Straus, Model Analysis 5.2, p. 548).

# Prerequisites
- **Octatonic collection** -- the collection that produces this effect
- **Octatonic subsets** -- understanding what harmonies the collection contains

# Key Properties
1. The octatonic collection contains 4 major AND 4 minor triads
2. On any root within the collection, both major and minor thirds are available
3. The clash between the two thirds (e.g., G# and Gn over E) is characteristic
4. This enables quasi-tonal passages with built-in major/minor ambiguity
5. The blend cannot be resolved within the octatonic collection itself

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify an octatonic context
2. Look for simultaneous or alternating major and minor thirds on the same root
3. Note clashes between the third degree and its chromatic variant
4. The effect is structural (built into the collection) rather than incidental

# Context & Application
The major-minor blend is one of the most aurally distinctive features of octatonic music. It creates a harmonic quality that sounds simultaneously familiar (because triads are present) and strange (because major and minor coexist). This effect is especially characteristic of Bartok and Stravinsky.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Model Analysis 5.2, p. 548): Bartok, *Piano Sonata*, first theme -- E-major triads in the bass alternate with E-related material. Upper voice arpeggiates from G# (m. 1) through E (m. 7) to Gn (m. 14). When Gn arrives, G# is heard in the bass, creating characteristic major/minor clash within OCT1,2.

**Example 2** (Model Analysis 5.2, p. 558): Same piece, second theme -- Clash of A-major and A-minor triads, and of C-natural and C#. The incomplete triads form (014): [A, C, C#], the "tonic form of (014)."

# Common Errors
- Analyzing the major/minor clash as a "mistake" or non-structural element
- Trying to resolve the major/minor ambiguity rather than recognizing it as essential to the octatonic sound

# Common Confusions
- **Octatonic blend vs. tonal mode mixture**: In tonal music, major/minor mixture borrows from the parallel mode; in octatonic music, both are inherent in the same collection
