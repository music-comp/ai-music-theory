# Quick Definition
A triadic transformation relating major and minor triads that share the same third, with only one common tone and two voices each moving by semitone in the same direction.

# Formal Definition
The SLIDE transformation (also notated P') relates a major triad and a minor triad that share the same third. It is the obverse of P: while P retains the perfect fifth and flips around it, SLIDE retains the singleton (the third) and flips the perfect fifth around it. This contextual inversion around a single note causes two voices to move, but each moves by only one semitone, and they move in the same direction (parallel motion).

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
SLIDE transformation properties:
- Common tones: 1 (the third)
- Voice leading: 2 voices move by 1 semitone each, in the same direction
- Mode change: major <-> minor

Examples:
- SLIDE(C+) = C#- [E is common; C moves to C#, G moves to G#]
- SLIDE(F+) = F#- [A is common; F moves to F#, C moves to C#]

Voice-leading diagram for C+ to C#-:
```
C -> C# (up 1 semitone)
E -> E (common)
G -> G# (up 1 semitone)
```

The name "SLIDE" describes the parallel semitone motion of two voices.

# Musical Context/Application
SLIDE has large circulation in post-tonal music because:
1. It connects tonally distant triads smoothly
2. The shared third creates audible connection
3. The parallel semitone motion is distinctive
4. It combines well with P in chains

# Examples
Example 4-26 shows SLIDE relating C+ to C#-.

Example 4-29: John Adams's Harmonium uses SLIDE to connect Eb major and E minor, sharing G as their common third.

Example 4-33: Zwilich's Chamber Concerto alternates P and SLIDE, creating a chain of triad roots descending by semitone (Bb-A-Ab).

# Common Confusions
- SLIDE retains only the third, not the fifth (unlike P)
- The two moving voices move in the same direction, not contrary motion
- SLIDE connects triads whose roots are a semitone apart but with opposite modes
- P' and SLIDE are the same transformation
