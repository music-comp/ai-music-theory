# Quick Definition
A progression of motives or sets where each element is related by retrograde-inversion to the motives before and after it, created through alternating contextual inversions.

# Formal Definition
An RI-chain is a chain of sets (chords or melodic segments) in which each set is related to its neighbors by retrograde-inversion. These chains typically arise from the alternation of two different contextual inversions and form closed cycles that can be represented as contextual-inversion spaces. The term refers to the retrograde-inversion relationship between adjacent elements in the chain.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
To construct an RI-chain:
1. Define two contextual inversions (e.g., J and K) for a given set class
2. Apply them alternately: Set1 -J-> Set2 -K-> Set3 -J-> Set4...
3. The chain continues until returning to the starting set

For sc(014) with J (flip around i1) and K (flip around i4):
- A complete JK cycle produces 12 forms of (014)
- Each adjacent pair is related by retrograde-inversion

# Musical Context/Application
RI-chains provide:
1. A systematic way to generate extended progressions
2. Coherence through the consistent relationship between adjacent elements
3. A spatial framework for tracing melodic or harmonic motion
4. Connection to twelve-tone concepts of retrograde and inversion

# Examples
Example 4-22: Webern's Concerto for Nine Instruments, op. 24, second movement. The melody uses six forms of (014) as links in an RI-chain, created by alternating J and K inversions. The chain starts at the top of a circular space and works its way around toward (but not quite back to) the starting point.

# Common Confusions
- RI-chains involve contextual RI relationships, not generic RI operations
- The "retrograde-inversion" relationship here is between adjacent melodic segments, not twelve-tone row forms
- Not all contextual inversion chains are RI-chains; the term specifically refers to chains where adjacent elements have an RI relationship
