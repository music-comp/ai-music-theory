# Quick Definition
An invariant is any musical quality or relationship preserved when a series is transformed, providing audible continuity and association as the music moves through different series forms.

# Core Definition
"When we listen to twelve-tone music, we don't need to be able to identify the forms of the series. Instead, we need to hear the musical consequences of the series, the musical results of its ongoing transformations. Any musical quality or relationship preserved when the series is transformed is called an *invariant*. As we hear our way through a piece, our ear is often led via a chain of invariants" (Straus, p. 327).

Key principle for invariance under inversion: "inversion always involves a double mapping: if I_n maps x onto y, then it also will map y onto x" (p. 327). This allows larger collections to be preserved: if two subsets in a series are related by I_n, inverting the whole series by I_n causes them to map onto each other.

# Prerequisites
- **Series class** -- invariants exist among the forms of a series class
- **Segmental subsets** -- the objects often preserved as invariants
- **Inversion** -- the primary operation for creating invariant relationships

# Key Properties
1. Interval succession is invariant under transposition
2. Set-class membership of subsets is invariant under all transformations
3. Specific pitch-class collections can be held invariant through careful transformation choice
4. Inversionally symmetrical sets can map onto themselves
5. Invariants create "chains of association" through the music

# Construction / Recognition
- To preserve a subset under inversion: find an equivalent subset elsewhere in the series
- Calculate the index number that maps the two subsets onto each other
- Apply that inversion to the whole series: both subsets remain intact
- For self-mapping: use inversionally symmetrical subsets at the appropriate I_n level

# Context & Application
"What is important here, as in all twelve-tone music, is not the mere presence of the series, but its audible musical content and the chain of associations created by its transformations" (p. 334). Invariants are the primary means of creating continuity in twelve-tone music. They are more important for the listener than identifying series forms.

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 328--330): Schoenberg, String Quartet No. 4, third movement -- the dyads (C, B) and (Gb, F) are invariant between P0 and I5, creating an "associative path" through the music.

**Example 2** (pp. 330--331): Berg, Violin Concerto -- two augmented triads (048) and one (024) are held invariant between P7 and I7.

## Enables
- **Invariant dyads** -- the simplest invariant collections

# Common Errors
- Thinking invariants are merely theoretical (they are meant to be audible)
- Focusing on series form identification rather than hearing invariant chains
- Overlooking that composers design series specifically for invariant properties

# Common Confusions
- **Set-class invariance vs. pitch-class invariance**: Set-class membership is always preserved; specific pitch classes are preserved only under specific transformations
- **Invariance vs. identity**: An invariant collection may be reordered; it keeps its pitch-class content, not necessarily its order
