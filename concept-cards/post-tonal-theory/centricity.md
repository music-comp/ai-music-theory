# Quick Definition
The sense that a particular note or pitch class functions as central or focal in post-tonal music, without the functional harmonic relationships that define traditional tonality.

# Formal Definition
Centricity describes the ways post-tonal music establishes notes as central or focal points. Unlike traditional tonality, where the tonic is reinforced through functional harmonic progressions (pre-dominant to dominant to tonic), centricity in post-tonal music relies on other means: duration, dynamics, frequency of occurrence, registral prominence, and inversional symmetry. There is a spectrum of centric effects, from music where centricity plays no significant role through music with vague or contested centricity to music vividly organized around one or more centric tones.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
Centricity can be established through:
1. Direct emphasis: longer, louder, more frequent, higher or lower notes
2. Inversional symmetry: axis of symmetry functioning as pitch(-class) center
3. Registral extremes: notes at the top or bottom of textures
4. Repetition and sustained tones
5. Convergence: melodic lines arriving at the centric tone

Centricity differs from tonality in:
- No requirement for functional harmony
- No requirement for triadic structures
- Possible multiple or competing centric tones
- Center may be implied rather than sounded

# Musical Context/Application
The concept of centricity allows analysts to discuss pitch organization in music that is neither atonal (no center) nor traditionally tonal (functional harmony). Many composers write music that is "in" or "on" a particular note without being "in the key of" that note.

# Examples
Example 5-1: Stravinsky's Serenade in A establishes A through inversional convergence, not V-I progressions. The bass leaps down by P4 to A while the upper voice leaps up by P4 to A.

Example 5-2: Stravinsky's Symphony in C ends with non-triadic chords holding E and C in tension; C is present but not unequivocally established as tonic.

Example 5-3: Stravinsky's Concerto in D presents D-major, D-minor, and B-minor triads in static confrontation without clear tonal resolution.

Schoenberg's op. 11, no. 1 has been analyzed in E, F#, and G by different scholars - tonality operates like a "ghost" that cannot be pinned down.

# Common Confusions
- Centricity and tonality are related but different; centricity does not require functional harmony
- A piece can be "in A" without being "in A major" or "in A minor"
- Multiple centric tones can compete within a single passage
- The absence of a clear center is also a valid analytical finding
