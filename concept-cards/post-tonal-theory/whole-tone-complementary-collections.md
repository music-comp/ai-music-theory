# Quick Definition
The two whole-tone collections (WT0 and WT1) are pitch-class complements that together exhaust all twelve pitch classes, and composers frequently exploit the contrast between them within a single composition.

# Core Definition
The two whole-tone collections, WT0 (even pitch classes: 0,2,4,6,8,10) and WT1 (odd pitch classes: 1,3,5,7,9,11), are complementary: they share no pitch classes and together account for all twelve. This complementary relationship creates a natural source of contrast. Composers use the two collections in opposition -- melody in one and accompaniment in the other, or successive sections in different collections -- to create harmonic variety within the severely restricted whole-tone intervallic world (Straus, Ch. 5, pp. 271-273).

# Prerequisites
- **Whole-tone collection** -- the two collections being contrasted
- **WT nomenclature** -- identifying which collection is in use

# Key Properties
1. WT0 and WT1 are pitch-class complements (no shared notes)
2. Together they exhaust all 12 pitch classes
3. They are related by T1 (or any odd transposition)
4. The contrast between them is a primary source of variety in whole-tone music
5. Switching from one to the other changes every note by one semitone

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify whether a passage uses even or odd pitch classes
2. Track when the collection switches from WT0 to WT1 or vice versa
3. Note whether the two collections appear simultaneously (melody vs. accompaniment) or successively (section by section)

# Context & Application
The contrast between WT0 and WT1 compensates for the limited internal variety of each whole-tone collection. Since each collection has only one scalar ordering and very few subset types, the shift between the two complementary collections provides the primary source of harmonic change in extended whole-tone passages.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 271): Bartok, *String Quartet No. 1*, second movement -- A melody from one whole-tone collection is paired with an accompaniment from the other, creating maximum pitch-class contrast between the two layers.

**Example 2** (p. 272): Crumb, *Ancient Voices of Children* -- A vocalise from one whole-tone collection gives way to a final distant echo from the other, marking a formal and expressive shift.
