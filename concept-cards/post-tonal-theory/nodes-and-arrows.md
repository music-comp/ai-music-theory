# Quick Definition
Nodes and arrows are the basic components of transformational networks, where nodes contain musical elements (notes or sets) and arrows show the operations (Tn or In) connecting them.

# Core Definition
In transformational analysis, relationships are represented using networks of nodes and arrows. Nodes (circles) contain musical elements such as individual pitch classes or pitch-class sets. Arrows show the operations (Tn or In) that connect the nodes, indicating how the music moves from one element to another. When two networks share the same arrow structure, they are called isographic. Double-headed arrows are used for inversions (since In is its own inverse).

# Prerequisites
- **Transposition (Tn)** -- one of the operations labeling arrows
- **Inversion (In)** -- the other operation labeling arrows
- **Mapping** -- arrows represent mappings between nodes

# Key Properties
1. **Nodes**: circles or boxes containing musical content (pitch classes, sets, or set names)
2. **Arrows**: directed lines labeled with operations (T0, T3, I5, etc.)
3. Single-headed arrows for transposition (Tn and T(12-n) are different)
4. Double-headed arrows for inversion (In is its own inverse)
5. Networks can operate at multiple structural levels simultaneously

# Construction / Recognition
To build a transformational network:
1. Identify the musical elements to serve as nodes
2. Determine the Tn or In operations relating them
3. Draw nodes with their contents
4. Connect with labeled arrows showing the operations
5. Compare with other networks for isography

# Context & Application
Networks provide a visual representation of how the music moves between sets or notes. They show not just what sets are present but how the music navigates among them. Comparing networks reveals structural similarities between passages or pieces, even when pitch content differs. "We will make frequent use of networks of this kind to represent musical motion in post-tonal music" (Straus, Ch. 2).

# Examples
**Example 2-12** (p. 68, Stravinsky, *Agon*): Two isographic networks:
- (a) Note-level: Bb --T3--> Db
- (b) Set-level: [G, Ab, Bb, B] --T3--> [Bb, B, Db, D]

The same T3 operation moves the music forward at both the note level and the set level, demonstrating multi-level coherence.

**Example 2-31c** (p. 80): A network connecting four sets by I1, I7, and T6. This network structure applies to both Wuorinen (*Twelve Short Pieces*, no. 3) and Stockhausen (*Klavierstuck III*), making those passages isographic.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Mapping** -- arrows represent the mappings between elements
- **Transposition / Inversion** -- the operations labeling the arrows
## Enables
- **Isography** -- comparison of network structures
## Related
- **Composing-out** -- networks often reveal multi-level motivic projection

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming arrows indicate chronological order. **Correction**: Arrows primarily show transformational connections, though they often align with temporal order.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Nodes vs. arrows as the focus. **Clarification**: "Sometimes we focus on the contents of the nodes, and sometimes on the nature and direction of the arrows. Both are perfectly reasonable things to do" (Straus, Ch. 2).
