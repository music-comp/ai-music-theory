# Quick Definition
An arrangement of an inversionally symmetrical set in register such that the pitch intervals form a palindrome from bottom to top; the symmetry is realized in concrete pitch space, not just abstract pitch-class space.

# Core Definition
Pitch symmetry occurs when an inversionally symmetrical pitch-class set is arranged in register so that the pitch intervals (measured in semitones) from bottom to top are identical to those from top to bottom. This makes the symmetry audible and visually apparent. Any inversionally symmetrical set can be arranged pitch-symmetrically, but composers often choose not to, leaving the symmetry to exist only in pitch-class space. The axis of pitch symmetry is a specific pitch (or the midpoint between two pitches), not a pitch class (Straus, pp. 124-127).

# Prerequisites
- **Inversional symmetry** -- the abstract property being realized in register
- **Pitch space** -- the concrete registral domain where pitch symmetry operates

# Key Properties
1. Pitch intervals form a palindrome from bottom to top
2. There is a central pitch (odd cardinality) or central interval (even cardinality) as the axis
3. Any inversionally symmetrical pc set can be arranged this way
4. Pitch symmetry is audible and visually apparent on the staff
5. It is a stronger property than pitch-class symmetry

# Construction / Recognition
To create a pitch-symmetrical arrangement:
1. Start with an inversionally symmetrical pc set
2. Choose a central pitch as the axis
3. Place each note so that its distance above the axis equals the distance of its inversional partner below
4. Verify: pitch intervals from bottom to top = pitch intervals from top to bottom

# Context & Application
While inversional symmetry is always structurally present, pitch symmetry makes it perceptually obvious. Composers use pitch symmetry for dramatic effect, to establish a central tone, or to create audible balance. The choice between pitch-symmetrical and pitch-class-symmetrical arrangements is a significant compositional decision.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 124, Ex. 3-15b): Three hypothetical pitch-symmetrical arrangements of sc(01348), with E as the center of symmetry in each case. The pitch intervals form palindromes.

**Example 2** (p. 126, Ex. 3-17): Varese, Hyperprism -- [C, C#, D] arranged around C#:
- D enters 23 semitones below middle C#
- C enters 23 semitones above middle C#
- Grace notes reinforce: F (4 above C#) balanced by A (4 below C#)
- C# is literally the central tone in a vast pitch space

**Example 3** (p. 125, Ex. 3-16): Boulez, Le Marteau -- [C#, D, E, F] at the melody's end is pitch-symmetrical (intervals 1-2-1), while [A, Bb, C, C#] at the beginning is not.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Inversional symmetry** -- the abstract property being realized

## Enables
- **Pitch centricity** -- pitch symmetry can establish the axis pitch as a center of gravity

## Contrasts With
- **Pitch-class symmetry** -- symmetry in abstract pc space without registral realization

# Common Errors
- Confusing pitch symmetry with pitch-class symmetry (they are different levels of realization)
- Thinking all inversionally symmetrical sets must be arranged pitch-symmetrically

# Common Confusions
- The axis in pitch space is a single pitch; the axis in pitch-class space is a pair of pitch classes a tritone apart
- Pitch symmetry is a compositional choice about registral arrangement, not an inherent property of the set class
