# Quick Definition
The continuum of pitch-center effects in post-tonal music, ranging from music where centricity plays no significant role, through music with vague or contested centricity, to music vividly organized around one or more centric tones.

# Core Definition
Post-tonal music exhibits a **spectrum of centric effects** rather than a binary tonal/atonal distinction. At one extreme, represented by much twelve-tone music, there is little or no sense of centricity (though pitch classes are never treated identically). At the other extreme, many post-tonal pieces are deeply preoccupied with questions of centricity. Between these poles lies a vast range of music where centricity is vague, contested, multiple, or shifting (Straus, Ch. 5, p. 248).

# Prerequisites
- **Centricity** -- the fundamental concept of pitch-class focal points
- **Tonality vs. centricity** -- understanding why these are distinct concepts

# Key Properties
1. Centricity exists on a continuum, not as a binary property
2. Multiple centric tones can compete within a single passage
3. A center may be implied without being sounded (silent center)
4. The absence of a clear center is itself a valid analytical finding
5. Tonality operates as a "ghost" in some post-tonal music -- present but impossible to pin down

# Construction / Recognition
Means of establishing centricity without functional harmony:
1. Duration: longer notes attract attention as centers
2. Dynamics: louder notes are perceived as more important
3. Frequency of occurrence: repeated notes gain emphasis
4. Registral prominence: extreme high or low notes
5. Metric placement: notes on strong beats
6. Convergence: melodic lines arriving at the centric tone
7. Inversional symmetry: axis notes function as centers
8. Framing: notes at beginnings and endings of phrases

# Context & Application
The spectrum concept allows analysts to discuss pitch organization in music that is neither fully atonal nor traditionally tonal. Many composers write music that is "in" or "on" a particular note without being "in the key of" that note. Stravinsky's titled works (*Serenade in A*, *Symphony in C*, *Concerto in D*) exemplify this: they provide contexts for and resistance to the named note as centric tone without traditional tonal confirmation.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 245): Stravinsky, *Serenade in A* -- A is established through inversional convergence (bass and soprano converge symmetrically on A), not V-I progressions. The piece is "in A" but not "in A major" or "in A minor."

**Example 2** (p. 246): Stravinsky, *Symphony in C* -- The final chord holds E and C in tense, unresolved relationship. Bn moves to C suggesting resolution, but Bb simultaneously reinstates Bn, negating the effect.

**Example 3** (p. 247): Stravinsky, *Concerto in D* -- D-major, D-minor, and B-minor triads confront each other statically. D is present "more by implication than by vigorous contextual reinforcement."

**Example 4** (p. 248): Schoenberg, op. 11, no. 1 -- Tonality operates "like a ghost." Three published analyses identify three different keys (E, F#, G). All are right because no key shapes the structure reliably.

# Common Errors
- Forcing a single tonal analysis on music with contested centricity
- Assuming the absence of functional harmony means absence of centricity
- Treating centricity as binary (tonal or atonal) rather than as a spectrum

# Common Confusions
- **Centric vs. tonal**: A piece can be strongly centric without being traditionally tonal
- **Atonal vs. non-centric**: "Atonal" music may still have centric effects
