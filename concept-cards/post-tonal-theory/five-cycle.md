# Quick Definition

The interval cycle produced by moving repeatedly by perfect fourth or fifth (interval 5 or 7), passing through all twelve pitch classes before returning to the starting point; also known as the circle of fourths/fifths.

# Formal Definition

The **5-cycle (C5)** is the interval cycle generated by repeated transposition by interval class 5 (the perfect fourth) or interval class 7 (the perfect fifth). Because GCD(5, 12) = 1, there is only one distinct 5-cycle, which passes through all twelve pitch classes before returning to its origin. This cycle is the familiar "circle of fourths" or "circle of fifths" from tonal theory.

Moving clockwise around the cycle (by i5) = motion by perfect fourth ascending
Moving counterclockwise (by i7) = motion by perfect fifth ascending (or fourth descending)

Contiguous segments of C5 yield important diatonic and pentatonic collections.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition

**Properties of C5:**
- Interval: 5 semitones (perfect fourth) or 7 semitones (perfect fifth)
- Number of distinct cycles: GCD(5, 12) = 1
- Pitch classes per cycle: 12/1 = 12
- The single C5 contains all pitch classes

**Sequence (ascending by fourths from C):**
C - F - Bb - Eb - Ab - Db - Gb - B - E - A - D - G - (C)

**Segment collections:**
- 5 contiguous notes of C5 = pentatonic collection, sc(02479)
- 7 contiguous notes of C5 = diatonic collection, sc(013568T)

**Relationship to C2:**
Every other note in C5 produces a C2:
- C5: C-F-Bb-Eb-Ab-Db-Gb-B-E-A-D-G
- Every other: C-Bb-Ab-Gb-E-D = C2_0 (whole-tone)

The 5-cycle can be understood as two interlocking 2-cycles five semitones apart.

# Musical Context/Application

The 5-cycle provides:
- **Circle of fifths**: The foundational structure of tonal harmony
- **Diatonic collections**: Any seven adjacent notes form a diatonic scale
- **Pentatonic collections**: Any five adjacent notes form a pentatonic scale
- **Quartal/quintal harmony**: Stacked fourths or fifths

The C5 is central to:
- Tonal key relationships (closely related keys are adjacent on C5)
- Post-tonal quartal harmony
- Pentatonic and diatonic compositional practice
- Stravinsky's and Bartok's diatonic/folk-influenced works

# Examples

**Ives, "The Cage"** (Example 4-8):
- Accompanying chords are five-note segments of C5
- Chords arranged registrally to reveal their cyclic (stacked-fourth) origins
- Creates quartal sonority typical of post-tonal practice

**Bartok, String Quartet No. 3** (Example 4-9):
- Final chord: four-note segment of C5 (C#-G#-D#-A#)
- Quartal spacing emphasizes the cyclic origin
- Bartok considered the quartet "in C#" - both first and last chords relate to C#

**Diatonic and pentatonic derivation:**
- Diatonic C major = C5 segment: F-C-G-D-A-E-B (7 notes)
- Pentatonic on C = C5 segment: F-C-G-D-A (5 notes)
- Both are maximally even at their cardinalities

**Affinity with C2:**
In Ives's "The Cage," the C5 chord segments relate to the C2 melody:
- Every other note of a C5 segment produces a C2 segment
- This creates harmonic-melodic coherence between the cycles

# Common Confusions

- **C5 vs. C7**: Both intervals (perfect fourth and fifth) generate the same cycle traversed in opposite directions; there is no separate "C7"
- **One cycle, not multiple**: Unlike C2, C3, C4, and C6, the 5-cycle passes through all 12 pitch classes
- **Segments vs. complete cycle**: Partial traversals (diatonic, pentatonic) are more common than complete cycles
- **Fourths vs. fifths**: C5 ascending = fourths; C5 descending = fifths; they are inversionally equivalent
