# Quick Definition
The systematic use of inversional symmetry to establish pitch centers in post-tonal music, where the axis of symmetry functions as the centric tone, providing a structural (rather than functional) basis for centricity.

# Core Definition
**Inversional symmetry** is the most systematic means of establishing centricity in post-tonal music. An inversionally symmetrical set has an axis of symmetry -- a midpoint around which all notes balance. This axis may function as a pitch or pitch-class center. The technique replaces functional harmony's dominant-tonic relationship with structural balance as the basis for centricity: notes are drawn toward the axis not by harmonic function but by their symmetrical relationships with other notes (Straus, Ch. 5, pp. 248-260).

# Prerequisites
- **Inversional axis** -- the point around which notes balance
- **Centricity** -- the sense of a focal pitch
- **Inversional symmetry in pitch space** -- specific registral balance

# Key Properties
1. Inversional balance can establish centricity without functional harmony
2. In pitch space: axis is a single pitch
3. In pitch-class space: axis is two pitch classes (poles) a tritone apart
4. Both poles of a pc axis have potential centric function
5. The axis may be sounded or silent (implied by symmetry)
6. Notes "seek" their inversional partners, creating directional motion toward balance
7. Cadential force arises when all notes find their partners (contracting wedge)

# Construction / Recognition
**Finding the axis in pitch space:**
1. For any balanced pair, axis = (upper pitch + lower pitch) / 2
2. Verify that multiple pairs share the same axis

**Finding the axis in pitch-class space:**
1. Sum the two pitch-class integers of any balanced pair
2. The sum n determines the axis: poles at n/2 and n/2 + 6
3. If n is even, the axis passes through two pitch classes
4. If n is odd, the axis passes between two pairs of pitch classes

**The twelve axes** (identified by sum n):
- Sum 0: C-F#; Sum 1: C/Db-F#/G; Sum 2: Db-G; ... Sum 11: F/F#-B/C

# Context & Application
This technique is central to the music of Bartok, who frequently organizes passages around inversional axes that function as centric tones. It also appears in Gubaidulina, Ligeti, Finney, and many other composers. The twelve axes parallel the twelve keys and enable "modulation" from axis to axis, shaping large-scale form.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 249): Gubaidulina, *String Trio* -- B4 established as central by all three instruments. Every new note is balanced around B4: A# (1 below) by C (1 above); A3 by C#6; G#3 by D6.

**Example 2** (p. 253): Bartok, *String Quartet No. 5* -- Symmetrical wedge around axis C/Db-F#/G in pitch-class space. Octave displacements make the symmetry clearer in pc space than pitch space.

**Example 3** (p. 254): Bartok -- Axis A-Eb, with A as a silent center (every pc except A is sounded). Later at T6, Eb becomes emphasized while the pc axis A-Eb remains constant.

**Example 4** (p. 255): Finney, *Fantasy for Solo Violin* -- Axis D-Ab maintained through the passage. When the central pitch shifts from D4 to Ab4, the same balanced pairs (Db-D#, C-E, B-F, etc.) continue.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Inversional axis** -- the structural mechanism
- **Centricity** -- the effect achieved

## Enables
- **Axis modulation** -- moving from one axis to another
- **Silent center** -- centricity of unsounded axis tones
- **Contracting wedge** -- cadential effect through convergence on axis

## Contrasts With
- **Functional tonality** -- establishes centricity through harmonic function rather than symmetry

# Common Errors
- Expecting strict numerical symmetry (approximate symmetry is very common)
- Analyzing only pitch space when pitch-class space is more revealing
- Confusing the two poles of a pc axis (both can be centric)

# Common Confusions
- **Pitch space vs. pitch-class space**: In pitch space, the axis is one pitch; in pc space, it is two points a tritone apart
- **Sounded vs. structural**: The axis note need not be sounded to function as center
