# Quick Definition
A voice-leading pattern where notes converge symmetrically inward toward a central axis pitch, creating strong cadential force as the voices arrive at the center.

# Core Definition
A **contracting wedge** occurs when voices that begin at various distances from the axis converge symmetrically toward the central pitch, arriving at or very near the axis. This convergent motion creates strong **cadential force**, as the axis pitch becomes the goal of the voice-leading motion. The contracting wedge is the inversion of the expanding wedge and establishes centricity through goal-directed convergence (Straus, Ch. 5, p. 251).

# Prerequisites
- **Inversional axis** -- the point toward which voices converge
- **Inversional symmetry in pitch space** -- the balanced convergent motion

# Key Properties
1. Voices begin at various distances from the axis
2. Motion is symmetrically inward toward the axis
3. Creates strong cadential arrival on the axis pitch
4. Can replace V-I cadential function in post-tonal music
5. The converging voices "find" the center through balanced motion

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify voices at various distances from a central pitch
2. Track whether they move symmetrically inward
3. Verify that the goal of convergence is the axis pitch
4. Look for cadential effect at the point of convergence

# Context & Application
Contracting wedges provide cadential force in post-tonal music without functional harmony. The arrival at the axis pitch is heard as a resolution because of the directed, balanced motion from periphery to center. This technique is particularly effective at phrase endings or formal boundaries.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 251): A passage where lines begin on E in four different octaves and converge on the cadential Bb. The strong cadential effect comes from the symmetrical inward motion.

**Example 2** (p. 245): Stravinsky, *Serenade in A* -- The bass leaps down by P4 to A while the upper voice leaps up by P4 to A, creating cadential convergence on the centric tone.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Inversional axis** -- the convergence target
- **Expanding wedge** -- inverse operation

## Enables
- **Cadential centricity** -- provides closure without functional harmony

## Contrasts With
- **Expanding wedge** -- diverges from axis rather than converging to it

# Common Errors
- Assuming convergence always involves strict semitone-by-semitone motion (larger intervals are common)

# Common Confusions
- **Contracting wedge vs. V-I cadence**: Both create a sense of arrival, but contracting wedges use inversional balance rather than harmonic function
