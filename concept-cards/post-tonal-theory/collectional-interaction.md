# Quick Definition
The productive relationship between different referential collections in post-tonal music, including shifts from one collection to another and the interpenetration of collections through shared subsets.

# Formal Definition
Collectional interaction describes how the four main referential collections (diatonic, octatonic, whole-tone, and hexatonic) occur in productive relationship with each other in post-tonal music. Music may shift from one collection to another to create large-scale form, and passages can be understood in terms of the interpenetration of one collection by another. Diatonic and octatonic collections make a particularly effective pair because, despite structural differences, they share many subsets including triads, scale segments, and seventh chords.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
Types of collectional interaction:

1. Sequential: Music shifts from one collection to another
   - Same collection type, different transposition (OCT0,1 to OCT1,2)
   - Different collection type (DIA to OCT)

2. Simultaneous: Different collections overlap
   - Shared subsets link contrasting collections
   - One collection "colors" another

Common shared subsets between DIA and OCT:
- Triads (major and minor)
- (0235) "Dorian tetrachord"
- Seventh chords (minor, half-diminished, dominant)

Pivot subsets: Sets that belong to both collections create points of intersection.

# Musical Context/Application
Collectional interaction creates:
1. Large-scale harmonic structure through collection changes
2. Harmonic color through collection mixture
3. Formal articulation at collection boundaries
4. Connections between contrasting harmonic areas

# Examples
Example 5-30: Stravinsky's Symphony of Psalms - The "Psalms chord" (E minor) belongs to both OCT1,2 on E and E Phrygian (diatonic). The chord links octatonic and diatonic sections; the centric tone (E) remains constant while the collection shifts.

Example 5-31: Same piece - The Psalms chord returns, now followed by diatonic music (E Phrygian) rather than octatonic.

Example 5-32: Stravinsky's Serenade in A - Combines A Aeolian and F Ionian (diatonic collections sharing 6 common tones). F and A compete as centers.

Example 5-33: Bartók's "Harvest Song" - Three phrases use OCT1,2, OCT2,3, and DIA6b, articulated by different tetrachord relationships (T6 for octatonic, T5 for diatonic).

# Common Confusions
- Collections can share subsets even when structurally different
- Interaction can be successive (one then another) or simultaneous (both at once)
- Shared subsets act as pivots between collections
- Collection change need not coincide with centric change (or vice versa)
