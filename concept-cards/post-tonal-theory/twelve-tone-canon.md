# Quick Definition
A twelve-tone canon is a contrapuntal texture where one voice presents a series form as the leader and another voice follows with a related series form (often inversionally related), creating systematic intervallic relationships.

# Formal Definition
Twelve-tone composers, particularly Webern, were fond of canons—contrapuntal textures where a leading voice is imitated by a following voice. In twelve-tone canons, the following voice typically presents a transformation of the series (often an I-form when the leader presents a P-form). The interval of imitation and the choice of related series forms create systematic pitch-class relationships. Inversional canons are especially significant because the index number determines which pitch-class dyads will recur between the voices.

# Musical Context/Application
- Canons create structural rigor while allowing expressive freedom
- Webern associated canons with Baroque contrapuntal tradition
- Inversional canons create fixed vertical dyads (determined by index number)
- The interval of imitation affects rhythmic and pitch relationships
- Canons can span multiple sections of a movement

# Examples
- Webern, String Quartet, op. 28, Variation 4:
  - Two-part canon with P10 as leading voice, P7 as following voice
  - Interval of imitation: three beats later
  - The series is shared among instruments, moving after each tetrachord
  - Leading voice on beats 2 and 4 (weak); following voice on beats 1 and 3 (strong)
  - The canon continues through the entire variation
- Webern, Quartet, op. 22: Inversional canon with P1 paired with I11

# Common Confusions
- Expecting twelve-tone canons to sound like tonal canons—the relationship is structural
- Assuming canons must use the same series form—inversionally related forms are common
- Not recognizing that the series is often divided among instruments
- Overlooking how the index number determines the canon's vertical content
