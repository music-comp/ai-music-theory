# Quick Definition
A twelve-tone canon is a contrapuntal texture where one voice leads with a series form and another follows with a related form, creating systematic pitch-class relationships determined by the interval of imitation and the index number.

# Core Definition
Twelve-tone canons involve a leading voice presenting one series form while a following voice presents a related form (often inversionally related). "Webern was fond of canons -- they are a constant feature of his music" (Straus, p. 359). The interval of imitation and the choice of series forms create systematic relationships. In the inversional case, the index number determines which pitch-class dyads recur between voices.

# Prerequisites
- **Series class** -- the forms available for canonic treatment
- **Invariant dyads between series forms** -- determined by the index number

# Key Properties
1. Leading voice and following voice present different (or same) series forms
2. Index number determines vertical dyad content in inversional canons
3. The interval of imitation affects which dyads are shared
4. Series can be divided among instruments
5. Rhythmic differentiation helps distinguish the voices

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the leading and following voices
- Determine the series forms used
- Calculate the index number
- Note the interval of imitation (temporal distance between voices)

# Context & Application
Webern associated canons with Baroque contrapuntal tradition. Serial canons create structural rigor while allowing expressive freedom. The canon in Webern's op. 28 "continues until the end of the movement, cutting across the sectional divisions" (p. 359).

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 360--363, Ex. A6-2): Webern, String Quartet, op. 28, Variation 4 -- two-part canon with P10 as leader and P7 as follower (entering three beats later). Because the interval of imitation is odd, the two voices contain different ic1 dyads. Leader moves on beats 2 and 4 (weak); follower on beats 1 and 3 (strong), making the canon easier to hear. Series shared among instruments, moving to a new instrument after each tetrachord.

# Common Errors
- Expecting twelve-tone canons to sound like tonal canons (the relationship is structural)
- Not recognizing that the series may be divided among instruments

# Common Confusions
- **Canon at inversion vs. canon at transposition**: Both are possible; inversional canons create different dyad content than transpositional ones
