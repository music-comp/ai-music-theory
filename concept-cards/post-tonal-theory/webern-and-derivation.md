# Quick Definition
Webern's twelve-tone music is characterized by derived series and extreme motivic concentration, where a single trichord or tetrachord set class shapes all structural levels -- from note-to-note intervals to large-scale series relationships.

# Core Definition
"Webern's music is highly concentrated motivically. It tends to make intensive use of just a few intervals or sets" (Straus, p. 334). By employing derived series, Webern "guaranteed a high degree of motivic concentration." The approach creates "three levels of structure unfolding at the same time: the motions from note to note, from trichord to trichord, and from series to series" (p. 336). The generator shapes not just pitch content but "instrumentation, registers, and articulations" (p. 338).

# Prerequisites
- **Derived series** -- Webern's primary constructive technique
- **Generator set class** -- the motivic building block

# Key Properties
1. Economy and concentration are hallmarks of Webern's style
2. Works are short because generating materials are restricted
3. The generator permeates all dimensions (pitch, instrumentation, register, articulation)
4. Transformations at the note level (T_n, I_n) are reflected at the series level
5. "The serial idea... enabled him to shape the motivic and intervallic flow at each of these levels of structure" (p. 336)

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the derived series and its generator
- Trace the generator in non-serial aspects (melodic line, registral groupings, timbral pairings)
- Compare transformations connecting notes within trichords with those connecting series forms
- Look for multi-level projection of the same intervals

# Context & Application
Webern represents one of five major approaches to twelve-tone composition surveyed in the chapter. His approach is distinguished by its intensive focus on a generating set class, which shapes the music from micro to macro levels. This contrasts with Schoenberg's hexachordal/combinatorial approach and Stravinsky's rotational method.

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 334--338, Exx. 6-28 through 6-34): Webern, Concerto for Nine Instruments, op. 24, second movement, mm. 1--10 -- derived from sc(014). Four discrete trichords ordered as P7, RI6, R1, I0. Intervals <+4,-1>, <-1,+4>, <+1,-4>, <-4,+1>. Every circled trichord is (014). The first two notes span T4, and the first two series span T4. The first two notes are related by I6, and the first two series are related by I6. The violin's four notes form (014); the melodic trichords are linked by the same T_n and I_n found within (014).

# Common Errors
- Thinking derivation is mechanical -- it enables profound musical expression
- Overlooking the generator's influence on non-pitch parameters
- Assuming brevity indicates simplicity -- it reflects concentration

# Common Confusions
- **Webern's approach vs. Schoenberg's**: Webern prioritizes motivic concentration; Schoenberg prioritizes hexachordal/aggregate relationships
- **Multi-level projection**: The same intervals connect notes within trichords AND series forms -- this is deliberate, not coincidental
