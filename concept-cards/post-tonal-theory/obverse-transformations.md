# Quick Definition
Triadic transformations that are the obverses of L and R: they retain a single common tone and flip the remaining dyad, producing two-voice semitone motion (L') or whole-tone motion (R').

# Formal Definition
L' and R' are the obverse transformations of L and R respectively. While P, L, and R retain a dyad and flip a single note around it, P', L', and R' do the opposite: they retain a singleton and flip the dyad.

- L' relates triads where the root of the major triad becomes the fifth of the minor triad (and vice versa). It involves contextual inversion around the note not part of the major third, with two voices each moving by one semitone.

- R' relates triads where the fifth of the major triad becomes the root of the minor triad (and vice versa). It involves contextual inversion around the note not part of the minor third, with two voices each moving by two semitones.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
L' transformation properties:
- Common tones: 1
- Voice leading: 2 voices move by 1 semitone each, in the same direction
- Relates triads whose roots are a perfect fourth apart

R' transformation properties:
- Common tones: 1
- Voice leading: 2 voices move by 2 semitones each, in the same direction
- Relates triads whose roots are a tritone apart

The obverse relationship:
- P retains fifth, flips third --> P' (SLIDE) retains third, flips fifth
- L retains minor third, flips fifth --> L' retains non-M3 tone, flips M3
- R retains major third, flips fifth --> R' retains non-m3 tone, flips m3

# Musical Context/Application
The obverse transformations complete the system of common-tone-preserving triadic transformations. In PLR-cycles (Example 4-28), triads lying opposite each other are related by P', L', or R'.

# Common Confusions
- L' and R' are less commonly used than L and R but complete the transformational system
- The prime notation (') indicates the obverse relationship, not a different operation altogether
- These transformations still preserve one common tone, just a different one than their non-prime counterparts
