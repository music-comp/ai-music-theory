# Quick Definition
Twelve-counting is the analytical technique of identifying which series form is being used at each point in a twelve-tone piece, numbering each note by its order position in the series.

# Core Definition
"One good way of getting oriented in a twelve-tone work is by identifying the series forms, informally known as *twelve-counting*. This can provide a kind of low-level map of a composition" (Straus, p. 319). The first step is identifying the series, usually presented explicitly at the beginning. The "usual twelve-counting procedure" involves "identifying the order position each pitch occupies in the series form" (p. 319). Series forms can be identified by constructing a matrix and looking up note sequences, or "more directly by applying our knowledge of the intervallic relationships between series forms" (p. 320).

# Prerequisites
- **12x12 matrix** -- reference tool for looking up series forms
- **Series class** -- the family of forms being identified
- **Intervallic relationships** -- for identifying forms without a matrix

# Key Properties
1. Numbers each note by its order position (1--12) within the series form
2. Provides a structural outline, not a complete analysis
3. Notes can overlap between forms (one note serving as last of one form and first of next)
4. Multiple series forms may sound simultaneously
5. Some series forms may be incomplete or have notes distributed among voices

# Construction / Recognition
**Direct method (using matrix):**
1. Identify the series from the opening statement
2. Construct a 12x12 matrix
3. Look at the first few notes of an unknown passage
4. Find which of the 48 forms begins with those notes

**Intervallic method:**
1. Compare the passage's ordered pc intervals to the known series intervals
2. Same intervals in same order = P-form
3. Complementary intervals in same order = I-form
4. Same intervals reversed = RI-form
5. Complementary intervals reversed = R-form
6. Calculate the index number to determine the specific transposition level

# Context & Application
"A twelve-count like this doesn't do much to help us hear the song better... but it does give a rough structural outline of the piece. It also gives a clarifying context for those intervallic relationships" (p. 322). Twelve-counting is a starting point, not an end point, for analysis.

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 319--322, Exx. 6-11 through 6-14): Webern, "Wie bin ich froh!" -- the melody presents P7 (notes numbered 1--12). The accompaniment's first notes (F#, F, D) have intervals that match P7's final intervals in reverse, identifying it as RI7. The index number (6 + 8 = 5 + 9 = 2 mod 12) confirms the relationship. The entire song uses only P7, R7, I7, and RI7.

# Common Errors
- Assuming every note belongs to exactly one series form (overlap is common)
- Forgetting that series forms may be presented simultaneously in different voices
- Thinking twelve-counting alone reveals musical meaning

# Common Confusions
- **Twelve-counting vs. hearing**: "It is difficult and musically unrewarding to hear the piece as a P0 followed by an RI5 and an R0. It is easy and musically rewarding to hear the chain of invariants moving through the music" (p. 330)
- **Structural outline vs. analysis**: Twelve-counting is preliminary; the real analysis examines invariants, subsets, and musical relationships
