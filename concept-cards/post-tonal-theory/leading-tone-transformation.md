# Quick Definition
A triadic transformation relating major and minor triads that share a minor third, where the third of the major triad becomes the root of the minor triad (and vice versa), with one voice moving by semitone.

# Formal Definition
The L (Leading-tone) transformation relates a major triad and a minor triad that contain the same minor third. Specifically, the third of the major triad becomes the root of the minor triad (and vice versa). The transformation is a contextual inversion around the shared minor third, producing parsimonious voice leading with one voice moving by semitone. L is its own inverse: L(L(x)) = x.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
L transformation properties:
- Common tones: 2 (the notes forming the minor third)
- Voice leading: 1 voice moves by 1 semitone
- Mode change: major <-> minor

Examples:
- L(C+) = E- [E and G are common; C moves down to B]
- L(E-) = C+ [E and G are common; B moves up to C]

Voice-leading diagram for C+ to E-:
```
C -> B (down 1 semitone - the "leading tone" motion)
E -> E (common)
G -> G (common)
```

The name "Leading-tone" refers to the characteristic semitone motion.

# Musical Context/Application
The L transformation is central to:
1. LP-cycles (hexatonic systems)
2. Hexatonic pole relationships (L followed by more L operations)
3. Neo-Romantic harmonic progressions
4. Minimalist opera (Adams, Glass)

# Common Confusions
- The "leading tone" in the name refers to the semitone voice motion, not necessarily to scale degree 7
- L relates triads whose roots are a major third apart (C+ and E-)
- L is distinct from L', which retains only one common tone
