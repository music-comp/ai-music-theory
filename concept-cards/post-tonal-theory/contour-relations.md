# Quick Definition
The study of melodic shapes based on relative pitch height rather than specific pitches or intervals; contour analysis focuses on patterns of higher and lower without requiring knowledge of exact notes.

# Formal Definition
Contour relations analyze musical lines and gestures in terms of their general up-and-down motion, independent of specific pitch content. This approach:
- Represents relative pitch positions as integers (0 = lowest, then ascending)
- Groups these representations into contour segments (CSEGs)
- Classifies CSEGs into equivalence classes (CSEG-classes)
- Identifies relationships through contour transformations (inversion, retrograde, retrograde-inversion)

Contour analysis extends beyond pitch to other musical parameters including dynamics and durations, wherever relative ordering can be meaningfully described.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
Contour relations use:
- Ordered integers: 0 (lowest) through n-1 (highest)
- Angle brackets to indicate ordering: <...>
- Four operations: Identity, Inversion, Retrograde, Retrograde-Inversion
- CSEG-classes analogous to set classes

The number of CSEG-classes grows rapidly with segment length:
- 2-note CSEGs: 1 class
- 3-note CSEGs: 2 classes
- 4-note CSEGs: 8 classes

# Musical Context/Application
Contour analysis is particularly valuable for:
- Music where pitch is indeterminate but direction is specified
- Atonal music where set-class relationships may be less salient than shape
- Music perception research (contour is often perceived before exact intervals)
- Analyzing non-pitched parameters using the same theoretical framework
- Experimental music with graphic or proportional notation

Contour provides a less precise but sometimes more perceptually relevant level of analysis than pitch-class theory.

# Examples
From Example 3-36: Crawford Seeger, String Quartet, first movement
- Three fragments share CSEG <2013> despite different intervals and set classes
- Unity through contour rather than pitch content

From Example 3-39: Stockhausen, Klavierstuck II
- Upper melody: CSEG <3201> in pitch
- Dynamics of upper melody: also <3201>
- Lower melody: CSEG <0132> (contour inversion)
- Same contour expressed in both pitch and dynamics

From Example 3-40: Feldman, Projection No. 1
- Pizzicato notes: CSEG <102> (middle-low-high)
- Durational pattern: also CSEG <102> (medium-short-long)
- Contour unifies pitch and duration

From Example 3-41: Cage, Variations II
- Four events characterized by duration, dynamics, pitch, and timbre
- Each dimension has its own contour
- Analysis through contour allows discussion of otherwise indeterminate music

# Common Confusions
- Thinking contour analysis replaces pitch analysis (they are complementary)
- Assuming contour is only useful when pitch is indeterminate (it adds insight even to fully determined music)
- Confusing contour operations with pitch-class operations (they are analogous but distinct)
- Not recognizing that contour can apply to non-pitch parameters
