# Quick Definition
Segmentation is the analytical process of dividing music into meaningful pitch-class groupings for set-class analysis.

# Formal Definition
Segmentation refers to the ways of carving up music into meaningful musical groupings for analysis. In post-tonal analysis, one main task is identifying principal sets and showing how they are transposed and inverted. Since important sets cannot be known in advance, analysts must enter the world of the piece through listening, playing, and singing, proposing and testing hypotheses about which musical ideas are fundamental and recurring.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
**Approaches to segmentation:**

1. **Melodic segments**: Consider all contiguous note groups
   - In a 6-note melody: notes 1-2-3, 2-3-4, 3-4-5, 4-5-6 (imbrication)
   - May span rests or phrase boundaries

2. **Harmonic/Simultaneity**: Notes sounding at any moment
   - Move through the music considering all notes sounding together

3. **Registral association**: Notes grouped by register
   - Highest/lowest notes of a passage
   - High points of successive phrases

4. **Rhythmic association**:
   - Notes on successive downbeats
   - Notes at beginnings of rhythmic figures
   - Notes with longest durations

5. **Timbral association**:
   - Notes by a single instrument
   - Notes with distinctive articulation

# Musical Context/Application
Segmentation requires balance between imaginative seeking and musical common sense. Notes grouped together must share some distinctive quality (proximity, register, loudness, length) that groups them and distinguishes them from surrounding notes. If a segmentation cannot be made palpably real through repeated listening, it should be abandoned.

# Examples
From the model analyses:
- Example A1-8 (Webern, "Wie bin ich froh!"): Multiple segmentation strategies reveal how the initial three-note melodic figure permeates the passage through various groupings: melodic fragments, registral groupings, and combinations of piano and voice.

- Example A2-1 (Schoenberg, Book of the Hanging Gardens): The opening gesture is segmented into component subsets, revealing nested motivic relationships.

# Common Confusions
Segmentation is not arbitrary note-picking. Notes must be associated by some musically meaningful quality. Multiple segmentations of the same passage are often valid and complementary, revealing different aspects of the musical structure. The goal is to describe the richest possible network of relationships.
