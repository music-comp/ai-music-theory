# Quick Definition
Segmentation is the analytical process of dividing music into meaningful pitch-class groupings for set-class analysis.

# Core Definition
Segmentation refers to "ways of carving [music] up into meaningful musical groupings" (Straus, Ch. 2). Since important sets cannot be known in advance, analysts must enter the piece through listening, playing, and singing, proposing and testing hypotheses. The process is circular: "You can't know what the main ideas are until you see them recur; but you can't find recurrences until you know what the main ideas are. The only practical solution is to poke around in the piece, proposing and testing hypotheses as you go."

# Prerequisites
- **Pitch-class set** -- the result of segmentation
- **Set class** -- the framework for recognizing recurrences

# Key Properties
1. Multiple valid segmentations of the same passage are common
2. Notes must share some distinctive quality to be grouped together
3. Segmentations should be aurally justifiable ("palpably real")
4. The goal is "to describe the richest possible network of musical relationships"

# Construction / Recognition
**Five strategies for grouping notes (Straus, pp. 86--87):**

1. **Melodic segments**: Consider all contiguous note groups in a melody (imbrication). Groups may span rests or phrase boundaries.

2. **Simultaneities**: Move through the music considering all notes sounding together at each moment.

3. **Registral association**: Group notes by register -- highest notes, lowest notes, high/low points of successive phrases.

4. **Rhythmic association**: Group notes on successive downbeats, at beginnings of rhythmic figures, or notes with longest durations.

5. **Timbral association**: Group notes by instrument, articulation (staccato, pizzicato), or other timbral markers.

# Context & Application
Segmentation requires "a balance between imaginative seeking and musical common sense." Do not restrict yourself to obvious groupings, but stay within what can be meaningfully heard. "You can't pluck notes out in some random way, just because they form a set that you are interested in." If a grouping cannot be made aurally real after good-faith effort, abandon it.

# Examples
**Example 2-32** (p. 81, Schoenberg, *Three Piano Pieces*, op. 11, no. 1): Multiple segmentations reveal sc(014):
- Melodic fragment: first three notes B--G#--G
- Sustained chord in m. 4: G--G#--B
- Registral grouping: G--Bb--B in mm. 4--5
- Contour high points: B (m. 1), G (mm. 4--5), G# (m. 10)
- Combinations of melody and chord

The passage is "virtually saturated with occurrences of members of this set class."

**Model Analysis 2.1** (p. 91, Schoenberg, *Book of the Hanging Gardens*, op. 15, no. 11): Multiple segmentation strategies reveal sc(0347) and sc(0126) at melodic, harmonic, small-scale, and large-scale levels.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Arbitrary note-picking to force a desired set class. **Correction**: Grouped notes must share a musically meaningful quality (proximity, register, rhythm, timbre).
- **Error**: Seeking a single all-encompassing explanation. **Correction**: Post-tonal music often resists single explanations; seek multiple meaningful strands.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Segmentation is objective. **Clarification**: Segmentation involves analytical judgment. Multiple valid segmentations may coexist; the C in m. 4 of Schoenberg op. 11 no. 1 functions in "at least three different ways" simultaneously.
