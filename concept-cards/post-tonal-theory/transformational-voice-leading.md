# Quick Definition

Voice leading understood through the pitch-class mappings induced by transposition (Tn) and inversion (In) operations, where the mappings between corresponding pitch classes in successive sets define transformational voices.

# Formal Definition

**Transformational voice leading** is an approach to voice leading in post-tonal music that focuses on the pitch-class mappings created by transposition and inversion operations. When a set is transformed by Tn or In, each pitch class in the original set maps to a specific pitch class in the transformed set. These mappings define **transformational voices** that connect successive harmonies.

Key distinctions:
- A **line** is a literal, concrete melodic succession in a specific register
- A **transformational voice** is an abstract mapping resulting from a harmonic transformation
- Transformational voice leading traces the pitch-class counterpoint created by T and I operations

This approach allows analysis of voice-leading connections even when actual registral voice leading may be obscured by octave displacement or textural changes.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition

**Transposition mappings (Tn):**
For Tn applied to set X: each pc x in X maps to pc (x + n) mod 12
- All voices move by the same interval
- Produces parallel motion in pitch-class space

**Inversion mappings (In):**
For In applied to set X: each pc x in X maps to pc (n - x) mod 12
- Voices move by complementary intervals around the index n
- May produce voice crossing in the transformational voices

**To identify transformational voice leading:**
1. Determine the T or I relating two successive sets
2. Calculate the mapping for each pitch class
3. Draw lines connecting mapped pitch classes
4. These lines represent the transformational voices

# Musical Context/Application

Transformational voice leading serves several analytical purposes:
- **Connecting harmonies**: Shows how one chord connects to the next through systematic mappings
- **Multi-level analysis**: Can operate at surface, middleground, and background levels
- **Motivic voice leading**: When intervals of transposition replicate motive intervals
- **Voice-leading reduction**: Simplifying complex progressions to essential transformations

The approach is particularly useful when:
- Registral positions do not clearly define voices
- Harmonies belong to the same set class and are related by T or I
- Traditional voice-leading concepts do not apply

# Examples

**Webern, Six Songs, op. 14, no. 1, "Die Sonne"** (Example 4-14):
- Five chords, all members of sc(016)
- Horizontal and diagonal lines trace pitch-class mappings
- Three transformational voices move through the progression
- Level 1: Five-chord progression with individual T and I operations
- Level 2: Simplifies to two inversional moves exchanging vocal and bass parts
- Level 3: Entire progression is T8 of first chord onto last chord
- Harmonies bound together at multiple hierarchical levels

**Webern, Movements for String Quartet, op. 5, no. 3** (Example 4-15):
- Groups of two or three chords, all members of sc(014)
- Registration reflects transformational voice leading consistently
- Viola crosses above second violin following the voice-leading logic
- Intervals of transposition (T1, T3, T4) = intervals within (014)
- Voice leading follows a motivic path

**Sessions, Piano Sonata, first movement** (Example 4-17):
- Five chords representing four different set classes
- Fuzzy transpositions and inversions connect the chords
- Transformational voices cross in this progression
- Demonstrates voice leading between non-identical set classes

# Common Confusions

- **Transformational voice vs. registral voice**: A transformational voice is defined by pitch-class mapping, not by register; the actual notes may appear in different octaves
- **Abstract vs. literal**: Transformational voice leading describes abstract relationships; actual voice leading may differ
- **T vs. I**: Transposition produces parallel motion (all voices move the same direction); inversion can produce voice crossing
- **Multiple levels**: A single progression can be analyzed at multiple hierarchical levels, each revealing different voice-leading relationships
