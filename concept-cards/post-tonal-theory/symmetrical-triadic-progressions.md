# Quick Definition
Progressions of triads arranged symmetrically around a central triad, where triads above and below the center balance each other, establishing centricity through inversional symmetry rather than functional harmony.

# Formal Definition
Symmetrical triadic progressions organize triads around a central triad so that for every triad at a certain distance above the center, there is a corresponding triad at the same distance below. This creates inversional balance with the central triad as the axis, establishing its centricity not through dominant-tonic relationships but through symmetrical structure.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
Organization principles:
1. Central triad serves as axis of symmetry
2. Flanking triads balance at equal intervals above and below
3. Triad roots may relate by:
   - Equal semitone distances (C#+ and Eb+ flanking D+)
   - Inversionally related intervals

The central triad gains prominence through:
- Position at the axis of balance
- Arrival point for converging motion
- Departure point for diverging motion

# Musical Context/Application
Symmetrical triadic progressions provide:
1. Centricity without functional harmony
2. Alternative to V-I as cadential confirmation
3. Structural balance and coherence
4. Connection between large-scale form and local detail

# Examples
Example 4-36: Britten's Serenade for Tenor, Horn, and Strings, "Sonnet" - Major triads arranged symmetrically around D major. C#+ and Eb+ surround D+ symmetrically (1 semitone each direction). C+ at the cadence balances E+ at the beginning (2 semitones each direction). D+ is established as tonic through inversional symmetry, not traditional harmonic function.

# Common Confusions
- Symmetrical arrangement differs from functional progression even when using the same triads
- The central triad need not be traditionally "tonic" in character
- Symmetry may be approximate rather than strict
- The axis of symmetry applies to triad roots, not necessarily to all voices
