# Quick Definition
A property of musical passages where every pitch is balanced by another pitch at an equal distance on the opposite side of a central axis pitch, creating a mirror-like symmetry.

# Formal Definition
Inversional symmetry in pitch space occurs when every note in a passage or set has a corresponding partner at an equal registral distance on the opposite side of a central pitch (the axis). This axis of symmetry is a specific pitch that serves as the literal midpoint around which all notes balance. Unlike pitch-class space, where octave equivalence creates a circular space, pitch space is linear, so the axis is a single specific pitch.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
For a passage symmetrical around axis pitch A:
- If note N is d semitones above A, there must be a note d semitones below A
- The axis is the arithmetic mean of any balanced pair: axis = (N1 + N2) / 2

Example with axis B4 (MIDI 71):
- A#3 (58) is 13 below; C6 (84) is 13 above
- A3 (57) is 14 below; C#6 (85) is 14 above
- G#3 (56) is 15 below; D6 (86) is 15 above

Verification: For any balanced pair, (lower + upper) / 2 = axis pitch

# Musical Context/Application
Inversional symmetry in pitch space:
1. Establishes the axis pitch as a central tone
2. Creates audible balance and symmetry
3. Can organize entire passages or movements
4. Is particularly clear when both balanced notes are heard simultaneously or in close proximity

# Examples
Example 5-4: Gubaidulina's String Trio - All three instruments establish B4 as a central tone. New notes are balanced around it: A#(one below) balanced by C (one above); A3 by C#6; G#3 by D6; G3 by D#6. The axis B4 is literally the central tone in the passage.

# Common Confusions
- Pitch space symmetry involves specific registers; pitch-class space symmetry does not
- The axis in pitch space is a single pitch, not a pair of pitch classes
- Some notes may lack partners (the passage may be approximately rather than strictly symmetrical)
- The axis pitch may or may not be sounded
