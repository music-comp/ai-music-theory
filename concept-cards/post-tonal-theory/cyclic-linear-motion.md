# Quick Definition

Melodic motion that follows the path of an interval cycle, using one of the six cycle types (C1-C6) to shape and direct linear movement in post-tonal music.

# Formal Definition

**Cyclic linear motion** occurs when a melody or melodic line follows the sequence of pitch classes defined by an interval cycle. Rather than moving by varied intervals, the line proceeds systematically by a single repeated interval (or its complement). The cycle may be traversed completely or partially, in either direction (clockwise or counterclockwise on the pitch-class clock). Cyclic linear motion provides a systematic, non-tonal means of organizing melodic direction and creating a sense of goal-directed motion.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition

To identify cyclic linear motion:
1. Extract the melodic boundary tones or structural pitches
2. Check if consecutive pitches are related by a consistent interval class
3. Identify which cycle type (C1-C6) governs the motion
4. Note the direction: ascending (clockwise) or descending (counterclockwise)
5. Determine if the cycle is complete or partial

Common patterns:
- C1: Chromatic stepwise motion (ascending or descending by semitone)
- C2: Whole-tone scalar motion
- C3: Motion through a diminished seventh chord
- C4: Motion through an augmented triad
- C5: Motion by fourths or fifths (quartal motion)
- C6: Alternation between tritone-related pitches

# Musical Context/Application

Cyclic linear motion serves several functions:
- **Goal-directed motion**: Provides systematic approach to cadential pitches
- **Melodic coherence**: Creates predictable, audible patterns
- **Structural framework**: Organizes melodic boundary tones over large spans
- **Non-tonal logic**: Replaces tonal scale-degree motion with cyclic motion

Applications include:
- Cadential approaches (chromatic lines to resolution)
- Structural melodies (boundary tones following cycles)
- Sequential passages organized by interval cycles
- Large-scale melodic planning across sections

# Examples

**Bartok, String Quartet No. 6, fourth movement** (Example 4-6):
- Cello approaches C-minor cadence with descending C1: E-Eb-D-Db-C
- First violin approaches same cadence with ascending C2: A-B-C#-Eb
- Two different cycles converge on the cadential goal
- The C3 (minor-third cycle) shapes the main theme of the movement

**Varese, Density 21.5** (Example 4-7):
- First ten measures: principal melodic boundary tones follow C3_1 (C#-E-G-Bb)
- The melody gradually rises an octave from C#4 to Db5 within this framework
- Arrival on D5 (fff) in measure 11 signals a shift of harmonic orientation
- The C3 provides large-scale melodic direction

**Stravinsky, The Rite of Spring, Introduction** (Model Analysis 4.1):
- Voice-leading reduction reveals melodic motion through C-A-F#-D# (C3)
- The diminished seventh chord (cycle of minor thirds) unifies the passage
- Large-scale melodic structure follows cyclic path

# Common Confusions

- **Cyclic vs. scalar motion**: Cyclic motion follows a single interval systematically; scalar motion may use varied intervals within a scale
- **Surface vs. structural**: Cyclic linear motion may be evident on the surface or only at a deeper structural level (boundary tones)
- **Complete vs. partial traversal**: Most cyclic linear motion uses partial segments of cycles, not complete traversals
- **Single line vs. compound line**: Cyclic patterns may be distributed across multiple voices forming a compound melody
