# Quick Definition
Post-tonal theory is the body of analytical concepts and tools developed to describe and understand the pitch organization of post-tonal music.

# Formal Definition
Post-tonal theory comprises a broad consensus among music theorists regarding the basic musical elements of post-tonal music, including pitch, interval, motive, and harmony. Like theories of scales, triads, and harmonic progressions in tonal music, post-tonal theory introduces basic theoretical concepts appropriate to the music of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.

# Musical Context/Application
Post-tonal theory provides analytical tools for understanding music that does not follow traditional tonal conventions. Key developments in the field include:
- Transformational networks and graphs
- Composing-out techniques
- Transformational voice leading
- Voice-leading space
- Harmonic quality analysis
- Triadic post-tonality
- Inversional symmetry and inversional axes
- Interval cycles
- Contextual inversion and inversional chains

# Examples
Classic treatises in post-tonal theory include:
- Allen Forte, The Structure of Atonal Music (1973)
- John Rahn, Basic Atonal Theory (1980)
- George Perle, Serial Composition and Atonality (6th ed., 1991)
- David Lewin, Generalized Musical Intervals and Transformations (1987)
- Robert Morris, Composition with Pitch Classes (1987)

# Common Confusions
Post-tonal theory is sometimes mistakenly thought to apply only to twelve-tone or serial music. In fact, it encompasses a much broader range of analytical approaches applicable to various non-tonal musics, including freely atonal works that do not use serial techniques.
