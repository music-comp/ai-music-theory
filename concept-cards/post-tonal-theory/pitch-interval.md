# Quick Definition
A pitch interval (pi) is the distance between two pitches measured in semitones, moving through pitch space.

# Formal Definition
A pitch interval (abbreviated pi) is the distance between two pitches, measured by the number of semitones between them. A pitch interval is created when we move from pitch to pitch in pitch space. It can be as large as the range of our hearing or as small as a semitone. Pitch intervals may be understood as either ordered (indicating direction) or unordered (indicating only distance).

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
Pitch interval calculation:
- Count the number of semitones between the two pitches
- Ordered pitch interval (opi): includes direction (+/-)
  - + indicates ascending interval
  - - indicates descending interval
- Unordered pitch interval (upi): absolute value only

Example:
- C4 ascending to E4: opi = +4, upi = 4
- E4 descending to C4: opi = -4, upi = 4

# Musical Context/Application
Pitch intervals describe the actual registral motion in a melody or between voices. Unlike pitch-class intervals (which operate mod 12), pitch intervals preserve information about register and can exceed 11 semitones. Analysis using pitch intervals captures contour and registral relationships.

# Examples
**Example 1-11** (Schoenberg, String Quartet No. 3, first movement): A melodic passage analyzed for both ordered and unordered pitch intervals. The ordered pitch intervals (<+1, -11, -1, +3, -13, +1>) focus attention on the contour of the line and its balance of rising and falling motion. The unordered pitch intervals (1, 11, 1, 3, 13, 1) ignore contour and concentrate on the spaces between pitches.

# Common Confusions
Pitch intervals differ from pitch-class intervals in that they measure actual registral distance without reducing to mod 12. A pitch interval of 15 (minor ninth) remains 15, whereas the equivalent pitch-class interval is 3. Choose pitch intervals when register and contour matter; use pitch-class intervals for more abstract analysis.
