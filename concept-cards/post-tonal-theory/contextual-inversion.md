# Quick Definition
An inversion operation defined with reference to a particular internal feature of the harmony being inverted, rather than with reference to specific pitch classes. The effect varies depending on the musical context.

# Formal Definition
Common-tone-preserving contextual inversion inverts a harmony around one or more of its own notes to produce another harmony. Unlike generic inversion (In), which is defined with reference to specific pitch classes (e.g., I5 maps C onto F regardless of context), contextual inversion is defined by the structure of the harmony itself. Examples include "invert around the semitone" or "invert around the first note of a series."

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
Contextual inversions are typically labeled with arbitrary letters (J, K, L, etc.) and defined by:
1. Which note(s) remain fixed (common tones)
2. Which structural feature serves as the axis of inversion

For sc(014):
- J = flip around the i1 (the two notes forming i1 map onto each other)
- K = flip around the i4 (the two notes forming i4 map onto each other)

For a four-note series:
- J = flip around and retain last two notes
- K = flip around and retain first note
- L = flip around and retain first and last notes

# Musical Context/Application
Contextual inversions are particularly significant when they occur in chains, creating extended progressions of harmonies. They generate RI-chains (chains where each motive is related by retrograde-inversion to adjacent motives) and can be used to create contextual-inversion spaces that map all possible positions and relationships.

# Examples
Example 4-22: Webern's Concerto for Nine Instruments, op. 24, second movement. The melody consists of instances of (014) that form links in a chain of contextual inversions, alternating J and K. A complete cycle of JK inversions creates a circular space of twelve positions.

Example 4-23-4-25: Stravinsky's "Musick to heare" from Three Shakespeare Songs uses a four-note series with contextual inversions J, K, and L connecting P and I forms of the series.

# Common Confusions
- Contextual inversion differs from generic In in that its effect depends on the specific harmony
- The labels (J, K, L) are arbitrary and must be defined for each analytical context
- Contextual inversions always preserve some common tone(s) with the original set
