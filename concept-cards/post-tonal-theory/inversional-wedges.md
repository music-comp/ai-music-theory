# Quick Definition
Voice-leading patterns where notes move symmetrically outward from (expanding wedge) or inward toward (contracting wedge) a central axis pitch.

# Formal Definition
Inversional wedges are voice-leading configurations in which notes radiate symmetrically outward from a central tone (expanding wedge) or converge symmetrically inward toward a central tone (contracting wedge). In an expanding wedge, paired voices move away from the axis at equal rates in opposite directions. In a contracting wedge, paired voices approach the axis from opposite directions, converging on it. Contracting wedges often have strong cadential force.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
Expanding wedge:
- Voices begin at or near the axis
- Move outward by gradually increasing distances
- Pattern: axis -> +/-1 -> +/-2 -> +/-3 semitones...

Contracting wedge:
- Voices begin at a distance from the axis
- Converge toward the axis
- Creates cadential arrival on the axis note

Verification: At each moment, the average of the upper and lower voices equals the axis pitch.

# Musical Context/Application
Inversional wedges provide:
1. Clear establishment or reinforcement of the axis as central
2. Goal-directed voice leading toward (contracting) or away from (expanding) the center
3. Cadential force when converging on the axis
4. Dramatic effect through symmetrical voice motion

# Examples
Example 5-5: Ligeti's Ten Pieces for Wind Quintet, no. 9 - Three instruments in canon share a line beginning on Eb, with a wedge of notes expanding outward by gradually increasing distances. The axis is Eb.

Example 5-6: Shows a contracting wedge with strong cadential force. Lines begin on E in four different octaves and converge on the cadential Bb.

Example 5-7: Bartók's String Quartet No. 5 - A symmetrical wedge begins with C-Db and expands to G-F#, then returns to C-Db in a different octave.

# Common Confusions
- Wedges operate in pitch space (specific registers), not abstract pitch-class space
- Expanding and contracting refer to registral motion, not dynamic change
- The wedge establishes the axis as central through motion, not just static symmetry
- Wedges need not be strictly systematic; approximate wedges are common
