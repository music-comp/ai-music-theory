# Quick Definition
Voice leading that minimizes motion between chords, ideally with most voices stationary and any moving voices moving by the smallest possible intervals (semitones).

# Formal Definition
Parsimonious voice leading connects harmonies in the smoothest possible way, with voices moving as little as possible. The most parsimonious voice leading involves two voices remaining motionless (as common tones) while the single moving voice moves by only one semitone. Slightly less parsimonious options include: two common tones with one voice moving by whole step, or one common tone with two voices each moving by semitone.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
Degrees of parsimony (for triads):

Most parsimonious (total motion = 1 semitone):
- P: 2 common tones, 1 voice moves 1 semitone
- L: 2 common tones, 1 voice moves 1 semitone

Moderately parsimonious (total motion = 2 semitones):
- R: 2 common tones, 1 voice moves 2 semitones
- SLIDE (P'): 1 common tone, 2 voices move 1 semitone each
- L': 1 common tone, 2 voices move 1 semitone each

Less parsimonious (total motion = 4 semitones):
- R': 1 common tone, 2 voices move 2 semitones each

# Musical Context/Application
Parsimonious voice leading is valued for:
1. Creating smooth, connected progressions
2. Maintaining coherence without functional harmony
3. Binding tonally distant chords together
4. Achieving the characteristic sound of neo-Romantic and minimalist triadic music

# Common Confusions
- Parsimony measures total voice-leading motion, not just the number of moving voices
- Parsimonious does not mean functional; these progressions lack traditional harmonic function
- Maximum parsimony (no motion) would require identical chords; the transformations balance parsimony with actual harmonic change
