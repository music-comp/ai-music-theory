# Quick Definition
Post-tonal music refers to Western art music of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries that operates outside the traditional tonal system of major and minor keys.

# Formal Definition
Post-tonal music encompasses a broad range of compositional approaches developed after the common-practice tonal period, including atonal, twelve-tone, and other non-tonal organizational systems. This music typically abandons traditional key centers, functional harmony, and diatonic scales in favor of new pitch organizations based on pitch-class sets, interval structures, and serial procedures.

# Musical Context/Application
Post-tonal music emerged in the early twentieth century through the work of composers seeking alternatives to tonal organization. The "classical" prewar repertoire includes music by Schoenberg, Stravinsky, Bartok, Webern, and Berg. The approach has continued through composers including Adams, Ades, Babbitt, Berio, Boulez, Britten, Cage, Carter, Cowell, Crawford Seeger, Crumb, Dallapiccola, Davies, Debussy, Feldman, Glass, Gubaidulina, Ives, Ligeti, Lutoslawski, Mamlok, Messiaen, Musgrave, Reich, Ruggles, Saariaho, Schnittke, Sessions, Shostakovich, Stockhausen, Tower, Varese, Wolpe, Wuorinen, and Zwilich.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes assume post-tonal music is random or lacks organization. In fact, post-tonal music often exhibits intense motivic and intervallic coherence, though organized by different principles than tonal music. The term "atonal" (meaning "without tonality") is sometimes considered pejorative by composers who prefer "post-tonal" or simply describe their music as organized by different means.
