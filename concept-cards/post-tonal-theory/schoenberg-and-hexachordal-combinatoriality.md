# Quick Definition
Schoenberg's mature twelve-tone music is characterized by I-combinatorial series, where hexachords related by inversion combine to form aggregates, enabling both local phrase connections and large-scale twelve-tone area organization.

# Formal Definition
In his mature twelve-tone music, Schoenberg always constructs series in which the two hexachords are related by inversion (I-combinatorial). If the two hexachords are related by I_n, combining two series forms related by the same I_n will produce aggregates of all twelve pitch classes. Schoenberg uses inversional combinatoriality to create local combinations and associations among series forms as well as to organize large-scale motion through twelve-tone areas.

# Musical Context/Application
Combinatoriality serves Schoenberg in multiple ways:
- **Local connections**: Aggregate formation links consecutive series forms
- **Common-tone links**: Hexachords with same content create smooth transitions
- **Phrase articulation**: Aggregate completion marks phrase boundaries
- **Large-scale form**: Twelve-tone areas group combinatorially related forms
- **Modulation**: Motion between areas creates harmonic progression

# Examples
- Schoenberg, String Quartet No. 4:
  - P2 is combinatorial with I7 (index = 9)
  - Third movement opens with P0 followed by RI5; second hexachord of P0 + first hexachord of RI5 = aggregate
  - Movement begins in area A2; modulates to A4, then A8 (climax at m. 165)
- Schoenberg, Piano Piece, op. 33a:
  - Uses P10 and I3 (and retrogrades) throughout first theme
  - Based on I-combinatorial pair
  - Areas organize the entire formal structure

# Common Confusions
- Assuming Schoenberg invented combinatoriality—he discovered/developed the concept
- Thinking combinatoriality is purely mechanical—it serves expressive musical purposes
- Overlooking the connection between local aggregate formation and large-scale area structure
- Not recognizing that the combinatorial index shapes all series relationships in a piece
