# Quick Definition
A compound interval is any interval larger than an octave (more than 12 semitones). Via octave equivalence, compound intervals are considered equivalent to their within-octave counterparts for pitch-class analysis.

# Core Definition
Compound intervals are intervals larger than one octave. In pitch space, they retain their full size (e.g., a minor ninth = 13 semitones). In pitch-class space, however, compound intervals are reduced to their within-octave equivalents via octave equivalence (mod 12), so a minor ninth (13) becomes a semitone (1). This reduction is one of the steps from pitch intervals to interval classes.

# Prerequisites
- **Intervals Calculated in Semitones** — compound intervals are measured in semitones
- **Octave Equivalence** — the principle by which compound intervals reduce to simple ones

# Key Properties
1. Any interval larger than 12 semitones is compound
2. In pitch space: compound intervals retain their full size (pi 13, 14, 15, ...)
3. In pitch-class space: compound intervals reduce mod 12 (pi 13 becomes pci 1)
4. For interval class: further reduce by taking the smaller complement (pi 13 = ic 1)
5. Compound intervals and their simple equivalents belong to the same interval class

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Take any simple interval (0-11 semitones)
2. Add one or more octaves (12, 24, 36 ...)
3. The result is a compound interval

## To Recognize:
1. Any interval spanning more than 12 semitones
2. In traditional terms: a ninth, tenth, eleventh, etc.

# Context & Application
Compound intervals arise frequently in post-tonal music, which often spans wide registral ranges. Whether to analyze them as compound (preserving registral information) or reduce them to their simple equivalents depends on the analytical question. Example 1-9 shows enharmonic and octave-equivalent intervals in Carter, where major thirds (4) and compound major thirds (16) are all treated as equivalent.

# Examples
**Example 1-9** (p. 24): In Carter, String Quartet No. 3, seven harmonic intervals include both simple major thirds and compound (octave-equivalent) major thirds. All are understood as "4s, or compound (i.e., octave-equivalent) 4s."

**Example 1-10** (p. 24): The interval table extends beyond the octave: minor 9th = 13, major 9th = 14, minor 10th = 15, major 10th = 16.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Always reducing compound intervals without noting the analytical choice involved
  **Correction**: Reduction to ic is an abstraction. Sometimes the specific compound interval (e.g., the difference between a major ninth and a major second) matters analytically.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking compound and simple versions of an interval sound identical
  **Clarification**: They belong to the same interval class but sound different due to registral distance. Interval-class membership captures an abstract similarity, not acoustic identity.
