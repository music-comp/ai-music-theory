# Quick Definition
A triadic transformation relating major and minor triads that share the same root, with two common tones (the perfect fifth) and one voice moving by semitone.

# Formal Definition
The P (Parallel) transformation relates a major triad and a minor triad that contain the same perfect fifth and share the same root. The transformation is a contextual inversion around the shared perfect fifth. When applied, the third of the triad moves by one semitone (up if minor to major, down if major to minor) while the root and fifth remain as common tones. P is its own inverse: P(P(x)) = x.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
P transformation properties:
- Common tones: 2 (root and fifth)
- Voice leading: 1 voice moves by 1 semitone
- Mode change: major <-> minor

Examples:
- P(C+) = C- [C and G are common; E moves to Eb]
- P(A-) = A+ [A and E are common; C moves to C#]

Voice-leading diagram for C+ to C-:
```
C -> C (common)
E -> Eb (down 1 semitone)
G -> G (common)
```

# Musical Context/Application
The P transformation is fundamental in:
1. Parallel major/minor relationships in post-tonal contexts
2. Chains combining P with other transformations (e.g., LP-cycles)
3. Creating mode mixture without functional implications
4. Minimalist and neo-Romantic compositional techniques

# Common Confusions
- "Parallel" refers to parallel major/minor keys (same tonic), not parallel voice motion
- P is distinct from SLIDE (P'), which also relates parallel-third triads but retains only the third
- P always changes mode; it cannot relate two major or two minor triads
