# Quick Definition
Pitches separated by one or more perfect octaves are understood as equivalent and belong to the same pitch class.

# Core Definition
Octave equivalence is the principle that pitches one or more octaves apart are functionally equivalent and are members of the same pitch class. Western musical notation reflects this by giving the same letter name to octave-related pitches. Equivalence does not mean identity: octave-equivalent pitches may differ in register, rhythm, timbre, and other respects, yet they share an essential sameness that allows musical ideas to be recognized across registral displacement.

# Prerequisites
- **Pitch** — understanding that each note has a specific frequency and registral location

# Key Properties
1. Octave-related pitches share the same letter name and pitch-class integer
2. Equivalence does not imply identity; register, rhythm, and timbre still matter musically
3. Octave equivalence is the basis for reducing pitch space to pitch-class space (mod 12)
4. Adding or subtracting 12 semitones yields the same pitch class

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Take any pitch
2. Move up or down by one or more octaves (multiples of 12 semitones)
3. The resulting pitch is octave-equivalent to the original

## To Recognize:
1. Two pitches share the same (or enharmonic) letter name
2. They differ by a whole number of octaves (12, 24, 36 ... semitones)

# Context & Application
Octave equivalence is one of the two foundational equivalence relations (along with enharmonic equivalence) that define pitch class. In post-tonal theory, it allows analysts to reduce the infinite range of pitches to just twelve pitch classes, enabling set-theoretic operations. Composers exploit octave equivalence to redistribute melodic material across registers while preserving essential pitch-class identity.

# Examples
**Example 1-1** (p. 18): Two versions of a melody from Schoenberg, String Quartet No. 4, first movement. The second version is so widely registrally displaced that the first violin cannot play all the notes and the cello must assist. Despite differences in rhythm and range, the two melodies are recognizably the same because they are octave equivalent.

**Example 1-2** (p. 18): In Schoenberg, Three Piano Pieces, op. 11, no. 1, the first three melody notes and the sustained notes in mm. 4-5 are octave equivalent, both containing B, G#, and G.

**Example 1-3** (p. 19): The viola melody notes G, B, and C# return as a cadential chord at the end of the phrase, related by octave equivalence.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating octave-equivalent pitches as identical
  **Correction**: Equivalence means they belong to the same class, not that they are the same in every musical respect. Register, timbre, and context still matter.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming octave equivalence eliminates the importance of register
  **Clarification**: Octave equivalence is an analytical abstraction. Section 1.13 (Spacing and Register) shows that registral placement profoundly affects the sound of a collection even when pitch-class content is unchanged.
