# Quick Definition
Pitches separated by one or more perfect octaves are understood as equivalent, sharing the same pitch-class name.

# Formal Definition
Octave equivalence is the principle that pitches one or more octaves apart are functionally equivalent and belong to the same pitch class. Western musical notation reflects this equivalence by giving the same letter name to octave-related pitches. For example, the name "A" applies not only to a specific pitch (such as the A a minor third below middle C) but also to all pitches one or more octaves above or below it.

# Mathematical Formulation/Recognition
In pitch-class space (mod 12), adding or subtracting 12 semitones results in the same pitch class:
- If pitch p has pitch-class value n, then p + 12 and p - 12 also have pitch-class value n
- Octave equivalence can be expressed as: pitch class = pitch mod 12

# Musical Context/Application
Octave-related pitches sound similar and typically function identically in post-tonal music. This allows composers to present musical ideas across different registers while maintaining their essential identity. Octave equivalence is fundamental to the concept of pitch class and enables the analysis of musical relationships that span wide registral distances.

# Examples
**Example 1-1**: Two versions of a melody from Schoenberg's String Quartet No. 4, first movement, that are octave equivalent despite differences in rhythm and range. The second version spans such a wide range that the first violin cannot play all notes; the cello must assist.

**Example 1-2**: In Schoenberg's Three Piano Pieces, op. 11, no. 1, the first three melody notes and the sustained notes in measures 4-5 are octave equivalent, both containing B, G#, and G.

**Example 1-3**: In Schoenberg's Three Piano Pieces, the viola melody notes G, B, and C# return as a cadential chord at the phrase's end.

# Common Confusions
While octave-equivalent pitches are considered equivalent in terms of pitch class, they are not identical. Differences in register, articulation, rhythm, and timbre remain musically significant. Octave equivalence is an abstraction that allows certain kinds of analysis while acknowledging that registral placement still matters compositionally.
