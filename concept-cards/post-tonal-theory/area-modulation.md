# Quick Definition
Area modulation is the twelve-tone equivalent of tonal modulation, where music moves from one twelve-tone area to another, creating large-scale harmonic motion analogous to key changes.

# Formal Definition
Because of combinatoriality, Schoenberg is able to "modulate" from area to area, creating a sense of harmonic motion at the highest levels of structure. Unlike tonal modulation where the diatonic collection changes content when transposed, the twelve-tone series always contains all twelve pitch classes. Harmonic areas cannot be created on the basis of changing content. Combinatoriality provides a solution by grouping families of series forms based not on total content (always the same) but on the content of their hexachords.

# Musical Context/Application
- Creates large-scale formal articulation in twelve-tone music
- Areas can be related by intervals suggestive of tonal relationships
- Composers may create progressions analogous to I-II-V-I
- The interval of transposition between areas can compose-out series segments
- Area motion is more subtle than tonal modulation but serves similar formal functions

# Examples
- Schoenberg, String Quartet No. 4, first movement:
  - Opens in A2 (mm. 1-31)
  - Modulates to A4 in the passage at mm. 148-169
  - Reaches climactic A8 at m. 165 (tritone from opening)
  - Large-scale progression: A2 → A4 → A8 → (return to A2)
  - This resembles I → II → V → I in tonal terms
  - The interval progression (up 2, up 4) composes out the (027) of the series opening

# Common Confusions
- Expecting area modulation to sound as obvious as tonal key change
- Thinking modulation requires changing pitch-class content
- Not recognizing the relationship between area-level intervals and series intervals
- Assuming area boundaries are always clear—they can be gradual or overlapping
