# Quick Definition
Area modulation is the twelve-tone equivalent of tonal modulation, where music moves from one twelve-tone area to another, creating large-scale harmonic motion analogous to key changes.

# Core Definition
"Because of combinatoriality, Schoenberg is able to 'modulate' from area to area, creating a sense of harmonic motion at the highest levels of structure" (Straus, p. 344). Unlike tonal modulation (where the diatonic collection changes content), twelve-tone area changes involve different hexachordal groupings while maintaining the same aggregate content. "Combinatoriality provides a solution to this problem because it groups together families of series forms based not on their total content (which is always the same) but on the content of their hexachords" (p. 344).

# Prerequisites
- **Twelve-tone areas** -- the structural units being moved between
- **Hexachordal combinatoriality** -- the basis for area definition

# Key Properties
1. Motion from area to area creates large-scale formal articulation
2. Intervals between areas can compose-out series segments
3. More subtle than tonal modulation but serves similar formal functions
4. Area relationships can suggest tonal analogies (I-II-V-I)

# Construction / Recognition
- Track which area is in use at each point in the composition
- Note the interval of transposition between successive areas
- Compare the area-level intervals to intervals within the series

# Context & Application
Area modulation is central to Schoenberg's large-scale formal organization. The interval relationships between areas often reflect the intervallic content of the series itself, creating structural depth.

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 343--344, Ex. 6-40): Schoenberg, String Quartet No. 4, first movement -- opens in A2 (mm. 1--31), modulates to A4, reaches A8 at the climactic m. 165 (opening melody returns a tritone away).

**Example 2** (pp. 368--369): Schoenberg, op. 33a -- progression A10-A0-A5-A10. "In traditional terms, that is a motion up a whole step, then up a perfect fourth, then a final descent by a perfect fifth" resembling I-II-V-I. "The large-scale motion from area to area thus composes-out the initial melodic idea" since the first three notes of the series form (027) (p. 369).

# Common Errors
- Expecting area modulation to be as audible as tonal key change
- Not recognizing that area-level intervals can reflect series intervals

# Common Confusions
- **Area modulation vs. tonal modulation**: Tonal modulation changes pitch-class content; area modulation changes hexachordal grouping while keeping the same aggregate
