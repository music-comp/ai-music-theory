# Quick Definition
The productive pairing of diatonic and octatonic collections, which share many subsets (triads, seventh chords, (0235), (0134)) that serve as pivot points for shifting between the two collection types.

# Core Definition
Diatonic and octatonic collections make a "particularly effective pair" because, despite their obvious structural differences, they share many subsets. The octatonic collection contains four major triads, four minor triads, and other diatonic formations including (0235), minor/half-diminished/dominant seventh chords. These shared subsets create points of intersection that enable smooth transitions between diatonic and octatonic passages. A single harmony can belong to both a diatonic and an octatonic collection, serving as a pivot between the two harmonic worlds (Straus, Ch. 5, pp. 276-279).

# Prerequisites
- **Diatonic collection** -- one of the interacting collections
- **Octatonic collection** -- the other interacting collection
- **Collectional interaction** -- the general framework

# Key Properties
1. DIA and OCT share many subsets (triads, seventh chords, (0235), (0134))
2. A single subset can pivot between the two collection types
3. The centric tone may remain constant while the collection shifts
4. Alternately, the collection may remain constant while the center shifts
5. The "Psalms chord" (E-minor triad) exemplifies a pivot harmony

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the collection type for each section/phrase
2. Look for shared subsets at the boundary between sections
3. Track whether the centric tone changes when the collection changes
4. Note the characteristic shift in harmonic "color" between DIA and OCT

# Context & Application
The interaction between diatonic and octatonic collections is central to the music of Stravinsky and Bartok. Stravinsky often establishes a harmony within one collection, then recontextualizes it within the other, creating a shift of harmonic environment while maintaining continuity through the shared subset. Bartok uses the contrast to differentiate formal sections (e.g., first theme in one octatonic collection, second theme in another).

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 277): Stravinsky, *Symphony of Psalms* -- The "Psalms chord" (E-minor triad plus (0134) = E-F-G-Ab-Bb-B-C#-D) is first heard as a subset of OCT1,2 on E, followed by octatonic music. Later, the same chord returns followed by diatonic music (E Phrygian). The E-minor triad belongs to both OCT1,2 and E Phrygian, linking the two collection types. The centric tone E remains constant.

**Example 2** (p. 278): Stravinsky, *Serenade in A* -- Combines A Aeolian with F Ionian (two diatonic collections sharing six common tones). F and A compete as centers; the downbeat sonority has A in outer voices but F in inner voice.

**Example 3** (p. 279): Bartok, *"Harvest Song"* -- Three phrases use OCT1,2, OCT2,3, and DIA6b. The shift from T6-related tetrachords to T5-related tetrachords marks the change from octatonic to diatonic.

# Common Errors
- Forcing all music into a single collection type when interaction is at work
- Ignoring the shared subsets that enable smooth transitions

# Common Confusions
- **Shift vs. blend**: Collections can shift sequentially (first OCT, then DIA) or blend simultaneously (shared subset as pivot)
