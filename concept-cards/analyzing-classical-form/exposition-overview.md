# Quick Definition
The exposition is the first required section of sonata form, presenting the principal melodic-motivic material and establishing the tonal conflict between home key and subordinate key through three thematic functions: main theme, transition, and subordinate theme.

# Core Definition
The exposition presents most of the melodic-motivic material used throughout the entire movement and establishes its fundamental tonal conflict. It contains three principal **thematic functions**: main theme (initiating), transition (medial), and subordinate theme (concluding). The exposition is almost always repeated (Caplin, Ch. 9, p. 285).

# Prerequisites
- **Sonata form overview** -- The exposition is the first of three required sections

# Key Properties
1. Three principal thematic functions: main theme, transition, subordinate theme
2. Begins in the home key, modulates to and confirms the subordinate key
3. Almost always repeated (with repeat signs or written out)
4. May contain a closing section (codettas) after the subordinate theme
5. May end with a retransition leading back to the home key for the repeat

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Home-key cadence (usually PAC) marks the end of the main theme
2. Subordinate-key HC and standing on the dominant mark the end of a modulating transition
3. SK: PAC marks the end of a subordinate theme
4. Additional SK: PACs mark additional subordinate themes
5. Closing section (codettas) follows the subordinate theme group
6. Retransition leads back to the home key

# Context & Application
Most landmarks of the exposition are cadences defining the *end* of thematic units. The beginning of units may be marked by salient melodic-motivic material, but such markers are a less reliable source of formal articulation than cadences. The exposition defines the standard of tight-knit organization (via the main theme) against which all other sections can be compared.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 287-292): Beethoven, Op. 2, No. 1, i -- complete exposition with sentence main theme, modulating transition, and subordinate theme with closing section.
**Example 2** (p. 293): Mozart, K. 283, ii -- slow-movement exposition for practice analysis.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Identifying thematic beginnings rather than cadential endings as the primary landmarks
  **Correction**: Cadences defining thematic closure are more reliable landmarks than melodic beginnings

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming the transition must be roughly one-third of the exposition
  **Clarification**: The transition is frequently quite short, often less than eight measures
