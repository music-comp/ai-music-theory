# Quick Definition
In classical form, "contrast" usually entails formal complication and increased emotionality -- a "middle function" at any level is typically more intense than a beginning function. The main exception is the trio of minuet/trio form, which brings simplification rather than intensification.

# Core Definition
The concept of "contrast" in classical form usually entails a sense of formal complication and increased emotionality. As a general rule, a "middle function" at any level of structure is more intense than a beginning function (e.g., continuation vs. presentation, development vs. exposition, interior theme vs. main theme, rondo couplet vs. refrain). The main exception to this principle is the trio of minuet/trio form (Caplin, pp. 650-651).

# Key Properties
1. "Contrast" typically means complexity and emotional intensification
2. Applies at all hierarchical levels: idea, phrase, theme, section
3. Middle functions are more intense than beginning functions
4. Examples: continuation > presentation, development > exposition
5. Main exception: trio brings simplification, not intensification

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. In analyzing any "contrasting" section, expect greater complexity
2. Note when a "middle" element is simpler than its surrounding "beginning" elements -- this is exceptional

# Context & Application
This principle illuminates the aesthetic logic of classical form: contrast serves to heighten drama and complexity. Understanding the trio as the exception highlights its unique aesthetic role.
