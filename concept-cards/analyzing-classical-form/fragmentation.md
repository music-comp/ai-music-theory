# Quick Definition
The most typical sign of continuation function: the immediate breaking down of the 2-m. unit size (established in the presentation) into shorter segments, typically 1-m. units repeated. Fragmentation concerns unit length exclusively, regardless of melodic content.

# Core Definition
The most typical sign of continuation function is the immediate breaking down of the 2-m. unit size (established in the presentation) into smaller segments. This process of shortening the units is termed fragmentation (p. 70). The process of fragmentation exclusively concerns the length of the musical units regardless of how the melodic content of the fragments relates to the preceding material. Fragments may contain motives from the b.i. or entirely new material (pp. 73-74).

# Prerequisites
- **Continuation phrase** — Fragmentation characterizes continuation function
- **Basic idea** — Fragmentation shortens the 2-m. unit size established by the b.i.

# Key Properties
1. Shortening of grouping units (2-m. to 1-m., then possibly to half-m.)
2. Most typical sign of continuation function
3. Concerns unit LENGTH exclusively, not melodic content
4. Fragments often defined through repetition (m. 5 repeated in m. 6)
5. May contain b.i.-derived motives or entirely new material
6. May be delayed (continuation may start with 2-m. units before fragmenting)
7. Fragmentation can bypass 1-m. stage directly to half-m. units

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Compare unit sizes in continuation with the 2-m. units of the presentation
2. Look for units shorter than 2 m. (typically 1 m. or half m.)
3. Check if shorter units are defined through repetition
4. Note: melodic content is irrelevant to identifying fragmentation

# Context & Application
Fragmentation relates exclusively to the "packaging" (grouping structure) of the material, not its content. Think of cereal boxes: box size (grouping) is independent of contents (melodic material). Fragmentation often coincides with liquidation but the two are distinct processes.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 56, Ex. 2.1): Beethoven, Op. 2, No. 1 -- 1-m. fragment at m. 5 repeated at m. 6.

**Example 2** (p. 71, Ex. 2.16): Mozart, K. 311, iii -- further fragmentation into half-m. units at m. 7.

**Example 3** (p. 72, Ex. 2.13): Beethoven, Op. 14, No. 2 -- delayed fragmentation; cont. begins with 2-m. unit, then bypasses 1-m. stage to half-m.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Identifying fragmentation based on melodic content changes
  **Correction**: Fragmentation is about unit length, not melodic content

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking fragments must derive from the b.i.
  **Clarification**: Fragments may contain b.i. motives or entirely new material; only length matters
