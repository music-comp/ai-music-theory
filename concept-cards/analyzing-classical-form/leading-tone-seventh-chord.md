# Quick Definition
A seventh chord built on the leading tone (VII7) that has dominant function when resolving to tonic, but loses this function in sequential contexts when progressing to non-tonic harmonies.

# Formal Definition
The leading-tone diminished triad in first inversion (VII6) and the leading-tone seventh chord (VII7 and its three inversions) have dominant function when they resolve to tonic harmony. These harmonies are not considered dominants when, in some sequential situations, they progress to nontonic harmonies, such as III.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Forms of the leading-tone seventh:
- **VII7**: Root position
- **VII6/5, VII4/3, VII4/2**: Inversions
- **vii°7**: Fully-diminished seventh (common in minor)
- **viiø7**: Half-diminished seventh (diatonic in major)

Function:
- Has dominant function when resolving to tonic
- Loses dominant function in sequential contexts (e.g., VII-III)
- Can substitute for V in certain progressions

The key distinction is resolution: the leading tone must resolve to tonic for the chord to express dominant function.

# Musical Context/Application
The leading-tone seventh chord:
- Functions as dominant when resolving to I
- Appears in prolongational progressions as subordinate harmony
- May appear in sequential progressions without dominant function
- The fully-diminished form (°7) is particularly associated with V/V function (VII7/V)

When VII progresses to III or other non-tonic harmonies in sequences, it becomes a pattern element rather than a functional dominant.

# Common Confusions
- Assuming VII always has dominant function (context-dependent)
- Treating VII in sequences as functional dominant
- Confusing VII7 with VII7/V (different functions: dominant vs. pre-dominant)
- Not recognizing the different seventh chord qualities (fully-diminished vs. half-diminished)
