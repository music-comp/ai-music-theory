# Quick Definition
If the exposition closes with a retransition leading back to the home key, the recapitulation may bring a similar passage leading instead to the subdominant (or occasionally supertonic) region for the beginning of the coda.

# Core Definition
If the exposition closes with a retransition leading back to the home key (for the repeat of the exposition), the recapitulation may conclude with a similar passage. However, this "retransition" no longer fulfills its nominal function of returning to the home key. Instead, it leads to the subdominant region (or occasionally the supertonic), functioning more as a "transition" to the coda. The coda typically starts somewhere in this passage -- at the point where the music departs from the exposition's path. Occasionally the passage moves toward the supertonic rather than the subdominant, redirecting in the measure following the initial subdominant approach.

# Prerequisites
- Knowledge of closing section treatment in the recapitulation
- Understanding of the coda as an after-the-end function

# Key Properties
- Parallels the exposition's retransition but with different harmonic destination
- Leads to subdominant (IV) or occasionally supertonic (ii) region
- Does NOT return to the home key (no longer true retransition function)
- Functions as a transition to the coda
- Coda starts somewhere within this passage
- The point of departure from the exposition's path marks the coda's start

# Construction / Recognition
Compare the closing section of the recapitulation with the exposition's. If the exposition ends with a retransition-like passage leading back to the tonic (for the repeat), check whether the recapitulation's version of this passage redirects toward the subdominant or supertonic. The moment of harmonic divergence from the exposition marks the beginning of the coda.

# Context & Application
This technique creates a smooth and often imperceptible transition between recapitulation and coda. The retransition-like material provides continuity with the exposition's closing gestures while the harmonic redirection signals the beginning of new formal territory. The subdominant destination is particularly appropriate because it moves the harmony away from the tonic without undermining the home-key stability, and it sets up harmonic motion that can eventually return to the tonic for the coda's own cadential goals.

# Examples
- Mozart, Symphony No. 36 ("Linz"), K. 425, i: recapitulation ends with reference to the exposition's retransition (mm. 264ff.), beginning to move to the subdominant at m. 267 but redirecting toward the supertonic at m. 268; m. 269 marks the start of the coda
- General pattern: exposition retransition leads back to I; recapitulation version leads to IV or ii for the coda

# Common Errors
- Treating the recapitulation's retransition-like passage as a true retransition
- Failing to recognize that the subdominant destination marks the onset of the coda

# Common Confusions
- This passage is NOT a true retransition -- it does not return to the home key but instead leads away from it toward the subdominant, functioning as a harmonic bridge to the coda
