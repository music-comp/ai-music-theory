# Quick Definition
Classical slow introductions unite two seemingly incompatible affective worlds: the stately, heraldic, and solemn (fanfares, dotted rhythms, forte) with the anticipatory, uncertain, and unstable (discontinuities, chromaticism, minor modality).

# Core Definition
"A notable characteristic of slow introductions in the classical style is the special way in which they unite the expression of two seemingly incompatible affective worlds" (p. 577). The stately realm is expressed through "slow tempo, fanfare gestures, tutti textures, dotted rhythms, and an initial forte dynamic." The anticipatory realm is created by "marked discontinuities in such dimensions as melody, rhythm, texture, and dynamics, instabilities of harmonic progression, minor modality, and chromaticism (at times extreme)." The stately qualities derive from the Baroque "French overture" style, but the anticipatory gestures are a classical innovation.

# Key Properties
1. **Stately/solemn**: slow tempo, fanfare gestures, tutti, dotted rhythms, forte
2. **Anticipatory/uncertain**: discontinuities, harmonic instability, minor mode, chromaticism
3. Some introductions lack the stately character but almost always contain uncertainty
4. French overture style is the Baroque antecedent (stately aspects only)
5. Classical innovation: the anticipatory, premonitory element

# Context & Application
The combination of solemnity and uncertainty makes the slow introduction a dramatically charged preparation for the exposition. The stately elements establish formal weight; the uncertain elements generate expectation and tension that the exposition's arrival resolves.

# Examples
**Example 16.3** (p. 579): Haydn, Symphony No. 104 — fortissimo fanfare (stately) but fermatas prohibit rhythmic continuity, lack of harmonic third raises modal doubt (uncertain); continuation's leading tone dangles at phrase ends (hesitant).
