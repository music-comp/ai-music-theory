# Quick Definition
Many expositions (perhaps the majority) contain multiple subordinate themes, each ending with a PAC in the subordinate key -- a subordinate-theme group. Typically two themes, but large expositions may have three or four. Different loosening devices are usually distributed among the themes, and one theme may be notably tighter than others.

# Core Definition
Many classical sonata expositions contain multiple subordinate themes, each ending with a **PAC in the subordinate key**, forming a subordinate-theme group. Typically two themes, but many large expositions contain three or four. Multiple themes are a principal means of enlarging the formal dimensions. A functionally neutral numbering system ("first," "second") labels themes by order of appearance. Different loosening devices are distributed among themes. Frequently, one theme is significantly more tightly knit -- often the first (especially with Mozart), sometimes between two looser themes for contrast, or the final theme for a "closing" quality. Boundary processes (elision, accompanimental overlap) maintain continuity between themes (Caplin, Ch. 12, pp. 403-409).

# Key Properties
1. Multiple themes, each ending with PAC in subordinate key
2. Typically two; large expositions may have three or four
3. Numbered neutrally ("first," "second") by order of appearance
4. Different loosening devices distributed among themes
5. One theme often notably tighter (first position common with Mozart)
6. Boundary processes (elision, overlap) maintain continuity
7. Closing section follows only the final theme of the group
