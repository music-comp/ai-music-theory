# Quick Definition
The coda allows the composer to create a different dynamic trajectory for the movement's ending than what the recapitulation's closing section would provide, either through enormous crescendos (Beethoven's 3rd and 7th Symphonies) or progressive dissolution (Coriolanus overture).

# Core Definition
"If a sonata-form movement ends with the closing section of the recapitulation, the final dynamic curve of the movement normally conforms to that established by the closing section of the exposition" (p. 563). When the exposition's dynamic ending is "not suitable for ending the complete movement," the coda shapes a new curve. Progressive dynamics lead to "powerful climax," while recessive dynamics lead to "complete dissolution of the texture."

# Key Properties
1. Without coda, movement's ending dynamic matches exposition's closing section
2. Coda enables a completely different dynamic trajectory
3. Progressive: crescendo to climax (Beethoven's 3rd, 7th Symphonies)
4. Recessive: diminuendo to dissolution (Coriolanus overture)

# Examples
**Example 15.9** (p. 564): Mozart, Piano Sonata in C minor, K. 457, i — exposition/recapitulation close in forte; coda continues forte to PAC (m. 176), then shifts to piano, pianissimo at final codetta — completely different dynamic curve.
