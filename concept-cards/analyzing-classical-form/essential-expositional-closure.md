# Quick Definition
Hepokoski and Darcy term the first SK: PAC the "essential expositional closure" (EEC), arguing it has greatest structural import. Caplin disagrees: from a purely syntactical perspective, all PACs are equally strong and fulfill the same requirements for thematic closure. Rhetorical strength varies ad hoc and cannot be generalized.

# Core Definition
The concept of multiple PACs within a subordinate-theme group raises the question of their relative importance. Hepokoski and Darcy suggest the **first SK: PAC** is of greatest structural import, terming it the "essential expositional closure" (EEC). Other theorists focus on the final, often highly dramatic cadential arrival. Caplin's position: from a purely **syntactical** point of view, all PACs are equally strong in fulfilling thematic closure requirements. From a **rhetorical** point of view, cadences display a range of expression (some weak, some dramatic), but there is no way to generalize about relative strength. Each subordinate-theme group should be analyzed on its own terms (Caplin, Ch. 12, pp. 409-410).

# Key Properties
1. Hepokoski-Darcy: first SK: PAC is structurally most important ("EEC")
2. Alternative view: final PAC (after dramatic ECP) is most important
3. Caplin: syntactically, ALL PACs are equally strong
4. Caplin: rhetorically, strength varies but cannot be generalized
5. Recommendation: analyze each group ad hoc for rhetorical differences
6. All PACs successfully close their individual subordinate themes

# Context & Application
This is a significant theoretical disagreement. Students should understand that Caplin does not adopt the EEC concept. In Caplin's framework, every PAC in the subordinate-theme group has equal formal weight, though each may project different rhetorical character.
