# Quick Definition
Melodic motion that departs from and returns to the same pitch, typically creating neighboring chords when harmonized, and often appearing within prolongational progressions where the prolonged harmony remains in the same position.

# Formal Definition
Neighbor motion refers to melodic movement that departs from a chord tone and returns to the same pitch. In the context of harmonic prolongation, neighboring motion is usually (but not necessarily) present in one or more of the voices when the prolonged harmony remains in the same position from beginning to end of the progression.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Characteristics of neighbor motion:
- Departs from and returns to the same pitch
- Creates neighbor tone melodic figures (upper or lower)
- When harmonized, creates neighboring chords
- Associated with prolonged harmony remaining in same position

Upper vs. lower neighbors:
- Upper neighbor: chord tone - step up - back to chord tone
- Lower neighbor: chord tone - step down - back to chord tone
- May be diatonic or chromatic

Important: The distinction between neighboring and passing chords is based on the position of the prolonged harmony, not on literal neighbor-tone motion in the voices.

# Musical Context/Application
Neighbor motion:
- Creates embellishment without changing harmonic position
- Is central to prolongational progressions
- Often appears in multiple voices simultaneously
- May not be literally present even when the chord is "neighboring"

The harmonic concept of "neighboring chord" refers to the position of the prolonged harmony (same position throughout), regardless of whether literal neighbor tones appear.

# Common Confusions
- Assuming neighboring chords must have literal neighbor-tone motion (not required)
- Confusing the melodic concept with the harmonic concept
- Not recognizing that the distinction is about harmonic position, not voice motion
- Treating all step-away-and-back motion as neighbor motion (context matters)
