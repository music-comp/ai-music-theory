# Quick Definition
A harmony that participates in prolonging another harmony because they share the same fundamental function and have two chord tones in common.

# Formal Definition
Some harmonies can participate in prolonging a given harmony because they express the same fundamental function as the prolonged harmony. In such cases, the original harmony and substitute harmony have two chord tones in common, which largely accounts for their functional similarity.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Common substitute relationships:
- **VI substitutes for I**: Share scale degrees 1 and 3
- **II substitutes for IV**: Share scale degrees 4 and 6
- **IV substitutes for II**: Share scale degrees 4 and 6

Direction of substitution:
- Most commonly, the substitute chord root lies a third **below** the original
- Less commonly, the root lies a third **above** the original
- When above, the substitute arises from passing motion with elimination of root

Passing chords may be introduced between original and substitute harmonies to create more complex prolongations.

# Musical Context/Application
Substitute harmonies:
- Extend the function of the original harmony
- Create variety within prolongational progressions
- The shared common tones explain their functional equivalence
- May combine with passing chords for complex prolongations

Note: In some examples, the chord following the substitute (e.g., II7 after VI, or V after II) may not be part of the tonic prolongation - context determines the boundaries of the prolongation.

# Common Confusions
- Assuming all VI chords are tonic substitutes (context matters)
- Thinking substitute harmonies are "weaker" than original harmonies
- Not recognizing that the harmony following a substitute may begin a new progression
- Confusing substitution (based on common tones) with neighboring or passing motion
