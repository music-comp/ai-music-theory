# Quick Definition
A harmony that participates in prolonging another harmony because both share the same fundamental function and have two chord tones in common. Most commonly, the substitute root lies a third below the original (e.g., VI substituting for I).

# Core Definition
Some harmonies can participate in prolonging a given harmony because they express the same fundamental function as the prolonged harmony. In such cases, the original harmony and substitute harmony have two chord tones in common, which largely accounts for their functional similarity (p. 35). Most commonly, the root of the substitute lies a third below the original. In some situations, a chord a third above participates in the prolongation, understood to arise from passing motion with simultaneous elimination of the root (p. 36).

# Prerequisites
- **Prolongational progression** — Substitute harmonies are one technique of prolongation

# Key Properties
1. Substitute shares the same fundamental function as the prolonged harmony
2. Two chord tones in common between original and substitute
3. Most common: substitute root a third below (VI for I, II for IV)
4. Less common: substitute root a third above (arising from passing motion with root elimination)
5. Passing chords may be introduced between original and substitute for complex prolongations
6. The harmony following the substitute may not be part of the prolongation

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify a Substitute Harmony:
1. Identify two harmonies sharing the same function
2. Verify they share two common tones
3. Check if the substitute root lies a third below or above the original
4. Determine the boundaries of the prolongation (subsequent harmonies may begin a new progression)

# Context & Application
Substitute harmonies extend the function of the original harmony and create variety within prolongational progressions. VI substituting for I (sharing scale degrees 1 and 3) is the most common example, occurring notably in deceptive resolutions. The shared common tones explain their functional equivalence.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the harmony following a substitute is always part of the same prolongation
  **Correction**: Context determines boundaries; the next chord may begin a new progression

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming all VI chords are tonic substitutes
  **Clarification**: Context matters; VI is a tonic substitute in prolongational contexts but may function differently in sequential contexts
- **Confusion**: Confusing substitution (based on common tones and shared function) with neighboring or passing motion
  **Clarification**: Substitution involves a chord of the same function, not merely an embellishing chord
