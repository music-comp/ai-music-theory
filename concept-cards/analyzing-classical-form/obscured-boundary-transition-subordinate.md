# Quick Definition
In some expositions (especially Haydn's), the boundary between transition and subordinate theme is obscured because the transition lacks a concluding function, the subordinate theme lacks an initiating function, or both. In extreme cases, the two functions fuse into a single thematic unit, annotated with a slash (transition/subordinate theme).

# Core Definition
Most sonata-form expositions (especially by Mozart and Beethoven) feature unmistakable demarcation between transition and subordinate theme: the transition clearly ends with an HC (or dominant arrival), the subordinate theme begins with an obvious initiating function, and a medial caesura aids articulation. In some expositions (especially Haydn's), this boundary is obscured. Three situations create obscured boundaries: (1) the transition lacks a concluding function; (2) the subordinate theme lacks an initiating function; (3) both functions are missing. In the first two cases, a clear boundary may still be recognized if marked contrasts in melodic material, texture, and rhythmic activity are present. When both are missing, the concept of form-functional fusion comes into play: a single thematic unit embraces both transition and subordinate-theme functions. In cases of conflict between harmonic considerations and melodic/textural ones, preference should normally be given to the powerful, form-defining role of harmony (Caplin, Ch. 12, pp. 422-429).

# Key Properties
1. Three situations: transition lacks ending, theme lacks beginning, or both
2. Contrasts in melody, texture, and rhythm can still mark boundaries
3. When all markers absent, form-functional fusion results
4. Fusion annotated with slash: transition/subordinate theme
5. Harmonic considerations take priority over melodic/textural ones
6. Most common in Haydn; rare in Mozart and Beethoven

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Check for clear HC or dominant arrival ending the transition
2. Check for clear initiating function beginning the subordinate theme
3. Look for contrasts in melody, texture, rhythm, and dynamics
4. If neither ending nor beginning is clear, consider fusion
5. Use harmonic criteria (root-position tonic prolongation in SK) to locate the most likely boundary
6. Accept that in some cases no precise boundary can be drawn

# Context & Application
When encountering form-functional fusion, standard annotation places both labels at the beginning of the thematic unit: "transition/subordinate theme" with the slash indicating fusion. This acknowledges that determining where one function ends and the other begins is not possible. The concept is particularly important for Haydn analysis but occasionally applies to Mozart and Beethoven as well.

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 423-424): Beethoven, "Appassionata" Op. 57, iii, mm. 64-96 -- Transition lacks clear ending; harmonic considerations (root-position tonic at m. 72) suggest subordinate-theme beginning, though melodic/textural change doesn't occur until m. 76. Harmonic criterion takes priority.
**Example 2** (pp. 427-428): Haydn, Symphony No. 93, i, mm. 46-77 -- Transition and subordinate-theme functions fuse into a single unit; annotated as "transition/subordinate theme."
**Example 3** (pp. 428-429): Mozart, Violin Sonata K. 380, i, mm. 11-27 -- Neither transition ending nor subordinate-theme beginning can be identified; the unit begins as transition and ends as subordinate theme; full fusion.
