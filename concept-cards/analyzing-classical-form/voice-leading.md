# Quick Definition
The horizontal movement of individual melodic lines as they progress from one chord to another, with proper voice leading avoiding parallel fifths and octaves while creating smooth melodic connections.

# Formal Definition
Voice leading refers to the melodic motion of individual voices as they move through harmonic progressions. Proper voice leading involves smooth melodic connections between chords while avoiding problematic parallel motion (fifths and octaves). Voice-leading considerations often determine chord choices and inversions within progressions.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Voice-leading principles:
- Avoid parallel fifths and octaves between any two voices
- Prefer stepwise motion where possible
- Resolve tendency tones appropriately (leading tone to tonic, seventh down)
- Common tones may be retained

Voice-leading problems and solutions in sequences:
- Descending-second sequence: Use first-inversion chords to eliminate fifths against bass
- Ascending-second sequence: Use 5-6 pattern to break up parallel fifths

The passing chord in Example 1.9c arises entirely from counterpoint, with incorrect seventh resolution ("the so-called seventh is doubled and incorrectly resolved"), thus receiving a chord quality label (m7) rather than a Roman numeral.

# Musical Context/Application
Voice leading affects:
- Chord choice within progressions
- Inversion decisions
- Sequential pattern design
- The identification of contrapuntal vs. harmonic events

Some chords arise "primarily from contrapuntal processes and only minimally from harmonic ones" - these receive special analytical notation (passing or neighboring labels).

# Examples
Example 1.21 (p. 45): "Sequential progressions by descending seconds... pose a potential problem of voice leading. If the chords were placed in root position, then parallel fifths could easily arise."

Example 1.22 (pp. 45-46): The ascending-second sequence uses "a 5-6 pattern formed by one of the upper voices against the bass" to avoid parallel fifths.

Example 1.9c (p. 34): A passing chord "arises entirely out of the counterpoint and thus should not be analyzed as a II7 harmony."

# Common Confusions
- Ignoring voice-leading origins of certain chords (treating all as fully harmonic)
- Not recognizing voice-leading solutions in sequences
- Analyzing contrapuntal passing chords with Roman numerals
- Overlooking the 5-6 pattern in ascending sequences
