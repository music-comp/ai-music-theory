# Quick Definition
The horizontal movement of individual melodic lines as they progress from one chord to another, with proper voice leading avoiding parallel fifths and octaves while creating smooth melodic connections.

# Core Definition
Voice leading refers to the melodic motion of individual voices as they move through harmonic progressions. It involves smooth melodic connections between chords while avoiding parallel perfect fifths and octaves. Voice-leading considerations often determine chord choices and inversions, particularly in sequential progressions. Some chords arise "primarily from contrapuntal processes and only minimally from harmonic ones" (p. 35), revealing voice leading as a chord-generating force in its own right.

# Prerequisites
- **Scale degree** — Voice leading operates within the diatonic (and chromatic) scale

# Key Properties
1. Avoid parallel perfect fifths and octaves between any two voices
2. Prefer stepwise motion where possible
3. Resolve tendency tones (leading tone to tonic, seventh downward)
4. Common tones may be retained in the same voice
5. Voice leading determines chord choice in sequences (inversions, 5-6, 7-6)
6. Some chords arise from voice leading rather than harmonic function

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Track the motion of each individual voice from chord to chord
2. Check for parallel perfect fifths and octaves between any voice pair
3. Verify resolution of tendency tones (leading tone, chordal sevenths)
4. Assess whether any chord arises primarily from voice-leading motion

# Context & Application
Voice leading affects chord choice within progressions, inversion decisions, sequential pattern design, and the identification of contrapuntal vs. harmonic events. In sequences, voice-leading problems (parallel fifths) drive the use of specific techniques: first-inversion chords for descending seconds, 5-6 pattern for ascending seconds. When a chord's voice leading reveals contrapuntal origin (doubled and incorrectly resolved seventh), it should receive quality symbols rather than Roman numerals.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 45): "If the chords were placed in root position, then parallel fifths could easily arise" -- voice leading drives the choice of first-inversion chords in descending-second sequences.

**Example 2** (pp. 45-46): The ascending-second sequence uses "a 5-6 pattern formed by one of the upper voices against the bass" to avoid parallel fifths.

**Example 3** (p. 34): Example 1.9c shows a passing chord that "arises entirely out of the counterpoint and thus should not be analyzed as a II7 harmony."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Ignoring voice-leading origins of certain chords (treating all as harmonic)
  **Correction**: Some chords arise from voice-leading motion and should be analyzed as contrapuntal events

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming voice-leading rules are absolute in all contexts
  **Clarification**: Contrapuntally derived chords may violate standard voice-leading rules (e.g., doubled sevenths) because they arise from melodic patterns, not harmonic syntax
