# Quick Definition
Dominant function creates tension and drives toward resolution to tonic, primarily expressed by V and VII chords. The cadential six-four (I in second inversion) functions as an embellishment of dominant harmony, not as tonic.

# Core Definition
Dominant function is most often represented by the major triad or the major-minor seventh chord built on the fifth scale degree. The leading-tone diminished triad in first inversion (VII6) and the leading-tone seventh chord (VII7 and its inversions) also have dominant function when they resolve to tonic harmony. These harmonies are not considered dominants when, in some sequential situations, they progress to nontonic harmonies such as III. In certain limited contexts, the harmony built on the third degree (III) can function as a substitute for the dominant (as in III6-I). In many cadential situations, tonic harmony in second inversion (I6/4) functions as an embellishment of dominant harmony -- the cadential six-four (p. 31).

# Key Properties
1. Primary chord: V (major triad or major-minor seventh on scale degree 5)
2. VII6 and VII7 have dominant function when resolving to tonic
3. VII chords lose dominant function in sequential contexts (e.g., progressing to III)
4. III can function as dominant substitute in limited contexts (III6-I)
5. The cadential six-four (I6/4) embellishes dominant in root position, notated as V(6/4-5/3)
6. Cadential dominant must appear and remain in root position to acquire cadential status

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify Dominant Function:
1. Look for V, V7, VII6, or VII7 chords
2. Confirm resolution to tonic (otherwise may be sequential/nonfunctional)
3. Check for cadential six-four embellishment over scale degree 5
4. In cadential contexts, verify the dominant is in root position

# Context & Application
Dominant function appears in cadential progressions (root-position V is essential), prolongational progressions (V as neighboring or passing chord), half-cadential goals (V triad, not seventh, as ultimate harmony), and deceptive progressions (V resolves to VI). The cadential six-four has dominant function -- this is a key analytical point.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating any V-I motion as cadential regardless of inversion
  **Correction**: The dominant must first appear and remain in root position for cadential status

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Treating cadential six-four as tonic function
  **Clarification**: I6/4 in cadential contexts functions as a dominant embellishment, not tonic
- **Confusion**: Assuming VII always has dominant function
  **Clarification**: In sequential contexts (e.g., VII progressing to III), VII loses its dominant function
