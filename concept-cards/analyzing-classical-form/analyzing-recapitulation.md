# Quick Definition
The methodology for analyzing a recapitulation proceeds by systematic bar-by-bar comparison with the exposition, identifying structural and ornamental changes and explaining why they were made.

# Core Definition
Because the recapitulation is largely modeled on the exposition, the most appropriate analytical methodology is one of comparison. The analyst compares the recapitulation bar by bar with the exposition, identifying any changes. When making this comparison, it is useful to recognize two basic types of changes: structural changes (involving harmonic-tonal organization, melodic-motivic material, grouping structure, and formal functions) and ornamental changes (involving dynamics, instrumentation, register, texture, accompanimental figuration, and melodic embellishments). The analyst should also ask and try to answer why these changes were made.

# Prerequisites
- Familiarity with recapitulation harmonic-tonal organization and the principle of remaining in the home key
- Understanding of ornamental versus structural changes

# Key Properties
- Structural changes are motivated by fundamental differences in function between recapitulation and exposition, or by development section content
- Ornamental changes are motivated by aesthetic concerns for variety and particular expressive values
- Most analysis focuses on structural changes since they most directly affect formal organization
- Ornamental changes are observed and explained as they arise in the examples

# Construction / Recognition
When analyzing a recapitulation: (1) compare it bar by bar with the exposition, (2) identify each change, (3) classify changes as structural or ornamental, (4) explain why each change was made in terms of the recapitulation's different formal functions, and (5) consider whether development section content motivated any deletions.

# Context & Application
This comparative methodology is the central analytical approach for Chapter 14 of Caplin's text. Structural changes typically involve deletions of thematic restatements, tonal adjustments to remain in the home key, compressions or fusions of formal units, and expansions of cadential areas. Ornamental changes may enhance the intensity of cadential arrivals without altering underlying structure.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, i: subordinate theme transposed to home key with ornamental changes in cadential phrase (added sixteenth notes, chromatic pre-dominant prolongation) creating greater intensity
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 576, i: main theme repeat deleted, transition compressed, flat-side tonicizations added for variety

# Common Errors
- Failing to compare bar by bar with the exposition, missing subtle changes
- Confusing ornamental changes with structural changes
- Assuming all changes are arbitrary rather than functionally motivated

# Common Confusions
- Structural changes are NOT the same as ornamental changes -- structural changes alter harmonic-tonal organization, melodic-motivic material, grouping structure, or formal functions, while ornamental changes affect surface parameters only
- Changes in the recapitulation are NOT random -- they are motivated either by the different formal functions of the recapitulation or by the content of the development section
