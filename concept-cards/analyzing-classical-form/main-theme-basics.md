# Quick Definition
The main theme presents the principal melodic-motivic material of the sonata form in a stable tonal and phrase-structural context, establishing the movement's standard of tight-knit organization. It always begins and ends in the home key, closing with a PAC, HC, or (rarely) IAC.

# Core Definition
The main theme is the **initiating** thematic function of the exposition, presenting the principal melodic-motivic material in a stable tonal and phrase-structural context. Tonal stability is achieved by beginning and ending in the home key and always closing with one of the three cadence types (most often PAC, sometimes HC especially in minor, rarely IAC). Phrase-structural stability is achieved by tight-knit organization relative to the more loosely organized transition and subordinate theme. The sentence form is particularly suitable for opening a sonata exposition; the period is used less often. Ternary forms are uncommon; small binary is rare (Caplin, Ch. 10, pp. 308-309).

# Key Properties
1. Presents principal melodic-motivic material for the entire movement
2. Begins and ends in home key; always closes with cadence (PAC, HC, or IAC)
3. Establishes movement's standard of tight-knit organization
4. Sentence favored for first movements; period for finales; no preference for slow movements
5. May be followed by closing section (after PAC) or standing on the dominant (after HC)
6. May be preceded by thematic introduction
7. Can be conventional or nonconventional in form

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Locate the opening thematic material in the home key
2. Identify the cadence type closing the theme
3. Assess the theme type (sentence, period, hybrid, nonconventional)
4. Note the tight-knit qualities as reference for comparison with later sections

# Context & Application
Main themes can be conventional (sentence, period, hybrid, small ternary) or nonconventional. First-movement forms particularly favor nonconventional organization. Even nonconventional main themes maintain relative tight-knit organization compared to subsequent thematic functions. Phrase-structural deviations (extensions, expansions) are more common in first-movement main themes than in finale main themes.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 287): Beethoven, Op. 2, No. 1, i, mm. 1-8 -- "Perfect" sentence as main theme.
**Example 2** (p. 320): Mozart, K. 543, i, mm. 26-71 -- Main-theme group with compound period + hybrid.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the sentence is always the correct form for a main theme
  **Correction**: Many main themes, especially in first movements, are nonconventional

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking a main theme must be "aggressive" or "masculine"
  **Clarification**: Main themes frequently exhibit hesitancy and discontinuity; lyrical character is also common
