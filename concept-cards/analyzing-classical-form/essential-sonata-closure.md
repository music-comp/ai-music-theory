# Quick Definition
The PAC that closes the subordinate theme in the recapitulation, confirming the home key and resolving the fundamental tonal opposition established when the exposition confirmed the subordinate key -- the most crucial cadential moment in the entire sonata form.

# Core Definition
The recapitulation resolves the fundamental conflict of tonalities when, following the sonata principle, the subordinate theme is transposed back into the home key. The PAC that closes the subordinate theme in the recapitulation thus confirms the home key and resolves the tonal opposition established in the exposition. This is the most powerful cadential emphasis in the entire form: in the exposition, the corresponding PAC confirmed the subordinate key (the "rival" tonality); in the recapitulation, it confirms the home key (the resolution of that rivalry). The cadential area of the subordinate theme may undergo greater expansion in the recapitulation than in the exposition, ensuring that the most rhetorically powerful cadential emphasis confirms the home key.

# Key Properties
- PAC closing the subordinate theme in the recapitulation
- Confirms the home key, resolving tonal opposition from the exposition
- Most powerful cadential emphasis in the entire form
- Completes the sonata's fundamental tonal narrative
- Cadential area may be more expanded than in the exposition
- Central to theories emphasizing teleological structure of sonata form

# Construction / Recognition
Locate the PAC that closes the subordinate theme group in the recapitulation. This cadence should be in the home key (not the subordinate key as in the exposition). Compare it with the corresponding cadence in the exposition: it may be more elaborate, with expanded cadential progressions, additional deceptive or evaded cadences, or more chromatic pre-dominant preparation.

# Context & Application
The concept of essential sonata closure is central to understanding sonata form as a teleological process -- the movement's entire trajectory aims toward this cadential goal. Hepokoski and Darcy have made this concept central to their theory of sonata form. Caplin's treatment emphasizes how the greater expansion of the recapitulation's cadential area ensures that the home key receives the most powerful treatment in the movement, with the subordinate theme's loose organization now serving to endow the home key with the greatest power of expression.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, i: subordinate theme cadential phrase (mm. 67-71) features ornamental changes creating greater intensity, with pre-dominant prolonged by additional harmonies and added sixteenth notes
- General pattern: recapitulation's subordinate theme PAC receives more elaborate cadential preparation than exposition's

# Common Errors
- Failing to recognize the essential sonata closure as the movement's primary cadential goal
- Analyzing the recapitulation's subordinate theme PAC as equivalent to the exposition's rather than as the resolution of the tonal conflict

# Common Confusions
- The resolution of dominant to tonic at the START of the recapitulation is NOT a cadence -- the development's dominant is an "ultimate" dominant that resolves to initiate a new section, not to close one; the essential sonata closure occurs much later, at the PAC closing the subordinate theme
