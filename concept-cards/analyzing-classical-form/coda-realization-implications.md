# Quick Definition
The coda gives the composer the last opportunity to realize implications from earlier sections, often resolving a "problem" or "disturbance" from the main theme that was not resolved elsewhere, such as eliminating a modal shift or restoring a deleted cadence.

# Core Definition
"Many of the compositional processes initiated in a work imply particular modes of continuation. Some of these implications are realized immediately, others are deferred until later in the movement, and still others may never be realized at all" (p. 565). "The coda gives the composer the last opportunity of realizing an earlier implication, often one that would not have found an appropriate realization in other sections." Implications "usually arise in relation to the main theme, particularly a 'problem' or 'disturbance' that is not resolved until the coda."

# Key Properties
1. Last opportunity to realize deferred implications
2. Implications typically arise from main theme
3. Often a "problem" or "disturbance" is resolved
4. Modal disturbances (unusual turn to minor) may be expunged
5. Deleted cadences may be restored
6. Pitch implications may be exploited before final resolution

# Examples
**Example 15.10** (p. 565): Mozart, Symphony No. 39, K. 543, ii — main theme's modal shift to minor (mm. 22-25) implied later return without disturbance; coda (m. 144) brings theme without explicit modal shift; also restores PAC deleted in recapitulation.

**Example 15.11** (p. 567): Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C minor, Op. 10, No. 1, ii — main theme's rhythmically intense continuation implied simpler version; coda (mm. 95-102) realizes this with "closing down" character in even note values.
