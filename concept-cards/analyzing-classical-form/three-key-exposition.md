# Quick Definition
A three-key exposition moves through three distinct tonal areas rather than the standard two (home key and subordinate key), with an intermediate key that may be tonicized but not fully confirmed, while the final key receives the essential confirming PAC.

# Core Definition
A three-key exposition occurs when the exposition moves through three distinct tonal areas: home key (HK), an intermediate key, and a final subordinate key (SK). The standard exposition moves from HK to SK; the three-key exposition inserts an intermediate tonal region between them. The intermediate key may be tonicized but not fully confirmed, while the final key receives the confirming PAC. In minor-mode movements, a common scenario involves home key (minor) to relative major (III, often a "false" subordinate key) to minor dominant (v, the true subordinate key).

# Key Properties
1. Three distinct tonal areas instead of standard two
2. Standard: HK to SK; three-key: HK to intermediate key to SK
3. Intermediate key may be tonicized but not fully confirmed
4. Final key receives the essential confirming PAC
5. Common in minor-mode movements: i to III to v
6. Intermediate key may function as "false" subordinate key

# Construction / Recognition
## To Analyze:
1. Identify all distinct tonal areas in the exposition
2. Determine which key receives the essential PAC (this is the true SK)
3. Assess whether intermediate keys are genuinely confirmed or merely tonicized
4. Consider how the three-key plan affects the recapitulation
5. The key with the final PAC determines the fundamental tonal plan

# Context & Application
Three-key expositions complicate the standard binary tonal opposition of sonata form. The analyst must determine which tonal area is the "true" subordinate key based on cadential confirmation. The three-key plan also raises questions about the recapitulation: if three keys appeared in the exposition, how many tonal areas will the recapitulation traverse? This topic receives more detailed treatment in later chapters on the transition and subordinate theme.
