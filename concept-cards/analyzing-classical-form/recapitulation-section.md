# Quick Definition
The third and final required section of sonata form, functioning to resolve the principal tonal and melodic processes left incomplete in earlier sections by restating exposition material in the home key.

# Core Definition
"The recapitulation functions to resolve the principal tonal and melodic processes left incomplete in the earlier sections" and "provides symmetry and balance to the overall form by restating the melodic-motivic material of the exposition" (p. 497). It brings back the three basic thematic units (main theme, transition, subordinate theme) in the same order, but with different formal functions. Unlike the exposition, the recapitulation "normally remains throughout in the home key." The analytical methodology is one of comparison, identifying structural and ornamental changes.

# Prerequisites
- **Exposition** — The material being recapitulated
- **Development section** — The preceding section whose instability is resolved
- **Subordinate key** — The key conflict being resolved

# Key Properties
1. Restates exposition material in roughly the same order
2. Remains throughout in the home key (very rarely does another key receive cadential confirmation)
3. Resolves the tonal conflict between home key and subordinate key
4. Roughly the same length as the exposition (unlike the shorter A' of small ternary)
5. Structural changes involve harmony, melody, grouping, formal function
6. Ornamental changes involve dynamics, instrumentation, register, texture
7. Transition must be adjusted to remain in the home key

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Locate the return of the main theme's basic idea in the home key after the development
2. Compare bar by bar with the exposition
3. Identify structural changes (harmonic-tonal, melodic-motivic, grouping, functional)
4. Identify ornamental changes (dynamics, instrumentation, register, texture)

# Context & Application
The recapitulation fulfills what Hepokoski and Darcy emphasize as a formal rotation: the recurrent recycling of musical materials in a given order. The resolution of V to I at the recapitulation's start is NOT a cadence — it is a "rebeginning," not a conclusion.

# Examples
**Example 14.1** (p. 502): Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 576, i — main theme identical to exposition; repeated theme shifts to flat-side subdominant (mm. 110-12); new model-sequence passage develops material; transition function assumed by main-theme material.

**Example 14.2** (p. 503): Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, i — subordinate theme retains same structure but transposed to home key; ornamental changes in cadential phrase create greater intensity.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Exposition** — Restates its material with modifications
- **Development section** — Resolves the instability generated there
## Enables
- **Coda** — May follow the recapitulation as an optional section
## Contrasts With
- **Exposition** — Same material but resolving rather than establishing tonal conflict

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Hearing an authentic cadence at the V-I resolution that initiates the recapitulation
  **Correction**: This is a "rebeginning," not a cadential close; the dominant at the end of the development is an "ultimate" dominant resolving to initiate a new section

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Expecting the recapitulation to be an exact transposition of the exposition
  **Clarification**: Structural and ornamental changes are the norm, especially in the transition
