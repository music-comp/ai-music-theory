# Quick Definition
A cadence is abandoned when something goes awry with the cadential dominant itself: it is either undermined (placed in or moved to an inversion) or omitted altogether, preventing any authentic cadential closure.

# Core Definition
Two situations typically give rise to cadential abandonment: (1) the cadential dominant is undermined -- placed in first or second inversion, or inverted after initially appearing in root position; (2) the cadential dominant is omitted altogether. In contrast with the evaded cadence (where the dominant is firmly in root position and the problem concerns the absent tonic), the abandoned cadence involves a problem with the dominant itself. In some cases, a specific moment of denied arrival can be identified; in others, the abandonment is recognized without a precise moment, and the cadential progression simply gives way to new material or a different cadential attempt.

# Prerequisites
- **Cadential deviations** — The broader category
- **Cadential progression** — Must understand the role of root-position dominant

# Key Properties
1. Problem is with the cadential DOMINANT (not the tonic)
2. Two causes: dominant undermined (inverted) or dominant omitted
3. Sometimes a specific moment of denied arrival can be located
4. Sometimes no precise moment; the progression simply gives way
5. Rarely used in simple main themes; more common in subordinate themes
6. May result in an HC ending the phrase instead

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Locate a passage where an authentic cadential progression seems to be forming
2. Check the cadential dominant: Is it in root position? Does it stay in root position?
3. If the dominant is inverted or omitted, the cadence is abandoned
4. Note whether the phrase continues to a different cadence type or new material

# Context & Application
Like cadential evasion, cadential abandonment is rarely used in simple main themes and appears more frequently in subordinate themes and development sections. The technique may result in a phrase closing with an HC instead of the originally implied authentic cadence.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 154): Beethoven, Violin Sonata Op. 12/3, i, mm. 1-13 -- ECP-supported continuation; dominant arrives in root position but bass ascends stepwise to invert it to V6/5, rendering resolution noncadential; cadence abandoned; phrase repeats and succeeds with PAC.
**Example 2** (p. 165): Beethoven, Sonata Op. 10/1, ii, mm. 1-8 -- cadential progression forming at m. 5 is abandoned when dominant becomes inverted (IV6-V6-I); phrase ends with HC instead.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Confusing abandoned with evaded cadence
  **Correction**: Check the dominant: if it is inverted or absent, the cadence is abandoned; if it is in root position and the tonic fails to appear properly, it is evaded

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Expecting every abandoned cadence to have a clear "denial moment"
  **Clarification**: Sometimes no precise moment of denied arrival can be located; the cadential progression simply gives way to different material
