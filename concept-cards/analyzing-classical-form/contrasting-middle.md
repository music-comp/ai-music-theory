# Quick Definition
The B section achieves contrast primarily through harmonic-tonal means (emphasizing dominant) and phrase-structural means (looser organization), and only secondarily through melodic-motivic means. It typically begins with dominant and almost always ends with that harmony, either as an HC or as a noncadential dominant arrival.

# Core Definition
The contrasting middle is more loosely organized than the exposition. Contrast is achieved foremost by harmonic-tonal means: the B section often starts on V and almost always ends on V, preparing the return of tonic at the recapitulation. Phrase-structural means also create looseness: the B section is less symmetrical, may undergo extensions, and rarely takes the form of a conventional theme type. Two typical internal structures are: (1) a standing on the dominant (simplest), and (2) a continuation phrase ending with HC (with model-sequence technique). Other options include a looser sentential structure or a modulating section with retransition. The B section always starts in the measure following A (never eliding). The period form is never used for a B section.

# Prerequisites
- **Small ternary** — The overarching form

# Key Properties
1. Contrast primarily through harmony and phrase structure, secondarily through motives
2. Emphasizes dominant (begins and usually ends on V)
3. Two typical structures: standing on dominant, or continuation with HC
4. Period form is never used for B section
5. A and B sections never elide
6. May include polyphonic textures, motivic imitation
7. If ending is a standing on dominant, there is NO cadence at the end

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 228): Haydn, Capriccio H. XVII:1 -- B section as standing on dominant, 4 mm.
**Example 2** (p. 231): Haydn, Sonata H. 40, ii -- B section as continuation with model-sequence technique, ending with HC.
