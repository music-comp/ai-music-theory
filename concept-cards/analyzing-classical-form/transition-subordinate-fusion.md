# Quick Definition
Transition/subordinate-theme fusion combines both functions into a single thematic unit, eliminating the transition's half cadence, standing on the dominant, and the subordinate theme's new basic idea. The boundary between the two functions is recognizable only after the fact.

# Core Definition
Form-functional fusion permits transition and subordinate-theme functions to be traversed more rapidly than when they occupy their own distinct groups. The process compresses the form by eliminating a number of phrase-functional elements: the half cadence (and subsequent standing on the dominant) for the transition and an initiating unit (a new basic idea) for the subordinate theme. This technique occurs with greater frequency in slow movements and in minuet form (Caplin, pp. 612-613).

# Prerequisites
- **Slow-movement sonata** -- The formal context where this fusion is most common

# Key Properties
1. Two functions combined in a single thematic unit
2. Eliminates: transition HC, standing on dominant, subordinate theme's new basic idea
3. The boundary is recognizable only after the fact -- it lies "in the middle"
4. More frequent in slow movements than fast movements
5. Also regularly found in minuet form

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Look for a single thematic unit that begins in the home key and ends in the subordinate key
2. The middle of the unit marks the boundary, but no clear ending or beginning is perceptible
3. Model-sequence activity typically brings the modulation
4. Cadential confirmation of the new key marks the subordinate-theme aspect

# Context & Application
This fusion is one of three compression techniques for slow-movement sonatas. It permits the two expositional functions to be traversed rapidly, fitting the aesthetic needs of a slow movement.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 613): Beethoven, Piano Sonata in D, Op. 10, No. 3, ii, mm. 9-17 -- transition begins after main theme; model-sequence brings modulation to C major; key confirmed by IAC and PAC; the middle of m. 13 is the boundary, recognizable only retrospectively.
