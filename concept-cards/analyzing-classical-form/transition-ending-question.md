# Quick Definition
The transition's structural end is marked by the HC or dominant arrival; its literal end is the last event before the subordinate theme begins (often a standing on the dominant or medial caesura). These two moments may coincide but often arise at different points in time. The opening tonic of the subordinate theme signals a beginning, not the end of the transition.

# Core Definition
For most transitions, it is important to distinguish between the structural end and the literal end. The structural end arises with the moment of half cadence or dominant arrival; the literal end occurs just before the entrance of the subordinate theme and is often associated with a medial caesura. Sometimes these two moments occur simultaneously (when the last event is the HC itself), but they often arise at different points in time, which must be carefully teased apart. A common confusion arises when the drive to resolve the prolonged dominant onto tonic at the start of the subordinate theme leads students to identify the tonic arrival as a cadence marking the transition's end. Caplin stresses: the initial tonic of the subordinate theme signals the beginning of that unit, not the end of the transition. "Resolution of dominant to tonic" must not be confused with "arrival at a functional end" (Ch. 11, pp. 353-354).

# Prerequisites
- **Half cadence** -- Marks the structural end
- **Standing on the dominant** -- Postcadential prolongation

# Key Properties
1. Structural end: HC or dominant arrival
2. Literal end: last event before subordinate theme (standing on dominant, medial caesura)
3. Two moments may coincide or be separated
4. Opening tonic of subordinate theme = BEGINNING, not end of transition
5. "Resolution of V to I" is NOT "arrival at a functional end"
6. Standing on the dominant fills the space between structural and literal ends

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Locate the HC or dominant arrival -- this is the structural end
2. Locate the last event before the subordinate theme begins -- this is the literal end
3. Note any standing on the dominant or medial caesura between them
4. Do NOT treat the subordinate theme's opening tonic as the transition's end
5. Accept that the two end-points may be simultaneous or separated

# Context & Application
The distinction between structural and literal ends is fundamental to understanding how transitions function. The standing on the dominant between the structural and literal ends serves to prolong the transition's final harmony, either sustaining and reinforcing the dominant arrival or dissipating it to clear the stage for the subordinate theme. Material from the HC itself may be used to construct the standing on the dominant.
