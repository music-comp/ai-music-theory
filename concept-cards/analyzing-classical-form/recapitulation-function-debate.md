# Quick Definition
In sonata without development, the section following the exposition functions more as a repetition than a return, since no contrasting development intervenes. Despite this theoretical difficulty, "recapitulation" remains pragmatically justified by its identical construction and its resolution of tonal conflict.

# Core Definition
A fundamental characteristic of any recapitulation is to bring a return following some intervening, contrasting unit. If a development is eliminated, then the section following the exposition will seem to function more as a repetition than a return. Despite this theoretical difficulty, it seems pragmatic to continue using the term recapitulation, since the construction is virtually identical to a regular sonata recapitulation, and the concept includes the notion of "resolution of tonal conflict" (Caplin, p. 594).

# Key Properties
1. Return vs. repetition: return follows contrasting material; repetition follows same/similar material
2. The listener on first hearing may believe the exposition is simply being repeated
3. Pragmatic reasons to keep the label: identical construction, tonal resolution
4. The functional sense of "return" is weakened but not eliminated

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Observe whether the material following the exposition is structurally identical to a regular recapitulation
2. Check for tonal adjustments (subordinate theme in home key)
3. Note that the sense of "return" depends on the listener's awareness of the overall form

# Context & Application
This debate illuminates a central issue in form-functional theory: the relationship between formal labels and experiential reality. Caplin resolves it pragmatically, privileging structural analogy over perceptual immediacy.
