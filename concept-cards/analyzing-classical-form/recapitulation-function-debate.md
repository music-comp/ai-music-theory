## The Theoretical Problem

A fundamental characteristic of any recapitulation is to bring a **return** following some intervening, contrasting unit. If the development is eliminated (as in sonata without development), the section following the exposition functions more as a **repetition** than a return.

## Listener's Perspective

The listener hearing the movement for the first time would not necessarily know that the appearance of the main theme following the exposition marks the beginning of a recapitulation. They could just as likely believe that:
- The exposition is simply being repeated (as in regular sonata form)
- A sonata-form development will follow

## Arguments for Using "Recapitulation"

Despite this theoretical difficulty, it is pragmatic to continue using the term "recapitulation" because:

1. **Identical Construction**: In virtually all respects, the construction of this section is identical to that of a regular sonata recapitulation

2. **Tonal Resolution**: The concept of recapitulation includes the notion of "resolution of tonal conflict" - the subordinate theme now appears in the home key

## Return vs. Repetition

| Return | Repetition |
|--------|------------|
| Follows contrasting material | Follows same/similar material |
| Creates sense of coming back | Creates sense of hearing again |
| Typical of genuine recapitulation | Typical of exposition repeat |

## Practical Solution

Use "recapitulation" for the second part of sonata without development, while acknowledging that the functional sense of "return" is weakened by the absence of a contrasting development section.
