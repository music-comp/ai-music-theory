# Quick Definition
A prolongational technique in which the root of the prolonged harmony is sustained in the bass throughout the progression while subordinate harmonies occur above.

# Formal Definition
The most forceful way of prolonging a harmony arises by means of pedal point. The pedal, which lies in the bass voice throughout the progression, contains the root of the prolonged harmony. Most often, this harmony appears at the beginning and end of the progression. The bass note of the subordinate harmonies is replaced by the pedal note, thus significantly reducing the structural status of these harmonies.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Characteristics of pedal point:
- Bass voice sustains or repeats the root of the prolonged harmony
- Subordinate harmonies lose their bass voice (replaced by pedal)
- Most forceful type of prolongation
- Prolonged harmony typically frames the progression

Analytical notation:
- Indicated by "ped." following the Roman numeral of the prolonged harmony
- All subordinate harmonies within pedal point are placed in parentheses
- No inversion is indicated for subordinate harmonies (their bass notes are replaced)
- Example: I ped. - (IV - V7) - I

# Musical Context/Application
Pedal point situations:
- Create strong sense of prolongation through bass stability
- Reduce structural weight of subordinate harmonies significantly
- Common at openings of themes (tonic pedal)
- Also found on dominant (dominant pedal) in development sections

Because subordinate harmonies lose their bass voice, their position (inversion) cannot be determined. They are analyzed in root position unless context implies a specific inversion.

# Common Confusions
- Assigning inversions to subordinate harmonies within pedal points (their bass is replaced)
- Confusing pedal point with sustained bass notes in other contexts
- Thinking pedal must be on tonic (dominant and other pedals exist)
- Overlooking how pedal point reduces the functional weight of subordinate harmonies
