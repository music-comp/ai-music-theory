# Quick Definition
When an antecedent modulates to the dominant region and closes with a literal PAC there, but the immediate return of the home key at the start of the consequent cancels the modulation. The PAC is retrospectively understood as a kind of HC in the home key. Notated PAC=>HC. Rare in simple 8-m. periods; more common in compound 16-m. periods.

# Core Definition
In some cases, a presumed antecedent appears to modulate to the dominant region, closing there with a PAC. Two principles would be violated: that it close with a weak cadence, and that it not modulate. Nonetheless, when the home key is immediately reinstituted at the beginning of the consequent, the sense of modulation is instantly canceled, and we can recognize that the antecedent has closed with a reinterpreted half cadence (p. 112). Both interpretations are valid for their temporal context: at the moment, a genuine PAC; in the broader period context, a reinterpreted HC. The symbol => stands for "becomes" and represents retrospective reinterpretation (p. 114).

# Prerequisites
- **Antecedent phrase** — The reinterpreted HC occurs at the end of an antecedent
- **Cadential differentiation** — The reinterpretation preserves weak-to-strong progression

# Key Properties
1. Antecedent modulates to dominant region with literal PAC
2. Consequent immediately returns to home key
3. PAC is retrospectively reinterpreted as HC in home key
4. Both interpretations valid for their temporal context
5. Notation: PAC=>HC
6. Rare in simple 8-m. periods; more common in compound 16-m. periods (Ch. 6)
7. The => symbol (from Schmalfeldt) denotes retrospective reinterpretation

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Look for a modulation to the dominant at end of antecedent with PAC
2. Check if the consequent immediately reinstitutes the home key
3. If so: the PAC is a reinterpreted HC (PAC=>HC)

# Context & Application
The reinterpreted HC is the one exception to the rule that antecedents do not modulate and must close with weak cadences. The rapid return to home key cancels the modulation. This technique arises more frequently in compound 16-m. periods where the antecedent has more space for modulation.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 113, Ex. 3.19): Haydn, Op. 64, No. 4, i -- antecedent modulates to D major (dominant of G) with PAC; consequent returns to G major, revealing HC.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Hearing a genuine modulation and analyzing the antecedent as ending in the dominant key
  **Correction**: The immediate return of home key at the consequent cancels the modulation

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking either the PAC or HC interpretation is "wrong"
  **Clarification**: Both are valid for their respective temporal contexts
