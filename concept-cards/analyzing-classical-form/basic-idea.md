# Quick Definition
A 2-m. unit that typically begins classical themes, containing the fundamental melodic-motivic material. It is small enough to group into phrases but large enough to be fragmented. The b.i. encompasses all musical components (melody, harmony, rhythm, texture), not just the tune.

# Core Definition
Most classical themes begin with a 2-m. basic idea. The idea itself usually contains various motives. As a 2-m. unit, the basic idea is small enough to group with other ideas into phrases and themes, but large enough to be broken down (fragmented) in order to develop its constituent motives (p. 60). The basic idea is the complete unit of music in all of its parts, including its harmonic, rhythmic, and textural components -- the basic idea is much more than just its "tune" (p. 61). When it first appears, the b.i. emphasizes tonic harmony, usually in root position (p. 58).

# Prerequisites
- **Theme definition** — Must understand themes before their constituent ideas

# Key Properties
1. Normatively 2 measures long (embraces two metrical downbeats, not necessarily two full measures)
2. Contains individual motives that may be detached and developed
3. Melodic content is typically characteristic (unique) rather than conventional
4. Usually projects "opening up" of melodic space through ascending gestures
5. First appearance emphasizes tonic harmony (usually root position)
6. Complete unit: melody + harmony + rhythm + texture
7. Often begins with an upbeat (anacrusis)
8. When a 2-m. b.i. contains a repeated 1-m. motive, the 2-m. unit is still the b.i.

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify a Basic Idea:
1. Look for the opening 2-m. unit of a theme
2. Identify its characteristic melodic-motivic content
3. Verify it is supported by tonic harmony
4. Note constituent motives within the b.i.
5. Use musical instincts for boundaries; avoid letting bar lines overly influence decisions (p. 62)

# Context & Application
The b.i. appears at the beginning of both sentences and periods. In sentences, it is immediately repeated (presentation). In periods, it is followed by a contrasting idea (antecedent) and returns at the start of the consequent. It is not always possible to predict whether a given b.i. will give rise to a sentence or period -- this depends on what follows in mm. 3-4 (p. 85).

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 56, Ex. 2.1): Beethoven, Op. 2, No. 1 -- b.i. with two motives: ascending arpeggio ("a") and turn figure ("b").

**Example 2** (p. 61, Ex. 2.3): Beethoven, Op. 14, No. 2 -- b.i. consisting of a repeated 1-m. motive (the 2-m. unit is still the b.i.).

**Example 3** (p. 62, Ex. 2.4): Haydn, Op. 33, No. 3, ii -- b.i. lasting one beat more than two full measures; still identified as "2-m." by two metrical downbeats.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating a 1-m. motive within the b.i. as the basic idea itself
  **Correction**: The 2-m. unit remains the b.i. even if it contains a repeated 1-m. motive (p. 61)

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Identifying the b.i. by its melody alone
  **Clarification**: The b.i. is the complete unit including harmony, rhythm, and texture
- **Confusion**: Expecting b.i. boundaries to align with bar lines
  **Clarification**: Use musical instincts; b.i. often begins with anacrusis and does not fill exact measures
