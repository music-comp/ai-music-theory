# Quick Definition
Haydn's expositions frequently differ from those of Mozart and Beethoven in their treatment of formal boundaries. He is well known for monothematic expositions (opening subordinate themes with main-theme material), more obscured boundaries between transition and subordinate theme, and more frequent fusion of transition and subordinate-theme functions.

# Core Definition
Most sonata-form expositions by Mozart and Beethoven feature unmistakable demarcation of the transition and subordinate theme, with the transition clearly ending with an HC (or dominant arrival) and the subordinate theme beginning with an obvious initiating function. In many Haydn expositions, however, the boundary between these functions is obscured. Haydn is well known for his monothematic tendency -- opening subordinate themes with the basic idea of the main theme. He more frequently creates fusion of transition and subordinate-theme functions, yielding single units embracing both functions with ambiguous formal boundaries. Many passages that traditional approaches assign to the transition are better understood, from the form-functional perspective, as belonging to the opening part of the subordinate-theme group (Caplin, Ch. 12, pp. 422-429).

# Key Properties
1. Monothematic tendency: subordinate themes often use main-theme material
2. Boundaries between transition and subordinate theme more obscured
3. Fusion of transition and subordinate-theme functions more frequent
4. Single thematic units may embrace both functions
5. Traditional approaches often misassign subordinate-theme material to the transition
6. Poses greater analytical challenges than Mozart or Beethoven

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Look for main-theme material at the start of the subordinate-theme area
2. Check for clear boundary markers (HC, medial caesura, new initiating function)
3. If boundary markers are absent, consider fusion of transition and subordinate-theme functions
4. Use harmonic considerations (root-position tonic prolongation in SK) to locate subordinate-theme beginning
5. Do not force Mozart/Beethoven expectations onto Haydn

# Context & Application
Understanding Haydn's expositional style is essential for analyzing his sonata forms without forcing them into frameworks derived primarily from Mozart and Beethoven. The concept of form-functional fusion, where transition and subordinate-theme functions merge into a single unit, is especially important for Haydn analysis. When fusion occurs, the annotation "transition/subordinate theme" (with slash indicating fusion) is appropriate.

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 427-428): Haydn, Symphony No. 93, i, mm. 46-77 -- Second part of two-part transition modulates to subordinate key; passage from mm. 53-74 appears as single unit fusing transition and subordinate-theme functions; no clear boundary between the two functions.
**Example 2** (pp. 425-426): Haydn exposition where subordinate theme lacks initiating function; boundary with transition obscured by absence of rhythmic, textural, or dynamic change.
