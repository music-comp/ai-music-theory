# Quick Definition
The classical period developed three conventionalized rhythmic-contour patterns to accompany a distinctive upper-voice melody: the Alberti bass (broken chord arpeggiation), the drum bass (repeated notes or octaves), and the murky bass (alternating bass and upper chord tones). All three give rise to variants and are especially associated with medial formal functions.

# Core Definition
Caplin identifies three common accompanimental patterns named by music historians: (1) the Alberti bass, a broken-chord pattern; (2) the drum bass, repeated notes or octaves in regular rhythm; and (3) the murky bass, alternating bass and higher chord tones. These patterns bring continuous rhythmic propulsion and forward drive. While they can appear anywhere in a classical movement (including the main theme), they are typically associated with medial phrase functions (continuation, contrasting middle) and medial thematic functions (transition, development core). Transitions are often the first place in the movement where such a pattern appears, helping support the generally increased dynamic momentum (Ch. 11, p. 337).

# Key Properties
1. Three named patterns: Alberti bass, drum bass, murky bass
2. All give rise to variants of their basic shapes
3. Bring continuous rhythmic propulsion and forward drive
4. Associated with medial phrase functions (continuation, contrasting middle)
5. Associated with medial thematic functions (transition, development core)
6. Often mark the point where a movement seems to be "getting under way"

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Look for a repetitive rhythmic-contour pattern in the accompaniment
2. Alberti bass: broken chord (e.g., C-G-E-G repeated pattern)
3. Drum bass: repeated notes or octaves in regular rhythm
4. Murky bass: alternating bass note and higher chord tone
5. Note their formal location -- typically medial functions

# Context & Application
The onset of a conventional accompanimental pattern frequently coincides with the beginning of a transition, marking the moment when the movement's dynamic energy begins to increase. Their presence signals forward momentum and continuation function. An accompanimental overlap occurs when the new pattern appears at the cadential arrival of the main theme, with the next thematic unit's true downbeat occurring in the following measure (e.g., Mozart K. 576, i, m. 16).

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming these patterns appear only in transitions
  **Correction**: They can appear anywhere, including main themes, but are most typically associated with medial functions

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing accompanimental overlap with structural elision when a new pattern begins at a cadence
  **Clarification**: In accompanimental overlap, only the accompaniment overlaps; the next theme's true structural beginning is in the following measure. In elision, the new theme structurally begins at the moment of cadential arrival
