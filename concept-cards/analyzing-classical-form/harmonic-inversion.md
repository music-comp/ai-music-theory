# Quick Definition
The positioning of a chord with a note other than the root in the bass voice, indicated by figured-bass symbols that significantly affect the chord's functional weight and behavior.

# Formal Definition
Harmonic inversion refers to the position of a chord based on which chord tone is in the bass voice. Inversions are indicated by standard figured-bass symbols following the Roman numeral (e.g., I6, V4/3). The inversion of a chord significantly affects its functional status, particularly for the dominant, where root position is essential for cadential function.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Inversion labels:
- **Root position**: Root in bass (I, V, etc.)
- **First inversion**: Third in bass (I6, V6, V6/5)
- **Second inversion**: Fifth in bass (I6/4, V4/3)
- **Third inversion (7th chords)**: Seventh in bass (V4/2)

Functional implications:
- Root-position I: Maximum stability, cadential goal
- I6: Less stable, often initiates cadential progressions
- Root-position V: Required for cadential status
- V6, V4/3, etc.: Cannot be cadential; used in prolongational contexts

The tonic triad in root position (I) is considerably more stable than in first inversion (I6).

# Musical Context/Application
Inversion affects:
- Harmonic weight and stability
- Cadential potential (V must be in root position)
- Voice-leading possibilities
- Formal function (I6 signals beginning of cadential motion)

Analytical notation: A change of inversion of the same harmony does not usually bring a repetition of the Roman numeral (shown as I——6).

Within pedal points, subordinate harmonies cannot have their inversion determined since their bass is replaced by the pedal.

# Examples
Example 1.11 (p. 37): The typical authentic cadential progression begins with I6 (first inversion) and ends with I (root position), showing how different inversions serve different functions.

The chapter emphasizes: "The tonic triad in root position (I) is considerably stabler than that triad in first inversion (I6). Indeed, we will see that the first-inversion tonic plays a special role in articulating harmonic directionality, especially by signaling the onset of a cadential progression" (p. 31).

# Common Confusions
- Treating all inversions as having equal functional weight
- Assuming inverted V can function cadentially (only root position)
- Not recognizing I6's role in signaling cadential motion
- Assigning inversions to harmonies within pedal points
