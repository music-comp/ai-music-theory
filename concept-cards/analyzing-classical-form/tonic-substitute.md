# Quick Definition
A harmony (usually VI) that can replace an expected tonic chord while maintaining tonic function, most commonly occurring after a root-position dominant.

# Formal Definition
In most contexts, the triad built on the submediant (VI) degree of the scale has tonic function and can frequently substitute for an expected I chord, especially when following a root-position V. This situation is termed a deceptive resolution of the dominant. The functional similarity between I and VI arises from their sharing of two common tones.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
The submediant (VI) serves as tonic substitute because:
- It shares two chord tones with the tonic triad (scale degrees 1 and 3)
- It maintains tonic function despite different root
- It commonly appears after root-position V in deceptive progressions

The principle extends to other harmonies sharing common tones with tonic, but VI is by far the most prevalent tonic substitute in classical practice.

# Musical Context/Application
Tonic substitutes appear in several formal contexts:
1. **Deceptive cadential progression**: V resolves to VI instead of I
2. **Prolongational progressions**: VI can participate in extending tonic function
3. **Sequential contexts**: VI may appear as part of pattern (though less functional here)

Important: The submediant should NOT be classified as pre-dominant even when it progresses directly to V. In the progression VI-V, a tonic-functioning harmony moves directly to dominant, bypassing pre-dominant function entirely.

# Common Confusions
- Classifying VI as pre-dominant when it progresses to V (it remains a tonic substitute that bypasses pre-dominant)
- Assuming VI always functions as tonic substitute (context matters; it can be nonfunctional in sequences)
- Thinking tonic substitutes are "weaker" versions of tonic (they serve different formal purposes)
