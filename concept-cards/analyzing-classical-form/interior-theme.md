## Definition

An interior theme occupies a central position within a form, always standing between statements of a main theme. It provides contrast through modal shift (typically to minore) or different tonal region while maintaining structural coherence.

## Characteristics

### Modal Contrast
- Most commonly set in the **minor mode** (minore) when the main theme is major
- May shift to **major mode** (maggiore) when the main theme is minor
- Sometimes set in submediant (vi), lowered submediant (bVI), or subdominant (IV)

### Important Distinction
- **Never** set primarily in the standard subordinate key (V for major, III or v for minor)
- This distinguishes interior theme fundamentally from subordinate theme

### Character
- Often brings *Sturm und Drang* affect
- Features highly active, rhythmically continuous accompanimental patterns
- Creates emotional intensification in the middle of the form

## Formal Organization

Usually related to small ternary (or small binary) form, but often distorted:

1. **Complete**: Small ternary closes with PAC, followed by retransition
2. **Cadentially Open**: A' section fails to achieve PAC; ends with HC or dominant arrival
3. **Truncated**: A' section eliminated; theme ends with B section on dominant
4. **Extremely Truncated**: Both B and A' sections eliminated; modulating A section followed by retransition

## Ending on Dominant

The interior theme typically ends on home-key dominant harmony, which:
- Motivates the return of the main theme
- Creates harmonic tension requiring resolution
- Allows the main theme's tonic opening to resolve the instability
