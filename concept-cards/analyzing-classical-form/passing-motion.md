# Quick Definition
Stepwise melodic motion connecting two different chord tones, typically creating passing chords when harmonized, and often appearing in the bass voice within prolongational progressions.

# Formal Definition
Passing motion refers to stepwise melodic movement that connects two different chord tones of a prolonged harmony. In the context of harmonic prolongation, passing motion in the bass usually lies between root-position and first-inversion forms of the prolonged harmony, with a variety of chords built over this passing tone.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Characteristics of passing motion:
- Stepwise (conjunct) melodic movement
- Connects two structural chord tones
- Creates passing chords when harmonized
- In bass: typically connects 1 to 3 (or vice versa)

Common bass passing motion:
- I (root position) - passing chord - I6 (first inversion)
- Bass: 1 - 2 - 3
- Various harmonies may be built over scale degree 2

Important: A passing chord may arise without any of the voices literally displaying passing motion. The term refers to the position change of the prolonged harmony, not necessarily literal stepwise motion in every voice.

# Musical Context/Application
Passing motion:
- Is central to prolongational progressions
- Creates smooth bass lines connecting chord positions
- The passing tone supports subordinate harmonies
- May or may not involve literal passing tones in all voices

The distinction between "passing" and "neighboring" harmonies is based on whether the prolonged harmony changes position, not on the presence of literal passing tones.

# Common Confusions
- Assuming passing motion must involve literal passing tones in all voices
- Confusing the harmonic concept (position change) with melodic concept (stepwise motion)
- Not recognizing passing motion in upper voices
- Treating all stepwise motion as passing (some is neighbor motion)
