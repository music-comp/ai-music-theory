# Quick Definition
Occasionally the main theme and transition fuse into a single thematic structure in the recapitulation, achieved by eliminating the end of the main theme and the beginning of the transition, and attaching what remains of the transition to the remnant of the main theme.

# Core Definition
Although the main theme and transition usually remain discrete units in the recapitulation as in the exposition, occasionally they fuse into a single thematic structure. This fusion is accomplished by eliminating the end of the main theme and the beginning of the transition, and attaching the close of the transition to what remains of the main theme. In the extreme case, all transition material may be eliminated: main theme material ends with an HC (instead of the original authentic cadence), and this HC serves as the transition's dominant goal. The fusion process is often accompanied by deletion of unnecessary repetitions, addition of new model-sequence technique, and emphasis on flat-side tonal regions.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of deletion of home-key cadence
- Knowledge of transition deletions and compressions in recapitulation

# Key Properties
- Main theme and transition combine into single thematic structure
- End of main theme eliminated
- Beginning of transition eliminated
- Remaining transition material attached to remaining main theme material
- In extreme cases, all transition material may be eliminated
- Main theme material may end with HC serving as transition's dominant goal
- Often accompanied by model-sequence technique and flat-side emphasis
- Two formal functions fused into single grouping unit

# Construction / Recognition
Compare the boundary between main theme and transition in the exposition and recapitulation. If the two units flow continuously into each other in the recapitulation without the cadential articulation that separated them in the exposition, fusion has occurred. Identify which portions of each unit have been retained and which eliminated.

# Context & Application
Fusion of main theme and transition is one of the more dramatic structural changes possible in the recapitulation. It reflects the reduced need for strong cadential articulation within the home key (since the subordinate theme will provide ample confirmation) and the increased value of forward momentum following the development section. The resulting single unit may exhibit characteristics of both formal functions: main-theme material at its beginning and transition-like harmonic trajectory at its end.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 576, i: main theme begins to repeat at m. 107 with polyphonic variation, shifts to flat-side subdominant, adds model-sequence technique, then draws on transition material (m. 118) to reach HC; thematic unit beginning at m. 107 opens with main-theme material but serves transitional function
- General pattern: main theme's repeat redirected into transitional function, reaching HC without independent transition section

# Common Errors
- Labeling a fused passage as purely "main theme" or purely "transition" when it serves both functions
- Assuming fusion always eliminates all transition material (partial fusion is more common)

# Common Confusions
- Fusion is NOT the same as simple deletion of the transition -- in fusion, elements of both main theme and transition are retained within a single unit, whereas deletion would remove the transition entirely without integrating its material
