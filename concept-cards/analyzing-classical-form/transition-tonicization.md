# Quick Definition
Modulating transitions typically reach the subordinate key through pivot-chord modulation. In major-mode movements, the submediant (vi) frequently serves as pivot, becoming the pre-dominant (ii) of the subordinate key -- a move so common it is "akin to waving a flag" that modulation is about to occur. Sequential progressions also effect smooth modulation, though the specific pivot chord may be harder to identify.

# Core Definition
In major-mode movements, the subordinate key (almost always the dominant region) is usually reached through pivot-chord modulation. One simple pivot sees the home-key tonic becoming the subdominant of the subordinate key. More often, there is a prominent move to the submediant (vi), which pivots to become the pre-dominant (ii) in the subordinate key. This move to vi is so common that it is "akin to waving a flag" that the modulation is about to take place. Sequential progressions are also effective in creating smooth modulation, though in such cases it is not always possible to identify just which harmony within the sequence is the actual pivot chord. In minor-mode movements, the modulation to the relative major (the conventional subordinate key) similarly employs the home-key iv or vi, pivoting to become ii or iv respectively. If the subordinate key is the minor dominant, modulation proceeds along lines similar to major-mode movements (Caplin, Ch. 11, pp. 339-340).

# Key Properties
1. Pivot-chord modulation is the primary technique
2. vi of home key = ii of subordinate key (major mode) -- most common pivot
3. Move to vi is a "flag" signaling impending modulation
4. I of home key = IV of subordinate key -- simple alternative pivot
5. Sequential progressions may obscure the specific pivot chord
6. Minor mode: iv or vi of home key pivots to ii or iv of relative major

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. In major mode, look for prominent vi before the modulation
2. Check whether vi = ii of the subordinate key (pivot-chord relationship)
3. In sequential passages, look for the point where harmonies begin functioning in the new key
4. In minor mode, look for iv or vi as pivot harmonies
5. The moment of modulation may be gradual rather than sudden in sequential contexts

# Context & Application
Understanding standard modulation techniques helps analysts track the transition's tonal journey and identify the pivot point. The prominence of vi as a pivot explains why the submediant often receives tonicization in transitions -- it serves a dual harmonic function that facilitates the modulation. Brief tonicization of remote regions (as a loosening device) should be distinguished from the structural modulation to the subordinate key.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating every tonicization within the transition as the "moment of modulation"
  **Correction**: Distinguish between brief tonicizations (loosening devices) and the structural modulation to the subordinate key; the latter is confirmed by the transition's final HC in the new key
