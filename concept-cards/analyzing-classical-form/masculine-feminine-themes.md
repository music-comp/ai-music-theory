# Quick Definition
The 19th-century view (from A. B. Marx) posited a dynamic "masculine" main theme opposing a lyrical "feminine" subordinate theme. This gendered distinction is largely rejected today; a more comprehensive approach (from Schoenberg and Ratz) views the distinction as tight-knit (main theme) vs. loose (subordinate theme), which is fully confirmed by the musical evidence.

# Core Definition
The typical 19th-century position, associated with A. B. Marx, held that a dynamic "masculine" main theme stands in opposition to a lyrical "feminine" subordinate theme. Marx's position was subtler than often thought: he made explicit that the "masculine" main theme is no more important than the "feminine" subordinate theme, seeing their union (like marriage) as essential to the sonata-form exposition. Today, the gendered distinction is generally rejected because the frequent absence of such character contrast in classical music has rendered it moot. A more comprehensive approach, drawn from Schoenberg and Ratz, views the distinction as one of formal organization: the subordinate theme is loosely organized while the main theme is relatively tight-knit. This view is fully confirmed by empirical evidence. Moreover, it is actually more often the transition -- not the main theme -- that conforms to the stereotypical "masculine" character (dynamically aggressive, assertive) (Caplin, Ch. 12, p. 380).

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Instead of characterizing themes as masculine/feminine, analyze their formal organization
2. Determine whether the theme is tight-knit (balanced grouping, conventional design) or loose (extensions, expansions, asymmetry)
3. Check the relationship between main theme and subordinate theme in terms of organizational looseness

# Context & Application
This terminological discussion is important for understanding the historiography of sonata-form theory. The shift from gendered character labels to formal-organizational categories represents a fundamental advance in analytical precision. The tight-knit vs. loose framework provides verifiable, structural criteria rather than subjective character descriptions, and it applies consistently across the classical repertoire.
