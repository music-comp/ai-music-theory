## "Masculine" vs. "Feminine" Themes

The typical 19th-century position (from A. B. Marx) held that a dynamic "masculine" main theme stands in opposition to a lyrical "feminine" subordinate theme.

### Modern View

Today, we generally reject this gendered distinction. The frequent absence of such contrast in music of the classical composers has largely rendered it moot.

### Marx's Subtlety

Marx's position was subtler than often thought:
- He made explicit that the "masculine" main theme is **no more important** than the "feminine" subordinate theme
- He saw the **union** of masculine and feminine (like marriage) as essential to the sonata-form exposition

### Alternative Understanding: Tight-Knit vs. Loose

A more comprehensive approach (from Schoenberg and Ratz) views the distinction as:
- Subordinate theme is **loosely** organized
- Main theme is relatively **tight-knit**

This view is fully confirmed by empirical evidence: subordinate themes (or groups) are, with rare exceptions, more loosely organized than main themes.

### Where "Masculine" Actually Appears

It is actually more often the **transition** that conforms to stereotypical "masculine" character (dynamically aggressive, assertive).
