# Quick Definition
A premature dominant arrival occurs when the harmonic arrival on the dominant does not coordinate with the end of melodic or grouping processes -- the music seems to get stuck "too early" on the dominant. The harmonic goal considerably precedes the melodic goal, creating a non-cadential ending for the transition.

# Core Definition
Frequently, the moment of dominant arrival does not coordinate with what we hear to be the end of various melodic or grouping processes. The music seems to get stuck "too early" on the dominant. This premature arrival can occur in two contexts: (1) with sequential progressions, where the final link of a descending-fifth sequential chain is the dominant, but ongoing melodic activity continues until later; and (2) with cadential progressions, where a cadential six-four is prolonged for several measures before resolving to the five-three position, creating non-coordination of harmonic arrival and melodic-motivic arrival. In both cases, the dominant appears to be the harmonic goal of the transition but the ongoing musical processes make the moment feel premature. A standing on the dominant then typically fills the space between the premature arrival and the start of the subordinate theme (Caplin, Ch. 11, pp. 355-358).

# Key Properties
1. Harmonic arrival on dominant precedes melodic/grouping completion
2. Music seems to get stuck "too early" on dominant
3. Two contexts: after sequential progressions and with prolonged cadential six-four
4. Non-coordination of harmonic and melodic goals
5. Creates a non-cadential ending rather than true HC
6. Standing on the dominant typically follows

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Locate the dominant arrival in the transition
2. Check whether melodic/motivic activity continues beyond the dominant arrival
3. Note if a sequential chain ends on the dominant while the phrase is still ongoing
4. Check for prolonged cadential six-four that delays resolution
5. The gap between harmonic arrival and melodic conclusion signals prematurity

# Context & Application
Premature dominant arrival is one of several types of non-cadential endings for transitions. The non-coordination between harmonic and melodic processes creates a distinctive effect: the dominant harmony sounds like the goal, but the music has not yet "finished saying what it has to say." Sforzandi or other dynamic emphasis on the arriving dominant may signal the composer's intention for this moment to function as the transition's harmonic goal despite the ongoing activity.

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 355-356): Beethoven, Symphony No. 4, Op. 60, i, mm. 81-108 -- Model-sequence pattern with descending-fifth progression; last harmonic link is dominant of subordinate key at m. 92, but melodic activity continues until m. 95; premature dominant arrival at m. 92.
**Example 2** (p. 357): Haydn, Piano Sonata H. 21, ii, mm. 7-12 -- Cadential six-four at m. 10 is prolonged by neighboring dominant sevenths for over two measures before resolving; harmonic arrival at m. 10, melodic arrival at m. 12.
**Example 3** (pp. 357-358): Mozart, String Quartet K. 464, iv, mm. 29-41 -- Premature dominant arrival at m. 33 in polyphonic texture; sforzandi in cello signal the intended harmonic goal despite ongoing counterpoint.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the resolution of a premature dominant as a cadence
  **Correction**: The tonic at the start of the subordinate theme is a beginning, not an end; the transition's structural end is at the (premature) dominant arrival
