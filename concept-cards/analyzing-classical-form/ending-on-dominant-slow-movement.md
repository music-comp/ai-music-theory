# Quick Definition
Some slow movements, particularly by Haydn, end with dominant harmony rather than a final PAC, forging a stronger bond between movements. The resolution to tonic occurs only at the beginning of the following movement.

# Core Definition
As a remnant of standard high-Baroque practice, a number of slow movements, particularly by Haydn, end with dominant harmony. The resolution to tonic occurs only at the beginning of the following movement. Such weaker closure forges a stronger bond between the movements yet may undermine the slow movement's independence (Caplin, p. 617).

# Key Properties
1. Dominant harmony as final sonority (no tonic resolution)
2. Tonic resolution deferred to the next movement's opening
3. Forges stronger inter-movement connection
4. May undermine slow movement's independence
5. In extreme cases, the slow movement seems like an introduction (e.g., Beethoven "Waldstein" Introduzione)
6. Three techniques: appended passage, getting "stuck," failed cadence

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Check the final harmony of the slow movement
2. If dominant, look for the technique used (appended passage, getting stuck, failed cadence)
3. Note how the following movement begins (typically resolving to tonic)

# Context & Application
This practice recalls the endings of interior themes in large ternary form, where dominant harmony motivates the main theme's return. At the movement level, it creates inter-movement dependency.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 618): The truncation of the recapitulation in Haydn, Piano Sonata in D, H. 24, ii results in the movement ending on the home-key dominant; the Presto finale follows immediately.
