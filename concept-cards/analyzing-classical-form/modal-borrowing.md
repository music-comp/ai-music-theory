# Quick Definition
The technique of using harmonies containing notes from the opposite modality of the prevailing mode (minor in major, or major in minor), also called mixture.

# Formal Definition
Modal borrowing (or mixture) is a compositional technique whereby chords containing notes from the minor scale are used in major-mode contexts, or vice versa. This technique enriches the harmonic vocabulary by introducing chromaticism while maintaining functional relationships.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Common examples of modal borrowing:
- In major: using bVI, bVII, iv, ii diminished (borrowed from parallel minor)
- In minor: using IV major, II major (borrowed from parallel major)
- Pre-dominant harmonies (II6, IV) can be enriched through mixture
- Creates expressive chromaticism within functional harmony

Modal borrowing maintains the functional identity of harmonies while altering their quality through chromatic inflection.

# Musical Context/Application
Modal borrowing appears frequently in:
1. **Pre-dominant harmonies**: IV becomes iv, II becomes ii
2. **Substitute harmonies**: Borrowed VI functioning as tonic substitute
3. **Sequential progressions**: Adding chromatic color to sequential patterns
4. **Cadential contexts**: bVI as deceptive resolution goal in major keys

The technique is especially useful for creating expressive contrast and adding harmonic variety without modulating to a new key.

# Examples
Example 1.13e (p. 38-39) demonstrates two consecutive diminished-seventh chords prolonging pre-dominant function. The first diminished-seventh is built on scale degree 4 and serves as a replacement for the pre-dominant II chord from the minor mode.

The chapter notes that both II6 and IV can be enriched through modal borrowing, adding dissonant sevenths and chromatic alterations.

# Common Confusions
- Confusing modal borrowing with modulation (borrowing maintains the original tonic; modulation establishes a new one)
- Not recognizing borrowed chords as maintaining their original function (borrowed iv still functions as pre-dominant)
- Assuming borrowed chords are always weaker or more unstable than diatonic versions
