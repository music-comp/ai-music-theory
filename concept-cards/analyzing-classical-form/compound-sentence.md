# Quick Definition
A 16-measure theme type built from an 8-m. compound presentation (repeated compound basic idea over tonic prolongation) followed by an 8-m. continuation ending with a cadence, often used to begin large public genres like symphonies and concertos.

# Core Definition
The compound presentation consists of a 4-m. compound basic idea and its repetition (statement-response, exact, or sequential), all supported by tonic prolongation. The continuation features standard characteristics (fragmentation, acceleration, sequence) with initial fragmentation typically reducing grouping from 4-m. to 2-m. units. The theme usually closes with a PAC, sometimes an HC. The compound sentence is often used to begin large, public genres (symphony, concerto) and is less frequent in smaller private genres. Many compound sentences compress their continuation into 4 measures, restoring balance through repetition or postcadential extension. If the compound sentence ends with an HC, a standing on the dominant may follow.

# Prerequisites
- **Sentence** — The simple version
- **Compound presentation** — The opening 8-m. unit
- **Compound basic idea** — The 4-m. initiating idea

# Key Properties
1. 16 real measures (8 + 8)
2. Compound presentation: c.b.i. + repetition (tonic prolongation)
3. Continuation: fragmentation to 2-m. units, then possibly 1-m.
4. Most often closes with PAC, sometimes HC
5. Associated with large public genres (symphony, concerto)
6. Continuation frequently compressed to 4 mm., restored by repetition/extension

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 190): Beethoven, Piano Concerto No. 3, i, mm. 1-16 -- statement-response compound presentation; continuation develops motive "y" with fragmentation, acceleration, and descending-third sequence; PAC.
**Example 2** (p. 204): Haydn, Symphony No. 83, i, mm. 1-16 -- compressed continuation leads to HC at m. 12; 4-m. standing on the dominant restores balance.
