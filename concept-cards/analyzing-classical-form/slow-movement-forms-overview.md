# Quick Definition
Classical slow movements employ inherently simpler formal types than fast movements to maintain consistent movement length despite slower pacing. The principal forms are large ternary, theme and variations, sonata without development, and five-part rondo.

# Core Definition
Most instrumental cycles contain at least one slow or moderately slow movement (Adagio, Largo, Andante) that normally occupies an interior position and is set in a contrasting tonality -- typically the subdominant -- or sometimes the contrasting modality of the cycle's overall key (Caplin, p. 587).

# Prerequisites
- **Small ternary** -- The basis for most theme types within slow-movement forms
- **Sonata form** -- Understanding required for sonata without development and slow-movement sonata

# Key Properties
1. Slow movements use simpler formal types to compensate for slower pacing
2. Principal types: large ternary, theme and variations, sonata without development, five-part rondo
3. Regular sonata form also appears but with modifications for compression
4. The slow movement normally resides in a contrasting key (often subdominant)
5. Theme and variations, though treated here, also appears in finales

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Determine the movement's tempo marking (Adagio, Largo, Andante)
2. Identify whether the form is tripartite (large ternary), variational, or bipartite (sonata without development)
3. Check for the presence or absence of a development section
4. Look for interior themes with modal contrast (minore/maggiore)
5. Consider five-part rondo if refrains alternate with couplets

# Context & Application
Slow movements appear in virtually all multi-movement classical works (sonatas, quartets, symphonies, concertos). The choice of form reflects both the composer's preference and the specific aesthetic goals of the work. Haydn favors the large ternary, Mozart the sonata without development.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming all slow movements use the same form
  **Correction**: Multiple form types are available; the choice depends on composer preference and aesthetic goals

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Equating formal simplicity with compositional simplicity
  **Clarification**: Simpler formal types are chosen to compensate for slower pacing, not because the music is less sophisticated
