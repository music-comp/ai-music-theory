# Quick Definition
The high-Baroque French overture style is the historical antecedent of the classical slow introduction, characterized by fanfare gestures, double-dotted rhythms, and a courtly sense of pomp and circumstance, but generally lacking the anticipatory, hesitant gestures that the classical period would add.

# Core Definition
The stately and heraldic qualities of the classical slow introduction surely derive from the high-Baroque French overture style, which is associated with an introductory movement (or part of a movement) and is characterized by fanfare gestures, double-dotted rhythms, and an overall courtly sense of pomp and circumstance. Generally lacking in the French overture, however, are the anticipatory, hesitant gestures that typify the slow introduction of a classical sonata form. Thus the later style period dramatizes the sense of introduction by making it a more premonitory event.

# Prerequisites
None (historical reference point)

# Key Properties
- Characterized by fanfare gestures
- Double-dotted rhythms (later written as double-dotted)
- Courtly sense of pomp and circumstance
- Stately, heraldic quality
- Forte dynamic
- Generally lacks anticipatory or hesitant gestures
- Generally lacks the uncertainty and instability of classical introductions
- Serves as historical ancestor of the classical slow introduction

# Construction / Recognition
Recognize the French overture style by its combination of slow tempo, double-dotted rhythms, fanfare gestures, and ceremonial character. The style appears in Baroque introductory movements and is echoed in the stately aspects of classical slow introductions, though the latter add elements of hesitancy and uncertainty not present in the Baroque model.

# Context & Application
Understanding the French overture style is essential for appreciating what the classical slow introduction inherits and what it transforms. The classical period retains the ceremonial grandeur of the French overture but adds anticipatory, uncertain, and unstable qualities that dramatize the sense of introduction. This historical perspective explains why classical introductions unite two seemingly incompatible affective worlds: the stately and heraldic with the uncertain and anticipatory.

# Examples
- General Baroque practice: Lully and Handel overtures feature double-dotted rhythms, fanfare gestures, and ceremonial character without the hesitant gestures of later classical introductions
- Classical transformation: Haydn's and Mozart's slow introductions retain the French overture's stately qualities while adding marked discontinuities, harmonic instabilities, and premonitory character

# Common Errors
- Treating classical slow introductions as purely ceremonial in the French overture manner, ignoring the added anticipatory elements
- Assuming the French overture style disappears entirely in the classical period

# Common Confusions
- The French overture style is NOT identical to the classical slow introduction -- the classical style retains the ceremonial aspects but adds hesitant, premonitory qualities that make the introduction a more dramatically charged event
