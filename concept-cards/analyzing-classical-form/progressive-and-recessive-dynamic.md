# Quick Definition
Framing functions frequently exhibit distinct dynamic processes: thematic introductions typically feature a progressive dynamic (increasing buildup of energy and anticipation), while closing sections often feature a recessive dynamic (diminution and dissolution of tension), though some closing sections sustain or increase energy.

# Core Definition
Caplin uses "dynamic" in a broad sense: not merely loudness but the systematic growing or diminishing of tension created by changes in texture, rhythmic activity, timbre, and intensity. Wallace Berry's terms are adopted: a progressive dynamic features increasing buildup of energy, typical of introductions; a recessive dynamic brings diminution and dissolution of tension, often associated with closing sections. However, Caplin notes that not all closing sections display recessive dynamic -- energy may be sustained or further increased.

# Key Properties
1. Progressive: increasing energy, anticipation, textural accumulation
2. Recessive: decreasing energy, dissolution, calming
3. "Dynamic" used broadly (not just loud/soft)
4. Introductions: typically progressive
5. Closing sections: often recessive, but not always
6. Subdominant emphasis in codettas contributes to recessive quality

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 167): Beethoven, Op. 135, iii, mm. 1-2 -- progressive dynamic in introduction: crescendo and accumulating texture.
**Example 2** (p. 168): Mozart, K. 332, iii, mm. 27-35 -- recessive dynamic in closing section: calando to pianissimo, stabilizing tonic pedal.
**Example 3** (p. 168): Mozart, K. 330, iii, mm. 13-20 -- closing section with sustained/increased energy (triplet sixteenth notes).
