# Quick Definition
Now and then, the final dominant of a transition gives no sense of being an ending harmony whatsoever: it receives no emphasis, resolves directly to tonic, and is accompanied by little or no rhythmic break. The transition is left without functional closure. The composer usually finds a clear way to articulate the subordinate theme's beginning (such as a presentation phrase), but sometimes the subordinate theme fuses with the transition.

# Core Definition
Now and then, the final dominant of a transition gives no sense of being an ending harmony whatsoever. The dominant does not appear to be the goal of the progression; it receives no emphasis and resolves directly to tonic at the beginning of the subordinate theme with little or no rhythmic break. As a result, the transition is left without any kind of functional closure. In such cases, the composer usually finds a clear way to articulate the sense that the subordinate theme has actually begun, such as the appearance of a basic idea in the context of a presentation phrase with clear establishment of root-position tonic. At other times, the subordinate theme fuses with the transition -- a situation where no clear point can be identified where the transition ends and the subordinate theme begins (Caplin, Ch. 11, pp. 365-366).

# Prerequisites
- **Where does the transition end?** -- The boundary question
- **Dominant arrival** -- Even non-cadential endings are absent here

# Key Properties
1. Final dominant gives no sense of ending whatsoever
2. Dominant receives no emphasis and resolves directly to tonic
3. No rhythmic break between transition and subordinate theme
4. Transition left without functional closure
5. Subordinate theme usually begins with clear presentation (to compensate)
6. Sometimes fusion occurs: no clear boundary at all

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Check whether the transition's final dominant receives any emphasis
2. Look for temporal extension, standing on the dominant, or medial caesura -- if absent, closure is missing
3. Determine whether the subordinate theme's beginning is clearly articulated (new basic idea, root-position tonic)
4. If neither transition ending nor subordinate-theme beginning is clear: consider fusion

# Context & Application
The omission of concluding function represents the most extreme loosening of the transition's ending. It creates maximum formal continuity between transition and subordinate theme at the cost of structural clarity. When the subordinate theme compensates with a strong initiating function (presentation), the boundary can still be located. When it does not, form-functional fusion results.

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 365-366): Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 457, i, mm. 17-27 -- Transition begins at m. 19 with main-theme basic idea; idea repeated over dominant of subordinate key; dominant receives no emphasis; no cadential articulation, standing on the dominant, or temporal extension; yet m. 23 clearly begins a presentation in root-position tonic of subordinate key, marking the subordinate theme's start despite the transition's lack of formal ending.
