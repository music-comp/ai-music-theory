# Quick Definition
In the recapitulation, a modulating transition from the exposition must be adjusted to remain in the home key by shifting toward the flat-side subdominant region, so that the tonicized IV harmony takes on the role of pre-dominant, leading to the final home-key dominant and allowing the subordinate theme to enter in the home key.

# Core Definition
A modulating transition in the exposition must be adjusted in the recapitulation to remain in the home key, ending there with dominant harmony, so that the subordinate theme can enter with home-key tonic. The most typical adjustment sees the music shift toward the flat-side subdominant region, even briefly, so that the tonicized IV harmony can eventually take on the role of pre-dominant, leading to the final home-key dominant of the transition. Thus the flat-side shift to the subdominant is balanced by a "sharp-side" shift back to the home key. This sharp-side shift is analogous to the sharp-side shift from the home key to the subordinate key found in the exposition. In both sections, the goal dominant is achieved by a sharp-side shift: in the exposition, this shift yields a new key; in the recapitulation, it yields the original key but sounding "fresh."

# Key Properties
- Modulating transition must be adjusted to stay in the home key
- Must end with dominant harmony of the home key
- Music shifts toward flat-side subdominant region (IV)
- Tonicized IV takes role of pre-dominant
- Pre-dominant leads to home-key dominant
- Flat-side shift balanced by sharp-side return to home key
- Sharp-side shift analogous to exposition's modulation
- Home key sounds "fresh" despite being the original key

# Construction / Recognition
Compare the transition in the recapitulation with the exposition's. Identify the point where the recapitulation's transition diverges harmonically from the exposition. Look for a flat-side shift (toward IV) followed by a sharp-side return to the home-key dominant. The goal is the same dominant harmony as in the exposition, but now functioning within the home key rather than a new key.

# Context & Application
Tonal adjustment is the fundamental structural change required by the recapitulation. Without it, the subordinate theme would enter in the subordinate key rather than the home key, and the essential sonata closure could not occur. The flat-side/sharp-side strategy allows the recapitulation to maintain harmonic interest (avoiding tonal monotony) while fulfilling its obligation to keep the subordinate theme in the home key. The analogy between the exposition's modulation and the recapitulation's tonal adjustment is elegant: both achieve their goal dominant through a sharp-side shift, but from different starting points.

# Examples
- General pattern: exposition transition modulates from I to V (sharp-side shift); recapitulation transition shifts to IV (flat-side), then returns to I's dominant (sharp-side), with the goal dominant sounding fresh
- The flat-side shift to IV is typically brief but sufficient to create harmonic variety

# Common Errors
- Expecting the recapitulation's transition to simply eliminate the modulation without providing a substitute harmonic trajectory
- Failing to recognize the flat-side/sharp-side analogy with the exposition

# Common Confusions
- Tonal adjustment is NOT simply "removing the modulation" -- it replaces the exposition's modulation with a new harmonic trajectory that explores the flat side before returning sharp-side to the home-key dominant
- The subordinate theme in the recapitulation does NOT begin on the subordinate key's tonic -- it begins on the HOME key's tonic, which is the whole point of tonal adjustment
