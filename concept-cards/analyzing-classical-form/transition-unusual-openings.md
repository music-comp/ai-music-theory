# Quick Definition
Beyond the four typical opening strategies (new material, main-theme material, false closing section, nontonic region), three unusual strategies occur frequently enough to warrant mention: (1) using later portions of the main theme (continuation or cadential idea); (2) beginning with B-section material from a small-ternary main theme; (3) having the A-prime section of a small ternary "become" a transition when it departs from the expected path and leads to dominant closure.

# Core Definition
Whereas the majority of transitions begin with one of the four typical opening strategies, three less common procedures occur frequently enough to warrant discussion. (1) Later portions of the main theme: the transition may open with material from the main theme's continuation phrase or cadential idea (rather than its basic idea), developing it to lead elsewhere. (2) B-section material: if the main theme is a small ternary, the transition may start with material from the B section, giving the impression that B and A-prime sections are going to be repeated in the manner of a rounded binary. (3) A-prime "becomes" transition: the main theme may be forming as a small ternary, but the A-prime section departs from the A section's path and leads to dominant closure instead of the expected home-key PAC. In retrospect, we understand that the A-prime section has "become" a transition. Two factors distinguish this from a two-part transition: (a) A and B sections of a ternary must not elide (if they do, it is a two-part transition); (b) B sections tend to emphasize home-key dominant at the beginning, whereas transitions tend to emphasize tonic (Caplin, Ch. 11, pp. 359-365).

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Check whether the transition material comes from later parts of the main theme
2. If the main theme is a small ternary, check for B-section material opening the transition
3. If A-prime material appears to depart, check whether it leads to home-key PAC (ternary) or dominant closure (transition)
4. Use two criteria to distinguish from two-part transition: elision and opening harmony emphasis

# Context & Application
These unusual strategies create different kinds of formal ambiguity. The A-prime-becomes-transition strategy is particularly significant because it creates a situation where three cadentially defined units (A with PAC, B with HC, A-prime/transition with HC) can be interpreted as either a ternary main theme or a main theme + two-part transition. Caplin provides clear criteria for distinguishing these interpretations.

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 359-360): Haydn, String Quartet Op. 50/1, iv, mm. 13-24 -- Transition begins by repeating the cadential gesture from the main theme's ending and develops it all the way to the HC.
**Example 2** (pp. 359-360): Beethoven, Piano Sonata Op. 31/3, ii, mm. 28-35 -- Transition starts with contrasting middle material from the small-ternary main theme; the V7/VI harmony that seemed "wrong" in the B section now serves as the "real" (and only) harmony of the transition.
**Example 3** (pp. 360-361): Beethoven, Piano Sonata Op. 2/3, iv, mm. 1-29 -- A-prime section begins at m. 19 but departs from A section's path at m. 22; modulates to subordinate key; HC at m. 29; A-prime retrospectively understood as modulating transition.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Automatically labeling three cadential units as two-part transition
  **Correction**: Check whether the second unit elides with the first (if yes: two-part transition) and whether it emphasizes dominant at the start (if yes: B section of ternary, not transition)
