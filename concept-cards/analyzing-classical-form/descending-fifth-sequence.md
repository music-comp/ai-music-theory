# Quick Definition
The most commonly used sequential progression, featuring chords whose roots descend by fifths (or ascend by fourths) in a "circle of fifths" pattern.

# Formal Definition
The most commonly used sequential progression features chords whose roots are organized into a series of descending fifths (most often alternating with ascending fourths). This "circle-of-fifths" progression can be varied in manifold ways through chord inversion, chromatic alteration, and added dissonances.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Basic pattern: III - VI - II - V - I (roots descend by fifth)

Compared to other sequences, the descending-fifth pattern features the strongest harmonic-functional expression. Since the root motion of a descending fifth lies at the basis of every dominant-to-tonic progression, this functional relation is implied, by analogy, at each link in the sequential chain.

For example:
- VI - II implies V-I relationship
- III - VI implies V-I relationship
- Even without actual leading tones, the "dominant-to-tonic" motion is suggested

Variations include:
- Chord inversions
- Chromatic alterations (secondary dominants)
- Added dissonances (sevenths throughout)

# Musical Context/Application
The descending-fifth sequence:
- Is the most frequently employed sequential progression
- Often appears between prolongational and cadential sections
- May facilitate modulation to new keys
- Creates strong forward momentum through implied dominant-tonic motion

The strong harmonic-functional expression distinguishes this sequence from other types, making it feel less purely contrapuntal.

# Common Confusions
- Not recognizing the implied V-I relationships at each step (even without leading tones)
- Treating the sequential harmonies as fully functional rather than pattern-driven
- Confusing with diatonic circle progressions that have genuine functional intent
- Assuming all descending-fifth motion is sequential (cadential V-I is not)
