# Quick Definition
In passages with alternating V and I chords, determining which harmony is primary (prolonged) requires careful analysis of metric position, resolution direction, melodic emphasis, and contextual function -- there is no mechanical procedure.

# Core Definition
Alternating V-I patterns can be heard as either tonic prolongation ((V) I - (V) I - (V) I) or dominant prolongation (V - (I) V - (I) V - (I) V). Determining factors include: metric position (which harmony falls on strong beats), resolution direction (does V resolve to I, or does I move to V), melodic emphasis (does the melody emphasize scale degree 1 or 5), and contextual function (what does the passage need to accomplish). No formula always works; musical judgment, careful listening, and consideration of context are required. In genuinely ambiguous cases, both interpretations may be valid and the passage may deliberately blur the distinction.

# Key Properties
1. Tonic prolongation: (V) I - (V) I -- I is primary, V is subordinate
2. Dominant prolongation: V - (I) V - (I) V -- V is primary, I is subordinate
3. Metric position is a key factor
4. Resolution direction helps distinguish
5. Melodic emphasis provides additional evidence
6. Contextual function constrains interpretation
7. No mechanical procedure exists

| Presentation phrase | Must be tonic prolongation |

# Context & Application
This analytical challenge arises most prominently in the contrasting middle of small ternary form, where the determination affects whether the B section is classified as a standing on the dominant. The same challenge recurs in other formal contexts: transition endings, postcadential areas, and development passages. When the analysis is genuinely ambiguous, acknowledging the ambiguity is preferable to forcing a single interpretation.
