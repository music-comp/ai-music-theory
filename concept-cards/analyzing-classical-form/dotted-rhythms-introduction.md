# Quick Definition
Dotted rhythms in slow introductions derive from the high-Baroque French overture style and contribute to the sense of solemnity, majesty, and ceremonial pomp that characterizes the stately side of classical slow introductions.

# Core Definition
The sense of solemnity in a slow introduction is expressed, in part, by dotted rhythms (often double-dotted), which derive from the high-Baroque French overture style. Along with slow tempo, fanfare gestures, tutti textures, and an initial forte dynamic, dotted rhythms create a courtly sense of pomp and circumstance. However, in the classical slow introduction, these stately qualities are combined with anticipatory, hesitant gestures -- marked discontinuities, harmonic instabilities, minor modality, and chromaticism -- that dramatize the sense of introduction by making it a more premonitory event than a purely ceremonial opening.

# Prerequisites
- Knowledge of French overture style and its characteristic features

# Key Properties
- Derives from high-Baroque French overture style
- Contributes to solemnity, majesty, and ceremonial quality
- Often double-dotted in the classical period
- Associated with fanfare gestures and forte dynamic
- Frequently in tutti texture
- Juxtaposed with hesitant, uncertain passages creating the characteristic dual affect

# Construction / Recognition
Identify dotted or double-dotted rhythmic patterns (dotted quarter + eighth, dotted eighth + sixteenth, or double-dotted equivalents) appearing in the slow tempo opening of a sonata movement. These patterns typically appear in forte dynamic with full orchestral texture and are associated with fanfare-like melodic gestures.

# Context & Application
Dotted rhythms are one half of the expressive duality that defines classical slow introductions. They project the stately, heraldic character inherited from the French overture tradition, while hesitant gestures project the anticipatory, uncertain quality unique to the classical style. The interplay of these two affects creates the dramatic tension that characterizes the slow introduction as a "before-the-beginning" section.

# Examples
- Haydn, Symphony No. 104 ("London"), i: opening fanfare with dotted rhythms creates dignified solemnity, immediately followed by hesitant continuation with dangling leading tones
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 7, Op. 92, i: extremely pronounced dotted rhythms in the extensive slow introduction create almost overwhelming sense of ceremony

# Common Errors
- Assuming dotted rhythms alone define the character of a slow introduction without considering the hesitant gestures that provide counterbalance
- Failing to recognize the French overture ancestry of classical dotted rhythm patterns

# Common Confusions
- Dotted rhythms in slow introductions are NOT the same as those in dance movements or marches -- in the introduction context, they specifically invoke the French overture tradition and are combined with hesitant, anticipatory qualities
