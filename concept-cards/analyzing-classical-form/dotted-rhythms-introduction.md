# Dotted Rhythms in Slow Introduction

The characteristic rhythmic pattern associated with slow introductions.

## Origin

Derives from high-Baroque "French overture" style, where dotted (often double-dotted) rhythms were standard.

## Character

Dotted rhythms contribute to:
- Sense of solemnity and majesty
- Courtly pomp and circumstance
- Heraldic, ceremonial quality
- Stately, dignified affect

## Typical Patterns

- Dotted quarter + eighth (or double-dotted equivalents)
- Associated with fanfare gestures
- Often in forte dynamic
- Frequently in tutti texture

## Relationship to Hesitancy

Though dotted rhythms suggest ceremony and grandeur, they may be juxtaposed with hesitant, uncertain passages to create the characteristic dual affect of classical slow introductions.

## Continuation in Beethoven

Beethoven's late works sometimes feature extremely pronounced dotted rhythms in introductions (e.g., Seventh Symphony), creating almost overwhelming sense of ceremony.
