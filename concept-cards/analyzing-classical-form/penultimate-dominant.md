# Quick Definition
The dominant harmony that occupies the "before-the-last" position in an authentic cadential progression, creating dynamic impulse into the final tonic.

# Formal Definition
In the authentic cadential progression, the dominant occupies the penultimate ("before-the-last") position and thus creates a powerful dynamic impulse into the final tonic. Whether a dominant is characterized as penultimate or ultimate has nothing to do with harmony per se; rather, it is a matter of how the dominant groups with its surrounding harmonies.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
The penultimate dominant:
- Precedes the final tonic in authentic cadential progressions
- Groups WITH the following tonic as part of the same progression
- May be a triad or seventh chord (V or V7)
- Must be in root position for cadential status
- Creates tension driving toward resolution

The penultimate position allows for greater dissonance:
- Adding a seventh is appropriate and common
- The dissonance enhances drive toward resolution

# Musical Context/Application
The penultimate dominant:
- Creates the characteristic tension before cadential resolution
- Groups with the following tonic (unlike ultimate dominant)
- May be embellished by cadential six-four
- Supports the strongest form of tonal confirmation

The distinction between penultimate and ultimate is about formal grouping: the penultimate dominant groups forward with tonic; the ultimate dominant ends the progression.

# Examples
Example 1.11 (p. 37): The V7 in the progression I6 - II6 - V7 - I occupies the penultimate position, driving toward the final tonic.

Example 1.12 (p. 38): The dominant embellished by cadential six-four (V with 6-4 to 5-3) in penultimate position.

The boxed text on pp. 39-40 explicitly distinguishes ultimate and penultimate dominants: "In the case of a penultimate dominant (as found in an authentic cadential progression), the dominant groups with the following tonic as part of the progression."

# Common Confusions
- Confusing penultimate dominant (authentic cadence) with ultimate dominant (half cadence)
- Thinking the distinction relates to chord structure rather than grouping
- Not recognizing that seventh chords are appropriate for penultimate but not ultimate dominants
- Forgetting that grouping, not harmony, defines the distinction
