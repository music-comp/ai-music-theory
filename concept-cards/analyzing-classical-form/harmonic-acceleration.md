# Quick Definition
A faster rate of harmonic change in the continuation compared to the presentation. Not always mechanical to identify: when the b.i. contains ornamental harmonies, the "effective" harmonic rhythm of the presentation may be slower than literally notated. Identifying acceleration requires musical judgment.

# Core Definition
Continuation function typically brings a faster rate of harmonic change compared to that of the presentation (p. 73). It can be tricky to determine whether or not the harmonies of the continuation change at a faster rate, especially when the basic idea itself contains multiple harmonies that may be merely ornamental and may not affect the broader sense of harmonic rhythm (p. 73). Identifying harmonic acceleration is not a mechanical procedure, but rather one that relies on a good deal of musical judgment and experience. There are few examples where harmonic acceleration alone is responsible for continuation function; rather, it is used in conjunction with other criteria (p. 75).

# Key Properties
1. Faster rate of harmonic change in continuation vs. presentation
2. May be evident on the surface or require analytical judgment
3. When b.i. has ornamental harmonies, effective harmonic rhythm may be slower than notated
4. Not a mechanical procedure; requires musical judgment
5. Rarely sole indicator of continuation function; usually combined with other characteristics
6. Even ornamental harmonies in continuation can project acceleration

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Compare harmonic rhythm of presentation with continuation
2. Distinguish structural from ornamental harmonies
3. Judge the "effective" harmonic rhythm, not just literal changes
4. Look for more frequent root-position changes in the continuation

# Context & Application
Harmonic acceleration is particularly effective for expressing continuation function when fragmentation is absent (see Mozart K. 402, Ex. 2.19). Even embellishing harmonies that do not affect the prolongational structure can contribute to the perception of acceleration.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 73, Ex. 2.18): Beethoven, Op. 119, No. 1 -- ornamental harmonies in presentation give effective rate of one per measure; continuation clearly has two per measure.

**Example 2** (p. 74, Ex. 2.19): Mozart, K. 402, i -- acceleration compensates for absence of fragmentation.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Mechanically counting harmonies without considering structural weight
  **Correction**: Distinguish ornamental from structural harmonies; judge effective rate

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Ignoring ornamental harmonies in the continuation because they don't affect prolongation
  **Clarification**: Even ornamental harmonies contribute to the perception of acceleration
