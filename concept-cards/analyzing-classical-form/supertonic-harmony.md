# Quick Definition
The triad or seventh chord built on the second scale degree (II), functioning as a pre-dominant, with the first-inversion form (II6) being more frequently employed than the subdominant (IV).

# Formal Definition
Many harmony texts suggest that the subdominant triad (IV) is the most typical pre-dominant harmony. Examination of the classical literature reveals, however, that the supertonic triad in first inversion (II6) is more often employed for this function. Both II6 and IV can be enriched through the addition of dissonant sevenths, and greater variety can be gained through modal borrowing.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Forms of supertonic harmony:
- **II6**: First inversion (most common pre-dominant)
- **II**: Root position (less common as pre-dominant)
- **II7**: With added seventh
- **II°, II°7**: Diminished forms in minor or through mixture

Position in cadential progressions:
- Typically follows initial tonic (I6)
- Precedes cadential dominant (V)
- Standard progression: I6 - II6 - V7 - I

The preference for II6 over IV in classical music may relate to the smoother bass motion (scale degree 4 to 5) and the stronger pull toward dominant.

# Musical Context/Application
Supertonic harmony:
- Is the most frequently used pre-dominant in classical music (as II6)
- Shares scale degree 4 with subdominant (IV)
- May be enhanced with seventh or through modal borrowing
- Creates strong half-step motion in bass (4 to leading tone to 5)

The recognition that II6 is more common than IV as a pre-dominant is an important correction to the emphasis of many textbooks.

# Common Confusions
- Assuming IV is the "primary" pre-dominant (II6 is actually more common)
- Not recognizing the functional equivalence of II and IV
- Forgetting that II6 places scale degree 4 in the bass (like IV)
- Confusing II in minor (diminished) with II in major (minor triad)
