# Quick Definition
When material from the exposition's closing section is deleted in the recapitulation but restored at the end of the coda, the coda can give the impression of being interpolated within the recapitulation rather than appended after it.

# Core Definition
If material from the exposition's closing section is deleted in the recapitulation (because the composer either employs new material for the recapitulation's closing section or starts the coda immediately after the subordinate-theme group), the coda likely restores that deleted material as its own closing section. When this happens, the coda can give the impression of being interpolated in, rather than following on, the recapitulation. Haydn frequently employs this technique, which, given his tendency to significantly rewrite the recapitulation vis-a-vis the exposition, can create considerable ambiguity as to whether the movement contains an independent coda.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of the coda as an after-the-end function
- Knowledge of closing section treatment in recapitulation

# Key Properties
- Exposition's closing section material is deleted from the recapitulation
- Deleted material reappears at end of coda's closing section
- Creates impression the coda is inserted within rather than appended after the recapitulation
- Particularly associated with Haydn's compositional practice
- Creates analytical ambiguity about coda independence

# Construction / Recognition
Compare the closing sections of exposition, recapitulation, and coda. If the recapitulation's closing section uses different material from the exposition's, and the coda's closing section restores the exposition's original closing material, the coda functions as an interpolation. The analytical rule (coda starts where recapitulation stops corresponding to exposition) helps locate the boundary, but the perceived effect may still be one of interpolation rather than addition.

# Context & Application
This technique is one reason why identifying the start of a coda can be analytically challenging. The effect is especially pronounced in Haydn, who significantly rewrites recapitulations and frequently blurs the boundary between recapitulation and coda. The listener may experience the coda not as a separate section but as an interruption within an ongoing recapitulatory process.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Trio in C, H. 27, iii: new closing section at m. 202 replaces exposition's closing material; original material restored at end of coda (mm. 255-61), creating interpolation effect
- General Haydn pattern: rewritten recapitulation closing section followed by coda that restores original exposition closing material

# Common Errors
- Assuming the coda always begins at an audibly clear boundary
- Treating every Haydn movement with restored closing material as lacking a true coda

# Common Confusions
- Interpolation is an effect of perception, not a formal category -- analytically, the coda still begins where the recapitulation stops corresponding to the exposition, even if the listener perceives continuity rather than a new section
