# Quick Definition
A shift to minor mode in the subordinate theme brings into play tonal regions (bIII, bVI, bVII, bII) that would otherwise be considered remote in a strictly major-mode context. Prominent tonicization of these regions is a loosening device typical of subordinate themes but not of main themes, and in extreme cases can reach regions a tritone removed from the key.

# Core Definition
A shift to minor mode in the subordinate theme introduces tonal regions that would be considered remote in a strictly major-mode context: bIII (flat-three), bVI (flat-six), bVII (flat-seven), and bII (flat-two/Neapolitan area). The prominent tonicization of these regions is another loosening device typical of subordinate themes but not of main themes. In extreme cases, ascending-third sequential progressions at the beginning of the theme can result in tonicization of regions a tritone removed from the key -- the most distant possible tonal relationship. The tonicization of bVI is especially common and may be prolonged across several measures, often incorporated into an ECP through chromatic variants. An augmented triad may appear as a chromatic variant of the initial cadential tonic, and a diminished-seventh chord may substitute for the pre-dominant, while the sustained bass note implies prolongation of the bVI region (Caplin, Ch. 12, pp. 419-420).

# Key Properties
1. Modal shift to minor enables remote regions: bIII, bVI, bVII, bII
2. Prominent tonicization = loosening device for subordinate themes
3. Not found in tight-knit main themes
4. Extreme case: tritone-related tonicization (most distant relationship)
5. bVI tonicization especially common, often incorporated into ECP
6. Chromatic variants: augmented triads, diminished sevenths

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Note any shift to minor mode within the subordinate theme
2. Check for prominent tonicization of chromatic regions (bIII, bVI, bVII, bII)
3. Track how the remote region is reached (often through sequential progressions)
4. Note if the tonicization is incorporated into an ECP
5. Observe how the music returns to the subordinate key for the confirming PAC

# Context & Application
Tonicization of remote regions is one of several harmonic-tonal loosening devices available in subordinate themes (alongside modal shift and modulating subordinate themes). The exploration of remote regions creates tonal instability that contrasts dramatically with the stable tonic prolongation of the main theme, contributing to the subordinate theme's overall loose character. The eventual return to the subordinate key for the confirming PAC creates a sense of tonal drama and resolution.

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 419-420): Mozart -- Repeat of subordinate theme begins with shift to minor mode; descending-fifth sequential progression leads to bVI region; ingenious variation of ECP incorporates augmented triad as chromatic variant of initial cadential tonic; sustained bass implies bVI prolongation.
**Example 2** (p. 420): Ascending-third sequential progression at theme's beginning results in tonicization of bIII and then a region a tritone removed from the key -- the most distant possible tonal relationship.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating remote tonicizations as genuine key changes
  **Correction**: These are tonicizations (brief emphases), not true modulations; the subordinate key is always restored for the confirming PAC
