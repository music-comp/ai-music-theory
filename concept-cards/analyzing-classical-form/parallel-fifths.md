# Quick Definition
A voice-leading error where two voices move in parallel motion maintaining the interval of a perfect fifth, avoided in common-practice harmony through techniques like first-inversion chords and the 5-6 pattern.

# Core Definition
Parallel fifths occur when two voices move in parallel motion maintaining a perfect fifth, creating loss of voice independence. This problem is particularly acute in sequential progressions with stepwise root motion. Caplin discusses two solutions: for descending-second sequences, "the unembellished form ... finds all of the chords in first inversion, thus eliminating any interval of a fifth against the bass" (p. 45); for ascending-second sequences, "the parallel fifths are broken up by means of a 5-6 pattern" (p. 45).

# Prerequisites
- **Voice leading** — Parallel fifths are a voice-leading concern

# Key Properties
1. Two voices moving in same direction maintaining a perfect fifth
2. Considered a voice-leading error in common-practice style
3. Creates loss of voice independence
4. Especially problematic in stepwise sequential progressions
5. Solution for descending seconds: use all first-inversion chords
6. Solution for ascending seconds: use the 5-6 pattern

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Check any two voices for parallel motion at the interval of a perfect fifth
2. In sequences, assess whether root-position stepwise motion creates parallel fifths
3. Look for the corrective techniques: first-inversion chords or 5-6 pattern
4. Note that chromatic alterations can also help by creating secondary dominants

# Context & Application
Parallel fifths are a concern in stepwise sequential progressions, direct chord-to-chord motion in root position, and voice-leading between any pair of voices. Composers resolve this through strategic inversions, interpolated passing chords, contrapuntal patterns (5-6, 7-6), and chromatic alterations. The German sixth's resolution also requires attention to avoid parallel fifths between lowered 6-to-5 and lowered 3-to-5.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 45): Example 1.21 discusses the descending-second sequence: "If the chords were placed in root position, then parallel fifths could easily arise."

**Example 2** (pp. 45-46): Example 1.22 shows the ascending-second sequence using "a 5-6 pattern formed by one of the upper voices against the bass" to avoid parallel fifths.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Not recognizing where parallel fifths would occur without intervention
  **Correction**: Root-position stepwise motion between adjacent chords frequently creates parallel fifths

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming all parallel motion is prohibited
  **Clarification**: Only parallel perfect fifths and perfect octaves are prohibited; parallel thirds, sixths, etc. are fine
