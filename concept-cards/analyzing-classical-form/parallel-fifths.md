# Quick Definition
A voice-leading error where two voices move in parallel motion maintaining the interval of a perfect fifth, typically avoided in common-practice harmony through specific chord voicings and techniques.

# Formal Definition
Parallel fifths occur when two voices move in parallel motion (same direction, same interval) while maintaining the interval of a perfect fifth. This voice-leading problem must be addressed in sequential progressions, particularly those with stepwise root motion, through techniques such as using first-inversion chords or introducing a 5-6 pattern.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
The problem:
- Parallel perfect fifths between any two voices
- Considered a voice-leading error in common-practice style
- Creates loss of voice independence
- Must be avoided or broken up

Solutions in sequential progressions:
- **Descending-second sequence**: Use all first-inversion chords (eliminates fifth against bass)
- **Ascending-second sequence**: Use 5-6 pattern (fifth resolves to sixth before next fifth)
- Both sequences may also use chromatic alterations creating secondary dominants

The 5-6 pattern generates intervening first-inversion chords that break the parallel motion.

# Musical Context/Application
Parallel fifths are a concern in:
- Stepwise sequential progressions (both ascending and descending)
- Direct chord-to-chord motion in root position
- Voice-leading between any pair of voices

Composers resolve this issue through:
- Strategic inversions
- Interpolated passing chords
- Contrapuntal patterns (5-6, 7-6)
- Chromatic alterations

# Examples
Example 1.21 (p. 45) discusses the descending-second sequence: "If the chords were placed in root position, then parallel fifths could easily arise. Therefore, the unembellished form of the descending-stepwise progression finds all of the chords in first inversion, thus eliminating any interval of a fifth against the bass."

Example 1.22 (pp. 45-46) shows the ascending-second sequence: "The stepwise ascent usually remains in root position, while the parallel fifths are broken up by means of a 5-6 pattern formed by one of the upper voices against the bass."

# Common Confusions
- Not recognizing where parallel fifths would occur without intervention
- Forgetting that first-inversion eliminates fifths against bass
- Missing the 5-6 pattern as a parallel-fifth solution
- Assuming all parallel motion is prohibited (only perfect fifths and octaves)
