# Quick Definition
Fragmentation must be identified in relation to the largest grouping unit defined by repetition, not just the surface-level unit sizes. In a compound sentence, the repeated compound basic idea (4 m.) is the largest unit; so when 2-m. fragments appear in the continuation, this IS fragmentation, even though individual ideas within the c.b.i. were already 2 m.

# Core Definition
Caplin warns that recognizing fragmentation requires identifying the largest grouping unit established by repetition in the preceding material. In a compound presentation, for example, the compound basic idea (4 m.) is the largest repeated unit. When 2-m. units appear in the continuation, this constitutes fragmentation relative to those 4-m. units, even though the individual ideas (basic idea, contrasting idea) within the c.b.i. were each 2 m. Focusing only on surface-level groupings would miss this fragmentation entirely (p. 201). Further fragmentation can occur at multiple levels: 4-m. units to 2-m. units (first stage), then 2-m. units to 1-m. units (second stage).

# Key Properties
1. Fragmentation is always relative to the LARGEST repeated grouping unit
2. In compound presentations, the 4-m. c.b.i. is the repeated unit, not its 2-m. internal ideas
3. Surface-level groupings can be misleading
4. Multiple stages of fragmentation are possible (4 m. to 2 m. to 1 m.)
5. The principle applies to both simple and compound themes

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Locate the largest grouping unit established by repetition in the presentation
2. In a compound sentence, this is the 4-m. compound basic idea
3. When continuation units are shorter than this largest unit, fragmentation is present
4. Check for further stages of fragmentation (2 m. to 1 m.)
5. Do not be misled by the internal sub-units of compound ideas

# Context & Application
This analytical principle is especially important in compound sentences, where the compound presentation's internal structure (2-m. basic idea + 2-m. contrasting idea) might suggest that 2-m. continuation units are not fragmented. Caplin explicitly warns against this error (p. 201), using the Beethoven Violin Concerto (Ex. 6.9) as illustration: the 4-m. c.b.i. is the relevant unit, so 2-m. fragments in the continuation clearly project fragmentation.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 201, Ex. 6.9): Beethoven, Violin Concerto, Op. 61, i -- compound presentation with repeated 4-m. c.b.i.; continuation fragments to 2-m. units, clearly projecting fragmentation relative to the 4-m. repeated unit.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Viewing fragmentation only in terms of surface-level unit sizes (e.g., 2-m. ideas)
  **Correction**: Always compare to the largest grouping unit defined by repetition; in compound themes, this is the 4-m. c.b.i., not its internal 2-m. sub-units

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking there is no fragmentation when continuation units are the same size as the basic idea within a compound presentation
  **Clarification**: The relevant comparison is to the entire compound basic idea (4 m.), not to its internal components
