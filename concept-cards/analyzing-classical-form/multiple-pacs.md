# Quick Definition
When a subordinate-theme group contains multiple PACs, the question of their relative importance arises. Hepokoski-Darcy privilege the first PAC as "essential expositional closure," while Caplin argues that syntactically all PACs are equally strong; differences are rhetorical (expressive), not structural, and each exposition should be analyzed on its own terms.

# Core Definition
The concept of multiple subordinate themes brings with it the notion that a sonata exposition will contain multiple PACs in the subordinate key. Hepokoski and Darcy argue that the first SK: PAC is of greatest structural import, terming it the "essential expositional closure" (EEC) -- the principal tonal goal of the exposition. An alternative view emphasizes the final PAC, which often comes after a massive ECP and features the greatest cadential articulation. Caplin distinguishes syntactical from rhetorical strength: syntactically, all PACs are equally strong because they all fulfill the necessary requirements for thematic closure; rhetorically, cadences display a range of expression, from weak and tame to highly dramatic and assertive, and this varies from piece to piece. Caplin recommends analyzing each subordinate-theme group on its own terms rather than assuming any PAC is automatically most important (Ch. 12, pp. 409-410).

# Key Properties
1. Multiple subordinate themes produce multiple PACs in subordinate key
2. Hepokoski-Darcy: first PAC = "essential expositional closure" (EEC)
3. Alternative: final PAC may be most dramatically powerful
4. Caplin: syntactically, all PACs are equally strong
5. Differences among PACs are rhetorical, not structural
6. Each exposition should be analyzed ad hoc

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Count the PACs in the subordinate-theme group
2. Assess each PAC's rhetorical weight (dynamic level, preparation, dramatic context)
3. Note which is first and which is last
4. Avoid automatically privileging either
5. Consider the specific compositional context

# Context & Application
This theoretical debate has practical analytical implications. If one adopts Hepokoski-Darcy's EEC concept, all material after the first PAC is "postcadential" closing material. If one follows Caplin's view that multiple genuine subordinate themes can each close with a PAC, the formal analysis recognizes distinct thematic units with individual formal structures. Caplin's position allows for richer formal analysis of the subordinate-theme group.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the first PAC is always the most structurally important
  **Correction**: Syntactically all PACs are equivalent; evaluate rhetorical differences on a case-by-case basis

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating syntactical and rhetorical cadential strength
  **Clarification**: Syntactical strength depends on whether cadential requirements are met (all PACs are equal); rhetorical strength depends on dramatic context, preparation, and emphasis (this varies)
