# Quick Definition
A type of b.i. repetition where the original (model) is completely transposed to another scale degree (sequence): both melody AND harmony transposed by the same interval and direction. Rare in presentations because tonic prolongation is required. Distinguished from statement-response by checking if harmony is transposed (sequential) or changes from I-emphasis to V-emphasis (statement-response).

# Core Definition
In this type, the original, also termed a model, is completely transposed to another scale degree to create a sequence. Both the melody and the harmony (as well as all accompanying material) are transposed by the same interval and direction (p. 66). Because the overall harmonic support of a presentation phrase must be tonic prolongational, sequential repetition (with its implied sequential progression) is rarely used at the beginning of the sentence. When it is, the sense of broader tonic prolongation usually emerges later in the theme (p. 67).

# Key Properties
1. Complete transposition: melody AND harmony by same interval/direction
2. Also called model-sequence technique
3. Rare in presentations (conflicts with tonic prolongation requirement)
4. When used, tonic prolongation often deferred to continuation
5. Distinguished from statement-response by harmonic context, not melody
6. Typical patterns: I/II or I/VII (model on I, sequence on II or VII)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Check if BOTH melody and harmony are transposed by the same interval
2. If yes: sequential repetition
3. If melody is transposed but harmony changes from I to V emphasis: statement-response
4. Key test: compare harmonic progressions, not just melodic transposition

# Context & Application
Sequential repetition is rare in simple 8-m. sentences. It creates instability that may delay tonic prolongation. The distinction from statement-response is crucial: compare Exs. 2.10 (statement-response, melody up a step but I to V) with 2.3 (sequential, complete transposition up a step).

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 61, Ex. 2.3): Beethoven, Op. 14, No. 2 -- b.i. transposed sequentially up a step; both melody and harmony rise by a step.

**Example 2** (p. 67, Ex. 2.12): Beethoven, Op. 30, No. 1, ii -- b.i. transposed sequentially down a third.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Classifying as sequential whenever the melody is transposed
  **Correction**: Both melody and harmony must be transposed by the same interval

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing sequential with statement-response when melody rises by a step
  **Clarification**: Check the harmony: if it shifts I to V = statement-response; if fully transposed = sequential (p. 67)
