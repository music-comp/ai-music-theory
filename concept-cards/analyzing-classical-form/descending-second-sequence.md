# Quick Definition
A frequently used sequential progression with roots descending by step, typically using first-inversion chords throughout to avoid parallel fifths.

# Formal Definition
Sequential progressions by descending seconds occur often in the literature; however, they pose a potential problem of voice leading. If the chords were placed in root position, then parallel fifths could easily arise. Therefore, the unembellished form of the descending-stepwise progression finds all of the chords in first inversion, thus eliminating any interval of a fifth against the bass.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Basic pattern: IV6 - III6 - II6 - I6 (roots descend by step, all in first inversion)

Voice-leading considerations:
- Root-position stepwise motion creates parallel fifths
- First-inversion eliminates fifths against bass
- All chords in first inversion maintains proper voice leading

Common embellishment:
- A series of 7-6 suspensions
- Creates alternation between seventh chords and triads
- Adds melodic interest while maintaining descending motion

# Musical Context/Application
The descending-second sequence:
- Is frequently used in classical music
- Requires careful voice-leading management
- First-inversion chords solve parallel fifth problem
- Often embellished with 7-6 suspensions

Example 1.4 (Mozart, K. 332, mm. 54-56) references a "stepwise-descending sequential progression."

# Common Confusions
- Using root-position chords (creates parallel fifths)
- Not recognizing the voice-leading motivation for first-inversion usage
- Confusing with descending passing motion within prolongational progressions
- Missing the 7-6 suspension embellishment
