# Quick Definition
An arrangement of the twelve pitch classes by ascending or descending perfect fifths, underlying the most common sequential progression type and the fundamental V-I harmonic relationship.

# Formal Definition
The circle of fifths describes the relationship among pitch classes organized by the interval of a perfect fifth. This relationship underlies the most commonly used sequential progression, which features chords whose roots are organized into a series of descending fifths. The circle-of-fifths progression is frequently called by this name due to its characteristic root motion.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
The circle-of-fifths sequence:
- Roots descend by fifth (or ascend by fourth)
- Pattern: III - VI - II - V - I (and further)
- Features strongest harmonic-functional expression of all sequence types
- Each step implies dominant-to-tonic motion by analogy

Functional implication:
- Root motion of descending fifth underlies every V-I progression
- This functional relation is implied "by analogy" at each sequential step
- Even without actual leading tones, dominant-to-tonic motion is suggested

# Musical Context/Application
The circle of fifths in sequential progressions:
- Creates the most frequently used sequential pattern
- May be varied through inversions, chromatic alterations, added dissonances
- The functional implication distinguishes it from other sequence types
- Can extend through many fifths before returning to functional harmony

# Common Confusions
- Treating every circle-of-fifths motion as functional (in sequences, the motion is pattern-based)
- Not recognizing the implied V-I analogy at each step
- Confusing circle motion with literal modulation through all keys
- Assuming circle-of-fifths sequences must complete the full circle
