# Quick Definition
An arrangement of pitch classes by perfect fifths that underlies the most common sequential progression type, featuring roots descending by fifth and implying dominant-to-tonic motion at each step.

# Core Definition
The circle of fifths describes the relationship among pitch classes organized by the interval of a perfect fifth. In Caplin's usage, it primarily refers to the descending-fifth ("circle-of-fifths") sequential progression, the most frequently used sequence type in classical music. "Since the root motion of a descending fifth lies at the basis of every dominant-to-tonic progression, this functional relation is implied, by analogy, at each link in the sequential chain" (p. 43).

# Prerequisites
- **Root motion** — The circle-of-fifths sequence is classified by its descending-fifth root motion
- **Harmonic destabilization** — Sequences function to destabilize harmonic activity

# Key Properties
1. Roots descend by fifth (or ascend by fourth): III-VI-II-V-I
2. Most frequently used sequential progression type
3. Each step implies dominant-to-tonic motion by analogy
4. Features the strongest functional implication of all sequence types
5. Can be varied through inversions, chromatic alterations, and added dissonances

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Look for a series of chords whose roots descend by fifth
2. Verify the consistent intervallic pattern between successive roots
3. Note that the functional implication (V-I analogy) distinguishes this from other sequences
4. Check for chromatic variants that intensify the functional implications

# Context & Application
The descending-fifth sequence is especially appropriate for destabilizing harmonic activity within a key or for effecting modulation. Its functional suggestiveness (each step implying V-I) makes it the most harmonically convincing sequence type. It may be varied "in manifold ways through changes in the position of the chords and through chromatic alteration" (p. 42).

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Root motion** — Classified by its descending-fifth root motion pattern
## Enables
- **Harmonic destabilization** — Primary means of destabilizing a tonal center
## Related
- **Chromatic alteration** — Can intensify the circle-of-fifths pattern through secondary dominants
- **Dominant seventh chord** — The V-I analogy at each step relates to dominant function
## Contrasts With
- None specific; other sequence types (ascending fifth, descending/ascending second, descending/ascending third) are less common or less functionally suggestive

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating every circle-of-fifths motion as fully functional harmony
  **Correction**: Within sequences, the motion is pattern-based; only the first and last harmonies typically carry genuine functional weight

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming circle-of-fifths sequences must complete the full circle through all twelve keys
  **Clarification**: Sequential progressions typically traverse only a portion of the circle before re-engaging functional harmony
