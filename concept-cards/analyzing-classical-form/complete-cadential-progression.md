# Quick Definition
A cadential progression that contains all of its component harmonic functions: initial tonic, pre-dominant, dominant, and (for authentic) final tonic.

# Formal Definition
A cadential progression is complete if it contains all of its component harmonic functions. For an authentic cadential progression: I6 - II6 - V7 - I; for a half-cadential progression: I6 - II6 - V. The distinction between complete and incomplete progressions describes harmonic content, not formal or structural strength.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Complete authentic cadential progression:
- Initial tonic (usually I6) + Pre-dominant (II6 or IV) + Dominant (V or V7) + Final tonic (I)

Complete half-cadential progression:
- Initial tonic (usually I6) + Pre-dominant (II6 or IV) + Dominant (V triad)

All four functions (or three for half cadence) must be present for completeness.

# Musical Context/Application
Whether a composer employs a complete or incomplete cadential progression normally depends on circumstances unique to the given context. There is nothing inadequate about incomplete cadential progressions such that they require a following complete progression to resolve some deficiency.

The choice between complete and incomplete is a matter of compositional design, not hierarchical quality.

# Common Confusions
- Assuming complete progressions are "stronger" or preferable to incomplete ones (both are equally viable)
- Thinking incomplete progressions are "defective" and require resolution
- Confusing completeness of harmonic content with formal or rhetorical strength
