# Quick Definition
A subordinate harmony within a prolongational progression where the prolonged harmony remains in the same position (root position or inversion) at the beginning and end of the progression.

# Formal Definition
An individual harmony is prolonged by one or more neighboring chords when the prolonged harmony remains in the same position (root position or inversion) from the beginning to the end of the progression. In such cases, melodic neighbor-tone motion is usually (but not necessarily) present in one or more of the voices.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Neighboring chord characteristics:
- Prolonged harmony retains same position throughout
- Example: I - V6 - I (tonic remains in root position)
- Melodic neighbor-tone motion often present but not required
- Creates embellishment without change of harmonic position

The distinction between neighboring and passing chords:
- Based on position of the PROLONGED harmony, not literal voice motion
- Neighboring: prolonged harmony stays in same position
- Passing: prolonged harmony changes position

# Musical Context/Application
The simple progression I - V - I, often taught as a "cadence formula," is frequently better understood as prolongational when:
- The V is in first inversion (V6)
- The melody embellishes scale degrees 3 or 5 (rather than 1)
- The context is non-cadential

Neighboring chords typically feature:
- Dominant harmonies (V6, V4/3) neighboring tonic
- Subdominant harmonies (IV) neighboring tonic
- Other functional relationships at different scale degrees

# Common Confusions
- Basing the distinction on literal melodic neighbor motion (it's about harmonic position)
- Confusing all I - V - I progressions as cadential (many are prolongational with neighboring V)
- Not recognizing that the same chord can be neighboring in one context and passing in another
- Thinking neighboring always requires stepwise voice motion
