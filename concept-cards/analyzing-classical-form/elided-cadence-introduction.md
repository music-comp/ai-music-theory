# Quick Definition
Often enough, slow introductions close with an authentic cadence that elides with the beginning of the exposition, so that a single moment simultaneously ends the introduction and begins the main theme.

# Core Definition
In the majority of cases, the harmonic goal of a slow introduction is the dominant of the home key, articulated by an HC or dominant arrival and followed by a standing on the dominant. Often enough, however, introductions close with an authentic cadence that elides with the beginning of the exposition. The resolution to tonic at the start of the main theme simultaneously serves as the cadential goal of the introduction and the initiating harmony of the exposition. Some such cadences could alternatively be heard as evaded rather than elided, in which case the introduction would end without genuine cadential closure. In exceptional cases, the structural end of the introduction may actually occur somewhat after the exposition has begun.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of truncated ternary form in slow introductions
- Knowledge of cadence types and elision

# Key Properties
- Dominant at end of introduction resolves to tonic at start of exposition
- Single moment serves dual function: ending the introduction and beginning the main theme
- Alternative hearing as evaded cadence is sometimes possible
- Distinction between elided and evaded depends on degree of discontinuity
- In exceptional cases, the introduction's structural end may occur after the exposition has begun
- Creates seamless connection between introduction and fast movement

# Construction / Recognition
Examine the boundary between slow introduction and exposition. If the final dominant harmony of the introduction resolves directly to the tonic that begins the main theme with no gap, the cadence is likely elided. Consider whether marked discontinuity in dynamics, texture, or melodic line suggests an evasion instead. If the introduction material continues into the faster tempo, the structural end may extend past the notated tempo change.

# Context & Application
The elided cadence at the end of an introduction creates a powerful moment of formal overlap, where the resolution of one large-scale section simultaneously initiates another. This technique heightens the sense of inevitability at the beginning of the exposition and integrates the introduction more closely with the body of the movement. The ambiguity between elision and evasion is characteristic of the formal looseness typical of slow introductions.

# Examples
- Haydn, Symphony No. 104 ("London"), i: fermata on dominant seventh at m. 16 resolves to tonic beginning the exposition, creating an elided (or possibly evaded) cadence
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in B-flat, K. 454, i: resolution to tonic at m. 14 initiates the exposition while completing the introduction
- Beethoven, Violin Sonata in A minor ("Kreutzer"), Op. 47, i: introduction literally ends on a D-minor sonority sustained into the beginning of the main theme

# Common Errors
- Always assuming the cadence is elided when it may be evaded
- Placing the structural end of the introduction exactly at the tempo change when it may occur earlier or later

# Common Confusions
- An elided cadence is NOT the same as an evaded cadence -- elision implies the cadence is genuinely completed (with the resolution serving double duty), while evasion implies the expected resolution is deflected and no true closure occurs
