# Quick Definition
Surface rhythm acceleration is an increase in rhythmic activity at the musical surface -- shorter note values or greater quantity of attacks in the continuation compared to the presentation. It is one of four continuation characteristics. It is particularly effective in the absence of harmonic acceleration as a means of projecting continuation function.

# Core Definition
Both fragmentation and harmonic acceleration bring about a general sense of increased rhythmic action appropriate for giving the continuation a sense of thematic mobility. As well, such rhythmic animation can be created by the durational values of the individual events lying at the very surface of the musical texture (p. 75). The durational patterns formed by the attack points of every note in a passage create varying rates of activity. In comparison to the presentation, continuation function frequently features shorter note values (or a greater quantity of the same note values), hence an increased motion in the surface rhythm (p. 75). Increasing surface rhythm is particularly effective in the absence of harmonic acceleration (p. 75).

# Prerequisites
- **Continuation phrase** — Surface rhythm acceleration occurs in the continuation
- **Presentation phrase** — The reference point for measuring acceleration

# Key Properties
1. Shorter note values in continuation compared to presentation
2. Or greater quantity/density of same note values
3. One of four continuation characteristics (independent process)
4. Particularly effective when harmonic acceleration is absent
5. Works together with fragmentation and harmonic acceleration
6. Creates sense of being "in the middle" -- medial function
7. Measured by comparing attack-point density between presentation and continuation

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Compare the predominant note values in the presentation to the continuation
2. Look for shorter note values (e.g., quarter notes to eighth notes)
3. Look for greater density of attacks
4. Note whether rhythmic motives from the b.i. come to dominate the texture
5. Particularly significant when other continuation characteristics are weak or absent

# Context & Application
Surface rhythm acceleration often works in coordination with fragmentation and harmonic acceleration to create the overall effect of continuation function. When a continuation begins by maintaining the 2-m. grouping structure of the presentation (delaying fragmentation), surface rhythm acceleration can still project continuation function. It is especially diagnostic when harmonic acceleration is absent or ambiguous (p. 75). The characteristic may arise from focusing on faster rhythmic motives that were only briefly introduced in the basic idea, or from eliminating slower note values.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 75, Ex. 2.17): Mozart, K. 465, i -- the eighth-note motive of the basic idea comes to dominate the continuation's rhythmic texture, creating accelerated surface rhythms.
**Example 2** (p. 75, Ex. 2.18): Beethoven, Op. 119, No. 1 -- change from predominantly quarter notes in presentation to eighth notes in continuation; obvious surface rhythm acceleration.
**Example 3** (p. 71, Ex. 2.16): Mozart, K. 311, iii -- surface rhythm acceleration is particularly effective in the absence of harmonic acceleration.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Looking only at the melody for surface rhythm changes
  **Correction**: Surface rhythm includes ALL attack points in the texture, not just the melody

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking surface rhythm acceleration is the same as harmonic acceleration
  **Clarification**: Surface rhythm = attack-point density of individual notes; harmonic acceleration = rate of harmonic change. They are independent processes that often but not always coincide
