# Quick Definition
The dominant harmony that serves as the goal (final harmony) of a half-cadential progression, occupying the ultimate (last) position.

# Formal Definition
In a half-cadential progression, the dominant itself becomes the goal harmony and thus occupies the ultimate position. Whether a dominant is characterized as ultimate or penultimate has nothing to do with harmony per se; rather, it is a matter of how the dominant groups with its surrounding harmonies.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
The ultimate dominant:
- Serves as the harmonic goal of the half-cadential progression
- Must be a root-position **triad** (not seventh chord)
- Does NOT group with the following tonic (which begins a new progression)
- Possesses sufficient stability to represent a harmonic end

Requirements for stability as ultimate:
- Root position (inversion destroys cadential potential)
- Consonant triad form (seventh would overly destabilize)

# Musical Context/Application
The ultimate dominant:
- Ends contrasting middles, transitions, and similar formal units
- Usually resolves to tonic, but that tonic initiates a new progression
- Within the half-cadential progression itself, represents sufficient repose
- Creates the harmonic goal that defines the half cadence

The distinction between ultimate and penultimate is determined by grouping structure, not by the harmonic content of the dominant itself.

# Common Confusions
- Adding a seventh to the ultimate dominant (destroys its stability as an ending harmony)
- Confusing ultimate dominant (half cadence) with penultimate dominant (authentic cadence)
- Thinking the following tonic belongs to the half-cadential progression (it begins a new one)
- Assuming the distinction is about chord content rather than grouping
