# Quick Definition
Sonata form is the preeminent full-movement form of the classical period, consisting of three required sections -- exposition, development, and recapitulation -- that create, intensify, and resolve a dramatic conflict of tonalities. Most instrumental cycles have at least one movement in sonata form.

# Core Definition
Sonata form is the preeminent full-movement form in the classical period. Most instrumental cycles (sonatas, quartets, symphonies, etc.) have at least one movement in sonata form -- typically the first movement, though slow movements and finales are often written in sonata form as well. Many historians and theorists consider it to be the period's most highly developed and complex compositional design, the one in which composers reveal their greatest technical skill and expressive potential. Sonata form consists of three required sections, each with a unique section function: **exposition**, **development**, and **recapitulation**. The exposition is almost always repeated; the development and recapitulation together are sometimes repeated. The resulting structure ||: Exposition :|| ||: Development--Recapitulation :|| resembles the "rounded binary" version of the small ternary, with the three sections corresponding to A, B, and A'. Sonata form continued to exert enormous influence over compositional practice in later musical styles and remained a viable form, albeit highly modified, at least until the middle of the 20th century (Caplin, Ch. 9, p. 283).

# Prerequisites
- **Sentence** -- The most common theme type for main themes
- **Period** -- Used for some main themes, especially in finales
- **Small ternary** -- Sonata form's large-scale structure parallels the rounded binary

# Key Properties
1. Three required sections: exposition, development, recapitulation
2. Exposition almost always repeated (with repeat signs or written out)
3. Two optional framing functions: slow introduction (before exposition) and coda (after recapitulation)
4. Fundamental tonal conflict between home key and subordinate key
5. Most instrumental cycles have at least one movement in sonata form
6. Preeminent full-movement form of the classical period
7. Reveals composers' greatest technical skill and expressive potential
8. Continued to influence composition through the mid-20th century

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Locate the exposition's beginning (obvious, especially with tempo change after slow introduction)
2. Find the end of the exposition (marked by repetition structure or repeat signs)
3. The development begins after the exposition's close
4. The recapitulation starts with the return of main-theme material in the home key
5. The coda begins when the recapitulation no longer corresponds with the exposition

# Context & Application
Sonata form is not merely a template but a dynamic compositional process involving tonal conflict and resolution. The study of sonata form builds directly on the concepts learned in Part I of the textbook (sentence, period, hybrid, compound themes, small ternary), applying the same formal functions at larger scales. It is used most commonly for first movements but also for slow movements and finales. The overview chapter uses Beethoven's Piano Sonata Op. 2, No. 1 as the primary illustrative example.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the coda as a required section of sonata form
  **Correction**: Only exposition, development, and recapitulation are required; slow introduction and coda are optional framing functions

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming sonata form is only used for first movements
  **Clarification**: While most common in first movements, sonata form is also frequently used for slow movements and finales
