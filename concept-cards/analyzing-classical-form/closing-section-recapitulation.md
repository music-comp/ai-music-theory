# Quick Definition
The closing section following the final PAC is normally retained in the recapitulation as it appeared in the exposition, though it may be extended when no coda follows or altered when a coda does follow.

# Core Definition
The closing section that follows the final PAC of the subordinate theme is normally retained in the recapitulation as it appeared in the exposition. If no coda follows, the closing section may be extended to provide a greater sense of "after the end" for the movement as a whole. If the recapitulation is followed by a coda, the final codettas may be altered or eliminated, with material "saved" for the coda's own closing section. When the exposition closes with a retransition leading back to the home key, the recapitulation may bring a similar passage leading instead to the subdominant (or occasionally supertonic) for the start of the coda.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of closing sections in the exposition
- Familiarity with recapitulation harmonic-tonal organization

# Key Properties
- Normally retained from exposition without significant structural change
- May be extended when no coda follows to create greater sense of finality
- Final codettas may be altered or eliminated when a coda is present
- Material may be "saved" for the coda's closing section
- May lead to subdominant region when functioning as transition to coda

# Construction / Recognition
Compare the closing section of the recapitulation bar by bar with the exposition's closing section. Note whether: (1) it is retained intact, (2) it is extended for greater finality, or (3) codettas are altered or eliminated to set up a coda. If a retransition-like passage leads away from the home key toward the subdominant, the coda likely begins somewhere in that passage.

# Context & Application
The treatment of the closing section depends heavily on whether a coda follows. Without a coda, the closing section must serve as the movement's final utterance and may need extension. With a coda, the closing section may be truncated or rewritten, since its material can be restored later in the coda's own closing section -- a technique particularly associated with Haydn.

# Examples
- General pattern: exposition's closing section retained intact in recapitulation, providing same after-the-end quality in home key
- Haydn frequently rewrites closing sections in recapitulation, saving original material for coda

# Common Errors
- Assuming the closing section must always be identical in the recapitulation
- Failing to recognize retransition-like passages as leading to coda rather than repeating exposition function

# Common Confusions
- A closing section leading to the subdominant is NOT a true retransition -- it no longer fulfills the retransition's function of returning to the home key but instead serves as a transition to the coda
