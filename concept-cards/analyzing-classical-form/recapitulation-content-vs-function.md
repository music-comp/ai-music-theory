# Quick Definition
Musical content (the melodic-motivic material) and formal function (the role a passage plays in the form) must be distinguished in recapitulation analysis: material originally associated with one function may be refashioned to serve an entirely different function.

# Core Definition
We must distinguish between a unit's musical content -- its melodic-motivic material, textures, and rhythms -- and its formal function -- what role the passage plays in the form. In a so-called reversed recapitulation, the melodic-motivic material may be reordered (subordinate before main), but the passage containing main-theme material does not function as a main theme. Rather, it is refashioned to function as a subordinate theme, exhibiting the loosening devices associated with that function: extended continuations, expanded cadential progressions, and evaded or deceptive cadences. Terminology sometimes confuses this distinction, and care must be taken not to assume that the presence of certain material determines function.

# Key Properties
- Musical content and formal function are independent dimensions
- Material from one formal function may be refashioned to serve another
- "Reversed" recapitulations illustrate this principle dramatically
- Loosening devices (extended continuations, expanded cadential progressions, deceptive cadences) serve as evidence of subordinate-theme function
- Function must be assessed independently from content
- Terminology can mislead if content is conflated with function

# Construction / Recognition
When analyzing the recapitulation, assess each passage's formal function independently from its melodic-motivic content. Even if a passage uses main-theme material, it may function as a subordinate theme if it exhibits loose organization, expanded cadential area, and other subordinate-theme characteristics. Similarly, subordinate-theme material may be refashioned to serve a different function.

# Context & Application
This principle is essential for analyzing unusual recapitulations, particularly those by Haydn, who frequently restructures the recapitulation's content. The distinction between content and function prevents misleading analytical conclusions: calling a passage a "main theme" simply because it uses main-theme material obscures its actual role in the form. Caplin uses this principle to argue that many so-called "reversed" recapitulations are better understood as normal recapitulations where the content has been redistributed among the standard formal functions.

# Examples
- In a "reversed" recapitulation: subordinate-theme material appears first, functioning as main theme; main-theme material appears second, refashioned with loosening devices to function as subordinate theme
- Evidence of functional change: extended continuations, expanded cadential progressions, evaded or deceptive cadences applied to main-theme material

# Common Errors
- Assuming that the presence of main-theme material automatically means a passage functions as a main theme
- Labeling formal functions based solely on content without examining phrase-structural and harmonic evidence

# Common Confusions
- A "reversed" recapitulation is NOT simply the same recapitulation with units swapped -- the material is refashioned to serve its new functional role, acquiring the characteristic loosening devices of its new position in the form
