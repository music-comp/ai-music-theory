# Quick Definition
A transition acquires markedly loose formal expression by starting directly with continuation function, bypassing a clear sense of formal initiation. The most evident expression is through model-sequence technique; without sequences, continuation can be signaled by short groups (less than two bars), fast harmonic changes, and highly active surface rhythm.

# Core Definition
In some cases, the transition acquires a markedly looser formal expression by starting directly with continuation function, bypassing entirely a clear sense of formal initiation. The most evident sense of beginning with a continuation occurs when the music presents a model for sequential repetition. In the absence of model-sequence technique, the sense of beginning with continuation function can be expressed by relatively short groups (less than two bars), relatively fast harmonic changes, and a highly active surface rhythm. We are so accustomed in the classical style to understanding repeated one-bar units as fragments that their appearance at the start of a new thematic unit, especially one whose overall functionality is "medial," may signal continuation function in the absence of a prior formal initiation. The lack of a full presentation phrase renders the transition's beginning less solid, and thus looser in expression, than the opening of the main theme (Caplin, Ch. 11, pp. 347-350).

# Prerequisites
- **Continuation** -- The function that replaces initiation
- **Model-sequence technique** -- The most evident way of expressing continuation at the start
- **Transition loosening devices** -- Context for this omission

# Key Properties
1. Transition begins directly with continuation function
2. Most evident: model-sequence technique
3. Without sequences: short groups, fast harmony, active rhythm
4. Repeated one-bar units may signal fragmentation/continuation
5. Lacks the solidity of a presentation opening
6. Renders the transition's beginning markedly loose

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Check whether the transition opens with a presentation (tonic prolongation, repeated basic idea)
2. If not, look for model-sequence technique signaling continuation
3. Check for short groups (less than 2 bars), fast harmonic changes, active rhythm
4. Repeated one-bar units at the start suggest fragmentation, hence continuation
5. Note the absence of tonic prolongation that would characterize initiation

# Context & Application
Omission of initiating function is one of seven loosening devices for transitions and also occurs in subordinate themes (though the parallel concept has different formal implications). The technique effectively removes the formal grounding that a presentation provides, throwing the listener directly into the middle of a formal process. This is particularly effective when the preceding main theme has established clear formal initiation, making the contrast with the transition's "groundless" beginning more striking.

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 348-349): Mozart, Violin Sonata K. 454, ii, mm. 21-29 -- After main theme's PAC, accompanimental overlap introduces new pattern; transition's opening 2-measure unit immediately tonicizes submediant; harmonic context is manifestly sequential (descending-fifths); continuation function, not presentation; second phrase is exclusively cadential.
**Example 2** (pp. 349-350): Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 283, i, mm. 16-22 -- Transition begins with 1-measure idea repeated a step higher; repeated one-bar units may signal fragmentation; entire transition may be understood as continuation function up to the HC.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Labeling a sequential opening as a "presentation" because the idea is repeated
  **Correction**: If the harmonic context is sequential (not tonic prolongation), the function is continuation, not presentation; repeated ideas in sequential context are model-sequence, not basic idea repetition
