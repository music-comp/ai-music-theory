# Quick Definition
The intervallic relationship between successive chord roots, forming the basis for classifying sequential progressions into six types.

# Formal Definition
Root motion describes the interval and direction of movement between the roots of successive harmonies. Sequential progressions are classified into six types based on the size and direction of the interval between the roots of individual chords: ascending and descending motion by fifths, thirds, or seconds.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Six types of root motion in sequences:
1. **Descending fifth**: Most frequent (III-VI-II-V-I)
2. **Ascending fifth**: Infrequent (I-V-II-VI)
3. **Descending third**: Frequent (I-VI-IV-II)
4. **Ascending third**: Most infrequent (I-III-V)
5. **Descending second**: Frequent (IV-III-II-I)
6. **Ascending second**: Frequent (I-II-III-IV)

Root motion by fourths, sixths, and sevenths is logically accommodated through inversion into these six categories (e.g., ascending fourth = descending fifth).

# Musical Context/Application
Understanding root motion:
- Classifies sequential progressions
- Explains the special status of descending-fifth motion (V-I relationship)
- Helps identify non-functional patterns in sequences
- Distinguishes functional progressions from pattern-based ones

The descending-fifth motion has special status because it underlies the fundamental V-I relationship.

# Common Confusions
- Not recognizing inversional equivalence (ascending fourth = descending fifth)
- Treating all root motions as equally common or functional
- Assuming root motion alone determines harmonic meaning (context matters)
- Conflating root motion with bass motion (they may differ)
