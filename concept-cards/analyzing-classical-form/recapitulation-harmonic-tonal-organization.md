# Quick Definition
The recapitulation normally remains throughout in the home key, contrasting with the exposition's dramatic opposition between home and subordinate keys; flat-side tonal regions are explored to avoid monotony, while the subordinate theme stays entirely in the home key.

# Core Definition
Unlike the exposition, which features a dramatic opposition between the home key and the subordinate key (with the latter eventually "winning out" over the former), the recapitulation normally remains throughout in the home key. Very rarely does another key receive cadential confirmation within the recapitulation. To avoid monotony, however, the recapitulation often explores flat-side tonal regions (subdominant, supertonic, and regions from modal mixture such as flat-VI, flat-III, flat-II). Tonicizations of these regions are especially likely in the main theme and transition units. The subordinate theme, by contrast, remains entirely in the home key and is usually harmonized in the same basic way as in the exposition. The recapitulation begins with home-key tonic, resolving the dominant at the end of the development -- but this is NOT an authentic cadence; the dominant is an "ultimate" dominant that resolves to begin a new section.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of large-scale tonal structure in sonata form

# Key Properties
- Remains throughout in the home key (very rarely is another key cadentially confirmed)
- Flat-side regions (IV, ii, flat-VI, flat-III, flat-II) explored to avoid monotony
- Flat-side tonicizations most likely in main theme and transition
- Subordinate theme remains entirely in home key
- Subordinate theme usually harmonized same as in exposition
- Opens with home-key tonic resolving development's dominant
- Opening tonic is NOT an authentic cadence (it begins a new section)

# Construction / Recognition
Identify the home key at the beginning of the recapitulation. Track the tonal plan through the main theme and transition, noting any flat-side tonicizations. Confirm that the subordinate theme remains in the home key. Be careful not to interpret the opening tonic as a cadential resolution -- it is the start of a new formal section, not the conclusion of the development.

# Context & Application
The recapitulation's tonal design resolves the fundamental conflict established in the exposition. The subordinate key that "won" in the exposition is now replaced by the home key. The flat-side tonicizations serve a purely local function of providing harmonic variety without undermining the home key's supremacy. The restriction of flat-side exploration to the main theme and transition (with the subordinate theme staying in the home key) reflects the different functional requirements of each section.

# Examples
- General pattern: main theme may tonicize IV or flat-VI briefly; transition shifts flat-side before returning sharp-side to the home-key dominant; subordinate theme follows in home key throughout
- Beethoven: opening of recapitulation often given dramatically different dynamic (louder, more forceful), which may misleadingly suggest a cadential quality

# Common Errors
- Interpreting the opening tonic of the recapitulation as an authentic cadence resolving the development's dominant
- Expecting the subordinate theme to modulate as it did in the exposition

# Common Confusions
- The V-I at the boundary of development and recapitulation is NOT a cadence -- it is the resolution of an "ultimate" dominant that initiates a new section, especially when the opening tonic appears with great dynamic force giving a misleading cadential quality
