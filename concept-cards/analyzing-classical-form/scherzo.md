# Quick Definition
The scherzo is a variant style of the minuet featuring a faster tempo and livelier, driving character. It does not differ from the minuet in formal organization -- despite claims by some theorists, "scherzo form" is indistinguishable from minuet form.

# Core Definition
In the hands of Haydn and Beethoven, the scherzo emerged as a variant style of the minuet, one that features a faster tempo and a livelier, driving character. But since the scherzo does not differ from the minuet as regards formal organization, the two styles are treated together in relation to a single formal type. Despite the claims of some theorists, especially Schoenberg, it is impossible to distinguish "scherzo form" from minuet form (Caplin, pp. 629-630).

# Prerequisites
- **Minuet style** -- The style from which the scherzo derives
- **Minuet form** -- The formal organization shared by both

# Key Properties
1. Faster tempo than minuet
2. Livelier, driving character
3. Identical formal organization to minuet form
4. No separate "scherzo form" exists
5. Developed primarily by Haydn and Beethoven
6. Cadential/postcadential boundary often blurred (rhythmic drive must be maintained)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Check for fast triple meter with driving character
2. Confirm the formal organization follows minuet form
3. Note blurred cadential/postcadential boundaries due to rhythmic intensity

# Context & Application
The term "scherzo" is used only when the movement is undoubtedly in scherzo style and calling it a "minuet" would be awkward. The scherzo became increasingly common in 19th-century symphonies.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 629): Haydn and Beethoven developed the scherzo style; Beethoven frequently replaced the minuet with a scherzo.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Believing scherzo has a different form from the minuet
  **Clarification**: Scherzo and minuet share identical formal organization; only the style (tempo, character) differs
