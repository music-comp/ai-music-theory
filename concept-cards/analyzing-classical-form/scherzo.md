## Definition

The scherzo emerged as a variant style of the minuet, featuring a faster tempo and a livelier, driving character. It was developed primarily by Haydn and Beethoven.

## Key Distinction

Despite claims of some theorists (especially Schoenberg), it is **impossible to distinguish "scherzo form" from minuet form**. The formal organization is identical.

## Stylistic Characteristics

| Feature | Scherzo vs. Minuet |
|---------|-------------------|
| Tempo | Faster than minuet |
| Character | Livelier, more driving |
| Rhythmic energy | Greater intensity |
| Formal structure | Identical to minuet |

## Terminological Usage

Reference to "scherzo" is made only when:
- The movement is undoubtedly in scherzo style
- Speaking of it as a "minuet" would be awkward

## Effect on Formal Analysis

### Cadential/Postcadential Boundary
In scherzos, the boundary between cadential and postcadential functions is often blurred because:
- A sudden change of material for closing section could disrupt rhythmic drive
- Closing section content often consists of prior cadential ideas
- The intensity of prevailing rhythmic drive must be maintained

## Historical Development

- Haydn: Early development of scherzo style
- Beethoven: Prominent use replacing minuet movements
- The scherzo became increasingly common in 19th-century symphonies
