# Quick Definition
A slow introduction may be organized as a compound sentence, with a compound presentation (compound basic ideas) followed by continuation with fragmentation and a cadence, though the structure often exhibits problematic features typical of the introduction's formal looseness.

# Core Definition
A slow introduction may be organized as a compound sentence, featuring a compound presentation (built with compound basic ideas) followed by a continuation with fragmentation, a half cadence in a related key, and a standing on the dominant. The structure may be problematic in some respects: there may be a lack of tonic prolongation at the end of the compound presentation, deviations from expected continuational patterns, and modal inflections creating ambiguity. Such an introduction may literally end on its final sonority (often not the home-key dominant), which is then understood as a pre-dominant in the home key (such as iv), leading directly into the main theme's opening harmony. The end of the introduction may also anticipate the main theme's melody, creating a smooth transition while maintaining a distinct identity.

# Key Properties
- Compound presentation with compound basic ideas
- Continuation with fragmentation
- Half cadence often in a related key (not necessarily the home key)
- Standing on the dominant (of the related key)
- May lack tonic prolongation at end of compound presentation
- Modal inflections create ambiguity
- Final sonority may function as pre-dominant in home key
- May anticipate main theme's harmony and melody

# Construction / Recognition
Identify a slow introduction whose opening features compound basic ideas (each combining two contrasting elements) stated and repeated (compound presentation), followed by fragmentation and a cadence. Note any deviations from standard compound sentence organization, such as lack of tonic prolongation in the presentation or unusual harmonic goals. Examine how the introduction's final harmony connects to the main theme.

# Context & Application
The compound sentence is one of several possible formal organizations for slow introductions, though as with all introduction forms, the fit is rarely exact. The formal looseness characteristic of introductions means that analytical labels like "compound sentence" must be applied flexibly, with acknowledgment of the deviations and irregularities that inevitably arise. The anticipatory connection to the main theme (both harmonic and melodic) is a particularly sophisticated feature of this type.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Violin Sonata in A minor ("Kreutzer"), Op. 47, i: introduction resembles compound sentence with compound presentation (mm. 1-4), fragmentation beginning at upbeat to m. 9, HC in C major (m. 13), standing on dominant; ends on D-minor sonority functioning as iv in A minor, with violin anticipating main theme's E-F motive from m. 16 onward

# Common Errors
- Forcing a compound sentence analysis onto an introduction that does not fit the model
- Ignoring the problematic features that distinguish introduction form from standard compound sentence

# Common Confusions
- A compound sentence in an introduction is NOT identical to a standard compound sentence in a theme -- introductions exhibit formal looseness, deviations, and irregularities that make the label approximate rather than exact
