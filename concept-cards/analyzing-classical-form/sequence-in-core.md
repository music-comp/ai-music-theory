# Quick Definition
Following its initial statement, the model of a core is sequenced one or more times, with the sequential pattern sometimes changing during the course of the core.

# Core Definition
The model is sequenced one or more times after its initial statement (p. 452). A single sequential pattern may persist throughout (descending fifth, ascending second), but "the pattern often changes in the course of the core." The model's structure is normally retained in the sequence, but multi-phrase models may be altered: "the first phrase usually conforms to the original model, but subsequent phrases may be changed or eliminated." Large models may produce incomplete sequences even on first repetition.

# Prerequisites
- **Core** — The formal context
- **Core model** — What is being sequenced
- **Sequential progression** — The harmonic technique supporting the sequence

# Key Properties
1. Model sequenced one or more times after initial statement
2. Sequential pattern may be consistent or may change
3. Structure of model normally retained in sequenced version
4. Multi-phrase models may be altered during sequence
5. Large models may have incomplete sequences
6. Common patterns: descending fifths, ascending stepwise, descending thirds

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. After identifying the model, look for its transposed repetition
2. Determine the interval and direction of transposition
3. Note whether the sequential pattern changes at later repetitions
4. Check whether the model is altered during sequence (especially in multi-phrase models)

# Examples
**Example 13.1** (p. 446): Mozart, K. 310, i — 4-m. model sequenced twice down a perfect fifth (mm. 62-65, 66-69).

**Example 13.4** (p. 456): Mozart, K. 387, i — apparent 2-m. model sequenced down a third, but closer analysis reveals a 4-m. ascending-stepwise sequence when the descending-third pattern alternates with ascending steps.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the sequential pattern must remain constant throughout the core
  **Correction**: Patterns often change, and the underlying harmonic progression may continue even when the surface pattern shifts
