# Quick Definition
When a main theme ends with a half cadence, the transition often begins with material that seems to continue the main theme -- as though a consequent were starting after an antecedent's HC. Once the music modulates instead of completing the expected consequent, the "continuation" is retrospectively understood as the beginning of the transition, and the boundary between main theme and transition is dissolved.

# Core Definition
This opening strategy is typically employed when the main theme ends with a half cadence. The return of the opening basic idea gives the initial impression that the main theme may not yet be over: since the HC can close a large-scale antecedent, the return of the basic idea implies the start of a matching consequent. Once the music no longer corresponds to the antecedent and modulates to the new key, the listener understands in retrospect that the return of the basic idea marked the beginning of the transition and that the prior HC truly closed the main theme. The strategy can also be used when the main theme has closed with an authentic cadence, frequently with the beginning of the transition eliding with the end of the main theme. The "dissolving" quality lies in the seamless transformation of one thematic function into another (Caplin, Ch. 11, pp. 340-341).

# Key Properties
1. Main theme ends with HC (typically)
2. Opening basic idea returns, suggesting a consequent
3. Music modulates instead of completing the expected consequent
4. Listener retrospectively reassigns the returning material as transition
5. Prior HC is retrospectively confirmed as main theme's ending
6. Can also occur after PAC, often with elision
7. Creates seamless connection between main theme and transition

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Note a main theme ending with HC
2. Check whether the following material uses the main theme's basic idea
3. Determine when the music departs from the expected consequent path
4. The formal beginning of the transition is at the return of the basic idea (not the moment of departure)
5. The boundary is understood retrospectively

# Context & Application
This is one of four principal opening strategies for transitions (alongside new material, false closing section, and nontonic region). It creates maximum continuity between main theme and transition at the cost of momentary formal ambiguity, which is resolved retrospectively. If the transition is nonmodulatory, the combined main theme and transition can sometimes give the impression of a "reversed period" (consequent followed by antecedent), but closer examination usually reveals that re-reversing the units is not satisfactory.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 333): Beethoven, Piano Sonata Op. 49/1, i, mm. 1-17 -- Main theme (simple sentence) closes with HC at m. 8; sentence begins to be repeated as though forming a compound consequent, but modulates to the subordinate key at m. 12, revealing the second sentence as a modulating transition.
**Example 2** (p. 341): Mozart, Violin Sonata K. 403, i, mm. 1-9 -- Main theme (sentence) closes with PAC at m. 4; repeated with chromatic embellishments ending with HC at m. 8, creating a "reversed period" that is better understood as main theme + nonmodulating transition.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Locating the beginning of the transition at the moment of modulation
  **Correction**: The transition begins at the return of the basic idea, not where the music departs from the expected path; the modulation point is in the middle of a formal process, not a beginning
