# Quick Definition
A frequently occurring sequential progression featuring roots descending by thirds, often embellished with passing chords that introduce dominant-tonic implications.

# Formal Definition
The descending-third progression is a frequently occurring sequence. The unembellished form features simple root descent by thirds. More often, however, the leap in the bass voice is filled in by stepwise motion, which produces intervening passing chords in first inversion.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Basic pattern: I - VI - IV - II (roots descend by thirds)

Embellished forms:
- Passing chords fill in the bass leaps with stepwise motion
- Each root-position harmony is followed by a passing chord whose root is a fifth above
- The passing chord stands as a "dominant" (literally or by analogy) to the root-position chord
- These dominant-like passing chords resolve "deceptively" to the next root-position chord

Functional interpretation:
- V/VI resolving deceptively to IV
- V/IV resolving deceptively to II
- Creates chain of implied dominant-tonic relationships

The functional interpretation becomes more evident when passing chords are placed in root position themselves.

# Musical Context/Application
The descending-third sequence:
- Is frequently used in classical music
- Often embellished with passing chords creating dominant implications
- The passing chords introduce harmonic-functional color
- Creates stepwise bass lines when embellished

The embellished version with passing dominants creates a rich harmonic texture while maintaining sequential logic.

# Common Confusions
- Not recognizing the implied dominant-tonic relationships in the embellished form
- Confusing the "deceptive" resolution of passing chords with actual deceptive cadences
- Treating the passing chords as structurally equal to the main harmonies
- Missing the stepwise bass connection in embellished versions
