# Quick Definition
Not every expanded dominant in a subordinate theme constitutes an internal HC. The critical distinction is whether the dominant is the **ultimate** harmony (the goal of the progression = internal HC) or merely **penultimate** (will resolve to tonic = part of ECP) or **passing** (connects two other harmonies = neither HC nor ECP goal).

# Core Definition
Not every passage involving expanded dominant harmony in a subordinate theme is an internal HC with standing on the dominant. For this identification, the dominant must be experienced as the **ultimate** harmony of an ongoing cadential progression. A **penultimate** dominant (within an ECP, where it will resolve to tonic) is not an internal HC. Some dominants are neither ultimate nor penultimate: they undergo major expansion but function as **passing chords** in a larger harmonic pattern. The distinction depends on whether the harmony is the **goal** of the progression or merely a passing element, regardless of dramatic gesture or duration (Caplin, Ch. 12, pp. 403-404).

# Key Properties
1. Ultimate dominant = goal of progression = internal HC possible
2. Penultimate dominant = within ECP, will resolve to tonic = NOT internal HC
3. Passing dominant = connects other harmonies = NOT internal HC
4. Duration and dramatic gesture alone do not determine the distinction
5. Harmonic function (goal vs. passing element) is decisive

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 403): Beethoven, Op. 2/2, i -- Expanded diminished seventh (dominant function) for 9 measures, but passing between I and I6; NOT an internal HC.
