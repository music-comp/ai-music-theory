# Quick Definition
Double-bar lines with repeat signs instruct the performer to repeat the development and recapitulation together; Mozart typically places the coda after the repeat, while Haydn frequently includes the coda within the repetition scheme.

# Core Definition
The notation of double-bar lines with repeat signs instructs the performer to repeat the development and recapitulation together. Mozart, in particular, often separates his codas from the two prior sections by means of this notational device, placing the double-bar lines before the coda begins. However, we must be careful: there are many cases, especially in Haydn, where the coda itself is included within the repetition scheme. The presence of a double-bar line and repeat signs at the very end of the movement does not exclude the possibility that a genuine coda exists even when it is required to be performed again along with the development and recapitulation.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of the coda as an after-the-end function
- Knowledge of closing section treatment in the recapitulation

# Key Properties
- Double-bar and repeat signs indicate repetition of development and recapitulation
- Mozart typically places codas after (outside) the repeat
- Haydn frequently includes codas within the repeat scheme
- Notation alone does not determine formal structure
- Material after the double-bar is not necessarily the coda
- A coda may exist even within a repeated section

# Construction / Recognition
When analyzing coda boundaries, examine the placement of double-bar lines and repeat signs. If they appear before the end of the movement (Mozart's typical practice), the coda likely begins after the repeat signs. If they appear at the very end (common in Haydn), the coda may be included within the repeated section. In either case, the formal analysis must be based on the music's structural characteristics, not the notation alone.

# Context & Application
This notational convention is one of several factors that complicate the identification of the coda's start. It is most relevant in Classical-period repertoire where the convention of repeating the development and recapitulation was standard practice. The different approaches of Mozart and Haydn reflect their broader compositional differences: Mozart tends toward clearer sectional boundaries, while Haydn favors ambiguity and formal integration.

# Examples
- Mozart, Symphony No. 36 ("Linz"), K. 425, i: double-bar and repeat signs at m. 232 separate the development-recapitulation from the coda beginning at m. 232
- Haydn: frequently includes coda material within the repetition scheme, requiring the coda to be performed again with the development and recapitulation

# Common Errors
- Assuming material after a double-bar line is necessarily the coda
- Assuming a coda cannot exist within a repeated section
- Letting notation alone determine formal structure

# Common Confusions
- The notation does NOT determine the formal structure -- a coda is identified by where the recapitulation stops corresponding to the exposition, regardless of where repeat signs are placed
