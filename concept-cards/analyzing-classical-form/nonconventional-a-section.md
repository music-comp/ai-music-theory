# Quick Definition
On occasion, the A section of a small ternary takes a nonconventional form that does not correspond to any standard theme type (sentence, period, or hybrid), though it must still end with a PAC and typically retains recognizable functional elements.

# Core Definition
A nonconventional A section does not match the patterns of sentence, period, or hybrid themes but may still contain elements of these types. It must still end with a PAC to provide the cadential closure required of the exposition. Even in nonconventional forms, a basic idea typically begins the section and cadential function closes it. A nonconventional A section might consist of two complete simple themes combined, or feature unusual grouping structures, or present an extended basic idea followed by cadential phrase only. The analytical approach should identify any conventional functional elements, note the cadential structure, describe the unique organization, and avoid forcing a conventional label where it does not fit.

# Key Properties
1. Does not correspond to sentence, period, or hybrid
2. Must still end with PAC
3. May contain elements of conventional types
4. Basic idea typically begins the section
5. Cadential function always closes the section
6. May consist of compound structures or unusual groupings
7. Should not be forced into a conventional label

# Construction / Recognition
## Analytical Approach:
1. Identify any conventional functional elements (basic idea, continuation, cadential)
2. Note the cadential structure (PAC required)
3. Describe the unique organization on its own terms
4. Do not force a conventional label if it does not fit
5. Consider whether the section is compound (multiple themes combined)

# Context & Application
Nonconventional A sections are relatively uncommon but demonstrate that the small ternary form accommodates a range of internal organizations. The key requirement is that the A section must provide sufficient cadential closure (PAC) to establish the home key before the contrasting middle introduces instability. This is the same principle that applies to nonconventional main themes in sonata form (Ch. 10).
