# Quick Definition
The breaking down of the grouping structure defined by the model (and its sequences) into progressively smaller units, eventually motivating the core's formal close.

# Core Definition
"Most cores produce an extensive process of fragmentation, which breaks down the grouping structure defined by the model (and its sequences) and eventually motivates a formal close to the core" (p. 452). The fragmentation may continue ideas from the model or introduce new content. The fragments themselves may establish a new model for sequential repetition. When the sequential process becomes exhausted, the remaining core may be supported by prolongational progressions similar to a regular continuation phrase.

# Prerequisites
- **Core** — The formal context
- **Core model** — The unit being fragmented
- **Fragmentation** — The general process of reducing grouping units

# Key Properties
1. Breaks down grouping structure from model size to smaller units
2. Eventually motivates formal close (HC or dominant arrival)
3. May continue model ideas or introduce new content
4. Fragments may themselves form new models for sequential repetition
5. Sequential process may be exhausted, replaced by prolongational progressions
6. Typical progression: 4-m. model -> 2-m. fragments -> 1-m. fragments -> cadence

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Note where the full model ceases to be sequenced
2. Identify the reduction in grouping unit size
3. Look for possible new sequential patterns within the fragments
4. Locate the cadential process that the fragmentation leads to

# Examples
**Example 13.1** (p. 446): Mozart, K. 310, i — fragmentation at m. 70 introduces new 1-m. idea that becomes a model for stepwise-descending sequences; further fragmentation at m. 73 into one-beat units leading to HC.

**Example 13.3** (p. 454): Haydn, H. 49 — 4-m. model reduced to 2-m. unit (m. 97), then 1-m. segments (m. 101); sequential activity ends at fragmentation, replaced by prolongational progressions to HC at m. 103.
