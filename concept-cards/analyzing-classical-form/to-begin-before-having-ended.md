# Quick Definition
Caplin argues that we must accept that something new can begin without there having been an ending to what precedes it. A subordinate theme can begin (with a genuine thematic structure in the subordinate key) even though the transition has not ended with a clear HC, medial caesura, or standing on the dominant. This creates a formal "disruption" but not a fundamental violation of form.

# Core Definition
Many theorists would see the subordinate theme beginning only where a prominent HC, standing on the dominant, and medial caesura mark a clear end of the transition. This view ignores genuine thematic structures that precede such markers -- structures that have clear beginning, middle, and ending functions, reside fully in the subordinate key, and conform to subordinate-theme requirements except for not ending with a PAC. Caplin argues that we must learn to accept that something new can begin without there having been an ending to what precedes this new beginning. The analogy is to spoken language: a speaker begins a sentence but before completing it (even though its ending is implied) moves right on to start another sentence. There is a clear disruption but not complete loss of comprehension. This understanding leads to the concept of the two-part subordinate theme: Part 1 is a complete structure except for the PAC (ending with an internal HC), and Part 2 brings new initiation leading to the required PAC (Caplin, Ch. 12, pp. 402-404).

# Key Properties
1. New formal process can begin without prior process ending
2. Challenges theorists who require medial caesura for subordinate-theme onset
3. Creates formal "disruption" but not fundamental violation
4. Analogous to overlapping sentences in speech
5. Enables the concept of two-part subordinate theme
6. Part 1: complete structure ending with internal HC (no PAC)
7. Part 2: new initiation leading to required PAC

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Look for a genuine thematic structure in the subordinate key before the medial caesura
2. Check whether it has initiating, middle, and concluding functions
3. If it ends with HC rather than PAC, it is Part 1 of a two-part subordinate theme
4. Part 2 begins with new initiation and achieves the PAC
5. The transition may lack a clear ending in such cases

# Context & Application
This principle is fundamental to Caplin's approach and distinguishes it from approaches (like Hepokoski-Darcy's) that require a medial caesura to "open up" space for the subordinate theme. By accepting that formal processes can overlap -- with one beginning before another has ended -- we can recognize subordinate-theme function in passages that other theories would assign entirely to the transition.

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 402-404): Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 457, i -- A genuine thematic structure (presentation + continuation) appears in the subordinate key before any clear transition ending; this constitutes Part 1 of a two-part subordinate theme, ending with internal HC; Part 2 follows with new initiation leading to PAC.
