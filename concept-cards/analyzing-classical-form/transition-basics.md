# Quick Definition
The transition is the medial thematic function of the exposition, serving to destabilize the home key, loosen the formal organization, and liquidate characteristic motivic material to clear the stage for the subordinate theme. Its harmonic goal is dominant harmony, articulated by an HC or dominant arrival.

# Core Definition
The transition serves three primary functions: (1) to **destabilize the home key** so the subordinate key can emerge as a rival; (2) to **loosen the formal organization** as phrase-structural destabilization; (3) to **liquidate characteristic motivic material** to clear the stage for the subordinate theme. Its harmonic goal is dominant harmony, most often of the subordinate key but occasionally of the home key, articulated by an HC or noncadential dominant arrival. Three tonal categories: modulating (most common), nonmodulating (significant minority), and two-part (nonmodulating first part + modulating second part). Loosening devices include: extension of continuation, expansion of cadential function, omission of initiating function, asymmetrical grouping, modal shift, tonicization of remote regions, and nonconventional structures (Caplin, Ch. 11, pp. 330-332).

# Prerequisites
- **Exposition** -- The transition is the second thematic function
- **Main theme** -- Transition follows and loosens relative to main theme

# Key Properties
1. Three primary functions: destabilize, loosen, liquidate
2. Harmonic goal: dominant (SK or HK), articulated as HC or dominant arrival
3. Three tonal categories: modulating, nonmodulating, two-part
4. Four opening strategies: new material, main-theme material, false closing section, nontonic region
5. Sentential functions (presentation, continuation, cadential) most common; period rare
6. Typically concludes with standing on the dominant and possibly medial caesura
7. Often quite short (sometimes less than 8 measures)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Locate the end of the main theme (cadence)
2. Identify the transition's harmonic goal (dominant of SK or HK)
3. Note the tonal category (modulating, nonmodulating, two-part)
4. Identify phrase functions and loosening devices relative to the main theme
5. Find the HC or dominant arrival, standing on the dominant, and possible medial caesura

# Context & Application
Transitions are frequently quite short, often less than eight measures. Many passages traditionally identified as "transition" are better understood as belonging to the subordinate-theme group. The transition is often where the movement seems to "get under way" with continuous accompanimental patterns and dynamic drive.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 333): Beethoven, Op. 49/1, i, mm. 9-17 -- Short modulating transition using main-theme material, with "failed" consequent.
**Example 2** (p. 334): Mozart, K. 576, i, mm. 15-27 -- Nonmodulating transition beginning with false closing section.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the transition must be roughly one-third of the exposition
  **Correction**: Transitions are frequently quite short (under 8 measures)

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing "resolution of dominant to tonic" at the start of the subordinate theme with a cadence ending the transition
  **Clarification**: The initial tonic of the subordinate theme signals its beginning, not the end of the transition
