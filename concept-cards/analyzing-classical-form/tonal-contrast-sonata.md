# Quick Definition
The fundamental tonal contrast in sonata form is the dramatic conflict between home key and subordinate key: established in the exposition, intensified in the development, and resolved in the recapitulation. Tonal contrast is primary; melodic-motivic contrast is secondary.

# Core Definition
Sonata form is often described as creating, and then resolving, a dramatic conflict of tonalities between the home key and a closely related subordinate key. This conflict is established in the exposition (main theme confirms HK, transition destabilizes it, subordinate theme confirms SK), intensified in the development (through exploration of additional, more remote tonal areas), and resolved in the recapitulation (subordinate theme returns in home key, which emerges victorious). The principal source of contrast between main and subordinate themes lies in tonality, not in melodic-motivic content. Even in monothematic expositions (where the subordinate theme uses main-theme material), tonal contrast still creates formal differentiation -- different key creates different function.

# Prerequisites
- **Home key** -- One pole of the tonal conflict
- **Subordinate key** -- The other pole

# Key Properties
1. Dramatic conflict between HK and SK
2. Three-stage drama: establish (exposition), intensify (development), resolve (recapitulation)
3. Tonal contrast is primary; melodic-motivic contrast is secondary
4. Even monothematic expositions have tonal contrast
5. Different key = different function, regardless of shared melodic material
6. Home key "emerges victorious" in recapitulation

# Construction / Recognition
## The Three-Stage Tonal Drama:
| Stage | Section | Tonal Action |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Establish | Exposition | HK confirmed, then SK confirmed as rival |
| Intensify | Development | Additional tonal areas explored (minor regions) |
| Resolve | Recapitulation | SK material returns in HK; HK emerges victorious |

# Context & Application
Understanding that tonal contrast is the primary driver of sonata form corrects a common misconception derived from 19th-century theory (especially A. B. Marx) that characterizes sonata form primarily in terms of thematic contrast ("masculine" main theme vs. "feminine" subordinate theme). While thematic contrast may exist, it is the tonal conflict that generates the form's dramatic power. This understanding also explains why monothematic expositions are fully effective as sonata forms.
