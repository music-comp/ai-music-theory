# Quick Definition
The sequential pattern least frequently used by classical composers, featuring roots ascending by thirds, with each passing chord serving as "dominant" of the following main harmony.

# Formal Definition
The ascending-third progression is the sequential pattern least frequently used by the classical composers. Its unembellished form is rarely found, if ever. A more viable version employing passing chords is shown, though it too seldom appears in the classical literature. It is more frequently used in the Romantic era, especially by Chopin.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Basic pattern: I - III - V (roots ascend by thirds)

Embellished form:
- Each passing chord is the "dominant" of the following main harmony
- V/III leads to III
- V/V leads to V
- Creates chain of applied dominant resolutions

The unembellished form without passing chords is essentially never found in classical practice.

# Musical Context/Application
The ascending-third sequence:
- Is the rarest of the six sequential types in classical music
- The unembellished form is not found in classical practice
- More common in Romantic music (especially Chopin)
- Requires passing chords to be viable

The rarity of this sequence may relate to its weak tonal direction - ascending thirds move away from functional grounding more than other patterns.

# Common Confusions
- Expecting to find this sequence in classical music (it is very rare)
- Looking for an unembellished version (it essentially doesn't exist in classical practice)
- Confusing with ascending-fifth sequence (different interval of transposition)
