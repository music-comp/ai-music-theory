# Quick Definition
The least frequently used sequential progression in classical music, with roots ascending by thirds (I-III-V). Its unembellished form is rarely if ever found; the embellished version with passing dominants is also seldom used until the Romantic era (especially Chopin).

# Core Definition
The ascending-third progression is the sequential pattern least frequently used by the classical composers. Its unembellished form is rarely found, if ever. A more viable version employing passing chords is shown, though it too seldom appears in the classical literature. It is more frequently used in the Romantic era, especially by Chopin. Each passing chord is the "dominant" of the following main harmony (p. 44).

# Prerequisites
- **Sequential progression** — This is one of six types

# Key Properties
1. Rarest of the six sequential types in classical music
2. Basic pattern: I-III-V (roots ascend by thirds)
3. Unembellished form essentially never found in classical practice
4. Embellished form has passing dominants (V/III leading to III, etc.)
5. More common in Romantic music, especially Chopin

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Look for roots ascending consistently by thirds
2. Check for passing dominant chords at each step

# Context & Application
Extremely rare in classical practice. Its weak tonal direction may explain its infrequency.
