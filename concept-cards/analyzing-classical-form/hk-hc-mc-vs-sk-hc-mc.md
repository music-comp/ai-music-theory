# Quick Definition
Transitions ending with an HC in the home key (I:HC) correspond to nonmodulating transitions, while those ending with an HC in the subordinate key (V:HC) correspond to modulating transitions. Caplin acknowledges this Hepokoski-Darcy terminology but views the medial caesura as a textural feature rather than a functional prerequisite for subordinate-theme function.

# Core Definition
The theory of sonata form proposed by James Hepokoski and Warren Darcy distinguishes two types of half-cadence endings at the medial caesura: (1) I:HC MC, where the transition ends with an HC in the home key (nonmodulating transition), after which the subordinate theme begins with a direct modulation; and (2) V:HC MC, where the transition ends with an HC in the subordinate key (modulating transition), after which the subordinate theme begins with tonic of the subordinate key. Caplin acknowledges the medial caesura as an important characteristic of how many transitions reach their culmination but does not see the device as a prerequisite for subordinate-theme function. Rather, Caplin's form-functional perspective recognizes that all sonata expositions contain a subordinate theme and sees the distinction between two-part and continuous expositions as a matter of textural and rhythmic organization, with little functional significance (Ch. 11, pp. 331-332).

# Key Properties
1. I:HC MC: transition ends with HC in home key (nonmodulating)
2. V:HC MC: transition ends with HC in subordinate key (modulating)
3. Caplin views medial caesura as textural feature, not functional prerequisite
4. All expositions contain subordinate-theme function regardless of medial caesura
5. Hepokoski-Darcy: medial caesura is necessary for "two-part exposition"
6. Caplin: distinction is textural/rhythmic, not functionally significant

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Determine whether the transition modulates or remains in the home key
2. Identify the key of the HC at the transition's structural end
3. Check for a literal textural gap (silence or fermata) -- this is the medial caesura proper
4. Note that dominant arrival or fermata on the final sonority may serve similar roles

# Context & Application
The distinction between home-key and subordinate-key HCs is important for understanding how the subordinate key emerges. In a nonmodulating transition (I:HC), the subordinate key is not yet established; the subordinate theme must begin with a direct modulation. In a modulating transition (V:HC), the subordinate key's dominant has already been reached; the subordinate theme begins in tonic of that key. Caplin restricts the term "medial caesura" to cases with a literal caesura (moment of silence) or fermata.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the medial caesura as required for subordinate-theme function
  **Correction**: Caplin argues subordinate-theme function exists regardless of whether a medial caesura is present; the caesura is textural, not structural

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming Caplin and Hepokoski-Darcy have the same view of the medial caesura
  **Clarification**: Hepokoski-Darcy see it as a structural prerequisite; Caplin sees it as an important but non-essential textural feature
