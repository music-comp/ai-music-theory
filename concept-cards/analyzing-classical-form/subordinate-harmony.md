# Quick Definition
The intervening chords (neighboring, passing, or substitute harmonies) that serve to extend a prolonged harmony within a prolongational progression.

# Formal Definition
Subordinate harmonies are the intervening chords within a prolongational progression that have a meaning different from that of the prolonged harmony. They serve to sustain the prolonged harmony through time by functioning as neighboring, passing, or substitute harmonies. Their structural status is reduced compared to the prolonged harmony they embellish.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Types of subordinate harmonies:
- **Neighboring chords**: Between prolonged harmonies in same position
- **Passing chords**: Between prolonged harmonies in different positions
- **Substitute harmonies**: Chords sharing function with the prolonged harmony

Within pedal point situations:
- The bass note of subordinate harmonies is replaced by the pedal note
- This significantly reduces the structural status of these harmonies
- They are analyzed in root position unless context implies specific inversion

# Musical Context/Application
Subordinate harmonies:
- Are placed in parentheses in analytical notation to highlight the prolonged harmony
- May include dominant, subdominant, and other functional chords
- Lose much of their independent functional status when subordinate
- In pedal point contexts, cannot be assigned a specific inversion

The distinction between subordinate and prolonged harmonies is essential for understanding harmonic rhythm and phrase structure.

# Common Confusions
- Analyzing subordinate harmonies as having full functional weight (their status is reduced)
- Assigning inversions to subordinate harmonies within pedal points (bass is replaced)
- Treating subordinate dominants as cadential (only root-position V can be cadential)
