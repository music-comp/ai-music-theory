## Definition

Theme and variations can refer to: (1) an instrumental genre, (2) a compositional procedure, or (3) a formal category. As a formal category, it is the least complex of the full-movement forms: a main theme, constructed as either a small ternary or small binary, is followed by an indefinite number of varied repetitions.

## Theme Structure

- Almost always built as a **small ternary** or **small binary**
- Small binary is prominent in variation themes because it avoids overexposing the basic idea (no internal return of the basic idea)
- Each variation normally retains the theme's overall form and phrase functions

## Typical Progression of Variations

1. **Early variations**: Ornamentations making the relation to theme evident
2. **Middle variations**: Melodic framework disappears; harmonic-formal plan remains
3. **Modal shift**: One variation shifts to minore (or maggiore)
4. **Slow variation**: Distinctly slower tempo (usually marked Adagio)
5. **Finale**: Final variation in faster tempo than the theme

## Structural Alterations

### Within a Variation
- Form may change, especially in minore
- Phrase functions may be altered while retaining overall structure

### Between Variations
- Extensions or interpolations may be added before the finale
- These additions break the regular succession and draw attention to the finale

### After Final Variation
- Brief closing section or full-fledged coda may follow
- Coda breaks the pattern of formal symmetry
- May bring back the theme in its original, unvaried form

## Double Variations

A related form using **two themes** as the basis for variation:
- Haydn regularly employs this form
- Second theme is typically minore (or maggiore)
- Themes alternate and become varied on each reappearance

## Examples

- Haydn, String Quartet in B-flat, Op. 50, No. 1, ii
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in A, K. 331, i
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E, Op. 109, iii
