# Quick Definition
Theme and variations is a formal category in which a main theme (built as small ternary or small binary) is followed by an indefinite number of varied repetitions. It is the least complex of the full-movement formal types.

# Core Definition
As a category of classical form, theme and variations is the least complex of the full-movement formal types. The basic plan: a main theme, constructed as either a small ternary or small binary, is followed by an indefinite number of varied repetitions. Variations normally adhere to the theme's overall form and phrase functions, though alterations occur in the minore, before the finale, and in the coda (Caplin, pp. 591-593).

# Prerequisites
- **Small ternary** -- One of two typical theme structures
- **Small binary** -- The other typical theme structure, unusually prominent in variations

# Key Properties
1. Three senses: genre, compositional procedure, and formal category
2. Theme is invariably a small ternary or small binary
3. Small binary is unusually prominent (avoids overexposing the basic idea)
4. Early variations: ornamental, making relation to theme evident
5. Later variations: melodic framework disappears, harmonic-formal plan remains
6. One variation shifts mode (minore/maggiore)
7. A distinctly slower variation (Adagio) often appears toward the end
8. The finale variation is frequently set in a faster tempo
9. No consistent overarching shape (not binary, ternary, rondo, or sonata-like)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Identify the theme and its formal type (small ternary or binary)
2. Confirm that subsequent sections are varied repetitions of the theme
3. Track the progressive increase in rhythmic complexity
4. Look for the minore variation and the Adagio variation
5. Identify the finale variation (faster tempo)

# Context & Application
Though treated in the chapter on slow movements, theme and variations appears frequently in outer movements, especially finales. Most examples in Caplin's chapter are actually from finale movements. The form is also found as an independent work.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 592): Mozart, Piano Sonata in A, K. 331, i -- variations reference different movement types (variation 4: trio-like, variation 5: slow-movement style, variation 6: finale character).
**Example 2** (p. 611): Beethoven, Piano Trio in C minor, Op. 1, No. 3, iii -- final variation followed by coda returning the theme in original setting.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Small ternary** -- Standard theme form
- **Small binary** -- Alternative theme form (unusually prominent here)
## Enables
- **Double variations** -- Uses two themes instead of one
- **Variation finale** -- The final, faster variation
- **Coda (variations)** -- Concluding section after the final variation
## Related
- **Minore/maggiore** -- The modal-shift variation
- **Extensions and interpolations (variations)** -- Added passages disrupting the regular succession
## Contrasts With
- **Large ternary form** -- Alternative slow-movement form with different organization
- **Sonata without development** -- Another alternative slow-movement form

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Expecting a clear overarching formal shape (binary, ternary, rondo)
  **Correction**: Variations form rarely projects such designs, though a broad shape of increasing complexity is often discernible

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking theme and variations is exclusively a slow-movement form
  **Clarification**: Though treated in the slow-movement chapter, it appears frequently in finales and as independent works
