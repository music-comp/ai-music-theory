# Quick Definition
The central, most dramatically charged portion of the development section, consisting of a large-scale model, its sequential repetition, fragmentation, a half cadence or dominant arrival, and a standing on the dominant.

# Core Definition
According to Caplin, the core consists of these elements in order: (1) establishment of a large-scale model, usually 4-8 measures; (2) sequential repetition of the complete model, one or more times; (3) fragmentation into smaller grouping units; (4) a half cadence or dominant arrival in either a development key or the home key; (5) a standing on the dominant (p. 444). The core "typically projects an emotional quality of instability, restlessness, and dramatic conflict" with forte dynamics and often Sturm und Drang character.

# Prerequisites
- **Development section** — The formal context in which the core appears
- **Model-sequence technique** — The primary compositional process driving the core
- **Fragmentation** — The process of breaking down grouping units
- **Standing on the dominant** — The postcadential unit concluding the core

# Key Properties
1. Model normally 4-8 measures; must be large enough for fragmentation
2. Sequential repetition one or more times; pattern may change during core
3. Fragmentation breaks grouping structure into progressively smaller units
4. Harmonic goal usually dominant (of home key or development key)
5. Forte dynamic, thick textures, continuous rhythmic activity ("chugging" effect)
6. Polyphonic devices (imitation, canon, fugal entries) may contribute
7. A large development may contain two cores

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Look for a large-scale model (4-8 measures) that is sequenced at least once
2. Identify fragmentation into smaller units following the sequence(s)
3. Locate the HC or dominant arrival marking the structural end
4. Find the standing on the dominant that marks the final boundary

# Context & Application
The core is the most structurally defined part of the development section. With its emphasis on sequential organization, it strongly projects the sense of "being in the middle" of the form, comparable to continuation function at a higher level. The term "core" (Kern) was first introduced by Erwin Ratz, though Caplin's treatment includes the standing on the dominant, which Ratz excluded.

# Examples
**Example 13.1** (p. 446): Mozart, Piano Sonata in A minor, K. 310, i — 4-m. model (mm. 58-61) of Sturm und Drang character, sequenced twice down a perfect fifth; fragmentation at m. 70 into 1-m. ideas with stepwise descent; HC at m. 74; standing on dominant to m. 80.

**Example 13.3** (p. 454): Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, H. 49, i — 4-m. model sequenced twice by descending thirds; fragmentation at m. 97 reduces to 2-m. then 1-m. units; HC in F minor at m. 103.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Model-sequence technique** — The primary compositional engine of the core
- **Continuation function** — The core is a magnified continuation
## Enables
- **Standing on the dominant** — Postcadential unit following the core's conclusion
- **Recapitulation** — Prepared by the core's dominant arrival
## Related
- **Pre-core** — The section preceding the core
## Contrasts With
- **Pseudo-core** — Has core-like character but lacks model-sequence organization
- **Transition-like core substitute** — Functions in place of core but resembles transition

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Identifying any sequential passage as a core
  **Correction**: A core requires a large-scale model (4-8 measures), not just brief sequential activity; small models (1-2 measures) suggest a transition-like passage

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating the core with the entire development section
  **Clarification**: The core is a specific formal unit within the development, usually preceded by a pre-core and concluded by the standing on the dominant
