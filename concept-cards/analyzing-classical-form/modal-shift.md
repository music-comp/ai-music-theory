# Quick Definition
A change from major to minor within a subordinate theme introduces a broader spectrum of pitches and creates looser formal expression. It can occur almost anywhere except at the final cadence, and is confirmed as a loosening device by its general absence in tight-knit main themes. The major modality is always ultimately restored.

# Core Definition
Almost all subordinate themes reside in major mode. A change from major to minor is frequently encountered within a subordinate theme, introducing a broader spectrum of pitches and creating **looser formal expression** (similar to chromaticism). This is confirmed as a loosening device by its general absence in tight-knit main themes. Modal shift can occur anywhere except at the final cadence: most commonly with continuation function, at the initial tonic of an ECP, or at the beginning of an expanded repeat. It is particularly dramatic at the theme's very beginning. The **major modality is always ultimately restored**, at least by the final cadence of the theme or group (Caplin, Ch. 12, pp. 419-420).

# Key Properties
1. Change from major to minor within subordinate theme
2. Introduces broader pitch spectrum = looser expression
3. Confirmed as loosening by absence in tight-knit main themes
4. Most common: with continuation, at ECP's initial tonic, at expanded repeat's start
5. Most dramatic: at very beginning of subordinate theme
6. Major mode ALWAYS restored by final cadence
7. Brings remote regions (bIII, bVI, bVII, bII) into play
