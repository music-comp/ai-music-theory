# Quick Definition
A brief melodic idea linking the end of one phrase to the beginning of the next, generating rhythmic continuity. It belongs to neither phrase and could be eliminated without upsetting the formal organization. Term introduced by William Rothstein.

# Core Definition
A lead-in consists of a brief melodic idea that links the end of the antecedent with the beginning of the consequent. It functions largely to generate rhythmic continuity and flow from one phrase to the next. Its melodic material belongs neither to the weak cadence nor to the return of the basic idea -- a lead-in could easily be eliminated without upsetting the melodic, harmonic, and formal organization of the phrases (p. 108). Term introduced by William Rothstein. Lead-ins are not confined to periods; they may be used in other formal contexts as well.

# Prerequisites
- **Period** — Lead-ins most commonly appear between antecedent and consequent

# Key Properties
1. Brief melodic idea linking two phrases
2. Generates rhythmic continuity and flow
3. Belongs to neither phrase (neither cadence nor return)
4. Could be eliminated without formal disruption
5. Not confined to periods
6. Distinguished from accompaniment continuity (when melody has a rest, no lead-in)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Look for melodic material between the cadential arrival and the return of b.i.
2. Check if it belongs to neither phrase (not part of cadence, not part of b.i. return)
3. Verify it could be removed without structural disruption

# Context & Application
Lead-ins promote rhythmic continuity. The effect can also be achieved by maintaining accompaniment through phrase boundaries, but this is not technically a lead-in (which involves melodic material).

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 106, Ex. 3.13, m. 4): Mozart, K. 466, ii -- lead-in between antecedent and consequent.

**Example 2** (p. 103, Ex. 3.10, m. 4): Mozart, K. 333, ii -- "extraordinarily elaborate" lead-in.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Period** — Common context for lead-ins
## Related
- **Elision** — Another boundary process (but different: elision overlaps, lead-in links)
## Contrasts With
- **Elision** — Lead-in creates space between phrases; elision overlaps them

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating sustained accompaniment as a lead-in
  **Correction**: Lead-ins involve melodic material, not just accompaniment continuing

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assigning the lead-in to one of the two phrases
  **Clarification**: It belongs to neither; it is formally parenthetical
