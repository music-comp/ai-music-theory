# Quick Definition
Many Haydn and Mozart slow introductions present material unrelated to the rest of the movement, but late works and Beethoven increasingly hint at ideas more fully realized in the exposition and later sections.

# Core Definition
"Many slow introductions by Haydn and Mozart present melodic-motivic material having no immediately obvious relation to the rest of the movement" (p. 578). "But in some of their late works, and in many by Beethoven, the slow introduction hints at ideas that are more fully realized later on." Anticipatory techniques at the end of the introduction may include anticipating the opening harmony, the opening melody, or both, creating a smooth transition into the exposition.

# Key Properties
1. Haydn/Mozart: material often unrelated to the rest of the movement
2. Late Haydn/Mozart and Beethoven: material increasingly foreshadows later ideas
3. Fanfare gestures common throughout
4. Introduction may anticipate exposition's opening harmony
5. Introduction may anticipate exposition's opening melody
6. Both harmonic and melodic anticipation may occur together

# Examples
**Example 16.4** (p. 581): Beethoven, "Kreutzer" Sonata — introduction ends on iv, anticipating the exposition's opening pre-dominant harmony; violin repeatedly sounds the opening motive E-F from m. 16 onward, anticipating the main theme's melody.
