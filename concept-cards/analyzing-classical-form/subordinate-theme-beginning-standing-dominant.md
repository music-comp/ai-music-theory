# Quick Definition
A subordinate theme can begin with a standing on the dominant, where the dominant from the end of the transition is held over as harmonic support. This standing yields directly to continuation or cadential function (not a standard initiating function), and a marked change of melodic-motivic material typically signals that the subordinate theme has begun despite the retained dominant harmony.

# Core Definition
The boundary between transition and subordinate theme can become somewhat blurred when the dominant from the end of the transition is held over to provide harmonic support for a standing on the dominant at the beginning of the subordinate theme. Such a standing on the dominant would not be followed by one of the standard initiating functions (presentation, compound basic idea), for the listener would then hear the standing as belonging to the transition and the initiating function as the true beginning of the subordinate theme. Instead, the standing yields directly to continuation or cadential functions. In most cases, a marked change of melodic-motivic material helps indicate where the transition ends and the subordinate theme begins. Sometimes the transition has its own standing on the dominant that differs from the one beginning the subordinate theme, and this change of material signals a new theme despite the retention of dominant harmony. The distinction from a weakened tonic prolongation depends on whether the melodic material implies dominant or tonic prolongation (Caplin, Ch. 12, pp. 415-417).

# Key Properties
1. Dominant from transition held over into subordinate theme
2. Standing does NOT lead to standard initiating function
3. If initiating function followed, standing would be heard as part of transition
4. Instead yields to continuation or cadential functions
5. Change of melodic material signals new theme despite retained harmony
6. Transition may have its own standing on dominant that differs from subordinate theme's
7. Must distinguish from weakened tonic prolongation (different implied prolongation)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Look for dominant harmony continuing from the transition into the next section
2. Check whether new melodic material appears over the retained dominant
3. Determine whether the material implies dominant prolongation (standing) or tonic prolongation over dominant pedal (weakened presentation)
4. If dominant prolongation: beginning with standing on the dominant
5. If tonic prolongation: weakened presentation (different concept)

# Context & Application
Beginning with a standing on the dominant creates maximum formal continuity between transition and subordinate theme at the cost of boundary clarity. The technique effectively replaces a standard initiating function with a prolongation of the transition's final harmony, creating a loose organizational character from the very start of the subordinate theme.

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 415-417): Beethoven, String Quartet Op. 18/3, i, mm. 43-59 -- Transition ends with HC at m. 47; twofold repetition of HC gesture creates transition's own standing on dominant (mm. 48-51); completely new material at m. 51 signals subordinate theme beginning with a new standing on dominant; 4-measure continuation leads to PAC at m. 57; highly symmetrical and compact despite omitted initiation.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Confusing a standing on the dominant with a weakened tonic prolongation over dominant pedal
  **Correction**: If the melodic material implies tonic prolongation, it is a weakened presentation; if it implies dominant prolongation, it is a standing on the dominant
