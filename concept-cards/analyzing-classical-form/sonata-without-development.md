# Quick Definition
Sonata without development is a two-part form consisting of a sonata exposition followed immediately by a recapitulation, without a development section. A retransition typically connects the two, and a coda may be appended.

# Core Definition
Sonata without development is a two-part form consisting of a sonata exposition followed immediately by a recapitulation, to which a coda may optionally be appended. The exposition is never repeated, because repetition would arouse expectations for a development section. A retransition regularly appears to smooth the connection. The recapitulation is constructed essentially the same as in regular sonata form (Caplin, pp. 593-594).

# Prerequisites
- **Sonata form** -- The complete form from which the development is omitted
- **Recapitulation** -- Functions the same way as in regular sonata

# Key Properties
1. Two parts: exposition + recapitulation (no development)
2. Exposition is never repeated
3. A retransition regularly connects exposition to recapitulation
4. The retransition must not project a "true development" sense
5. Recapitulation follows regular sonata norms with tonal adjustments
6. Secondary development frequently appears in main theme or transition
7. May feature a truncated recapitulation (subordinate theme eliminated)
8. Optional coda may follow

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Identify a sonata exposition (main theme, transition, subordinate theme)
2. Confirm no development section follows
3. Look for a retransition leading directly to the recapitulation
4. Verify the recapitulation adjusts material to the home key

# Context & Application
Most often used in slow movements. Mozart employs it most frequently, but examples appear in Haydn and Beethoven. Also used in fast-movement overtures. The form has received many names: "sonatina form," "slow-movement form," "overture form," "cavatina form," and Hepokoski and Darcy's "type 1" sonata form.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 593): Haydn, String Quartet in B-flat, Op. 33, No. 4, iii.
**Example 2** (p. 593): Mozart, Piano Sonata in F, K. 332, ii.
**Example 3** (p. 593): Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F minor, Op. 2, No. 1, ii.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming a truncated recapitulation makes the form a large ternary
  **Correction**: Even with a truncated recapitulation, the form is best understood as a deviated sonata without development; the listener initially experiences it as sonata form, and the large ternary interpretation emerges only retrospectively

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Why the exposition is not repeated
  **Clarification**: Repetition would create expectations for a development section, and the immediate recapitulation after repetition would be redundant (a third run-through of the same material)
