# Quick Definition
The modification of diatonic pitches through sharps, flats, or naturals to create chromatic harmonies such as secondary dominants, augmented sixths, and modal borrowing.

# Formal Definition
Chromatic alteration refers to the raising or lowering of diatonic pitches to create chromatic effects within tonal harmony. These alterations produce secondary dominants, augmented-sixth chords, Neapolitan harmonies, and chords borrowed from the parallel mode.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Types of chromatic alteration:
- **Raised pitches**: Create leading tones (raised 4 for V/V, raised 2 for V/III)
- **Lowered pitches**: Create mixture chords (lowered 6 in major, lowered 2 for Neapolitan)
- **Applied alterations**: Create secondary dominants of various degrees

Chromatic alterations in sequences:
- Convert diatonic passing chords to genuine secondary dominants
- Intensify dominant-tonic implications at each step
- Add harmonic interest to pattern-based progressions

# Musical Context/Application
Chromatic alterations:
- Enrich harmonic vocabulary beyond diatonic resources
- Create directed motion toward specific harmonies
- Enable modal borrowing (mixture)
- Intensify pre-dominant function (augmented sixths)

The circle-of-fifths and ascending-second sequences can be chromatically altered to strengthen functional implications.

# Examples
Example 1.22c (p. 45-46): "Each six-three chord becomes a genuine secondary dominant" through chromatic alteration in the ascending-second sequence.

Example 1.17c-g (pp. 42-43): The descending-fifth sequence "can be varied in manifold ways through... chromatic alteration."

Initial tonic may be "chromatically altered to become V/IV or V/II" (Example 1.14c-d, p. 39).

# Common Confusions
- Treating chromatic alterations as modulation (they often remain in the key)
- Not recognizing the functional implications of alterations
- Confusing mixture with secondary dominants
- Missing chromatic intensification in sequences
