# Quick Definition
The modification of diatonic pitches through sharps, flats, or naturals to create chromatic harmonies such as secondary dominants, augmented sixths, Neapolitan chords, and modal borrowing.

# Core Definition
Chromatic alteration refers to the raising or lowering of diatonic scale degrees to create harmonies beyond the diatonic vocabulary. These alterations produce secondary dominants, augmented-sixth chords, Neapolitan harmonies, and chords borrowed from the parallel mode. In sequential progressions, chromatic alterations can convert diatonic passing chords into "genuine secondary dominant[s]" (p. 45), intensifying dominant-tonic implications at each step.

# Prerequisites
- **Scale degree** — Must understand diatonic scale degrees to recognize alterations
- **Roman numeral analysis** — Chromatic chords are labeled relative to the diatonic framework

# Key Properties
1. Raised pitches create leading tones (e.g., raised 4 for V/V)
2. Lowered pitches create mixture chords (e.g., lowered 6 for augmented sixths)
3. Applied alterations create secondary dominants of various degrees
4. Chromatic alterations in sequences intensify functional implications
5. The initial tonic may be "chromatically altered to become V/IV or V/II" (p. 39)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Compare the pitch content of a chord against the prevailing diatonic scale
2. Note any sharps, flats, or naturals foreign to the key
3. Determine whether the alteration creates a secondary dominant, augmented sixth, Neapolitan, or mixture chord
4. In sequences, check whether diatonic chords have been chromatically altered to strengthen functional motion

# Context & Application
Chromatic alterations enrich the harmonic palette beyond diatonic resources. They create directed motion toward specific harmonies, enable modal borrowing (mixture), and intensify pre-dominant function through augmented sixths. In sequential progressions, chromatic alteration can transform pattern-based motion into chains of functional implications, as when each six-three chord in an ascending-second sequence becomes a genuine secondary dominant.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating chromatic alterations as modulations to new keys
  **Correction**: Many chromatic alterations remain firmly within the home key (secondary dominants, augmented sixths)

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing mixture (modal borrowing) with secondary dominants
  **Clarification**: Mixture borrows from the parallel mode; secondary dominants tonicize specific scale degrees through applied leading tones
