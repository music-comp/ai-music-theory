## Definition

Concerto form is a full-movement form derived from the Baroque "ritornello form" and strongly infused with elements of classical sonata form. It is used in the first movement of all classical concertos.

## Six Principal Sections

| Section | Function | Analogue |
|---------|----------|----------|
| Opening Ritornello | Sonata exposition (in home key) | — |
| Solo Exposition | Sonata exposition (with modulation) | Exposition |
| Subordinate-key Ritornello | Closing section (in subordinate key) | Closing section |
| Solo Development | Sonata development | Development |
| Solo Recapitulation | Sonata recapitulation | Recapitulation |
| Closing Ritornello | Closing section (in home key) + cadenza | Closing/Coda |

## Important Note

Concerto form does **not contain a section that functions like a sonata-form coda**.

## Usage

- First movement of all classical concertos (required)
- Sometimes in slow movements
- Rarely in finales (which typically use sonata-rondo)

## Historical Background

The classical concerto derives from the Baroque ritornello form:
- **Tutti passages** (ritornellos) played by full orchestra
- **Solo passages** featuring one or more instruments with accompaniment
- Opening ritornello brings primary melodic-motivic content
- Closing ritornello creates a frame for the movement

## Aesthetic Goals

The classical concerto must balance:
1. Highlighting the solo instrument against the larger forces
2. Ensuring the orchestra is not reduced to mere accompaniment

Techniques include:
- Solo plays ideas not previously heard by orchestra
- Solo has principal modulatory action
- Cadenza spotlights solo
- Orchestra provides textural frame (opening and closing ritornellos)
