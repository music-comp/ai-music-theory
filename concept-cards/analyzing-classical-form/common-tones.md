# Quick Definition
Pitches shared between two different chords, explaining functional similarity between substitute harmonies and enabling smooth voice-leading connections.

# Core Definition
Common tones are pitches belonging to both an original harmony and a substitute harmony. Caplin explains that "the original harmony and substitute harmony have two chord tones in common, which largely accounts for their functional similarity" (p. 35). This shared pitch content underlies the system of harmonic substitution fundamental to tonal function theory.

# Prerequisites
- **Chord quality** — Must understand chord construction to identify shared pitches

# Key Properties
1. I and VI share scale degrees 1 and 3 (explains VI as tonic substitute)
2. II and IV share scale degrees 4 and 6 (explains pre-dominant equivalence)
3. I and III share scale degrees 3 and 5
4. Common tones enable smooth voice-leading through pitch retention
5. The sharing of two tones largely accounts for functional similarity between chords

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Spell out the chord tones of both harmonies
2. Identify which pitches appear in both chords
3. Two shared tones typically indicate functional similarity
4. Note how common tones facilitate voice-leading connections

# Context & Application
Common tones are significant for understanding substitute harmonies, voice-leading connections between chords, and functional similarity within harmonic categories. The principle explains why VI can substitute for I (tonic function) and why II can substitute for IV (pre-dominant function). In voice-leading practice, common tones are typically retained in the same voice for smoothest connection.

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 35-36): Example 1.10 demonstrates substitute harmonies where "the original harmony and substitute harmony have two chord tones in common."

**Example 2** (p. 35): VI substitutes for I because both share scale degrees 1 and 3, which "largely accounts for their functional similarity."

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Chord quality** — Shared pitches depend on the intervallic content of each chord
## Enables
- **Tonic substitute** — Common tones between I and VI explain tonic substitution
- **Submediant harmony** — VI's tonic function derives from common tones with I
## Related
- **Voice leading** — Common tones facilitate smooth voice connections
- **Prolonged harmony** — Substitute harmonies sharing common tones participate in prolongation
## Contrasts With
- **Pivot chord** — Pivot chords share function in two keys; common tones share pitches within one key

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming any shared pitch constitutes meaningful common-tone relationship
  **Correction**: The functional significance of common tones relates to shared chord tones between functionally similar harmonies, not incidental pitch overlap

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing common-tone connections with pivot-chord modulation
  **Clarification**: Common tones explain functional similarity within a key; pivot chords function across two keys during modulation
