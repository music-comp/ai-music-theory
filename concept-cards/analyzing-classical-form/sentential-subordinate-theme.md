# Quick Definition
Most subordinate themes employ sentential functions (presentation, continuation, cadential), but the result is not a tight-knit sentence: loosening techniques produce a "sentential" or "sentence-like" organization. Distinctively, continuation and cadential functions often occupy separate, distinct phrases rather than fusing into one, as they would in a tight-knit sentence.

# Core Definition
Most subordinate themes are constructed out of the three sentential functions: presentation, continuation, and cadential. However, the resulting form is not a tight-knit sentence but rather a sentential or sentence-like organization -- loosening techniques prevent the compact, efficient expression of a true sentence. A key distinction is that unlike a tight-knit sentence (where continuation and cadential fuse into a single phrase), subordinate themes often give continuation and cadential their own distinct groups, defined by melodic-motivic content, rhythmic patterning, and accompanimental figuration. The continuation phrase often does not end with a cadence; the cadence is saved for a phrase of uniquely cadential function. The forward-driving character of sentential functions creates momentum toward the cadential goal, allows for dramatic buildup to the essential PAC, and permits extensive elaboration without losing formal direction (Caplin, Ch. 12, pp. 380-381, 246-248).

# Key Properties
1. Employs sentential functions: presentation, continuation, cadential
2. NOT a tight-knit sentence; rather "sentential" or "sentence-like"
3. Continuation and cadential often occupy distinct, separate phrases
4. Continuation phrase often does not end with a cadence
5. Cadence saved for a phrase of purely cadential function
6. Forward-driving character supports dramatic buildup to PAC

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Identify presentation, continuation, and cadential functions
2. Check whether they appear in a loose, expanded manner
3. Note if continuation and cadential have separate phrases (not fused)
4. Observe whether the continuation ends non-cadentially
5. Determine whether the length and complexity vastly exceed simple 8-measure sentence norms

# Context & Application
The question of whether to label these structures "sentences" is one Caplin addresses explicitly: when loosening techniques do not exceed the deviations discussed for tight-knit themes, the label "sentence" may apply. But when the length and complexity vastly differ from the norms of a simple 8-measure sentence, it is more appropriate to speak of a "sentential" or "sentence-like" organization, restricting the term "sentence" to more tightly knit manifestations.
