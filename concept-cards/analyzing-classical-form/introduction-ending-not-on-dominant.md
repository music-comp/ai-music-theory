# Quick Definition
While most slow introductions end on the dominant of the home key, some conclude on a different harmony -- such as a minor triad functioning as subdominant (iv) -- which serves as the pre-dominant for the main theme's opening, anticipating both its harmony and sometimes its melody.

# Core Definition
In the majority of cases, the harmonic goal of a slow introduction is the dominant of the home key, articulated by an HC or dominant arrival and followed by a standing on the dominant. In some exceptional cases, however, the introduction ends on a different harmony. Ending on a minor triad is unusual, since that chord cannot be construed as a dominant. The logic becomes clear when the minor sonority functions as a pre-dominant (such as iv in the home key) for the main theme's opening harmony. Rather than choosing a harmony whose resolution would bring the pre-dominant at the start of the exposition, the composer introduces this pre-dominant in advance, toward the end of the introduction, and simply sustains it for the new beginning. In addition to anticipating the opening harmony of the main theme, the end of the introduction may also anticipate that theme's melody.

# Key Properties
- Most introductions end on dominant of home key (HC or dominant arrival)
- Some end on other harmonies, especially pre-dominant sonorities
- Minor triad ending functions as iv in home key
- Pre-dominant is introduced in advance, then sustained into the exposition
- May also anticipate the main theme's opening melody
- Creates smooth but unusual connection between introduction and exposition

# Construction / Recognition
Examine the final harmony of the slow introduction. If it is not the dominant of the home key, determine its function in relation to the main theme's opening harmony. A minor triad that cannot function as a dominant likely serves as a pre-dominant (iv or ii) leading to the dominant at the start of the main theme. Look for melodic anticipation of the main theme in the introduction's closing measures.

# Context & Application
This technique represents an exceptional strategy for connecting the slow introduction to the exposition. Instead of the conventional pattern (dominant preparation creating expectation for tonic), the composer introduces a pre-dominant harmony early, creating an even smoother harmonic connection between the two sections. The technique is particularly effective when the main theme itself begins with a pre-dominant-to-dominant progression.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Violin Sonata in A minor ("Kreutzer"), Op. 47, i: introduction literally ends on D minor at m. 15, understood as iv in A minor (the home key); the violin part repeatedly sounds the opening motive E-F from m. 16 onward, anticipating the main theme's melody; the main theme then begins with this pre-dominant (iv) leading to V (mm. 19-20)

# Common Errors
- Assuming every introduction must end on the dominant
- Treating a non-dominant ending as an error rather than a deliberate compositional strategy

# Common Confusions
- A pre-dominant ending is NOT a failed dominant arrival -- it is a deliberate harmonic strategy that allows the introduction to anticipate and connect smoothly with the main theme's opening harmony
