# Quick Definition
A progression that confirms a tonal center by bringing harmonic functions in order: (initial) tonic, pre-dominant, dominant, and (final) tonic. The dominant must first appear and remain in root position. Three kinds: authentic, half, and deceptive.

# Core Definition
Cadential progressions confirm a tonal center by bringing the fundamental harmonic functions in this order: (initial) tonic, pre-dominant, dominant, and (final) tonic. Essential for the idea of cadential progression is the requirement that the dominant must first appear, and then remain, in root position (pp. 26-27). Three kinds exist: an authentic cadential progression ends with a root-position tonic; a half-cadential progression ends with a root-position dominant triad (not seventh); a deceptive cadential progression replaces the final tonic with another harmony (p. 27).

# Prerequisites
- **Harmonic function** — Must understand the functional ordering T-PD-D-T
- **Harmonic progression** — Must know the three progression types

# Key Properties
1. Functional ordering: (initial) tonic - pre-dominant - dominant - (final) tonic
2. Central axiom: dominant must first appear and remain in root position
3. Three kinds: authentic (ends on I), half (ends on V triad), deceptive (V to substitute)
4. Complete: contains all functions; incomplete: omits initial tonic and/or pre-dominant
5. Nothing defective about incomplete progressions (p. 37)
6. Initial tonic typically in first inversion (I6)
7. Annotated with horizontal square bracket in analysis

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify a Cadential Progression:
1. Look for the functional succession T-PD-D-T (or subset)
2. Verify that V is in root position from its first appearance
3. Determine the type: authentic (ends on root-position I), half (ends on V triad), deceptive (V to non-tonic)
4. Determine completeness: are initial tonic and pre-dominant both present?
5. Mark with horizontal square bracket in analysis

# Context & Application
Cadential progressions are essential for formal closure -- they define phrase endings and thematic boundaries. The distinction between cadential progression (harmonic event) and cadence type (formal event) is important: cadential progressions may occur in contexts that do not give rise to genuine cadences (such as codettas). Analytical notation uses horizontal square brackets to embrace the fundamental harmonies.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 26, Ex. 1.2): Basic cadential progressions: I6-II6-V7-I (authentic cadential).

**Example 2** (p. 28, Ex. 1.4): Mozart, K. 332, mm. 56-57 -- incomplete half-cadential progression initiated by IV6, embellished by Italian augmented sixth.

**Example 3** (p. 29, Ex. 1.5): Haydn, Symphony No. 93, mm. 35-36 -- authentic cadential progression completes the theme.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Placing a square bracket around any V-I motion
  **Correction**: Only bracket genuine cadential progressions where V is in root position

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking complete progressions are "stronger" than incomplete ones
  **Clarification**: Both are fully viable; context determines which form is appropriate (p. 37)
- **Confusion**: Confusing cadential progression (harmonic) with cadence type (formal event)
  **Clarification**: The three cadential progressions (authentic, half, deceptive) are distinct from the three cadence types (PAC, IAC, HC); deceptive progressions do not produce a basic cadence type
