# Quick Definition
It is a central axiom that dominant harmony can acquire cadential status only when it initially appears in root position and retains that position until resolution. Any inversion of the dominant automatically destroys its cadential potential.

# Core Definition
"It is a central axiom of the approach to harmony and cadence in this textbook that dominant harmony can acquire cadential status only when it initially appears in root position and retains that position until its resolution to tonic. Any inversion of the dominant automatically destroys its cadential potential" (p. 36). One exception: V4/2 in a deceptive cadential progression (V-V4/2-I6), where the bass motion from 5 to 4 represents contrapuntal passing motion rather than genuine change of position (p. 41).

# Prerequisites
- **Cadential progression** — Must understand what cadential progressions are
- **Prolongational progression** — Must understand the alternative classification

# Key Properties
1. Cadential V: must be in root position from first appearance through resolution
2. Prolongational V: any inverted dominant (V6, V4/3, etc.)
3. I-V6-I is prolongational, not cadential
4. I-V-I (with root-position V) may be cadential
5. Even momentary inversion destroys cadential status
6. Exception: V4/2 in deceptive progression (bass motion 5-4 is passing, not genuine inversion)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Apply the Axiom:
1. Identify all dominant harmonies in the passage
2. Check if each dominant first appears in root position
3. Verify it remains in root position until resolution
4. If inverted at any point: classify the progression as prolongational
5. Check for the one exception: V4/2 in deceptive cadential context

# Context & Application
This axiom is the most distinctive and consequential analytical principle in Caplin's approach. It provides an objective criterion for distinguishing formal cadences from mere harmonic motion. The restriction may seem overly limiting to students who have been taught that any V-I creates a cadence, but "it is to be hoped that the analytical gains from this restrictive definition of cadential progression will more than offset any initial discomfort it may cause" (p. 36).

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 28, Ex. 1.4): Mozart, K. 332, mm. 50-53 -- V6 as neighboring chord within tonic prolongation; NOT cadential because dominant is inverted.

**Example 2** (p. 41, Ex. 1.16e): V4/2 in deceptive cadential progression to I6 -- the one exception, where bass motion 5-4 is passing rather than genuine change of position.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Labeling any V-I motion as cadential
  **Correction**: Only root-position V that remains in root position through resolution is cadential

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking this axiom is universally accepted in music theory
  **Clarification**: This is specifically Caplin's approach; other theorists may classify inverted V-I as cadential
- **Confusion**: Missing the V4/2 exception in deceptive progressions
  **Clarification**: When V moves to V4/2 before resolving to I6, the bass motion (5-4) is passing, not a genuine inversion
