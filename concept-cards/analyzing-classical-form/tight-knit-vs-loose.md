# Quick Definition
A fundamental distinction (first observed by Schoenberg) between structural stability (tight-knit) and instability (loose), determined by seven parameters: tonality, harmony, cadence, grouping structure, functional efficiency, motivic material, and thematic conventionality. The distinction is always relative, never absolute.

# Core Definition
Seven parameters contribute to whether a passage is tight-knit or loose. Tonality: home key (tight) vs. modulating/distant keys (loose). Cadence: PAC (tight) vs. no cadence (loose). Harmony: tonic prolongation (tight) vs. sequential/chromatic (loose). Grouping: symmetrical 4+4 (tight) vs. asymmetrical (loose). Functional efficiency: efficient (tight) vs. redundant with extensions (loose). Motivic material: uniform (tight) vs. diverse (loose). Thematic conventionality: period (most tight) vs. nonconventional (loose). Among conventional types, the period is most tight-knit, the sentence less so. The A section of a small ternary is tight-knit; the B section is loose. The A' section varies. Crucially, the distinction is always relative -- a passage is only tight-knit or loose in comparison with another.

# Prerequisites

# Key Properties
1. Seven parameters: tonality, harmony, cadence, grouping, efficiency, motives, thematic design
2. Period is most tight-knit conventional type; sentence is less so
3. Always relative, never absolute
4. A section: tight-knit; B section: loose; A': variable
5. Parameters may conflict (some tight, some loose)
6. First observed by Arnold Schoenberg

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating tight-knit and loose as absolute categories
  **Correction**: Every case is relative to the compositional setting; a sentence may be tight in one context and loose in another
