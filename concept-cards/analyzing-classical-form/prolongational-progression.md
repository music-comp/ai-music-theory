# Quick Definition
A progression that sustains a single harmony (the prolonged harmony) through intervening subordinate chords such as neighboring, passing, and substitute harmonies.

# Formal Definition
Prolongational progressions sustain in time an individual harmony (the prolonged harmony) through the use of intervening chords (subordinate harmonies) such as neighboring, passing, and substitute harmonies, as well as through pedal point. Unlike cadential progressions, prolongational progressions do not confirm tonality but rather extend or elaborate a single harmonic function.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Four main techniques of prolongation:

1. **Pedal Point**: The root of the prolonged harmony is sustained in the bass throughout
2. **Neighboring Chords**: Prolonged harmony retains its position (e.g., I - V6 - I)
3. **Passing Chords**: Prolonged harmony changes position (e.g., I - V6/5 - I6)
4. **Substitute Harmonies**: A harmony sharing function with the prolonged chord (e.g., I - VI)

In most prolongational progressions, the prolonged harmony appears at beginning and end. However, the subordinate harmony may initiate (V - I - V - I) or end the progression (I - V - I - V).

# Musical Context/Application
Prolongational progressions typically:
- Occur at the beginning of themes (presentations, basic ideas)
- Support non-cadential formal functions
- Create stability without confirming tonality
- May contain what looks like V-I but functions prolongationally (when V is inverted)

The simple progression I - V - I, often taught as a "cadence formula," is frequently better understood as prolongational when the melody embellishes scale degrees 3 or 5.

Analytical notation: Subordinate harmonies are placed in parentheses to highlight the prolonged harmony.

# Common Confusions
- Treating every I - V - I as cadential when many are prolongational
- Not recognizing that inverted dominants cannot be cadential
- Assuming prolongational progressions must always return to the prolonged harmony (they may end with subordinate chord)
- Confusing the terms "neighboring" and "passing" (based on position of prolonged harmony, not literal voice motion)
