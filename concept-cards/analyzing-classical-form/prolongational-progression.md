# Quick Definition
A progression that sustains a single harmony (the prolonged harmony) through intervening subordinate chords such as neighboring, passing, and substitute harmonies, or through pedal point. Unlike cadential progressions, prolongational progressions do not confirm tonality but extend a single function.

# Core Definition
Prolongational progressions sustain in time an individual harmony (the prolonged harmony) through the use of intervening chords (subordinate harmonies) such as neighboring, passing, and substitute harmonies. The harmonic technique of pedal point also serves to prolong a given harmony (pp. 25-26). The many prolongational progressions can be grouped into four main types: (1) pedal point, (2) neighboring chords, (3) passing chords, and (4) substitute harmonies (p. 33).

# Key Properties
1. Four main techniques: pedal point, neighboring chords, passing chords, substitute harmonies
2. The prolonged harmony usually appears at beginning and end of the progression
3. Subordinate harmonies may initiate (V-I-V-I) or end the progression (I-V-I-V)
4. Subordinate harmonies are placed in parentheses in analysis
5. The distinction between neighboring and passing is based on the positions of the prolonged harmony, not literal voice motion
6. Neighboring: prolonged harmony retains its position (e.g., I-V6-I)
7. Passing: prolonged harmony changes position (e.g., I-V6/5-I6)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify a Prolongational Progression:
1. Identify the prolonged harmony (usually tonic, but may be other scale degrees)
2. Determine if intervening chords function as embellishing (neighboring, passing, substitute)
3. Check if the prolonged harmony appears at the boundaries
4. Verify that dominant is NOT in root position (root-position V may signal cadential function)
5. Subordinate harmonies placed in parentheses in analysis

# Context & Application
Prolongational progressions typically occur at the beginning of themes (presentation phrases) and support non-cadential formal functions. The simple progression I-V-I, often taught as a "cadence formula," is frequently better understood as prolongational when the melody embellishes scale degrees 3 or 5 and V is not in root position. Most prolongational progressions prolong tonic, but any harmony can be prolonged.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating every I-V-I as cadential
  **Correction**: When V is inverted (V6, V4/3), the progression is prolongational, not cadential

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing "neighboring" and "passing" based on literal voice motion
  **Clarification**: The distinction is based on the positions of the prolonged harmony: same position = neighboring, changed position = passing (p. 35)
- **Confusion**: Assuming prolongational progressions must return to the prolonged harmony
  **Clarification**: The subordinate harmony may end the progression (e.g., I-V-I-V)
