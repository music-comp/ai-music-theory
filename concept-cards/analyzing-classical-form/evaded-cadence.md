# Quick Definition
An evaded cadence arises when the final cadential tonic fails to appear and the music that follows groups forward with the next phrase rather than backwards with the cadential progression, creating a salient disruption in grouping structure and the sense of "let's try one more time."

# Core Definition
In an evaded cadence, everything is prepared for the final cadential goal, but the sense of a goal event fails to materialize. What appears at the moment of expected arrival is not heard as belonging to the ongoing cadential process; rather, it belongs to the subsequent phrase. The event groups FORWARD, not backward. This grouping disruption is what allows an evaded cadence to arise even if the cadential dominant literally resolves to a root-position tonic -- that tonic is heard as a beginning, not an ending. Janet Schmalfeldt coined the term "one more time" technique specifically for this situation.

# Prerequisites
- **Cadential deviations** — The broader category
- **Cadential progression** — Must be in progress for evasion to occur

# Key Properties
1. The cadential group fails to finish before a new group begins
2. What follows the cadential dominant groups FORWARD (new beginning)
3. Can occur even when V resolves to root-position I (if that I sounds like a beginning, not an ending)
4. Creates the "one more time" effect -- music backs up and retries
5. Analytical notation: double slash (//) interrupts the cadential bracket
6. More common in subordinate themes than in simple main themes

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Locate a cadential progression approaching expected PAC
2. At the moment of expected arrival, does the music sound like an ending (deceptive) or a new beginning (evaded)?
3. Does the material reference earlier music ("backing up")?
4. Look for the double-slash notation in Caplin's analyses

# Context & Application
Evaded cadences arise infrequently in simple main themes but appear much more regularly in subordinate themes and loosely organized sections. The "one more time" technique creates a more palpable and dramatic effect than other cadential deviations because of the marked grouping disruption.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 153): Example 5.1 -- extended continuation leads to cadential progression at m. 9; expected PAC at m. 10 fails; music at m. 10 references m. 7 material, running through "one more time"; PAC finally at m. 13.
**Example 2** (p. 164): Mozart, K. 309, iii, mm. 13-19 -- cadential V resolves to root-position I at m. 16, but that I references the beginning of the passage and groups forward; evaded cadence despite V-I resolution.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking an evaded cadence requires the dominant to resolve to a non-tonic harmony
  **Correction**: The dominant may resolve to root-position tonic; the key criterion is grouping structure, not harmony

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating evaded cadence with deceptive cadence
  **Clarification**: Deceptive = replacement groups backwards (ending); evaded = what follows groups forward (new beginning). The grouping disruption is the defining feature.
