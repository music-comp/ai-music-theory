# Quick Definition
A majestic, heraldic gesture commonly opening slow introductions, typically sounded by the full orchestra in forte with dotted rhythms and triadic contour, yet often tempered by elements of uncertainty such as fermatas and lack of harmonic clarity.

# Core Definition
A slow introduction typically invokes a solemn, serious tone through fanfare motives that create an air of dignified solemnity. These motives are typically sounded by the full orchestra (tutti) in forte dynamic, featuring dotted rhythms, wide leaps, repeated notes, and triadic melodic contour. Yet these stately motives are often tempered by a general sense of hesitancy and uncertainty -- fermatas prohibiting rhythmic continuity, lack of a harmonic third raising doubt about the mode, and subsequent contrasting ideas of a hesitant, "limping" character. The combination ensures the music does not give the impression that the movement has truly begun.

# Prerequisites
- Knowledge of French overture style and its features

# Key Properties
- Creates air of dignified solemnity
- Typically sounded by full orchestra (tutti)
- Forte dynamic
- Features dotted rhythms
- Wide leaps (octaves, fifths, triadic intervals)
- Repeated notes and triadic melodic contour
- Often followed by hesitant, contrasting material
- Fermatas may prohibit rhythmic continuity
- Lack of harmonic third may create modal ambiguity

# Construction / Recognition
Look for the opening gesture of a slow introduction: a bold, declamatory figure with dotted rhythms, wide intervals, and forte dynamic in tutti texture. Confirm the fanfare character by noting triadic contour, military or heraldic associations, and the contrast with subsequent hesitant or uncertain material.

# Context & Application
The fanfare motive is one of the defining features of slow introductions in the Classical period, appearing most frequently in symphonies and overtures. It establishes the stately, ceremonial aspect of the introduction while leaving room for the anticipatory, uncertain qualities that differentiate the classical slow introduction from its Baroque French overture ancestor. The fanfare may return at climactic points within the introduction and may reappear in the coda as a recollection of main-theme material.

# Examples
- Haydn, Symphony No. 104 ("London"), i, mm. 1-2: fortissimo fanfare sounded by full orchestra, with fermatas prohibiting rhythmic continuity and lack of harmonic third raising doubt about mode
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in B-flat, K. 454, i, m. 1: majestic fanfare-like basic idea followed by contrasting lyrical yet hesitant idea
- Haydn, Symphony No. 97, i: fanfare opening with exclusively tonic support returns in the coda, where it functions as closing section

# Common Errors
- Identifying any forte opening gesture as a fanfare without confirming the characteristic triadic contour and dotted rhythms
- Treating the fanfare as unambiguously majestic without noting the uncertainty elements that typically accompany it

# Common Confusions
- A fanfare motive is NOT the same as a thematic introduction -- the fanfare is a melodic-motivic idea within the slow introduction, while a thematic introduction is a shorter, lower-level unit preceding a theme (see Caplin's distinction in "Taming the Terms")
