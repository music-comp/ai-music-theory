# Quick Definition
A harmony that functions both as the final chord of one progression and the first chord of the next, creating continuity between adjacent progressions.

# Core Definition
A linking harmony is a shared sonority serving a dual function: it simultaneously marks the end of one harmonic progression and the beginning of another. This creates continuity between adjacent progressions while maintaining the distinct identity of each. The analyst must determine from the broader musical context whether a harmony truly serves both progressions or belongs primarily to one (Caplin, pp. 25, 29).

# Prerequisites
- **Cadential progression** — Linking harmonies often connect sequential to cadential progressions
- **Prolongational progression** — Linking harmonies may connect different progression types

# Key Properties
1. Serves dual function: concludes one progression and initiates another
2. Creates formal continuity across progression boundaries
3. Most common between sequential and cadential progressions
4. Context determines whether a harmony truly links or belongs primarily to one progression
5. Creates formal ambiguity requiring analytical interpretation

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Look for a harmony at the boundary between two different progressions
2. Determine whether it can be analyzed as belonging to both progressions
3. Consider the broader musical context to decide if the linking function is genuine
4. If the harmony serves only one progression, it is not a linking harmony

# Context & Application
Linking harmonies commonly appear between sequential and cadential progressions, at phrase boundaries, and where formal units overlap. Identifying linking harmonies clarifies the relationship between adjacent formal units and reveals how composers create continuity across progression boundaries.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Mozart, K. 332, mm. 50-57): Example 1.4 shows IV6 at m. 56 as a linking harmony -- it both ends the sequential progression and initiates the half-cadential progression.

**Example 2** (Haydn, Symphony No. 93): Example 1.5 discusses whether the root-position tonic at m. 34 links the final two progressions; Caplin concludes the harmony belongs more to the sequence than to the cadence.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Harmonic progression** — Linking harmonies exist at the boundary of progressions
## Enables
- **Formal continuity** — Creates smooth connection between adjacent formal units
## Related
- **Pivot chord** — Similar dual-function concept but operates across keys, not progressions
## Contrasts With
- **Pivot chord** — A pivot chord functions in two keys; a linking harmony functions in two progressions within the same key

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming every shared harmony is a linking harmony
  **Correction**: Context determines whether a harmony truly serves dual function or belongs primarily to one progression

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating linking harmony with pivot chord
  **Clarification**: Linking harmonies connect progressions within a key; pivot chords connect different keys during modulation
