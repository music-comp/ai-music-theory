# Quick Definition
A harmony that functions both as the final chord of one progression and the first chord of the next progression.

# Formal Definition
A linking harmony is a shared sonority that serves a dual function: it simultaneously marks the end of one harmonic progression and the beginning of another. This shared harmony creates continuity between adjacent progressions while maintaining the distinct identity of each.

# Musical/Theoretical Formulation
Linking harmonies occur when:
- The final harmony of a sequential progression becomes the initial harmony of a cadential progression
- The final harmony of a prolongational progression initiates a new progression
- A harmony serves a concluding function in one context and an initiating function in another

The linking harmony creates formal ambiguity that the analyst must interpret based on the broader musical context.

# Musical Context/Application
Linking harmonies commonly appear:
1. Between sequential and cadential progressions
2. At phrase boundaries
3. Where formal units overlap

The identification of linking harmonies helps clarify the relationship between adjacent formal units and reveals how composers create continuity across progression boundaries.

# Examples
Example 1.4 (Mozart, K. 332, mm. 50-57): The IV6 at m. 56 is a linking harmony - it both ends the sequential progression and initiates the half-cadential progression.

The chapter notes that while it would be possible to see the root-position tonic at the end of m. 34 in Example 1.5 (Haydn, Symphony No. 93) as linking the final two progressions, the overall musical context suggests that this harmony belongs more to the sequence than to the cadence.

# Common Confusions
- Assuming every shared harmony is a linking harmony (context determines whether it functions in both progressions)
- Not recognizing when a harmony truly serves dual function versus belonging primarily to one progression
- Overlooking linking harmonies when analyzing phrase structure
