# Quick Definition

Burnham's characterization of Riemann's view that the formal categories of musical composition (period structure, metric hierarchy, harmonic function) are "naturally given categories that have come into existence historically" — forms that have become second nature through the historical development of musical art, making compositional logic a product of both nature and history.

# Core Definition

The "second nature of sonata form" is Burnham's framing concept for Riemann's belief that musical form represents natural law revealed through historical development. Riemann describes "the continued operation of artistic fantasy in both productive and receptive modes with naturally given categories that have come into existence historically" as "a fact of our spiritual life whose significance cannot be overestimated. These categories rid artistic creation of every vestige of caprice and make it into a logically necessary imperative" (quoted by Burnham, p. 459). For Riemann, the periodic prototype, harmonic functions (T, S, D), and the upbeat-to-downbeat impulse are not arbitrary conventions but natural principles that historical development has made intrinsic to musical art. Burnham characterizes this as "nothing less than natural law revealed by history" (p. 459).

# Prerequisites

- **Periodic function** — The fundamental formal category that exemplifies second nature
- **Hierarchical metric organization** — The multi-level structure seen as naturally given

# Key Properties

1. Musical form is neither purely natural nor purely conventional — it is "naturally given" but "come into existence historically"
2. The categories (period, harmonic function, metric hierarchy) eliminate "caprice" from composition
3. Compositional logic becomes a "logically necessary imperative" rather than arbitrary choice
4. Beethoven's development shows the progressive mastery of these naturally given forms
5. The concept justifies Riemann's analytical project: demonstrating logical necessity in every measure
6. The Beethoven piano sonata analyses are Riemann's "testament" — his final demonstration of this belief

# Construction / Recognition

The concept is not an analytical technique but a philosophical framework. It manifests in:
1. The treatment of the eight-measure period as a universal prototype, not a historical convention
2. The assignment of functions to every measure as demonstrating logical necessity
3. The narrative of Beethoven's development as progressive mastery of natural categories
4. The rejection of "caprice" as a basis for artistic production

# Context & Application

Burnham situates Riemann at the end of "the long nineteenth century, an age that mustered tenacious faith in the determining forces of both history and natural law" (p. 459). Riemann's is "easily the most developed, or at least the most discursive, expression of that belief as applied to a body of music." The Beethoven analyses constitute Riemann's final attempt to demonstrate this belief comprehensively, covering every measure of every sonata.

**Contemporary relevance**: Burnham notes that "however quixotic such an agenda may sound today" and "however inconsistent he must appear to us in his wavering criteria," Riemann's analyses still yield insights: "a certain *flair pour suivre la piste* arises whenever we stop to pick up the thread of any of his analyses" (p. 459). The method "stirs our own analytical preoccupations about phrase rhythm in the classical style."

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 459): Riemann's foreword to the Beethoven analyses: "To demonstrate the absurdity of those who despise form and rule, who trumpet the unfettered caprice of artistic production, is the most distinguished goal of my labors."

**Example 2** (p. 451): Riemann's chronological arrangement of analyses showing "the imposing growth and strengthening of [Beethoven's] artistic powers" — maturation as "ever firmer positioning of the actual structure, around which all accessory material is arranged."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Dismissing the concept as mere nineteenth-century ideology
  **Correction**: While the philosophical framework is historically situated, the analytical insights it generates (about phrase rhythm, closural energy, compositional strategies) remain valuable

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking "second nature" means the forms are completely natural
  **Clarification**: The forms are "naturally given" but "come into existence historically" — they are neither purely natural nor purely conventional, but something in between
