# Quick Definition

A neo-Riemannian analytical framework in which triads are grouped by their common-tone relationship to a single augmented triad, forming a region of six triads with consistent voice-leading distances that can illuminate chromatic passages resisting functional analysis.

# Core Definition

A Weitzmann region (named after Carl Friedrich Weitzmann's pamphlet *Der ubermassige Dreiklang*, 1853) groups the six consonant triads that each share two tones with a single augmented triad. In Rings's application to the Schubert Impromptu, the passage at mm. 78-82 involves triads that belong to a single Weitzmann region, lending "a consistency of voice-leading distance...that is not present in a hexatonic cycle, in which AVLS values range from 1 to 3" (Rings, p. 494, n. 9). The five non-identity operations within the region — N, P, L, R, and SLIDE — along with the identity E, form "a tidy dihedral group of order 6" (p. 494, n. 12).

# Prerequisites

- **PLR transformations** — P, L, R are among the operations within a Weitzmann region
- **Voice-leading efficiency** — The region is defined by parsimonious voice-leading to a central augmented triad

# Key Properties

1. Each Weitzmann region contains six consonant triads sharing two tones with one augmented triad
2. AVLS (aggregate voice-leading sum) values are consistent within the region
3. Five non-trivial operations relate the six triads: N, P, L, R, and SLIDE
4. These operations plus identity form a dihedral group of order 6
5. Weitzmann regions complement hexatonic systems: hexatonic cycles are drawn cyclically, Weitzmann regions join them via augmented triads

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify a Weitzmann Region

1. Identify an augmented triad as the region's center (e.g., {Db, F, A})
2. List the six consonant triads sharing two tones with that augmented triad
3. For {Db, F, A}: Db major, Db minor, F major, F minor, A major, A minor
4. Check whether the passage's triads belong to this set
5. Analyze relationships using the region's internal operations

# Context & Application

Rings uses Weitzmann region analysis as a representative neo-Riemannian approach to the Schubert Impromptu. He notes that "several augmented triads appear prominently on the surface of Schubert's Impromptu" (p. 494, n. 9), suggesting the piece "tends toward an augmented-triad sound world — especially in moments of transition." However, he cautions against pushing the idea too hard, since "all of these chords operate in ways far more traditional than the Lisztian possibilities that Weitzmann had in mind."

# Examples

**Schubert Impromptu, mm. 78-82** (pp. 494-496): The triads around m. 80 (Gb major, B minor, G minor) can be related through a Weitzmann region centered on an augmented triad. The analysis reveals voice-leading coherence in a passage that seems to defy functional logic.

**Augmented triads on the surface** (p. 494, n. 9): {Db, F, A} in mm. 4 and 58; {Gb, Bb, D} in m. 24; {Cb, Eb, G} in m. 73 — these surface augmented triads suggest the relevance of Weitzmann-region hearing.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming all chromatic passages fit neatly into a single Weitzmann region
  **Correction**: Some passages involve multiple regions or transitional movement between them

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Conflating Weitzmann regions with hexatonic systems
  **Clarification**: Hexatonic systems group triads by PL/LP cycles (three major, three minor); Weitzmann regions group triads by common tones with an augmented triad (three major, three minor, but differently selected)
