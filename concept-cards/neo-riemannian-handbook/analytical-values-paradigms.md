# Analytical Values and Paradigms

## Quick Definition

The divergent ideological assumptions underlying Riemannian vs. neo-Riemannian analysis: Riemann valued order, conformance, and universal tonal laws; neo-Riemannians value crisis, disruption, and tonal disunity.

## Formal Definition

**Analytical values** refer to the assumptions and ethical orientations that underlie distinct analytical perspectives. Rings identifies a fundamental **ideological shift** between Riemann and neo-Riemannian theory:

**Riemann's Values**:
- Order and conformance to putative universals of tonal harmony
- Demonstration of "relentless normativity"
- Music theory as "a bastion against historical change"
- Seeks to contain chromatic extravagances within rational bounds
- Values the "ordinary" within the compositionally extraordinary

**Neo-Riemannian Values**:
- Crisis and disruption of tonal order
- The compositionally extraordinary and idiosyncratic
- Tonal disunity as analytically productive
- Voice-leading coherence independent of tonal center
- Alignment with postmodern critical practices

This reveals that **technical differences mask deeper ethical divergences**: the same chromatic passage can be analytically constructed to show either conformance or crisis.

## Analytical Questions Addressed

- What do we value in music through analysis?
- Should analysis demonstrate order or reveal disruption?
- Is the analyst's role to contain or to explore?
- How do historical and ideological contexts shape analytical choices?

## What Each Approach Prioritizes

### Riemann Prioritizes:
- Monotonality: "One is constantly struck by the controlling force of the main tonic"
- Universal laws of tonal harmony
- The piece's exemplification of normative principles
- Pedagogical utility for future composers

### Neo-Riemannian Theory Prioritizes:
- Chromatically exceptional moments as analytical starting points
- Voice-leading patterns where there appears to be tonal strain
- Individual work valued for originality (Romantic ideology)
- Methodological pluralism

## The Paradox

Rings identifies a **methodological tension** in neo-Riemannian theory: it values both disruption AND coherence. The analysis begins with chromatically exceptional passages but then demonstrates their voice-leading coherence:

> "Surely a prime reason for the success of neo-Riemannian theory is that it allows analysts to dwell on the most remarkable sounding passages...But it is not the remarkable sound of those passages that is analyzed; it is their coherence."

This creates a **fact/value gap**: the analysis reveals coherence, but it is the sound of disruption that we cherish.

## Historical Context

**Riemann's Context** (late 19th century):
- Fear of transgressing tonal boundaries
- Theory as conservative brake against further historical change
- Appeals to natural science for universality
- The "Garden of Eden" as metaphor for tonal possibility

**Neo-Riemannian Context** (late 20th century):
- Atonality already institutionally privileged
- Rise of New Musicology and postmodern criticism
- Analysis as interpretive engagement with individual works
- Valorization of disunity and fragmentation in critical discourse

## Examples

### Rings's Case Study: Schubert Impromptu in G-flat

The same passage (mm. 78-82) receives radically different analytical treatments:

**Riemann's Analysis**: The minor Neapolitan is merely one of several progressions that "consolidate the tonic" with "wholly excellent effect"

**Neo-Riemannian Analysis**: The passage represents a "tonal crisis or trauma" requiring exploration of its "extratonal surplus"

### Key Insight

Riemann's functional analysis casts the passage as **syntactically normative** (T-S-D-T), while neo-Riemannian analysis reveals **voice-leading coherence within a Weitzmann region** but cannot capture phrase-level functional distinctions.

## Critical Insight

The choice between analytical approaches is not merely technical but **ethical**:

> "How we respond to such a passage analytically says much about what we value in music---and in musical analysis (the two are not necessarily the same)."

Both approaches share "pan-Riemannian" values: drive toward systematization, logical rigor, elegant symmetrical structures, and ultimately the detection of **order** in complex music.
