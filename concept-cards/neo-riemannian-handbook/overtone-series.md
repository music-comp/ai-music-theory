# Quick Definition

The natural series of frequencies produced when a vibrating body sounds, consisting of a fundamental pitch and its integer multiples. Historically used to justify major triad consonance, but ultimately rejected by Riemann as an insufficient foundation for harmonic theory.

# Core Definition

The **overtone series** (German: *Obertonreihe*) consists of all tones produced by a single vibrating body, ordered by frequency as integer multiples of the fundamental: 1:2:3:4:5:6:7:8... The first six partials outline a major triad, which led Rameau and others to claim the major chord as "natural" or acoustically generated.

In Riemann's "Die Natur der Harmonik" (1882), the overtone series is discussed as the traditional foundation that his predecessors used for harmonic theory. Rameau "discovered overtones and attempted to ground the major Klang in the *corps sonore*" (Ch. 2, p. 76). However, Riemann demonstrates that this foundation is inadequate: if partials 4:5:6 justify the major triad, why not partials 7:9:11:13 for dissonant formations? The overtone series cannot explain the minor triad's consonance at all, which Helmholtz admitted was a "clouded consonance" requiring special pleading (Ch. 2, pp. 79-80).

This critique motivated Riemann's shift from acoustic to psychological foundations: "The mistake Helmholtz made is now easy to recognize; he sought to explain from the nature of sounding bodies concepts that can only be explained from the nature of the perceiving mind" (Ch. 2, p. 83).

# Key Properties

1. Frequency ratios form the series 1:2:3:4:5:6:7:8... (fundamental, octave, fifth+octave, two octaves, major third+two octaves, fifth+two octaves, etc.)
2. The first six partials outline a major triad (the 7th and higher primes are not consonant in Western theory)
3. The series provides acoustic support for the major triad but cannot adequately explain minor triad consonance
4. Higher partials (7, 11, 13) belong to the series but produce intervals outside the diatonic system
5. All partials sound simultaneously with the fundamental; none "generates" the others

# Context & Application

The overtone series was central to pre-Riemannian harmonic theory:

- **Rameau (1722)**: Introduced the *son generateur* (generating tone), arguing the fundamental "generates" the fifth and third
- **Helmholtz (1863)**: Built his *Lehre von den Tonempfindungen* on physiological acoustics, using overtones to explain consonance via absence of beats
- **Oettingen (1866)**: Extended the framework dualistically, positing undertones as the mirror basis for minor

Riemann's "Die Natur der Harmonik" traces this history to show that the overtone series opens but cannot close the question of harmonic foundations. The psychological turn — recognizing that "consonance and dissonance are musical concepts, but not definite forms of sound waves" (Ch. 2, p. 83) — represents the decisive moment when harmony theory moved from physics to psychology.

# Examples

**Example 1** (Ch. 2, pp. 76-79): Riemann traces the history from Zarlino's string divisions through Rameau's *corps sonore* to Helmholtz's physiological approach, showing how each theorist used the overtone series to ground harmony in nature.

**Example 2** (Ch. 2, p. 83): Riemann demonstrates that even the six-four chord, "according to physical and physiological formulations, the most undisturbed and euphonious," can be "a dissonance musically." This proves that consonance cannot be reduced to acoustics.

**Example 3** (Ch. 2, pp. 80-82): The historical lineage of Hauptmann's insight that "there are three directly intelligible intervals: the octave, the fifth, and the (major) third" — all derivable from the overtone series via the prime numbers 2, 3, and 5.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating the overtone series as the cause of consonance perception
  **Correction**: Riemann showed that consonance is a psychological concept; the overtone series may correlate with consonance but does not cause it

- **Error**: Assuming overtone-series derivation validates the major triad while excluding all else
  **Correction**: If we accept partials 4:5:6, we must also account for 7:9:11:13, which produce non-diatonic intervals

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing overtones and harmonics are the same thing
  **Clarification**: "Overtones" excludes the fundamental (partial 1); "harmonics" includes it. The distinction matters for numbering.

- **Confusion**: Thinking Riemann rejected the existence of overtones
  **Clarification**: Riemann acknowledged overtones as acoustic phenomena but denied they could serve as the *foundation* for harmonic theory. The shift was from physics to psychology, not from science to speculation.
