# Voice Leading

## Quick Definition

The horizontal motion of individual melodic lines (voices) as they move from chord to chord, with "efficient" or "parsimonious" voice leading minimizing the distances voices must travel between chords.

## Formal Definition

**Voice leading** describes how individual pitches move when one chord progresses to another. In neo-Riemannian contexts, voice leading is formalized as:

A **voice leading** is a mapping from pitches in one chord to pitches in another, specifying which note moves to which. The notation (C4, E4, G4) -> (C4, Eb4, G4) indicates C moves to C, E moves to Eb, and G moves to G.

**Efficient voice leading** (also called **parsimonious motion**) occurs when:
- Individual voices move by small intervals (ideally semitones)
- The total distance moved by all voices is minimal
- No voice moves more than necessary

Tymoczko formalizes this: "By 'efficient' voice leading I mean, roughly, voice leading in which no voice moves very far."

## Theoretical Framework

**Contrapuntal vs. Harmonic Perspectives**:

Tymoczko distinguishes two views of inversional relationships:

1. **Contrapuntal dualism**: Inversion is a tool for cataloging voice-leading possibilities; inversional relationships are *by-products* of efficient voice leading

2. **Harmonic dualism**: Inversion is explanatorily basic; inversional relationships are significant in themselves

Tymoczko argues for the contrapuntal view: "Nineteenth-century composers were not explicitly concerned with inversional relationships as such; instead, these relationships appear as necessary by-products of a deeper and more fundamental concern with efficient voice leading."

**Why Voice Leading Creates Inversional Relationships**:

Since transposition and inversion are the only **distance-preserving** operations in pitch space:
- If a voice leading is efficient, its inversion is equally efficient
- Efficient voice leadings between any two set classes can be grouped into inversionally related pairs
- A musical style emphasizing efficient voice leading will necessarily exhibit inversional symmetry

## The "Second Practice" Model

Tymoczko proposes that 19th-century chromaticism combines:

1. **First practice**: Diatonic functional harmony inherited from the 18th century
2. **Second practice**: Chromatic voice leading emphasizing efficiency between familiar sonorities

The "second practice" sets few constraints: "Virtually any voice leading between familiar chords may be used, as long as it is efficient."

**Three Principles of Chromatic Style** (P1-P3):
- P1: If a sonority is acceptable, so is its inversion
- P2: Efficient voice leadings are desirable
- P3: Voice-leading prohibitions (e.g., parallel fifths) apply equally to ascending and descending motion

When all three hold, any acceptable voice leading will have an acceptable inversion.

## Musical Context

**Semitonal Voice Leadings Between Triads**:

Tymoczko catalogs all 16 "semitonal" voice leadings between consonant triads (where no voice moves more than a semitone). These can be grouped into:
- Inversionally related pairs
- Retrograde-related pairs
- Individually transposition-equivalent pairs

This explains why major-third related triads appear frequently: C major to E major (or Ab major) requires only two voices to move by semitone - the most efficient voice leading between distinct major triads.

**Voice Leading in Wagner**:

Example: The "Tarnhelm" motive (G#-B-D# to G-B-E) and "Valhalla" motive (Gb-Bb-Db to F-Bb-D) are inversionally equivalent - the same semitonal voice leading pattern.

Tymoczko's contrapuntal interpretation: Wagner was interested in *maximally efficient voice leading*, not in inversional relationships per se. The Tarnhelm-Valhalla connection is smoke from the locomotive, not the engine.

## Examples

### The Tristan Chord Resolutions

In Brahms's Intermezzo Op. 76 No. 4, the Tristan chord {F, G#, B, Eb} resolves three different ways:
- To F7 (a1)
- To Ab major (a2)
- To Eb major (a3)

Each resolution uses efficient voice leading. The piece "systematically explores the voice-leading possibilities of a few characteristic sonorities."

### Voice-Leading Distance Metrics

Tymoczko references formal measurements:
- **DVLS** (displacement voice-leading size): Sum of semitones each voice moves
- **AVLS** (average voice-leading size): DVLS divided by number of voices

These quantify "efficiency" precisely.

## Common Confusions

1. **Voice leading vs. chord progression**: Voice leading tracks *how* notes move; chord progression tracks *what chords* result

2. **Efficient vs. smooth**: "Smooth" often implies stepwise motion; "efficient" is more general (minimal total motion)

3. **Inversion in voice leading vs. chord inversion**: Voice-leading inversion (I-related voice leadings) differs from chord inversions (root position, first inversion, etc.)

4. **Neo-Riemannian transformations**: P, L, R are defined by voice-leading properties but represent harmonic relationships

## Theoretical Significance

Voice leading provides a bridge between:
- **Historical counterpoint** (Bach-style part writing)
- **Nineteenth-century chromaticism** (Wagner, Liszt)
- **Neo-Riemannian transformations** (defined by voice-leading efficiency)
- **Geometric approaches** (Tymoczko's voice-leading spaces)

The observation that efficient voice leadings form inversionally related pairs explains why dualistic terminology is useful for chromatic analysis, even if composers weren't thinking dualistically.
