# Quick Definition

The Viennese tradition of harmonic analysis that identifies chords by their root's position on the diatonic scale using Roman numerals (I-VII), contrasting with Riemannian function theory's reduction of all harmony to three categories (T, D, S).

# Core Definition

**Stufentheorie** (scale-degree theory) is the tradition of harmonic analysis originating in Vienna (Sechter, Weber, Richter) that classifies chords by the scale degree of their root. Each of the seven diatonic scale degrees receives its own Roman numeral, and chromatic alterations are indicated by accidentals applied to the numeral.

Holtmeier demonstrates that the opposition between Stufentheorie and Funktionstheorie is more complex than commonly assumed. The "middle ground between Viennese scale-degree theory and Riemannian function theory" is a central thesis of his chapter: "divergent to the common belief, there existed a middle ground between Viennese scale-degree theory and Riemannian function theory, voice-leading approaches and *Klang*-based approaches" (Ch. 1, abstract).

Key theorists in the Stufentheorie tradition include Gottfried Weber (Roman numeral notation), Simon Sechter (fundamental-bass theory with scale degrees), and Ernst Friedrich Richter. Sechter's fundamental-bass theory "not only comprises reference to thinking in underlying scale-degrees and chordal inversions, but also chordal progressions that nearly exclusively move along fifths and thirds" (Ch. 1, n. 66).

# Key Properties

1. Seven categories (I-VII) corresponding to the seven diatonic scale degrees
2. Each chord identified by its root's scale position
3. Chromatic alterations require additional accidentals on the Roman numeral
4. Position-dependent rather than context-dependent classification
5. Preserves the individuality of each diatonic chord
6. Requires numerous symbols for chromatically complex music ("not quite impossible, but rather cumbersome to analyze whole compositions harmonically" — Riemann-Lexikon, Ch. 1, p. 15)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Analyze a Passage Using Stufentheorie
1. Identify the prevailing key
2. Determine each chord's root
3. Assign the Roman numeral corresponding to the root's scale-degree position
4. Add quality indicators (major, minor, diminished, augmented)
5. Note inversions (figured-bass numerals)
6. For chromatic chords, add accidentals to the numeral or declare a modulation

# Context & Application

Stufentheorie arose from the thoroughbass tradition, where chords were identified by bass position. Holtmeier notes that the "harmonic 'theory' of this system is the rule of the octave" (Ch. 1, n. 63). Richter's *Lehrbuch der Harmonie* (1853) exemplified the approach: "ahistorical" and purely practical, with no musical examples from the repertoire (Ch. 1, n. 61).

The limitation that motivated Riemannian alternatives was analytical economy: Roman numeral analysis becomes cumbersome for chromatic Romantic repertoire. As Holtmeier quotes the *Riemann-Lexikon*: using Roman numerals requires "also accidentals for the scale-degree figure," making extended analysis unwieldy (Ch. 1, p. 15). The applied dominant concept, for example, is far more economically expressed in function theory notation.

However, Holtmeier also notes that "numerous theories of harmony, which in their author's eyes are based entirely on the theory of scale degrees... are in essence more practical theories of function operating with Roman numerals than they are genuine theories of scale degrees" (Ch. 1, p. 10).

# Examples

**Example 1** (Ch. 1, p. 10): Richter's analysis of a chromatic passage identifies: "Measure 3 presents a modulation to D minor, since C#-E-G-Bb no longer belongs to C major." Function theory interprets the same passage as an applied dominant within C major — no modulation needed.

**Example 2** (Ch. 1, p. 15): In C major, the chord E-G-B: Scale-degree theory identifies it as iii; function theory identifies it as Tp (tonic parallel) or Dp (dominant parallel), relating it to a primary function.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating every chromatic chord as evidence of modulation
  **Correction**: Function theory shows that many "modulations" in scale-degree analysis are actually applied dominants or mixture chords within a single key

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing Stufentheorie and Funktionstheorie are entirely incompatible
  **Clarification**: Holtmeier shows extensive middle ground between the two traditions; many nominally scale-degree theories are "in essence more practical theories of function operating with Roman numerals"

- **Confusion**: Assuming Roman numeral analysis is identical to Stufentheorie
  **Clarification**: Roman numerals are a notation; Stufentheorie is a theoretical framework about how chords relate to each other via scale-degree identity
