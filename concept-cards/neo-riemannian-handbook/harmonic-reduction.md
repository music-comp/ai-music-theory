# Quick Definition

The analytical practice of stripping complex harmonic passages down to their fundamental Klang progressions, revealing a skeletal harmonic structure beneath the melodic-contrapuntal surface. Developed by Riemann and significantly advanced by his successor Johannes Schreyer.

# Core Definition

**Harmonic reduction** is the analytical method of producing progressively simplified versions of a musical passage, revealing the underlying Klang progression beneath the surface detail. Holtmeier shows this was "inscribed into the very idea of the theory of functions" and was "one of its most central original ideas" (Ch. 1, p. 19).

The method uses Riemann's Klangschluessel notation to interpret extended passages with independent voice-leading as unfoldings of a single underlying Klang. Surface events — passing tones, neighbor notes, suspensions, consonant skips — are identified and "removed" layer by layer until the fundamental harmonic skeleton emerges.

Schreyer radicalized this approach with his concept of *Scheinharmonie* ("apparent harmony"): chords that assume "a different significance from the expected one" within a structural context, functioning as contrapuntal modifications rather than independent harmonic events (Ch. 1, p. 20). His related concept of "parenthesis" designated extended passages as composing-out of a single Klang.

# Key Properties

1. Multiple analytical levels, from surface to deep structure
2. Surface events classified as melodic (passing, neighbor, suspension) rather than harmonic
3. Extended passages may reduce to a single prolonged Klang
4. *Scheinharmonien* are "apparent harmonies" — they sound but don't count as independent functions
5. The method anticipates Schenker's layered analysis but is rooted in function theory
6. Post-Schreyer function theory largely lost this reductive dimension, instead adding figured-bass annotations to function symbols

# Construction / Recognition

## To Produce a Harmonic Reduction
1. Begin with the musical surface (score)
2. Identify melodic figurations: passing tones, neighbor notes, suspensions, anticipations
3. Notate these as small noteheads (Schreyer's "vicariate")
4. Reduce to a two- or three-part harmonic skeleton
5. Identify "parenthetical" passages (extended composing-out of a single Klang)
6. Continue reducing until the fundamental cadential motion is revealed
7. Label with function symbols (T, S, D)

# Context & Application

Holtmeier traces the reductive tradition from early Riemann through Schreyer:

**Early Riemann** (1883): Analysis of Bach's A minor Fugue uses Klangschluessel notation to show how "almost never presents the chords directly but always masked by scalar movement" (Ch. 1, p. 19). The harmonic skeleton at the end of the analysis strips away all "local" passing Klange.

**Schreyer** (1905): Developed multi-level reductions, most impressively of the transition to the finale of Beethoven's Fifth Symphony (Ch. 1, pp. 22-25). Four successive sketches reduce 50 measures to a simple cadential combination of bass and soprano clausula. The concept of "parenthesis" allowed entire sections to be understood as elaborations of a single Klang.

**Schreyer vs. Riemann**: Ironically, Schreyer "argues against the late Riemann by using arguments of the early Riemann" (Ch. 1, p. 19). Riemann's own later analyses tended toward "total verticalization" — labeling every chord — while Schreyer preserved the reductive, contrapuntal dimension.

It is "astonishing and regrettable" that in later function theory "these reductive aspects are completely lost: the later tendency to add heaps of figured-bass annotations above the function symbols are actually a contradiction in terms" (Ch. 1, p. 29).

# Examples

**Example 1** (Ch. 1, pp. 19-20): Riemann's reduction of Bach's Fugue in A minor (WTC): melodic tones classified as W (neighbor note), D (passing note), A (consonant skip), etc. The "harmonic skeleton" at the deepest level shows the fundamental Klang progression.

**Example 2** (Ch. 1, pp. 22-25): Schreyer's four-stage reduction of Beethoven's Fifth Symphony finale transition: (1) close-to-score sketch, (2) condensed harmonic activities, (3) basic two-part texture revealing the soprano clausula, (4) final cadential formula T-D-T.

**Example 3** (Ch. 1, pp. 15-17): Schreyer's analysis of Mozart's "Dissonance" Quartet opening in three levels, reducing the complex dissonant opening to an "idealized pedal" on the dominant G.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Labeling every chord with a function symbol (over-verticalization)
  **Correction**: Harmonic reduction should identify which chords are structural and which are "apparent harmonies" (melodic phenomena)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Equating harmonic reduction with Schenkerian analysis
  **Clarification**: While there are striking parallels (both use multi-level reduction), harmonic reduction is rooted in function theory, not prolongational theory. Schreyer predates the mature Schenker and works from different premises.

- **Confusion**: Thinking harmonic reduction is standard practice in function theory
  **Clarification**: Holtmeier shows that the reductive dimension was largely lost in later function theory (Grabner, Maler, Distler), replaced by accumulating figured-bass annotations on function symbols
