# Quick Definition

Riemann's distinction between *Tonalität* — an expanded tonal system embracing chromatic as well as diatonic relations to a tonic — and *Tonart* — the familiar diatonic conception of key limited to scale-degree relationships within a single diatonic collection.

# Core Definition

In his *Musik-Lexikon* (first published 1882), Riemann "defined the concept [*Tonalität*] expressly to embrace chromatic as well as diatonic relations to a tonic, in contrast to *Tonart*, the familiar diatonic conception of key" (Kopp, p. 401). *Tonalität* encompasses relationships like chromatic mediants that fall outside diatonic scale-degree analysis, while *Tonart* is limited to the seven-note diatonic scale and its associated harmonic functions. This distinction is foundational to Kopp's argument that chromatic passages in nineteenth-century music should be understood as expansions of tonality rather than departures from it.

# Prerequisites

- **Tonality** — The basic concept of organization around a tonic, which *Tonalität* expands

# Key Properties

1. *Tonalität* embraces both chromatic and diatonic relations to a tonic
2. *Tonart* is restricted to diatonic scale-degree relationships
3. Riemann illustrated *Tonalität* with a chromatic mediant cadence (E - C - Ab - C), substituting chromatic mediants for dominants
4. The distinction implies that chromatic progressions can be tonal without being diatonic
5. Neo-Riemannian theory, by treating chromatic progressions as "extratonal," paradoxically narrows Riemann's own broader conception

# Construction / Recognition

## To Distinguish in Analysis

1. If a passage's harmonic relations can be fully explained by diatonic scale-degree functions, the framework is *Tonart*
2. If harmonic relations require chromatic mediants, common-tone connections, or other chromatic functional relationships, the broader framework of *Tonalität* applies
3. Both frameworks maintain tonic reference; neither implies atonality

# Context & Application

Kopp uses this distinction to critique the neo-Riemannian tendency to label chromatic passages as nontonal: "How ironic, then, that the relation between these three chords [the Tonalität cadence], cited by Riemann as a fundamental example of tonality in chromatic music, has come to serve as a fundamental example of nontonal triadic relations in neo-Riemannian theory, when reinterpreted as the product of the hexatonic cycle" (p. 401). The distinction matters analytically because it determines whether chromatic passages are heard as expansions of a tonal system or as departures from it.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 401, Ex. 14.1): Riemann's *Tonalität* cadence from *Musik-Lexikon*: E major - C major - Ab major - C major. This progression exemplifies *Tonalität* because the chromatic mediants substitute for dominants, creating a cadence through chromatic means that falls outside *Tonart* but remains fully tonal.

**Example 2** (p. 401): The hexatonic cycle interpretation of the same progression illustrates how neo-Riemannian theory reframes *Tonalität* as nontonality — the same chords are analyzed as products of PL operations operating "without reference to a tonic."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using *Tonalität* and *Tonart* interchangeably
  **Correction**: They denote fundamentally different scopes — *Tonalität* includes chromatic relations that *Tonart* excludes

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking *Tonalität* means the same as modern English "tonality"
  **Clarification**: Riemann's *Tonalität* specifically includes chromatic mediant relations as fundamental tonal relationships, which is broader than many modern uses of "tonality"

- **Confusion**: Assuming that music outside *Tonart* is atonal
  **Clarification**: Music can exceed the boundaries of *Tonart* while remaining fully within *Tonalität* — chromatic but still tonal
