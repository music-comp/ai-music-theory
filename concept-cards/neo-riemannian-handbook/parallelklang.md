# Quick Definition

A subsidiary chord that shares two tones with a primary function (T, D, or S) and represents that function by replacing its fifth with the sixth. In C major, A minor (Tp) is the parallel of the tonic C major, sharing C and E.

# Core Definition

The **Parallelklang** ("parallel chord") is one of the three principal tone-relations Riemann uses to connect subsidiary chords to primary functions. Riemann defined Parallelklange as "all chord-pairs which stand in the relation of the tonics of parallel keys, which we have developed from one and the same third by adding the upper or lower fifth" (*Musik-Lexikon*, 10th ed., cited in Ch. 1, n. 74).

The derivation: from a primary Klang, the fifth is replaced by the sixth (or in minor, the "under-fifth" by the "under-sixth"). This produces a subsidiary chord that shares two common tones with the primary function.

In Riemann's notation:
- **Tp** = Tonic parallel (A minor in C major; C major in A minor)
- **Sp** = Subdominant parallel (D minor in C major)
- **Dp** = Dominant parallel (E minor in C major)

From the *Musik-Lexikon* entry on "Dissonanz" (cited in Ch. 3, n. 43): "The sixth of a major chord and the under-sixth of a minor chord, entering in place of the fifth (for this), produces the parallel chord (*Parallelklang*) qualified for representation of the respective chord within the key. In C major, there arise apparently the A minor chord (Tp), D minor chord (Sp) and E minor chord (Dp)."

# Key Properties

1. Shares two common tones with the primary function it represents
2. Derived by replacing the fifth with the sixth (in major)
3. Creates a Scheinkonsonanz (apparent consonance) — sounds consonant but functions as a representative
4. In major keys, parallels lie above (a minor third up from the root)
5. In strict Riemannian dualism, parallels in minor lie below (symmetrically)
6. The parallel is the mildest form of functional substitution — maximum common-tone retention

# Construction / Recognition

## To Find the Parallel of a Major Klang
1. Start with the major triad (e.g., C-E-G)
2. Replace the fifth (G) with the sixth (A)
3. Result: A-C-E = A minor = Tp in C major

## To Find the Parallel of a Minor Klang (Dualistic)
1. Start with the minor triad (e.g., A-C-E)
2. Replace the under-fifth (A) with the under-sixth (G)
3. Result: C-E-G = C major = Tp in A minor

# Context & Application

Hyer discusses the Parallelklang as one of three "tone-relations" that Riemann uses to expand the functional system beyond the three primary Klange (Ch. 3, pp. 107-108). These tone-relations — Parallele, Variante, and Leittonwechsel — share the property of common-tone retention while changing one component of the Klang.

The practical significance: the parallel chord explains why vi in major (or III in minor) can function as a tonic substitute. Rather than being an independent harmonic entity (as in scale-degree theory, where vi is simply "the sixth scale-degree chord"), the Parallelklang Tp is the tonic *in a different guise* — it represents the tonic function through shared tones.

# Examples

**Example 1** (Ch. 3, n. 43): In C major: A minor (Tp), D minor (Sp), E minor (Dp). These "arise apparently" through sixth-for-fifth substitution.

**Example 2** (Ch. 1, n. 74): Riemann's definition: Parallelklange are chord-pairs "in the relation of the tonics of parallel keys" — the same concept underlies the term "relative major/minor" in English theory.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Confusing Riemannian Parallele with the English "parallel" (which means same-root mode change)
  **Correction**: Riemannian Parallele = English "relative" (C major ↔ A minor). The English "parallel" corresponds to Riemann's "Variante."

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking Parallelklange are independent harmonic entities
  **Clarification**: In function theory, they are representatives of primary functions. Tp is not "A minor" as an independent entity but "the tonic in a modified form."
