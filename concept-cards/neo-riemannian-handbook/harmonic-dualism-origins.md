# Quick Definition

The theoretical principle that major and minor triads are polar opposites of equal status — the major triad built upward from a generator and the minor triad built downward — tracing from Zarlino through Hauptmann and Oettingen to Riemann, who eventually shifted its justification from acoustics to psychology.

# Core Definition

**Harmonic dualism** (German: *harmonischer Dualismus*) is the principle that major and minor chords are not fundamentally different in kind (with minor as a "weakened" major) but are polar opposites, each generated by its own principle:

- **Major (Dur)**: Generated upward from a root through the overtone series (frequency ratios 4:5:6)
- **Minor (Moll)**: Generated downward from a "root" (the fifth in conventional terms) through an inverse series (string-length or wavelength ratios)

Riemann traced the lineage in "Die Natur der Harmonik" (Ch. 2, pp. 79-82):

1. **Zarlino (1558)**: First established the "double form" of consonant chords through harmonic vs. arithmetic string division — "Riemann and Oettingen rediscovered these in the 19th century"
2. **Hauptmann (1853)**: "Generated anew through philosophical speculation" the polar opposition of major and minor, independently rediscovering what Zarlino knew
3. **Oettingen (1866)**: Extended Helmholtz's concept of Klangvertretung to minor, positioning "the minor chord along with the major as an actual Klang"
4. **Riemann**: Systematized dualism into a complete harmonic theory, eventually shifting from acoustic to psychological justification

The critical developmental moment was the recognition that acoustic justification was insufficient. Helmholtz could ground the major triad in overtones but called minor "clouded consonance" — an asymmetric treatment. Riemann's mature position: "A physical grounding for the minor chord no longer matters much to us. The psychological fact of the understanding of tones in the sense of Klange remains fixed" (Ch. 2, p. 83).

# Key Properties

1. Major and minor are polar opposites, not strong and weak versions of the same thing
2. Originally grounded in acoustics (overtone series / undertone series)
3. Later grounded in psychology (Tonvorstellung) after acoustic foundations proved inadequate
4. The principle applies at multiple levels: chords, keys, functional relationships
5. "All we loyal students of Hauptmann... adopted harmonic dualism as a new concept from Hauptmann" (Riemann, Ch. 2, p. 81)
6. Riemann defined dualism as "the carrying through of a twofold (dual) kinship of tones, that in the major sense and that in the minor sense" (*Musik-Lexikon*, Ch. 1, n. 73)

# Construction / Recognition

## The Historical Lineage (from Ch. 2)
1. Zarlino: harmonic division (4:5:6 = major) vs. arithmetic division (10:12:15 = minor)
2. Tartini: attempted physical justification of minor through undertones
3. Hauptmann: philosophical derivation through dialectical logic
4. Oettingen: systematic dualistic harmony (*Harmoniesystem in dualer Entwickelung*, 1866)
5. Riemann: integration into function theory, eventual shift to psychological grounding

## The Psychological Turn
1. Acoustic justification fails: overtones support major but not minor
2. Helmholtz's "clouded consonance" explanation is unsatisfactory
3. Solution: "The principle of Klang representation no longer belongs within physics... but rather in psychology" (Ch. 2, p. 83)
4. "A healthy, natural musical instinct cries out for the unconditional recognition of the consonant character of the minor chord" (Riemann, from Ch. 5/Ian Bent translation — but the argument is previewed in Ch. 2)

# Context & Application

Steege's chapter shows that "Die Natur der Harmonik" (1882) captures the exact moment of transition from acoustic to psychological foundations. The essay traces the entire history of harmonic theory to arrive at the insight that the acoustic/physiological approach has reached its limits, and that psychology must take over.

The dualistic debate shaped the reception of Riemann's theory in fundamental ways:
- **Georg Capellen (1901)**: "The Impossibility and Superfluousness of Riemann's Dualistic Theory of Minor" — the most prominent attack
- **Louis/Thuille**: Rejected strict dualism while retaining three-function theory (monistic adaptation)
- **Post-Riemann function theory**: Almost universally abandoned dualistic minor derivation while keeping T/D/S framework

# Examples

**Example 1** (Ch. 2, pp. 79-80): Hauptmann's recognition that "there are three directly intelligible intervals: the octave, the fifth, and the (major) third" — and that all other intervals are derived from these.

**Example 2** (Ch. 2, pp. 82-83): Riemann's six-fold Klang representation of a single tone (E as root, third, or fifth in both major and minor directions) — demonstrating dualism at the most basic level.

**Example 3** (Ch. 2, p. 83): The definitive statement: "The mistake Helmholtz made is now easy to recognize; he sought to explain from the nature of sounding bodies concepts that can only be explained from the nature of the perceiving mind."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Thinking dualism requires belief in physically audible undertones
  **Correction**: Mature Riemann explicitly abandoned acoustic undertones; dualism rests on psychological and mathematical symmetry

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing that rejecting dualism means rejecting function theory
  **Clarification**: Most post-Riemann function theorists rejected dualism while keeping the three-function framework. Louis/Thuille is the paradigm case.

- **Confusion**: Assuming Riemann held the same position on dualism throughout his career
  **Clarification**: Riemann's position evolved dramatically: from attempted physical proof of undertones, through philosophical speculation, to psychological justification
