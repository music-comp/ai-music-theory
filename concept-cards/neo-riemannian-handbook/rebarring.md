# Quick Definition

Riemann's controversial analytical practice of repositioning bar lines in musical scores to align notation with his expressed-meter analysis, based on the claim that composers sometimes placed bar lines incorrectly relative to the "true" metric organization revealed by musical content.

# Core Definition

Rebarring is Riemann's practice of moving bar lines in existing musical scores to reflect what he considers the "true" expressed meter. When expressed-meter criteria (Harmoniewirkung, durational accent, Schlusswirkung, etc.) suggest that accents fall on positions different from those indicated by the composer's bar lines, Riemann proposes alternative barring. Caplin notes that Riemann "will never allow the first measure of a period to be strong" — if the sounding first measure is strong, "he posits a 1 function that precedes it as an imaginary upbeat" (Burnham, p. 442). In some analyses, Riemann argued that composers like Mozart and Beethoven had notated their bar lines in the wrong position.

# Prerequisites

- **Expressed meter** — Rebarring is motivated by discrepancies between expressed and notated meter
- **Notated meter** — The original barring that rebarring seeks to correct
- **Auftakttheorie** — The a priori model that rebarring seeks to preserve

# Key Properties

1. Bar lines are moved to align with expressed-meter criteria (harmonic change, durational accent, cadential effect)
2. The practice assumes composers may have notated meter incorrectly or conventionally
3. Always preserves the principle that first measures are weak (upbeat function)
4. Results in even-numbered measures aligning with harmonic changes
5. The practice is highly controversial and widely rejected by modern analysts
6. Reveals underlying tension between descriptive analysis and prescriptive theory

# Construction / Recognition

## Riemann's Rebarring Method

1. Analyze the passage using expressed-meter criteria
2. Identify points where expressed accent falls on notated weak beats
3. Propose new bar line placement that aligns expressed accents with notated downbeats
4. Justify the rebarring using one or more expressed-meter criteria
5. Present the rebarred version as revealing the "true" metric organization

# Context & Application

Rebarring is the most controversial aspect of Riemann's metric theory. While it demonstrates the power of expressed-meter analysis to reveal discrepancies between notation and perception, it also reveals the limitations of his a priori Auftakttheorie model. Modern theorists generally reject rebarring, arguing that the original notation reflects the composer's intentions and that discrepancies between notation and perception create meaningful musical effects (syncopation, metric dissonance) rather than errors.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 429): Beethoven, Piano Sonata Op. 27, No. 1, i. Riemann argues the notation places long values on upbeats, creating "incessantly hindering" syncopation effects. His rebarring moves bar lines so that long notes (durational accents) fall on downbeats.

**Example 2** (p. 431): Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 283, ii. Riemann claims all Schlusswirkungen fall "in the middle of the measure," concluding that Mozart "left out the bar lines of the accented measures rather than those of the unaccented ones."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming rebarring reveals objective truths about the music
  **Correction**: Rebarring reflects Riemann's theoretical commitments, not neutral observations; other analytical perspectives may be equally valid

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking rebarring means the composer made a mistake
  **Clarification**: While Riemann sometimes implied this, most modern analysts understand that the original notation is intentional and that metric discrepancies serve expressive purposes (syncopation, surprise)

- **Confusion**: Believing rebarring is a standard analytical tool
  **Clarification**: It is largely rejected in modern metric theory; it is studied primarily as a historical practice revealing the assumptions of Riemann's system
