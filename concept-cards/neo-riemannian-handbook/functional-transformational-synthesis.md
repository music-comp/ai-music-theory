# Quick Definition

Harrison's project across three essays to reconcile functional analysis (T, D, S as "sensuous" attitudes of musical objects) with transformational analysis (PLR as "energetic" operations between objects), producing analytic hybrids appropriate to different musical contexts.

# Core Definition

Harrison's three essays pursue a threefold purpose: "(1) to deflate immoderate enthusiasms for transformational approaches as cure-alls for various music theoretical and analytical anxieties; (2) to cross such approaches with others in the hopes of producing vigorous analytic hybrids; and (3) to identify cases in which transformation theory may indeed be the best first approach" (Harrison, p. 573). The synthesis draws on Kurth's distinction between the "sensuous" and "energetic" dimensions of harmony: functional theory models sensuousness (objects with mass, substance, tendency), while neo-Riemannian theory models energy (operations, motion, transformation). The reconciliation involves endowing objects with "functional attitudes" — tendencies and motive power arising from within — while using transformational labels where functional hearing is attenuated (pp. 553, 564-573).

# Key Properties

1. Functional theory models "being" (sensuousness); transformational theory models "doing" (energy)
2. T, D, S endow objects with attitudes, moods, and tendencies — "their own motive power"
3. PLR and other transformational labels are appropriate when functional tonics are absent or overcrowded
4. The two paradigms are complementary, not competing
5. Harrison identifies "absolute progressions" as the specific context where transformational analysis is most appropriate
6. Neither paradigm alone is sufficient; methodological pluralism is the goal

# Construction / Recognition

## When to Use Functional Analysis
- Clear tonic control governs the passage
- Chords exhibit recognizable D, S, or T attitudes
- Discharge and charge patterns are audible
- Phrase-level syntax matters

## When to Use Transformational Analysis
- "Absolute progressions" — each chord proposes itself as local tonic
- Too many potential tonics overcrowd the space (not tonic-free, but tonic-overcrowded)
- Root motion by seconds (contrapuntal/melodic values penetrate harmony)
- Modal chromaticism creates functional ambiguity

# Context & Application

Harrison's synthesis operates across three analytical domains: Essay I critiques the object/transformation dichotomy; Essay II reconciles through the nonatonic system and discharge functions; Essay III demonstrates through Vaughan Williams's *Fantasia on a Theme by Thomas Tallis*. The Vaughan Williams analysis shows how transformational labels (PLR) work in passages of "absolute progression" where functional analysis "must pretend that everything is okay" (p. 568), while functional analysis captures tonal gravity when present.

# Examples

**Vaughan Williams, *Tallis Fantasia*, introduction** (pp. 568-570): The opening chord progression floats in "very light, G-ish tonal gravity" with root changes by second. PLR labels model these "absolute progressions" where each chord tentatively proposes itself as T. The passage is disciplined by choosing the PLR word that "distorts a G-Phrygian scale the least."

**Vaughan Williams, rehearsal E** (p. 572): Call-and-response passages feature "the motion to and from the hexatonic pole, F#-d-F#" — a "signal response of the twentieth century to the sixteenth."

**Nonatonic discharge system** (pp. 554-564): The synthesis operates at the level of trichord-class interaction, with [037] concords and [026] discords related by discharge functions (sensuous dimension) and PLR cycles (energetic dimension).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using transformational analysis in all contexts indiscriminately
  **Correction**: Harrison argues transformational analysis is specifically appropriate for absolute progressions, not for passages with clear functional gravity

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the synthesis produces a single unified method
  **Clarification**: The synthesis is methodological pluralism — knowing when to use which approach, not combining them into one system

- **Confusion**: Believing "tonic-free" means absence of tonics
  **Clarification**: Harrison's "strategic retraction" (p. 573) clarifies that what seems tonic-free is actually "overcrowded with" local tonics
