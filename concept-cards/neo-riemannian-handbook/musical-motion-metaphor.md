# Quick Definition

The inescapable but philosophically problematic metaphor that music "moves," which Harrison argues transformational theory models by constructing a "ventriloquist's dummy" — objects that appear to be in motion but are actually inert things animated by external forces.

# Core Definition

Harrison explores the metaphor of musical motion through Lewin, Zuckerkandl, and Scruton. Lewin's transformational approach promises "a music theory that can model musical motion" by imagining a singer thinking "I am at s; what characteristic transformation do I perform to arrive at t?" (Harrison, pp. 549-550). But Zuckerkandl identifies a paradox: "How can the tone s move to the tone t and still be s? As soon as s starts, say, to be 'pushed,' does it not stop being the pitch s and become something else?" (p. 550). Scruton clarifies that "music is the intentional object of an experience that only rational beings can have, and only through the exercise of imagination. To describe it we must have recourse to metaphor" (p. 550). Since metaphor is inescapable, "the issues at hand are how much power gets invested in a particular metaphor, and to what extent does that power drain other, perhaps equally useful and important metaphors" (p. 550).

# Key Properties

1. Musical motion is metaphorical, not literal — tones do not physically move
2. Transformational theory models motion by treating objects as nodes connected (or separated) by arrows
3. There is slippage between two interpretations: moving FROM s to t vs. moving s ITSELF to t
4. The metaphor of motion can evacuate the concept of the musical object
5. Investing too much in one metaphor drains others (e.g., presence, substance, being)
6. The metaphorical status does not invalidate the modeling; it constrains what the model can capture

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify the Motion Metaphor at Work

1. Note language of "going to," "moving to," "progressing," "arriving" in harmonic descriptions
2. Check whether the analysis treats chords as moving things or as static points connected by operations
3. Consider whether the metaphor of motion captures or obscures the relevant musical experience

# Context & Application

The motion metaphor underlies all transformational theory. Harrison's critique is not that the metaphor is wrong but that it has costs: "objects lose stability as things-in-themselves and either become things-that-move...or are reduced solely to things-in-transformational-relations" (p. 550). This sets up his argument that T, D, S restore a "sensuous dimension" that the motion metaphor drains away.

# Examples

**Lewin's figure 0.1** (p. 549): Two points s and t connected by an arrow — the foundational image of transformational theory. The arrow represents either a measurement between static points or a force moving one to the other.

**Straus's description** (p. 550): Notes being "sent," "urged," and "pushed" — language that transforms Lewin's static arrow into an image of physical motion, creating the problematic strobe-light image (Harrison's example 20.2).

**Zuckerkandl's reductio** (p. 550): "If we attempt actually to connect tone with tone...taking real motion as our model, the result is the familiar screeching glissade of the siren."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Taking the motion metaphor literally
  **Correction**: Music does not literally move; the metaphor describes an intentional experience

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing that rejecting the motion metaphor means rejecting transformational theory
  **Clarification**: Harrison's point is that the metaphor is useful but has costs; awareness of those costs improves analytical practice
