# Quick Definition

A dissonant tone added to a triad that unambiguously identifies its harmonic function, with the dominant seventh in major and the subdominant added sixth in minor serving as inversionally related characteristic dissonances that signal their respective functions.

# Core Definition

In Riemann's theory, a **characteristic dissonance** (*charakteristische Dissonanz*) is a non-chord tone that, when added to a functional triad, unambiguously signals that triad's harmonic function. The term first appeared in Riemann's *Musik-Lexikon*, 5th edition (1900).

The characteristic dissonances of the primary functions are inversionally related (Ch. 7, p. 229):
- **Dominant in major**: The added seventh (e.g., G-B-D-F in C major) -- signals dominant function
- **Subdominant in minor**: The added sixth (e.g., D-F-A-B in A minor) -- signals subdominant function

These are dualistic mirrors: the dominant seventh adds a tone below the triad's upper boundary, while the subdominant sixth adds a tone above the triad's lower boundary.

# Key Properties

1. A triad without its characteristic dissonance could fulfill multiple functions
2. Adding the characteristic dissonance removes functional ambiguity
3. The dominant seventh (in major) and subdominant sixth (in minor) are inversional mirrors
4. Characteristic dissonances play a crucial role in cadential confirmation
5. In Rehding's analysis, the appearance of the characteristic dissonance marks the moment of full functional articulation

# Construction / Recognition

## Identifying Characteristic Dissonances
1. Start with a functional triad (T, D, or S)
2. Add the tone that uniquely identifies its function:
   - Dominant: Add the minor seventh above the root (or undertone-series tone below the prime)
   - Subdominant: Add the major sixth above the root (or overtone-series tone above the prime)
3. The resulting tetrachord is functionally unambiguous

## In Dualist Analysis
- A chord's function may be ambiguous until its characteristic dissonance appears
- The moment of disambiguation is analytically significant, especially for formal articulation
- In the Brahms Clarinet Trio analysis, D minor receiving its characteristic dissonance "identifies it unambiguously as a subdominant" (p. 237)

# Context & Application

Characteristic dissonances are central to Rehding's analysis of the Brahms Clarinet Trio. The D minor chord in the coda only achieves full subdominant function when it receives its characteristic dissonance (added sixth). This moment marks the completion of dualistic synthesis -- the subdominant side is "fully articulated" (p. 237).

The concept connects Riemann's harmonic theory to form: formal sections are defined not just by key areas but by the degree to which harmonic functions are fully articulated through their characteristic dissonances.

# Examples

**Brahms Clarinet Trio, coda** (Example 7.18, p. 237): D minor harmony receives its characteristic dissonance in the coda, "identifying it unambiguously as a subdominant." This moment completes the dualistic synthesis of the movement.

**Inversional relationship**: In C major, the dominant seventh G-B-D-F mirrors the subdominant sixth F-A-C-D in A minor. The added tones (F in the dominant, D/B in the subdominant) are inversionally placed.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating any dissonance as "characteristic"
  **Correction**: Only the specific added tone that uniquely identifies harmonic function qualifies

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: The dominant seventh is characteristic only in major
  **Clarification**: The dominant seventh functions in both modes, but its inversional mirror (the subdominant sixth) is the characteristic dissonance specific to the minor-mode subdominant
