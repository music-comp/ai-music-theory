# Quick Definition

Riemann's reconceptualization of consonance and dissonance as relational, psychological categories defined by tonal context rather than acoustic properties. A chord that sounds smooth can be functionally dissonant (Scheinkonsonanz), and all non-tonic chords contain an element of dissonance because they imply motion toward the tonic.

# Core Definition

In Riemann's psychological theory of harmony, **consonance** is "the unified comprehension of tones representing one and the same Klang in the context of this Klang," while **dissonance** is "the opposition to the Klang forming the principal content of mental representation, the disturbance of the unification of the same by one or several tones, which represent other Klange" (Ch. 2, p. 85).

This definition is entirely contextual: "consonance and dissonance are musical concepts, but not definite forms of sound waves" (Ch. 2, p. 83). The proof: "even a chord which is, according to physical and physiological formulations, the most undisturbed and euphonious can be a dissonance musically (for example, the six-four chord)" (Ch. 2, p. 83).

Key corollary: Only the tonic Klang is "uniquely a musical consonance in the strictest sense of the word — a chord that is capable of concluding and conditions no further progression" (Ch. 2, p. 84). Even the dominant triad "is not a perfect consonance" within a key because its mental representation includes the tonic against which it is measured. As Riemann explains, hearing V in C major means "the C-major chord itself enters the mental representation... the center of the mental representation lies outside it; an element of unrest occurs in it, the desire for a progression to the C-major chord, dissonance" (Ch. 2, pp. 84-85).

The concept of **Scheinkonsonanz** (apparent consonance) formalizes this: subsidiary triads sound consonant but are functionally "dissonant" because they represent — and differ from — the true tonic. Louis's concept of **Auffassungsdissonanz** (conceptual dissonance) captured the complementary phenomenon: chords that "outside of a musical context always produce consonance, can occasionally be used so that they create a dissonance for harmonic understanding" (Ch. 1, n. 137).

# Prerequisites

- **klang** — Consonance/dissonance are properties of how Klange relate to each other
- **klangvertretung** — The representation principle determines whether a chord is consonant or dissonant in context
- **tonality** — Consonance/dissonance require a tonal center for definition

# Key Properties

1. Consonance = unified representation of a single Klang in its own context
2. Dissonance = opposition between the principal Klang and tones representing other Klange
3. Only the tonic is truly consonant in the strictest sense
4. All other chords in a key contain an element of functional dissonance
5. The dominant seventh "can be added without changing its significance" because the dominant is already functionally dissonant
6. Scheinkonsonanz: sounds consonant, functions dissonantly
7. Auffassungsdissonanz (Louis): contextually dissonant despite acoustic consonance

# Construction / Recognition

## To Determine Consonance/Dissonance in Riemann's System
1. Identify the governing tonic
2. Is the chord the tonic itself? → True consonance (stable, conclusive)
3. Is it any other triad within the key? → Scheinkonsonanz (apparently consonant but functionally unstable)
4. Does it contain tones foreign to all primary Klange? → Stronger dissonance
5. The "degree" of dissonance correlates with the remoteness of the represented Klang from the tonic

# Context & Application

This psychological redefinition of consonance/dissonance was the keystone of Riemann's break from Helmholtz. Helmholtz explained consonance through absence of beats — a purely acoustic measure that could not distinguish between the tonic triad and the dominant triad (both are equally "beat-free"). Riemann's solution: consonance is not about sound quality but about tonal meaning.

The concept has far-reaching implications:
- **Added dissonances don't change function**: The dominant seventh merely makes explicit what is already implicit — the dominant's functional instability
- **Modulation reframed**: Key change means changing what counts as consonant
- **Extended tonality**: Chromatic chords can be consonant if their Klangvertretung is clear

# Examples

**Example 1** (Ch. 2, p. 83): The six-four chord — physically "the most undisturbed and euphonious" chord, yet musically a dissonance. This single example demolishes the acoustic theory of consonance.

**Example 2** (Ch. 2, pp. 84-85): The G-major chord in C major: "the center of the mental representation lies outside it... an element of unrest occurs in it, the desire for a progression to the C-major chord, dissonance."

**Example 3** (Ch. 2, p. 84): "A consonant chord as such is not yet entirely able to conclude a piece of music; this consonant chord must rather be the tonic, if it is really to have a conclusive effect."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using Riemann's consonance/dissonance as synonyms for acoustic smoothness/roughness
  **Correction**: In Riemann's system, consonance/dissonance are functional, not acoustic. A "rough" chord can be consonant if it is the tonic; a "smooth" chord can be dissonant if it is not.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking Riemann denied that acoustics play any role in consonance perception
  **Clarification**: Riemann acknowledged acoustics as a "supplementary science" but denied it could *define* musical consonance. The psychological definition supersedes but does not eliminate the acoustic dimension.
