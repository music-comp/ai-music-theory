# Chromatic Mediant

## Quick Definition

Major or minor triads whose roots lie a major or minor third above or below a tonic, with the mediant chord being of the same quality (major) as the tonic, creating direct third-related harmonic connections outside standard diatonic relationships.

## Formal Definition

**Chromatic mediants** are triads related to a tonic by major or minor third root relationships where both chords share the same mode (typically both major). Unlike diatonic mediants (iii and vi), chromatic mediants involve chromatic alteration and create distinctive tonal effects through their specific intervallic relationships.

**The Four Chromatic Mediant Types** (relative to a major tonic):

| Type | Root Relation | Example in C major |
|------|---------------|-------------------|
| Upper Sharp Mediant (USM) | M3 up | E major |
| Upper Flat Mediant (UFM) | m3 up | Eb major |
| Lower Sharp Mediant (LSM) | m3 down | A major |
| Lower Flat Mediant (LFM) | M3 down | Ab major |

**Key Characteristics**:
- Share exactly one common tone with the tonic
- Create distinctive voice-leading patterns with minimal chromatic motion
- The descending major-third mediant (LFM) contains a leading-tone progression to the goal chord
- Each type has a distinctive "sharp side" or "flat side" quality relative to the tonic

**Functional Status**: Following Riemann's late writings (1917), chromatic mediants may be considered to have independent functional status distinct from their diatonic counterparts, defined by intervallic relations, voice-leading content, and qualitative harmonic change rather than merely by scale degree.

## Musical Context

Chromatic mediants were a central feature of nineteenth-century harmonic practice:

- Create the sense of "distance-yet-connectedness" through shared common tones
- Provide direct third-related connections without passing through dominant relations
- Enable tonal progressions that sound coherent without fifth-based harmonic logic
- Form the basis of major-third cycles (hexatonic systems) and other chromatic progressions
- Allow composers to expand tonal boundaries while maintaining tonal coherence

Riemann explicitly cited chromatic mediants as exemplifying *Tonalitat* (expanded chromatic tonality) as distinct from *Tonart* (diatonic key).

## Examples

### Basic

**C major to its chromatic mediants**:
- C major -> E major (USM): Shares E; dramatic "sharp-side" brightness
- C major -> Eb major (UFM): Shares G; modal mixture quality
- C major -> A major (LSM): Shares E; enharmonically related to bVI
- C major -> Ab major (LFM): Shares C; contains B-C leading tone motion; most "natural-sounding" chromatic mediant

**Characteristic progression**: The descending major-third progression (e.g., C -> Ab) shares both the common-tone content and leading-tone resolution qualities of the dominant-tonic relation.

### From Repertoire

**Beethoven, "Emperor" Concerto, III**: Features a descending major-third cycle (C -> Ab -> Fb/E) at the centerpiece of an interior section. The passage maintains tonal orientation through cumulative memory of Eb major despite apparent hexatonic organization.

**Schubert, Piano Sonata D. 850, I, mm. 1-16**: Opens with chromatic mediant progressions (G minor -> F major -> C# major -> A major) creating a descending major-third cycle embedded within a diatonic framework.

**Wolf, "Und schlafst du, mein Madchen"**: Uses ascending major-third cycle (Gb -> Bb -> D) as pivotal structural points, each chord having distinct harmonic meaning relative to the ultimately revealed Eb major tonic.

## Common Confusions

- Chromatic mediants are not the same as diatonic mediants (iii, vi)—they involve chromatic alterations
- Despite hexatonic organization, chromatic mediant passages need not be "nontonal"—each chord can have distinct tonal meaning
- The four chromatic mediants are NOT equivalent—each has distinctive voice-leading and qualitative properties
- "Sharp side" and "flat side" refer to the qualitative direction relative to the tonic, not literal accidentals
- Chromatic mediants in context maintain tonal reference even when fifth-relations are absent
- Calling such progressions "extratonal" may miss how nineteenth-century listeners understood them within expanded tonality
