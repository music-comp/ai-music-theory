# Quick Definition

A chromatic mediant is a major triad whose root lies a major or minor third above or below a tonic, sharing exactly one common tone with it, creating a direct third-related harmonic connection outside standard diatonic relationships.

# Core Definition

Chromatic mediants are triads related to a tonic by major or minor third root relationships where both chords share the same mode (typically both major), involving chromatic alteration relative to the diatonic scale. Kopp classifies four distinct types, each with characteristic voice-leading and qualitative properties. He argues that these mediants possess "independent functional status" following Riemann's late writings (1917 *Handbuch der Harmonielehre*), defined "less by scale degree than by the constellation of intervallic relations between roots, voice-leading content, and qualitative harmonic change, in ways strongly analogous to, but separate from, functional fifth relations" (p. 406). Kopp proposes naming them "according to the intervallic and harmonic relations to the tonic — above or below, and to the sharp side or the flat side — which give them their distinctive qualities" (p. 406).

# Prerequisites

- **Klang** — Chromatic mediants are relationships between consonant triads
- **Tonality** — The concept requires a tonic reference point against which mediant relations are measured

# Key Properties

1. There are exactly four chromatic mediant types relative to a major tonic
2. Each type shares exactly one common tone with the tonic triad
3. The Lower Flat Mediant (LFM, descending M3) contains a leading-tone progression to the goal chord, giving it cadential quality
4. Each type has a distinctive "sharp-side" or "flat-side" quality relative to the tonic
5. Chromatic mediants create the impression of "distance-yet-connectedness" through shared common tones and smooth voice-leading
6. They can substitute for dominant/subdominant functions in expanded tonal contexts

| Type | Abbreviation | Root Relation | Example in C major |
|------|-------------|---------------|-------------------|
| Upper Sharp Mediant | USM | M3 up | E major |
| Upper Flat Mediant | UFM | m3 up | Eb major |
| Lower Sharp Mediant | LSM | m3 down | A major |
| Lower Flat Mediant | LFM | M3 down | Ab major |

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Chromatic Mediants

1. Determine the tonic triad
2. Find triads whose roots are a major or minor third above or below the tonic root
3. Verify that the mediant chord shares the same mode (both major) as the tonic
4. Identify the common tone between tonic and mediant
5. Classify as sharp or flat based on the qualitative direction relative to the tonic

## To Recognize in Context

1. Look for root-position major triads related by third to the tonic
2. Check for common-tone voice-leading connections
3. Note whether the progression moves "sharpward" or "flatward"
4. A descending major-third relation (LFM) will have the strongest cadential quality

# Context & Application

Chromatic mediants were a central feature of nineteenth-century harmonic practice, employed extensively by Beethoven, Schubert, and Wolf. Kopp argues that these relations are fundamentally tonal — each chord has "a separate and distinct harmonic meaning in relation to the key" (p. 403) — rather than being "extratonal" products of hexatonic voice-leading operations as neo-Riemannian theory sometimes asserts. Riemann himself cited a chromatic mediant cadence (E major - C major - Ab major - C major) as a fundamental example of *Tonalität* in his *Musik-Lexikon* (1882).

**Tonnetz representation**: Kopp demonstrates that flipping the diatonic key template along its longer edges on the Tonnetz produces exactly the four chromatic mediant keys, showing their "essential and unmediated harmonic nature within the tonal system" (p. 413-414).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 401, Ex. 14.1): Riemann's chromatic cadence from *Musik-Lexikon*: E major - C major - Ab major - C major. This juxtaposes the tonic with its two major-third chromatic mediants rather than its two dominants.

**Example 2** (p. 402-403, Ex. 14.2): Beethoven, "Emperor" Concerto, III, mm. 112-246. A descending major-third cycle C - Ab - Fb(E) at the centerpiece of the interior section. Each harmony maintains orientation to Eb major: "C major hovers brightly above the tonic; Ab major falls within the tonic sphere; and Fb major dips deeply below" (p. 403).

**Example 3** (pp. 404-406, Ex. 14.3-14.5): Schubert, Piano Sonata D. 850, I, mm. 1-16. Chromatic mediants (G minor - F major - C# major - A major) form a descending major-third cycle. F major functions as upper flat mediant; C# major as upper sharp mediant of the dominant.

**Example 4** (pp. 407-412, Ex. 14.6-14.8): Wolf, "Und schläfst du, mein Mädchen." An ascending major-third cycle (Gb - Bb - D) serves as pivotal structural points, with each chord having distinct harmonic meaning relative to the revealed Eb major tonic.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using altered Roman numerals (e.g., bVI, III) for chromatic mediants
  **Correction**: Kopp advocates naming by type (USM, UFM, LSM, LFM) to capture the qualitative and intervallic properties rather than reducing to scale-degree identity

- **Error**: Treating all four chromatic mediants as equivalent
  **Correction**: Each type has distinct voice-leading, common-tone, and qualitative properties; the LFM uniquely shares leading-tone resolution with the dominant-tonic relation

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing chromatic mediant passages are "nontonal" or "extratonal"
  **Clarification**: Kopp argues each chord maintains "a separate and distinct harmonic meaning in relation to the key" even within major-third cycles; hexatonic organization need not imply nontonality

- **Confusion**: Equating chromatic mediants with diatonic mediants (iii, vi)
  **Clarification**: Chromatic mediants involve chromatic alteration and share only one common tone with the tonic, unlike diatonic mediants which share two

- **Confusion**: Thinking "hexatonic" accurately describes music organized by chromatic third relations
  **Clarification**: Kopp notes such music "does not normally remain within the hexatonic set defined by its structural triads, but tends to be as locally diatonic or chromatic as its style" — "'hexatonic' seems like a misnomer" (p. 414)
