# Quick Definition

In dualist theory, consonance is defined as the perception of tonal unity within the Klang (major or minor triad), while dissonance arises from the simultaneous occurrence of multiple competing Klang-representations. Both major and minor are equally consonant, and neither derives its consonance from acoustic phenomena.

# Core Definition

Riemann's mature theory of consonance, as presented in "Das Problem" (Ch. 5), defines consonance psychologically rather than acoustically. Consonance is "the combination of tones that leads to a unitary conception -- a single Klang-representation." Following Oettingen, dissonance is "the simultaneous occurrence of two or more Klange" (p. 184), where Klang refers not to individual tones but to Klang-representations.

Riemann systematically dismantled acoustic explanations: "the emancipation of the theory of consonance from real overtones is totally indispensable" (p. 188). Neither major nor minor consonance can be fully justified by acoustics; both require psychological explanation.

# Prerequisites

- **Harmonic dualism** -- Consonance theory is embedded within the dualist framework that treats major and minor as equal
- **Klang** -- Consonance is defined in terms of Klang-representation (the mental image of a triad)

# Key Properties

1. Only two fundamental consonances exist: the major triad and the minor triad
2. Consonance means tonal unity -- a single Klang-representation, not "pleasantness"
3. Dissonance arises from competing Klang-representations, not from specific interval types
4. Both major and minor consonance derive from the same principle: simplest numerical ratios applied to different domains
5. The augmented triad is dissonant despite containing only consonant intervals, because it forces competing Klang-representations

# Construction / Recognition

## To Determine Consonance (Dualist Method)
1. Ask whether the simultaneity can be heard as a single Klang (major or minor triad)
2. If yes: consonant -- the mind assigns a unitary Klang-representation
3. If no: dissonant -- competing representations create tension

## Why Only Two Consonances?
- The octave creates identity, not relationship
- The fifth of the fifth (ninth) introduces a mediating prime
- Higher prime-number intervals (7, 11, 13) can be reinterpreted as members of the opposite series
- Therefore only the prime, third, and fifth form genuine consonance

# Context & Application

Riemann's critique targeted multiple acoustic theories: Helmholtz's "clouded consonance" (which treated minor as degraded major), Stumpf's "fusion" theory (which could not explain the augmented triad paradox), and Hostinsky/Capellen's "twin fundamentals" theory (which gave the minor chord multiple roots, contradicting the idea of consonance as unity).

The psychological turn was revolutionary: it freed theory from defending acoustic undertones and unified major and minor as equal consonances. As Riemann declared, "A healthy, natural musical instinct cries out for the unconditional recognition of the consonant character of the minor chord" (p. 184).

# Examples

**The Augmented Triad Paradox** (p. 186): C-E-G# contains only consonant intervals (two major thirds, a minor sixth), yet the simultaneity is dissonant. Riemann explains: the mind cannot assign a single Klang-representation -- E-G# implies E major while C-E implies C major or A minor. The competing representations create dissonance.

**Characteristic Dissonances** (from Rehding, Ch. 7): The dominant seventh in major mirrors the subdominant sixth in minor -- inversionally related dissonances that identify function. The "characteristic dissonance" unambiguously signals harmonic function (e.g., D7 implies dominant).

**The Diminished Triad**: B-D-F in C major functions as representing dominant (G7 without root). The mind supplies the missing G as prime -- the Klang is understood even when incomplete.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Ranking minor as less consonant than major based on overtone series
  **Correction**: Riemann showed that even major consonance cannot be fully justified acoustically; both modes are equally consonant by the same principle

- **Error**: Identifying dissonance solely by interval content
  **Correction**: The augmented triad proves that all-consonant intervals can produce dissonance when no single Klang-representation is possible

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Consonance means "pleasant sounding"
  **Clarification**: Dualist consonance means tonal unity -- a single Klang-representation, which is a cognitive rather than aesthetic category

- **Confusion**: The 7th partial (Bb above C) is consonant because it is "natural"
  **Clarification**: Being acoustically natural does not make an interval consonant in the dualist framework; the 7th partial is dissonant because it belongs to neither the over- nor under-series of the triadic Klang
