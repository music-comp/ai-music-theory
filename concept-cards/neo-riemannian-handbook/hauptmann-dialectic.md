# Quick Definition

Moritz Hauptmann's 1853 philosophical approach to harmonic dualism, in which the major triad "has" its intervals (active, outward-generating) while the minor triad "is had" by its intervals (passive, inward-receiving), deriving the major/minor opposition from dialectical logic rather than acoustics.

# Core Definition

In *Die Natur der Harmonik und Metrik* (1853), Hauptmann derived the major/minor distinction through Hegelian dialectic. The major triad is constructed such that the root **has** the fifth and third as outgrowths: C "has" G as its fifth, C "has" E as its third. The minor triad reverses this: in C minor, G **is had** as fifth by C, Eb **is had** as third by G -- the upper tone is the reference point.

Riemann recognized Hauptmann as the "originator of this idea" for modern theory (Ch. 5, p. 184), and Klumpenhouwer (Ch. 6) identifies Hauptmann's approach as foundational for the dualist perspective on mediation and opposition.

# Key Properties

1. Major: The root actively generates its intervals outward (haben/having)
2. Minor: The reference tone passively receives its intervals inward (sein/being)
3. The distinction is philosophical/dialectical, not acoustic
4. Hauptmann's "triad of triads" extends the dialectic to tonal structure (thesis-antithesis-synthesis)
5. Hauptmann was more cautious than later dualists -- he retained the major dominant in minor

# Construction / Recognition

## Major Triad (Active/Having)
- C **has** the fifth G (outward generation)
- C **has** the third E (outward generation)
- Direction: Upward from root

## Minor Triad (Passive/Being)
- G **is had** as fifth by C (inward reception)
- Eb **is had** as third by G (inward reception)
- Direction: Inward toward the upper reference tone

## Dialectical Extension (Triad of Triads)
- Thesis: Tonic (unmediated)
- Antithesis: Opposition (dominant and subdominant split from tonic)
- Synthesis: Tonic reinstated as center mediating both sides

# Context & Application

Hauptmann's approach predated Oettingen's acoustic formalization and Riemann's systematic dualism. His philosophical grounding made dualism independent of acoustic claims -- an advantage that Riemann would later rediscover when he abandoned acoustic foundations in 1905.

Rehding (Ch. 7) extends Hauptmann's "triad of triads" to formal structure, showing how the dialectical model implies that complete formal synthesis requires exploring both dominant and subdominant sides of the tonic.

# Examples

**The haben/sein distinction** (Hauptmann, *Die Natur*, p. 29, cited in Ch. 5): In C major, C "has" G and E. In A minor (from E), E "is had" by A and C. The relationship between root and upper tones reverses.

**Chain of thirds** (from Rehding, Ch. 7): F-A-C-E-G-B-D -- diatonic triads arranged by shared tones, with the tonic at center. This is Hauptmann's basic framework for tonal structure.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating Hauptmann as a strict dualist like Riemann
  **Correction**: Hauptmann retained the major dominant in minor mode, making him a more cautious or "soft" dualist

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: The haben/sein distinction is about acoustics
  **Clarification**: It is a philosophical/dialectical distinction about the direction of intervallic generation, not an acoustic claim
