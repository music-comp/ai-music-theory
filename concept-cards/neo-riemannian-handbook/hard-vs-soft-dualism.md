# Quick Definition

Klumpenhouwer's distinction between two forms of harmonic dualism: **soft dualism** treats major and minor triads as equal but does not require explicit inversional structure, while **hard dualism** additionally requires that the two triad types appear as mirror or inverted expressions of one another.

# Core Definition

In Chapter 6, Klumpenhouwer identifies two characteristic ideas of harmonic dualism:
1. **Equality**: Major and minor triads arise as "two distinct but equal expressions of a single structuring principle, together forming the totality of harmonic possibilities"
2. **Inversion**: Major and minor triads "appear as mirror or inverted expressions of one another"

**Soft dualism** exhibits only characteristic (1) -- equality without explicit inversion. **Hard dualism** exhibits both characteristics -- equality AND inversional structure. The terms are "named to recall the two forms of determinism, and not to suggest a preference for one form of dualism or the other" (Ch. 6, n. 10).

# Key Properties

1. Soft dualism: Major and minor are equal and independent but not necessarily inversional
2. Hard dualism: Major and minor are equal, independent, AND inversionally related
3. Most 19th-century dualists (Hauptmann, Oettingen, Riemann) were hard dualists
4. Attacks on dualism typically target hard dualism specifically
5. Neo-Riemannian PLR transformations embody hard dualism (contextual inversions)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Soft Dualism
- Major and minor treated as equally valid, independent consonances
- No explicit claim about inversional construction
- Example: A theory that gives major and minor equal analytical weight without deriving one from the other

## To Identify Hard Dualism
- Everything in soft dualism PLUS:
- Minor explicitly constructed as the inversion of major
- Root at fifth for minor (downward construction)
- Transformations between major and minor treated as inversions
- Example: Riemann's °g notation for C minor (prime at G, built downward)

# Context & Application

The distinction matters analytically because critiques of dualism often implicitly assume hard dualism, then declare dualism refuted when inversional structure is not perfectly realized in practice. Klumpenhouwer argues that defenders and critics must be clear about which form of dualism is under discussion.

Zarlino's generation of triads through arithmetic and harmonic means of the fifth "certainly qualifies as a hard dualist account" (Ch. 6, n. 13) -- the construction itself involves a mediating pitch, and the difference between arithmetic and harmonic means "stand in an inversional (reciprocal) relationship."

# Examples

**Hauptmann as cautious dualist**: Hauptmann retained the major dominant in minor mode, making him arguably a soft dualist who used dialectical rather than strictly inversional logic.

**Riemann as hard dualist**: Riemann's notation (c+ for C major, °g for C minor) explicitly encodes inversional construction, and his Schritte/Wechsel system treats major-to-minor and minor-to-major relationships as formal inversions.

**Belinfante's critique targets hard dualism**: The argument that function labels should reverse in minor assumes hard inversional consistency -- a specifically hard-dualist expectation.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Attacking soft dualism with arguments that only apply to hard dualism
  **Correction**: Distinguish which form of dualism is being critiqued; soft dualism does not require inversional construction

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: All dualists are hard dualists
  **Clarification**: Hauptmann and some modern neo-Riemannian approaches may be better characterized as soft dualist

- **Confusion**: Hard dualism is more "correct" or rigorous than soft dualism
  **Clarification**: The terms describe different theoretical commitments, not different levels of rigor or truth
