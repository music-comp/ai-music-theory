# Normative Analysis

## Quick Definition

Riemann's analytical method of measuring actual musical structures against idealized prototypes (particularly the eight-measure period), where deviations from the norm reveal compositional individuality and expressive effect rather than structural deficiency.

## Formal Definition

**Normative analysis** is the methodological approach underlying Riemann's metric theory, articulated clearly in his essay on Brahms's songs:

> "All of the freer forms are to be measured, today as in all times, according to the standard of the strictly regular; they obtain their individual quality and their individual effect through their deviation from the norm."

**Core Principles**:
1. An idealized norm exists (the eight-measure period with regular accent alternation)
2. Actual music deviates from this norm in various ways
3. Deviations are not failures but sources of expressive individuality
4. Analysis reveals how specific deviations create specific effects
5. The norm remains as a backdrop against which deviations are measured

**Aesthetic Consequence**: "The downright abolishment of normative concepts is of no use to anyone." Without the norm, we cannot appreciate the deviation.

**Application to Brahms**: Modified strophic songs provide ideal material for normative analysis—the two strophes serve as each other's norm, revealing how text-specific changes create expressive effects.

## Musical Context

Normative analysis connects Riemann's synchronic theory to diachronic historical understanding:

- The modern theme (Mannheim style onward) is distinguished by increased internal differentiation
- Recognizing this differentiation requires distinguishing "essential" from "accessory" material
- Historical progress involves "ever firmer positioning of the actual structure, around which all accessory material is arranged"

**Methodological Implications**:
- Analysis should identify the underlying periodic structure
- Surface elaborations should be distinguished from essential stations
- Compositional development (in Beethoven, for instance) involves increasingly sophisticated manipulation of the normative framework

**Critique and Defense**: While normative analysis has been criticized as "inflexible" or "Procrustean," it reveals structural relationships that purely surface-oriented analysis misses. The key is understanding deviation as meaningful, not deficient.

## Examples

### Basic

**Strophic norm applied**:
```
Strophe 1: | A | B | C | D | (musical sections)
Strophe 2: | A'| B'| C'| D'| (text-motivated variations)

Strophe 1 provides the norm;
Strophe 2 deviations reveal text-painting choices.
```

**Period structure norm**:
```
Norm:     1  2  3  4  5  6  7  8
Actual:   1  2  3  4  3a 4a 5  6  7  8

The repeated 3-4 is an expansion measured against the 8-measure norm.
```

### From Repertoire

**Brahms, "Immer leiser," Op. 105/2**: Riemann overlays the two strophes graphically to reveal:
- Only four measures return identically in the same formal position
- Melodic variations serve specific textual motivations
- The strophic structure provides the norm; deviations create expressive specificity

**Example of productive normative comparison**: "The words 'Ja, ich werde sterben mussen' are transferred to the higher register... the intensification of the second line would substitute a jealous emotion in place of poignant resignation."

**Brahms's own practice**: Brahms annotated his copy of Riemann's *Handbuch der Harmonielehre*, suggesting interest in normative harmonic concepts. His textual emendations to "Immer leiser" (changing "wehen/sehen" to "wehn/sehn") show him pursuing rhythmic symmetry between stanzas—a normative comparison at the compositional stage.

**Beethoven sonata analyses**: Riemann's chronological arrangement reveals "the imposing growth and strengthening of artistic powers" through the "ever firmer positioning" of the periodic structure—normative analysis applied to compositional development across a lifetime.

## Common Confusions

- Normative analysis does NOT claim all music must be 8 measures—it measures deviations from this norm
- Deviations are NOT failures—they are sources of individuality and expression
- The norm is a conceptual tool, not a prescriptive rule
- Critics who find Riemann "inflexible" may miss his appreciation for meaningful deviation
- Normative analysis can be applied to harmony, melody, and form, not just meter
- Historical change involves increasing sophistication in handling the norm, not its abandonment
- "Strophic norm" is different from "periodic norm"—both can operate in the same analysis
