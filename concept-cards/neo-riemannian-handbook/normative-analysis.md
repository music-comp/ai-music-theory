# Quick Definition

Riemann's analytical method of measuring actual musical structures against idealized prototypes (particularly the eight-measure period), where deviations from the norm reveal compositional individuality and expressive effect rather than structural deficiency.

# Core Definition

**Normative analysis** is the methodological approach underlying Riemann's metric theory. From his essay on Brahms's songs (translated by Berry, Ch. 17): "All of the freer forms are to be measured, today as in all times, according to the standard of the strictly regular; they obtain their individual quality and their individual effect through their deviation from the norm" (p. 464).

Core principles: (1) An idealized norm exists (the eight-measure period with regular accent alternation); (2) Actual music deviates from this norm; (3) Deviations are not failures but sources of individuality; (4) Analysis reveals how specific deviations create specific effects; (5) "The downright abolishment of normative concepts is of no use to anyone" (p. 464).

# Prerequisites

- **Auftakttheorie**: The end-accented metric model that provides the normative framework.

# Key Properties

1. **Norm as backdrop**: The eight-measure period provides the standard against which deviations register
2. **Deviation as meaning**: Deviations create individuality and expressive effect, not deficiency
3. **Strophic comparison**: Modified strophic songs provide ideal material -- strophes serve as each other's norm
4. **Historical application**: Compositional development across a career shows "ever firmer positioning" of periodic structure

# Construction / Recognition

Apply normative analysis by: identifying the underlying periodic structure; distinguishing surface elaborations from essential stations; comparing strophes (in strophic songs) to reveal text-motivated changes; measuring deviations against the normative prototype to reveal their expressive purpose.

# Context & Application

Berry (Ch. 17) shows Riemann applying this method to Brahms's Op. 105/2 and Op. 107/3. For Op. 105/2, Riemann overlays two strophes graphically to reveal that only four measures return identically in the same formal position; melodic variations serve specific textual motivations. The strophic structure provides the norm; deviations create expressive specificity.

Riemann's Beethoven sonata analyses apply normative analysis to compositional development across a lifetime: "the imposing growth and strengthening of artistic powers" through "ever firmer positioning" of the periodic structure.

# Examples

**Cross-strophic analysis** (Berry, Ch. 17, p. 467): In "Immer leiser," comparing strophes reveals: "The words 'Ja, ich werde sterben mussen' are transferred to the higher register... the intensification of the second line would substitute a jealous emotion in place of poignant resignation."

**Brahms's own normative practice** (p. 467): Brahms annotated his copy of Riemann's Handbuch der Harmonielehre. His textual emendations to "Immer leiser" (changing "wehen/sehen" to "wehn/sehn") show him pursuing rhythmic symmetry between stanzas -- normative comparison at the compositional stage.

**Period structure norm**: Norm: 1-2-3-4-5-6-7-8. Actual: 1-2-3-4-3a-4a-5-6-7-8. The repeated 3-4 is an expansion measured against the 8-measure norm.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming normative analysis claims all music must be 8 measures.
  **Correction**: The norm is a conceptual tool for measuring deviations, not a prescriptive rule.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking normative analysis is "inflexible" or "Procrustean."
  **Clarification**: Riemann appreciates meaningful deviation; the key insight is that deviation requires a norm to be meaningful.

- **Confusion**: Limiting normative analysis to meter.
  **Clarification**: It can be applied to harmony, melody, and form -- any domain where prototypes exist.
