# Quick Definition

Riemann's theory of musical form, which classifies forms by the number and arrangement of cadential formulas (Schlussformeln) within and between eight-measure periods, but which he notably refused to extend beyond the eight-measure level -- a limitation that left his theory without a genuine large-scale Formenlehre.

# Core Definition

Rehding describes Riemann's formal theory as built on two pillars (Ch. 7, pp. 220-224):

1. **Metric theory**: Music organizes into regular two-, four-, and eight-measure units with systematic accentual patterns (*Auftakttheorie*)
2. **Harmonic theory**: Each unit is articulated by cadential progressions (Schlussformeln) that establish tonic through S-D-T cycles

Riemann classified forms by how Schlussformeln scale up:
- First form: Simple A-B-A (one cadential cycle)
- Second form: Larger A-B-A (minuet/trio)
- Third form: Elaborate A-B-A (rondo)
- Fourth form: Sonata (multiple cadential cycles at different levels)

Crucially, Riemann stated he was "not interested in pursuing the formal element beyond the eight-measure period" (*Grosse Kompositionslehre*, 1902, 1:424). His published analyses "exceptionally few... actually go beyond the eight-measure level" (p. 240).

# Prerequisites

- **Harmonic dualism** -- Riemann's formal theory is embedded within his harmonic framework

# Key Properties

1. Forms classified by number and arrangement of Schlussformeln
2. The eight-measure period is the largest unit Riemann systematically analyzed
3. Organicist rhetoric (the whole as more than the sum of parts) is not carried out analytically
4. This limitation made Riemann vulnerable to Schenkerian critique (Federhofer)
5. The gap between metric-harmonic detail and large-scale form was never bridged

# Construction / Recognition

## Riemann's Four Forms (n. 14)
1. **First form**: A-B-A model (two-part song form) -- one expanded Schlussformel
2. **Second form**: Larger A-B-A (three-part song, minuet/trio)
3. **Third form**: Elaborate A-B-A (rondo forms)
4. **Fourth form**: Sonata forms -- multiple Schlussformeln at different hierarchical levels

## The Limitation
- Riemann analyzed eight-measure periods with extraordinary detail
- He described nine measures of the *Waldstein* slow movement as "a single gigantic F-major cadential progression" (cited p. 240) -- but this is the exception
- Large-scale tonal planning (sonata-level key relations) was not systematically addressed

# Context & Application

The gap between Riemann's detailed phrase-level analysis and his lack of a large-scale Formenlehre was both a historical weakness and, paradoxically, a strength. Federhofer and the Schenkerian camp attacked the absence of "a cohesive concept of large-scale structure." But in the late 20th century, Riemann's "almost postmodern refusal to decide what exactly constitutes musical form" aligned with increasing skepticism about formalist depth-models.

Rehding notes: "The essentially fractured nature of Riemann's theoretical concepts, their blithe indifference to formal totalities, corresponds precisely to the needs of the moment and would seem to make them into the formal theory for our times" (p. 241).

# Examples

**Riemann on form** (cited p. 222): "The supreme demand for all form, including musical form, is unity" which "can only fully unfold in the context of opposition."

**The Waldstein exception** (p. 240): Seidel's defense of Riemann cited Riemann's analysis of nine measures in the *Waldstein* slow movement as evidence of large-scale hearing. Rehding notes "a reference to one nine-measure phrase is not the most robust counterargument."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming Riemann had a complete theory of sonata form
  **Correction**: His formal theory was essentially limited to the phrase/period level

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Riemann's organicist language implies a Schenkerian-style depth model
  **Clarification**: Riemann's organicism was aesthetic rhetoric, not analytical method -- he did not trace long-range harmonic connections
