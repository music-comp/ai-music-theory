# Quick Definition

Riemann's mature concept (from "Ideen," 1914-15) that musical understanding involves actively imagining each tone as a representative of one of the three elements (root, third, or fifth) of a major or minor triad, making every tone's meaning context-dependent and multiply determined.

# Core Definition

Clark frames her chapter around Riemann's late concept of **tonal imagination** as articulated in "Ideen zu einer 'Lehre von den Tonvorstellungen'" (1914-15). At the heart of this concept lies the principle of **Klangvertretung**: any tone may "project or assume meaning as one of the three elements of a major or minor triad." This means that a single pitch, say A, can be imagined as the root of A major, the root of A minor, the third of F major, the third of F# minor, the fifth of D major, or the fifth of D minor — six possible triadic identities. Clark argues that this principle, properly understood, enriches neo-Riemannian theory's account of how tones acquire and change meaning through harmonic progression. Where neo-Riemannian theory typically focuses on chord-level transformations (P, L, R), Clark demonstrates that attending to the tone-level shifts of triadic identity reveals "an enriched view of structural and chromatic third relations" in Schubert's Lieder (Ch. 10).

# Prerequisites

- **Klangvertretung**: The principle of tone representation that grounds tonal imagination

# Key Properties

1. **Active mental process**: Tonal imagination is not passive reception but active construction of meaning
2. **Triadic identity**: Every tone is imagined as root, third, or fifth of some triad
3. **Six possible identities**: Each pitch class participates in exactly 6 triads (3 major, 3 minor)
4. **Context-dependent**: The same physical tone assumes different imagined identities in different harmonic contexts
5. **Continuous transformation**: As harmonies change, the imagined identity of a held or recurring tone transforms

# Construction / Recognition

To apply tonal imagination analytically:
1. Identify a significant pitch that persists or recurs across a passage
2. At each harmonic moment, determine which triad the pitch belongs to
3. Determine the pitch's role within that triad (root, third, or fifth)
4. Track how that role changes as the harmonic context shifts
5. Observe how PLR transformations at the chord level correspond to changes in tone identity

# Context & Application

Clark uses tonal imagination to analyze three Schubert Lieder, demonstrating that the concept reveals structural features invisible to standard neo-Riemannian chord-level analysis. The approach is particularly valuable for:
- Songs where a single pitch is sustained or repeated across changing harmonies
- Passages with chromatic third relations where common tones serve as pivots
- Analytical contexts where the listener's shifting perception of a fixed tone is the primary source of musical interest

# Examples

Clark analyzes Schubert's "Liedesend" (Ch. 10), demonstrating how attention to the changing triadic-functional identities of individual tones across chord changes reveals structural relationships. When a pitch like Db/C# persists across a modulation, its triadic identity shifts (e.g., from the third of Bb minor to the fifth of Gb major to the root of Db major), and these shifts map onto specific neo-Riemannian transformations at the chord level.

Riemann's original example 10.1 (from "Ideen"): three pairs of triads — C major/C minor, C major/A minor, C major/E minor — correspond to P, R, L transformations respectively. Clark points out that Riemann intended these to illustrate how individual tones shift their triadic identity, not merely how chords transform.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Reducing tonal imagination to chord-level transformations alone
  **Correction**: Clark's point is that attending to individual tone identities within chords reveals analytical insights that chord-level analysis misses

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Tonal imagination is the same as "hearing in a key"
  **Clarification**: Tonal imagination is more specific: it involves imagining each individual tone's triadic role (root, third, or fifth), not merely its scale-degree function within a key
