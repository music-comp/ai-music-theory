# Quick Definition

Tymoczko's demonstration that traditional tonal syntax relates major and minor through diatonic transposition rather than inversion -- I-IV-V-I in major becomes i-iv-V-i in minor through diatonic transposition, not inversion -- meaning Riemann's inversionally symmetric terminology describes a symmetry that common-practice tonality does not actually possess.

# Core Definition

Tymoczko demonstrates (Ch. 8, Section 2) that the harmonic patterns of traditional tonal music are NOT inversionally symmetric. Major-mode progressions do not invert to produce standard minor-mode progressions:

- Major: I - ii6/5 - V - I
- Diatonic transposition to minor: i - ii°6/5 - V - i (standard minor practice)
- **Inversion** of the major progression: i - v6/5 - iv - i (NOT standard practice)

"The harmonic patterns in traditional tonal music do not exhibit even approximate signs of inversional invariance." The relationship between major and minor modes is better described as **diatonic transposition** (shifting scale-degree patterns within a mode-adjusted framework) than as **inversion** (reflecting pitch space).

# Prerequisites

- **Inversional symmetry** -- The property whose absence from tonal syntax Tymoczko demonstrates

# Key Properties

1. I-IV-V-I is extremely common; its inversion i-v-iv-i is extremely rare
2. Major and minor relate by diatonic transposition, not inversion
3. Riemann's inversionally invariant terminology (Schritte/Wechsel) mismatches tonal practice
4. This explains why Riemann's function theory coexists "somewhat uncomfortably" with his dualism
5. The asymmetry dissolves in chromatic music, where efficient voice leading creates inversional patterns

# Construction / Recognition

## Testing for Inversional Invariance
1. Take a common major-mode progression
2. Apply pitch-space inversion
3. Check whether the result is a common minor-mode progression
4. If yes: inversional symmetry. If no: asymmetry.

## Result
- I-IV-V-I → i-v-iv-i: NOT common in minor
- I-vi-IV-V-I → i-III-v-iv-i: NOT common
- The standard minor progressions are diatonic transpositions, not inversions, of major progressions

# Context & Application

The tonal syntax asymmetry is Tymoczko's strongest argument against traditional harmonic dualism. If major and minor do not relate by inversion in actual compositional practice, then Riemann's inversionally invariant terminology describes a phantom symmetry.

However, this argument does not undermine neo-Riemannian applications to chromatic music. In the "second practice" of chromaticism, inversional symmetry emerges as a by-product of voice-leading efficiency. Riemann's terminology is mismatched to diatonic practice but well-suited to chromatic practice.

# Examples

**Riemann's function labels** (Ch. 8): Riemann would label vi as "Tp" (Tonikaparallele) in major but label VI as "tP" (different notation) in minor -- "even though the submediant triad behaves similarly in the two modes." The terminology fights the diatonic transposition symmetry.

**The dominant asymmetry** (Ch. 8): In both major and minor, V resolves to I/i. Inversion would predict iv resolving to i -- but this is plagal, not the structural backbone of tonal syntax.
