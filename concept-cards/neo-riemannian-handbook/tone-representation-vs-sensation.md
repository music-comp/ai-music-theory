# Quick Definition

The distinction between the physical/physiological experience of hearing a tone (Empfindung, sensation) and the mental act of understanding that tone as a representative of a harmonic context (Vorstellung, representation), which Riemann placed at the foundation of his mature theory.

# Core Definition

Clark opens her chapter by contextualizing Riemann's late rejection of physical and physiological foundations for music theory in favor of a theory grounded in **Tonvorstellungen** (tone representations). The key distinction is between **Empfindung** (sensation) — the passive, physical reception of sound — and **Vorstellung** (representation) — the active, mental construction of musical meaning. By the time Riemann wrote "Ideen zu einer 'Lehre von den Tonvorstellungen'" (1914-15), he "confessed that finding a physical foundation for music theory was a mistaken pursuit" and proposed instead a theory based on how the mind actively imagines and constructs tonal relationships. Clark argues this distinction is crucial for understanding why neo-Riemannian transformations (P, L, R) properly describe not acoustic events but changes in the listener's mental representation of tonal identities (Ch. 10).

# Prerequisites

Foundational concept with no prerequisites within this domain; draws on the broader philosophical tradition of German idealism.

# Key Properties

1. **Empfindung (sensation)**: Passive, physical, acoustic; what the ear receives
2. **Vorstellung (representation)**: Active, mental, constructive; what the mind imagines
3. **Priority reversal**: Riemann moved from privileging sensation (acoustic foundation) to privileging representation (psychological foundation)
4. **Implications for theory**: If musical meaning resides in representation, then music theory studies mental activity, not physics
5. **Neo-Riemannian relevance**: PLR transformations describe changes in mental representation, not acoustic motion

# Construction / Recognition

Recognize this distinction when:
- A theorist invokes "what the listener hears" versus "what the listener understands"
- Analysis accounts for the same physical sound having different meanings in different contexts
- Transformations are described as changes in understanding rather than changes in sound

# Context & Application

This distinction underpins the entire enterprise of neo-Riemannian theory as Clark presents it. If transformations describe changes in mental representation rather than acoustic events, then:
- Enharmonic equivalence is justified (the mind equates C# and Db)
- Common tones can shift triadic identity without changing pitch
- A single sustained pitch can undergo meaningful transformation through harmonic recontextualization

The distinction also explains why Riemann ultimately abandoned the undertone series as a physical justification for minor: the psychological theory of tone representation does not require acoustic grounding.

# Examples

Clark cites Riemann's own example from "Ideen" (example 10.1, Ch. 10): three pairs of triads (C major/C minor, C major/A minor, C major/E minor) corresponding to P, R, L. These illustrate not acoustic relations between chords but how the listener's mental representation of individual tones shifts as the harmonic context changes.

Clark's Schubert analyses demonstrate the practical consequence: when a pitch like C# is sustained across a modulation, its Vorstellung (mental representation) transforms even though its Empfindung (acoustic sensation) does not change.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating PLR transformations as descriptions of acoustic voice-leading motion
  **Correction**: In Clark's framing, they describe shifts in mental representation; voice-leading smoothness is a consequence of representational proximity, not its cause

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Riemann always held a psychological foundation for theory
  **Clarification**: Riemann's early work was acoustically grounded; the shift to psychological foundations was gradual and only fully articulated in "Ideen" (1914-15)
