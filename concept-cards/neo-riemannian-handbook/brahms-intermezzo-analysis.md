# Quick Definition

Tymoczko's extended analysis of Brahms's Intermezzo Op. 76 No. 4 as a demonstration of the "contrapuntal approach" to chromatic music, showing how the piece systematically explores the voice-leading possibilities of a few characteristic sonorities (particularly the Tristan chord) while maintaining compositional coherence through motivic connections.

# Core Definition

Tymoczko's analysis of Brahms Op. 76 No. 4 (Ch. 8, Section 5) exemplifies his contrapuntal approach to 19th-century chromaticism. The piece is in Bb major with a contrasting middle section in Cb major -- a semitonal key relation. The Tristan chord {F, G#, B, Eb} appears at three structurally significant points (labeled a1, a2, a3), resolving differently each time via efficient voice leading:

- To F7 (a1)
- To Ab major (a2)
- To Eb major (a3)

The piece "systematically explores the voice-leading possibilities of a few characteristic sonorities," demonstrating that chromatic compositions can be understood as explorations of voice-leading space rather than as functional progressions.

- a1: Tristan chord → F7 (traditional dominant preparation)

- Each uses minimal voice-leading motion to reach a familiar sonority

# Context & Application

The analysis demonstrates Tymoczko's thesis that chromatic music is best understood contrapuntally. Brahms does not follow a grand inversional scheme; instead, he "explores voice-leading possibilities" with compositional craft ensuring coherence.

The modulation from Bb major to Cb major involves iv of Bb becoming iii of Cb -- dualistically equivalent to Schubert's modulation in "Die junge Nonne" where VI of F minor becomes V of F# minor. "Schubert exploits the former property and Brahms the latter, with their two modulations being dualistically equivalent" (n. 43). The harmonic minor scale has two major triads a semitone apart; its inversion (harmonic major) has two minor triads a semitone apart.

# Examples

**The Tristan reference** (Ch. 8): "The appearance of the Tristan chord suggests that Brahms may sometimes have made similar reference to the work of his contemporaries" (n. 37). The piece engages with Wagnerian chromaticism through Brahmsian formal control.

**Voice-leading centrifugality** (Ch. 8): Efficient chromatic voice leading pulls the music away from tonal centers. Brahms controls this "centrifugal" force by returning repeatedly to the same sonorities and maintaining motivic connections.

**The Brahms-Schubert dualistic equivalence** (n. 43): The two modulations exploit inversionally related properties of harmonic minor and harmonic major scales, connecting these two composers' chromaticisms through shared voice-leading logic.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Analyzing the piece as functional harmony with chromatic embellishments
  **Correction**: Tymoczko shows the chromatic passages follow their own voice-leading logic, not functional syntax

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: The three Tristan chord resolutions are functionally equivalent
  **Clarification**: They resolve to different chords via different voice leadings; only the starting sonority is the same
