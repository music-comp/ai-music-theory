# Quick Definition

Riemann's fundamental analytical distinction between material that constitutes the essential harmonic argument of a period and material that is "inserted" or "appended" as accessory elaboration, forming the critical bridge between his prototypical eight-measure period and actual musical compositions.

# Core Definition

The essential-accessory distinction is Riemann's most consequential analytical tool. From his *Grosse Kompositionslehre*: "The most important distinction is between *actually constitutive, developing passages* and *insertions*... the *essential musical action* and the *moments of idle lingering* inserted between the main phases of that action" (quoted by Burnham, p. 450). Burnham identifies this as "the most palpable bridge between background prototype and foreground realization" (p. 446). In practice, "harmonic progression represents the evolving musical argument and thus helps determine the primary stations of his prototypical construction; patches of harmonic stasis serve as confirmation or filler within that structure" (p. 446).

# Prerequisites

- **Periodic function** — The distinction determines which measures carry essential functions and which are accessory

# Key Properties

1. Essential material advances the harmonic argument; accessory material confirms or fills
2. The distinction enables parsing extended movements into eight-measure periods
3. Accessory material receives alphabetic suffixes in periodic function labeling (3a, 4a, 8a)
4. Harmonic progression = essential; harmonic stasis = accessory
5. The distinction is the key to recognizing the "modernity" of a theme
6. Not evaluative but structural — accessory material creates expressive effects (comedy, drama)
7. Riemann sees this distinction as "the clear distinction between the essential and the subsidiary" that underlies all musical logic

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply the Distinction

1. Identify points of harmonic change — these mark essential stations
2. Identify passages of harmonic stasis — these are accessory insertions
3. Assign essential stations the primary periodic functions (1-8)
4. Label accessory material with alphabetic suffixes (3a, 4a, etc.)
5. The resulting skeleton should conform to the eight-measure prototype

# Context & Application

Burnham presents this distinction as the key to understanding how Riemann can parse entire Beethoven movements into periods. It also connects to Riemann's historical narrative: he claims "Allegro themes are the first best place to distinguish the new style from the old" (Burnham quoting Riemann, p. 446), and that "the modern theme is no longer only a melodic fragment but is much more an entire melody, more or less completed within itself" (p. 446). Recognizing the distinction allows the analyst to appreciate how Beethoven's "ever firmer positioning of the actual structure, around which all accessory material is arranged" (p. 451) constitutes artistic maturation.

Riemann further claims this distinction can "help elucidate Beethoven's developmental progress throughout the course of his piano sonatas" (p. 451) — the ability to handle increasingly complex accessory material marks artistic growth.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 446, Ex. 16.4): Op. 31, No. 3, Period IV. The pairs 3-4 and 5-6 are repeated at higher register (3a-4a, 5a-6a) — these repetitions are accessory material that "obviously serve to extend the fundamental period without furthering the harmonic argument."

**Example 2** (pp. 451-452): Op. 31, No. 1, Period III. Within sixteen measures, the essential harmonic argument moves from tonic to dominant. The multiple "8" functions represent accessory confirmation of the cadential goal.

**Example 3** (pp. 452-453): Op. 31, No. 3, Period VI. A 33-measure period where the essential structure is obscured by extensive accessory material (trills, arpeggios, delayed cadence), but the essential stations remain identifiable.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating the distinction as evaluative (essential = good, accessory = bad)
  **Correction**: The distinction is structural; accessory material creates the dramatic and comic effects that give the music its character

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the essential material is always obvious from the surface
  **Clarification**: Identifying it requires understanding harmonic progression at a deeper level; this is what makes analysis "reading between the lines"

- **Confusion**: Believing the distinction is unproblematic
  **Clarification**: Some theorists criticize the essential/accessory distinction as theoretically question-begging — the "essential" stations are those that fit the model, creating circularity
