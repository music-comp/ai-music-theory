# Tonnetz (Tone Network)

## Quick Definition

A two-dimensional lattice representing pitch-class relationships organized by perfect fifths (horizontal axis) and major thirds (vertical axis), used to visualize triadic relationships and voice-leading connections.

## Formal Definition

The **Tonnetz** (German: "tone network" or table of tonal relations) is a spatial representation of pitch classes arranged according to consonant interval relationships. In its standard form:

- **Horizontal axis**: Perfect fifths (e.g., C-G-D-A...)
- **Vertical axis**: Major thirds (e.g., C-E-G#...)
- **Diagonal axis**: Minor thirds emerge as the difference (7 - 4 = 3 semitones)

Triads appear as triangular regions on the Tonnetz:
- **Major triads**: Right triangles pointing one direction
- **Minor triads**: Right triangles pointing the opposite direction

Two geometric interpretations exist:
1. **Nonconforming (infinite plane)**: Assumes just intonation; enharmonically distinct pitches occupy different locations; extends infinitely
2. **Conforming (torus)**: Assumes equal temperament; enharmonically equivalent pitches share locations; wraps into a torus

## Historical Development

### Stage 1: Oettingen's Acoustical Matrix (1866)
Arthur von Oettingen's *Harmoniesystem in dualer Entwickelung* presented the Tonnetz as a **literal multiplication table** for calculating relative frequencies in just intonation. The expression 5^m × 3^n generates tone frequencies, with rows and columns representing exponents. The table served to:
- Illustrate derivation of tones in pure intonation
- Support his dual principles of tonicity and phonicity
- Distinguish syntonically distinct tones via *Striche* (comma marks)

### Stage 2: Riemann's Early Adoption (1873-1880)
Riemann adopted Oettingen's table in his dissertation "Ueber das musikalische Hören," using it to:
- Demonstrate intonational differences between enharmonic tones
- List 133 distinct intervals within an octave
- Support acoustical foundations for harmonic theory

The *Striche* (underlines/overlines) indicated syntonic comma differences among like-named tones.

### Stage 3: Metaphorical Transformation (1880s-1900s)
Riemann progressively shifted from acoustic to psychological understanding:
- Adopted Hauptmann's axiomatic view (fifth and third as first principles, not ratios)
- Introduced Drobisch's symbolic notation (Q for fifth, T for third)
- Recognized the Tonnetz as a **traversable landscape** rather than a static matrix
- In *Musik-Lexikon* (1894), added *Verwandtschaftsgrad* (degree of relatedness) measuring paths

### Stage 4: Psychological Foundation ("Ideen," 1914-1915)
In "Ideen zu einer 'Lehre von den Tonvorstellungen,'" Riemann fully embraced the path-combinatorial conception:
- Tones assume distinct meanings based on their **derivation as pathways**
- The Tonnetz mediates between phenomenal (tempered) sounds and noumenal (mental) meanings
- Enharmonic identification becomes "absolutely indispensable for our hearing of music"
- *Striche* no longer signify acoustical differences but distinct path derivations

### Stage 5: Neo-Riemannian Revival (1980s-present)
Modern theorists (Lewin, Hyer, Cohn) reinterpreted the Tonnetz:
- Assumed equal temperament (conforming Tonnetz on torus)
- Used for analyzing chromatic progressions independent of key
- Connected to PLR transformations and voice-leading geometry
- Revealed group-theoretic properties of triadic relations

## Multiple Rediscoveries

Martin Vogel observed the Tonnetz was "discovered twice" (Euler 1773, Oettingen 1866). Cohn catalogs further rediscoveries by generating interval classes:

| Axes | Theorists | Application |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| IC4 × IC5 | Euler, Oettingen, Riemann, Longuet-Higgins | Just intonation consonances |
| IC3 × IC4 | Weitzmann (1853), Balzano (1980) | Augmented/diminished cycles |
| IC3 × IC5 | Vial, Weber, Schoenberg, Lerdahl | Key relationships |
| IC3 × IC4 × IC5 | Hostinsky (1879), Krumhansl, Hyer, Cohn | Balanced consonances |

## Analytical Applications

### Regional Encapsulation
Diatonic regions appear as parallelograms bounded by syntonic images (e.g., two D naturals differing by syntonic comma). Sliding the parallelogram models modulation.

### Triadic Navigation
The Tonnetz maps:
- PLR transformations as movements between adjacent triangles
- Voice-leading parsimony as minimal geometric displacement
- Hexatonic and octatonic cycles as specific pathways

### Functional Analysis
Motion on the Tonnetz can be interpreted functionally:
- Leftward/upward = subdominant direction (authentic sensation)
- Rightward/downward = dominant direction (plagal sensation)
- Diagonal (major third axis) = neutralized voice leading

## Relationship to Chord and Key Space

Cohn argues for conflating pitch-class, chord, and key representations in the Tonnetz, contra Lerdahl's separation. The mutual implication works as:
- Pitch-class Tonnetz (nodes = pitches, edges = consonant dyads)
- Triadic Tonnetz (nodes = triads, edges = common-tone connections)
- Regional Tonnetz (encapsulated diatonic regions)

These are **geometric duals**: labels of one graph become nodes of the other.

## Common Confusions

- **Nonconforming vs. conforming**: Just-intoned Tonnetz is infinite; equal-tempered Tonnetz wraps into a torus
- **Acoustic vs. metaphorical**: Early Tonnetz calculated frequencies; later Tonnetz represents mental pathways
- **Pitch-class vs. triadic**: Same geometry represents both; they are dual graphs
- **Historical Tonnetz vs. neo-Riemannian**: Riemann subordinated Tonnetz to tonal function; neo-Riemannians treat it as primary
