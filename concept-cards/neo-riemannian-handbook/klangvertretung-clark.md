# Quick Definition

Clark's analytical method of tracking how individual tones change their triadic-functional identity (root, third, or fifth) across chord progressions in Schubert's Lieder, using Riemann's principle of Klangvertretung to reveal structural threads invisible to standard chord-level neo-Riemannian analysis.

# Core Definition

Clark (Ch. 10) demonstrates the analytical utility of Riemann's **Klangvertretung** principle by examining how individual tones shift their triadic-functional identity across chord changes in Schubert's Lieder. The method works as follows: when a tone is sustained or repeated across a harmonic progression, its role within the surrounding triad (root, third, or fifth) changes with each new chord. These shifts correspond to specific neo-Riemannian transformations at the chord level but reveal additional structural information. Clark argues this approach enriches neo-Riemannian analysis by adding a **tone-level layer** to the standard **chord-level** transformational analysis. The approach is grounded in Riemann's late theoretical work ("Ideen," 1914-15), where he proposed that "any tone may project or assume meaning as one of the three elements of a major or minor triad" — the six possible triadic identities per pitch class.

# Key Properties

1. **Tone-level analysis**: Tracks the changing triadic identity of individual pitches, not just chord-to-chord transformations
2. **Six identities per tone**: Each pitch class can be root, third, or fifth of 3 major and 3 minor triads
3. **Common-tone thread**: A sustained or repeated pitch provides structural coherence across remote modulations
4. **Enriched view of third relations**: The method reveals structural features of chromatic third relations invisible at the chord level
5. **Bridges diatonic and chromatic**: Tone-level tracking shows how tones move between diatonic and chromatic frameworks

# Construction / Recognition

To apply Clark's analytical method:
1. Identify a significant pitch that persists or recurs across a passage
2. At each chord change, determine which triad the pitch belongs to
3. Determine the pitch's role within that triad (root, third, or fifth)
4. Chart the sequence of identity shifts: e.g., "third of X -> fifth of Y -> root of Z"
5. Observe which PLR transformations at the chord level correspond to which identity shifts at the tone level
6. Identify patterns, symmetries, or structural threads in the identity sequence

# Context & Application

Clark's method is particularly valuable for analyzing:
- Schubert Lieder with chromatic third relations
- Any passage where a common tone persists across distant modulations
- Music where the same physical pitch acquires different harmonic meanings
- Situations where chord-level analysis alone cannot explain structural coherence

The approach connects to Riemann's "Ideen" (1914-15), where the original figure (example 10.1) showed three pairs of triads (C major/C minor, C major/A minor, C major/E minor) illustrating P, R, L — which Clark reinterprets as illustrations of how tone identities shift, not merely how chords transform.

# Examples

Clark analyzes three Schubert Lieder (Ch. 10), demonstrating how attention to "the changing triadic-functional identities of tones" offers "an enriched view of structural and chromatic third relations in the works" (abstract).

In Riemann's original example 10.1: the tone C appears as root of C major, root of C minor (under P), root of C major and third of A minor (under R), and root of C major and fifth of... The identity shift of each common tone reveals which neo-Riemannian operation is taking place at a more granular level than chord labels alone.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Applying Klangvertretung only at the chord level (as "chord represents function")
  **Correction**: Clark's innovation is applying it at the tone level (individual pitch represents Klang identity), revealing additional structure

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: This is the same as standard common-tone analysis
  **Clarification**: Standard common-tone analysis notes which pitches are shared; Clark's method additionally tracks how the shared pitch's triadic role transforms
