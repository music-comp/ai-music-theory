# Garden of Eden Metaphor

## Quick Definition

Riemann's spatial metaphor for the bounded realm of tonal possibility, within which all harmonic combinations exist as paths through a "magnificent Garden of Eden" that Heaven has left after the Fall.

## Formal Definition

In the closing pages of *Musikalische Syntaxis* (1877), Riemann describes his harmonic theory as mapping a **spatialized realm of tonal order**:

> "Thank God the combinations [of harmonies] are inexhaustible in number, and one cannot explore the area of harmony in its entirety by walking across it step by step but only by flying over it and surveying it from a bird's-eye view. It is sufficient, however, to recognize the chief paths through this magnificent Garden of Eden, which Heaven has left us after the Fall; everybody may then find new side paths for himself leading to ever new perspectives on regions never entered before."

This metaphor reveals both the **promise** and the **anxiety** of Riemann's theoretical project.

## Theological Implications

The invocation of Eden and the Fall carries profound implications:

1. **Harmonic Sin**: Transgression of tonal laws could bring "permanent banishment from the Garden"---banishment into the "atonal wilds"

2. **Boundaries with Peril**: Harmonies on the borders are fraught with danger and temptation

3. **Universality and Contingency**: The Garden appears universal and transhistorical, but the language "implies at the same time a premonition of its actual, contingent nature" (Rehding)

4. **Theory as Defense**: Riemann's system serves to fortify the boundary around tonal possibility

## The "Hauptwege" and "Seitenpfade"

Riemann distinguishes:

- **Hauptwege** (chief paths): The main, well-trodden routes through tonal space
- **Seitenpfade** (side paths): More exotic progressions that lead to "regions never entered before"

Schubert's minor Neapolitan represents one such "side path"---off the beaten track but still **admissible** within the Garden's bounds.

## Anxiety and Containment

Rings identifies an underlying **anxiety** in Riemann's containment project:

- The foreword to the 1917 *Handbuch der Harmonielehre* introduces new symbols specifically to capture Schubert's chord
- This late revision suggests Riemann remained preoccupied with ensuring the chord's "energy was contained within his new system"
- The function label requires **three alterations** to the basic S function, straining the system

## Historical Context

Alexander Rehding interprets this metaphor as reflecting:

> "A Spenglerian feeling that the end of an age---the end of German music---is imminent"

The Garden of Eden represents what **must be protected** from the looming threat of atonality.

## Function Theory as Fortification

Rehding observes that Riemann's **theory of functions** (introduced 1893) represented a way to:

- Control the overly permissive possibilities of earlier theories
- Better fortify the boundary around the Garden
- Contain chromatic extravagances within rational, labeled categories

## Neo-Riemannian Response

Neo-Riemannian theory, arising after atonality had "transgressed" these boundaries long ago, takes a different view:

> "We are lucky in that our historical position allows us, unlike Riemann, to relish those moments in which chromatic works threaten to overflow the rational bounds of our tonal theories"

The boundary-crossing that terrified Riemann becomes analytically **productive** for neo-Riemannians.

## Methodological Significance

The Garden metaphor illustrates how **spatial thinking** underlies harmonic theory:

- Tonal space as bounded territory
- Modulation as movement through that space
- Chromaticism as exploration of boundaries
- Analysis as cartography
