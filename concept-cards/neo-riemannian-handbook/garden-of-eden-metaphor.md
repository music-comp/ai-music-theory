# Quick Definition

Riemann's spatial metaphor for the bounded realm of tonal possibility, within which all harmonic combinations exist as paths through a "magnificent Garden of Eden" that Heaven has left after the Fall -- revealing both the promise and the anxiety of his theoretical project.

# Core Definition

In the closing pages of *Musikalische Syntaxis* (1877), Riemann describes tonal space as a Garden of Eden: "Thank God the combinations [of harmonies] are inexhaustible in number, and one cannot explore the area of harmony in its entirety by walking across it step by step but only by flying over it and surveying it from a bird's-eye view. It is sufficient, however, to recognize the chief paths through this magnificent Garden of Eden, which Heaven has left us after the Fall; everybody may then find new side paths for himself leading to ever new perspectives on regions never entered before" (quoted by Rings, Ch. 18, p. 494).

Rings identifies underlying anxiety in this metaphor: transgression of tonal laws could bring "permanent banishment from the Garden" -- banishment into "atonal wilds."

# Prerequisites

- **Monotonality principle**: The conviction that a single tonic controls an entire work provides the framework the Garden metaphor defends.

# Key Properties

1. **Bounded territory**: Tonal space has limits that define the Garden's edges
2. **Hauptwege and Seitenpfade**: "Chief paths" (conventional progressions) and "side paths" (chromatic experiments)
3. **Theological overtones**: Eden, the Fall, sin -- chromatic transgression threatens banishment
4. **Anxiety beneath universalism**: "The whole theory is built on a feeling of angst" (Rehding, via Rings)

# Construction / Recognition

The metaphor distinguishes: Hauptwege (main paths) = well-trodden tonal routes; Seitenpfade (side paths) = more exotic progressions leading to "regions never entered before." Schubert's minor Neapolitan represents one such "side path" -- off the beaten track but still admissible within the Garden's bounds.

# Context & Application

Alexander Rehding interprets the metaphor as reflecting "a Spenglerian feeling that the end of an age -- the end of German music -- is imminent" (quoted by Rings, p. 495). Riemann's function theory (introduced 1893) served to better fortify the boundary -- to control overly permissive harmonic possibilities. The 1917 Handbuch revision introducing new symbols for Schubert's minor Neapolitan shows Riemann still ensuring "its energy was contained within his new system" (p. 495).

Neo-Riemannian theory responds differently: "We are lucky in that our historical position allows us, unlike Riemann, to relish those moments in which chromatic works threaten to overflow the rational bounds of our tonal theories" (Rings, p. 495).

# Examples

**Schubert's Gb Impromptu, m. 80** (Rings, Ch. 18, pp. 493-495): The minor Neapolitan chord. Riemann's 1877 analysis treats it as unremarkable -- part of "the final consolidation of the primary key." His 1917 analysis requires three modifications to the basic S function to contain it: the Variante of the Leittonwechsel of the minor subdominant. This strain reveals the anxiety of containment.

**Function theory as fortification**: Rehding observes that function theory (1893) represented a way to "control and corral the overly permissive possibilities" of earlier theories, "better fortifying the boundary around the Garden of Eden" (p. 495).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Reading the Garden metaphor as purely positive/celebratory.
  **Correction**: It contains profound anxiety about the contingency and vulnerability of the tonal order.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking Riemann feared atonality as a future possibility.
  **Clarification**: Rehding argues the theory reflects a "premonition -- conscious or not -- of its actual, contingent nature" (p. 495), suggesting Riemann sensed tonal order was historically contingent, not eternal.
