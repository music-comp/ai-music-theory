# Quick Definition

The perspective in which musical events receive their metrical interpretation according to the mechanics of notation — time signatures, bar lines, and notational conventions — rather than from the musical content itself.

# Core Definition

Notated meter, as defined by Caplin, is the perspective that "assumes that musical events are understood to receive their metrical interpretation — that is, which events are deemed metrically accented and which are metrically unaccented — according to the mechanics of notation associated with that theory, such as time signatures and bar lines" (abstract, p. 415). In this view, the first beat after a bar line is accented by convention, and higher-level metric structure is determined by the hierarchical periodic model (Auftakttheorie) rather than by musical content. Caplin identifies this as one of Riemann's "two general perspectives" on meter, operating as an a priori framework into which actual music is fitted.

# Key Properties

1. Metrical accent is determined by notational conventions (bar lines, time signatures)
2. The periodic model (1-2-3-4-5-6-7-8) functions as a "notated meter" for hypermetric levels
3. At higher levels (beyond the measure), notation provides no explicit metric symbols, so the a priori model substitutes
4. Even-numbered measures are designated as strong regardless of musical content
5. The model operates independently of what the listener perceives

# Construction / Recognition

1. Read the time signature and bar line placement
2. Assign accent to the first beat of each measure (conventional metric hierarchy)
3. At higher levels, apply the eight-measure periodic model: odd measures are weak, even measures are strong
4. Designation proceeds from the theoretical model, not from listening

# Context & Application

Caplin uses the notated-meter perspective to explain how Riemann's Auftakttheorie functions as an a priori model. At the beat level, notated meter corresponds to conventional metric theory (bar lines define downbeats). At higher levels (two-measure, four-measure groups), there are no notational symbols equivalent to bar lines, so Riemann's periodic model provides the "notated meter" for these levels. This explains why Riemann can assign metric functions (1-8) to measures even when the musical content suggests a different accentual pattern.

**Critical limitation**: Riemann "never fully acknowledged" the distinction between notated and expressed meter (Caplin), often conflating the two perspectives or invoking one when the other would be more appropriate.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 417): At the measure level, standard notation places a bar line before the accented beat. The time signature specifies how many beats per measure and which beat value receives the primary accent.

**Example 2** (p. 418): At the double-measure level, no notational device exists to mark "strong" vs. "weak" measures. Riemann's periodic model (odd = weak, even = strong) fills this gap, functioning as notated meter for higher hierarchical levels.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming notated meter is always "correct" and expressed meter is secondary
  **Correction**: Both perspectives offer valid analytical lenses; notation may misrepresent the perceptual metric organization

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking notated meter only refers to time signatures and bar lines
  **Clarification**: At higher hierarchical levels, Riemann's periodic model itself functions as "notated meter" — an a priori scheme not derived from musical content
