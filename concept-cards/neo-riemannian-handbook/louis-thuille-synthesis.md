# Quick Definition

The synthesis of Viennese scale-degree theory and Riemannian function theory achieved in Rudolf Louis and Ludwig Thuille's *Harmonielehre* (1907), which absorbed functional principles while retaining Roman numerals, creating the most influential practical harmony textbook of early 20th-century Germany.

# Core Definition

The **Louis/Thuille *Harmonielehre*** (1907) represents the most significant synthesis of Stufentheorie and Funktionstheorie, a work that Riemann himself acknowledged (resentfully) as heavily dependent on his theories while noting it "reproduces in such extenso" his teaching "that one really cannot quite understand why the conveniently abbreviating symbols T for Tonic, D for Dominant, and S for Subdominant were not also employed" (Ch. 1, n. 130).

Louis's foundational methodological principle was empiricist: "For our understanding of harmony, the starting point is the most faithful and exhaustive, unprejudiced analysis of what the musician of our time and our culture actually hears in musical simultaneities and their connections" (Ch. 1, n. 117). This stood against Riemann's "speculative tendency" — Louis criticized Riemann's "unfortunate passion for intellectual construction, that preference for premature generalization and analogizing, that overgrowth of the subjective factor in theorizing" (Ch. 1, n. 122).

Key innovations include:
- **Auffassungsdissonanz** (conceptual dissonance): chords that are acoustically consonant but functionally dissonant — "chords that outside of a musical context always produce consonance, can occasionally be used so that they create a dissonance for harmonic understanding" (Ch. 1, n. 137)
- **Five tonal "genders"**: Pure major, minor-major, pure minor, major-minor, Dorian minor
- **Expanded diatonicism**: "For us moderns, who derive the key not from the scale but from the constituent principal triadic harmonies, the concept of diatonicism itself is vastly expanded" (Ch. 1, n. 153)
- Integration of contrapuntal thinking: voice-leading considerations balance against harmonic function

# Key Properties

1. Retains Roman numeral notation while absorbing three-function logic
2. Rejects strict harmonic dualism; adopts monistic (major-derived) approach
3. Emphasizes empirical listening over speculative construction
4. Integrates contrapuntal/voice-leading explanations alongside harmonic ones
5. The concept of Auffassungsdissonanz bridges acoustic and functional understandings of dissonance
6. Five tonal "genders" expand diatonicism beyond the major/minor binary

# Construction / Recognition

## To Recognize Louis/Thuille Analytical Approach
1. Roman numeral analysis is used (not T/D/S symbols)
2. But the underlying logic is functional (three primary functions govern interpretation)
3. Voice-leading considerations may override purely harmonic explanations
4. Chromatic chords are explained through expanded diatonicism rather than constant modulation
5. Contextual consonance/dissonance is recognized (Auffassungsdissonanz)

# Context & Application

Holtmeier frames the Louis/Thuille *Harmonielehre* as the culmination of his argument about the "middle ground" between Vienna and Leipzig. Louis, taught composition by the Bruckner student Friedrich Klose and influenced by Julius Bahnsen's *Realdialektik*, brought a philosophical pessimism that resisted Riemann's systematic optimism: "harmony theory is not a science... its principles are, in Kantian terms, not constitutive but only regulative" (Ch. 1, n. 124).

The priority dispute between Louis and Riemann is revealing. Riemann claimed the *Harmonielehre* was derivative; Louis countered that Riemann's functional insights were widely shared and not unique to him. The question of authorship is itself complex: while the book bears both names, "by far the most substantial part of the *Harmonielehre* was authored by Rudolf Louis" (Ch. 1, n. 116).

The work's lasting influence: "omnipresent in practice, almost completely absent in music-theoretical literature" (Ch. 1, n. 132).

# Examples

**Example 1** (Ch. 1, n. 141): The second-degree chord analyzed through Auffassungsdissonanz: "The subdominant meaning of the triad on the second degree is most clearly revealed when it appears in first inversion as a sixth chord... the chord acquires a certain resemblance to a sixth chord in which the sixth precedes the subdominant fifth as a suspension."

**Example 2** (Ch. 1, n. 153): Louis's expanded diatonicism: "For us moderns, who derive the key not from the scale but from the constituent principal triadic harmonies, the concept of diatonicism itself is vastly expanded." This allows chromatic chords to belong to the key.

**Example 3** (Ch. 1, pp. 40-42): Louis/Thuille's analysis of Tristan chord-like formations through the concept of "passing chords" — contrapuntal voice-leading produces "chordal progressions" that "resemble independent chords" but are structurally subordinate.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating Louis/Thuille as merely a scale-degree theory text
  **Correction**: Riemann recognized that it "reproduces" his functional teaching; the underlying logic is functional despite the Roman numeral notation

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Attributing the *Harmonielehre* equally to both authors
  **Clarification**: Holtmeier establishes that Louis wrote the theoretical substance; Thuille contributed the musical examples

- **Confusion**: Thinking Louis's critique of Riemann meant rejection of functional principles
  **Clarification**: Louis rejected Riemann's dualism and speculative method, not the three-function framework itself
