# Tetrachordal Structure

## Quick Definition

A four-note scalar fragment spanning the interval of a perfect fourth, serving as a fundamental building block for scale construction in Greek theory and Riemann's evolutionary model of scalar development.

## Formal Definition

A **tetrachord** is a four-note scale segment spanning a perfect fourth (ratio 3:4). In Riemann's *Folkloristische Tonalitätsstudien*, tetrachords represent an intermediate evolutionary stage between pentatonicism and full diatonicism.

**The three tetrachord types** (per Boeckh's nomenclature, adopted by Riemann):

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|------|---------|---------|
| **Dorian** | semitone + whole + whole | E-F-G-A |
| **Phrygian** | whole + semitone + whole | D-E-F-G |
| **Lydian** | whole + whole + semitone | C-D-E-F |

The critical difference lies in the *location of the semitone*.

**Derivation from Pentatonic** (Riemann's theory):
- Tetrachords arise when *pien* tones fill pentatonic "gaps"
- A three-note pentatonic fragment (trichord) + one *pien* = tetrachord
- Different *pien* placements yield different tetrachord types

## Historical Origin

### Greek Theory

Tetrachords were fundamental to Greek scalar theory:
- **Genera**: Diatonic, chromatic, enharmonic
- **Structure**: Two tetrachords + linking tone = complete scale
- **Fixed tones**: Boundary notes of the fourth
- **Movable tones**: Internal divisions

### Riemann's Innovation

Riemann bridged pentatonic and tetrachordal theory by:
1. Using *pien* (Chinese concept) to explain tetrachord formation
2. Showing how the central *pyknon* (three-note whole-tone cluster) expands to tetrachords
3. Applying Greek terminology to non-Greek repertoires

**Riemann's key claim**: Tetrachords are "three-note fragments of pentatonic scales with infixed *pien*."

### Historical Doubt

This explanation is "historically doubtful" according to Gelbart and Rehding, but it allowed Riemann to:
- View all scalar systems as combinations of two principles
- Create a unified prehistory of Western diatonicism
- Apply the same analytical tools to diverse repertoires

## Musical Context

### From Pyknon to Tetrachord

The pentatonic *pyknon* (e.g., D-E-G around *mese* E) can be filled out by adding a *pien*:

```
Pyknon: D - E - G (major third)
Add pien below D:
  C - D - E - G → Lydian tendency (whole-whole-semi to G)
Add pien above G:
  D - E - G - A → beginnings of Dorian (semi-whole-whole from D)
```

### Metric Placement of Pien

Riemann emphasized that whether the *pien* falls on a strong or weak beat determines its structural significance:

- **Weak beat *pien***: Ornamental, doesn't change tonal center
- **Strong beat *pien***: Structural, may induce "modulation" to new *pyknon*

### The Hexachord Connection

Riemann showed the hexachord as:
1. **Tetrachordal view**: Combination of Dorian + Phrygian + Lydian tetrachords
2. **Pentatonic view**: Two overlapping pentatonic scales with *pien*

This demonstrates that tetrachordal and pentatonic principles are complementary, not contradictory.

### Leading Tone Derivation

The *subsemitonium modi* (leading tone) arises from the "minor-third pyknon":
- Original "major-third pyknon": whole + whole (D-E-F#)
- "Minor-third pyknon": semi + whole or whole + semi (D-Eb-F or D-E-F)

When the minor-third pyknon becomes central, the semitone acquires leading-tone function.

## Examples

### Tetrachord Types in Staff Notation

```
Dorian:   E - F - G - A   (1/2 + 1 + 1)
Phrygian: D - E - F - G   (1 + 1/2 + 1)
Lydian:   C - D - E - F   (1 + 1 + 1/2)
```

### Deriving Tetrachords from Trichords

From *Folkloristische Tonalitätsstudien*, p. 38:

Starting trichord (E-G-A, with *mese* = A):
- Add III♭ (F): E-F-G-A = Dorian tetrachord
- Add III♮ (F#): E-F#-G-A = Phrygian tendency

### Scale Construction

A complete diatonic scale combines two tetrachords:
- **Dorian mode**: Dorian + Dorian (E-F-G-A + B-C-D-E)
- **Phrygian mode**: Phrygian + Phrygian (D-E-F-G + A-B-C-D)
- **Lydian mode**: Lydian + Lydian (C-D-E-F + G-A-B-C)

### Hexachord as Bridge

```
C - D - E - F - G - A
|_Lydian_|
    |_Phrygian_|
        |_Dorian_|
```

Or as overlapping pentatonics:
```
Pentatonic 1 (mese G): D - E - G - A - (C)
Pentatonic 2 (mese D): (A) - C - D - F - G
With pien B and F filling gaps
```

## Evolution/Variants

### Riemann's Scalar Evolution

1. **Anhemitonic pentatonic**: No semitones; major-third pyknon central
2. **Pentatonic with *pien***: Ornamental half-steps
3. **Tetrachordal organization**: Structural half-steps; semitone location determines mode
4. **Minor-third pyknon**: Recognition of third as harmonic element
5. **Full diatonicism**: Leading tone function established

### Tetrachords in Modern Theory

| Ancient/Medieval | Modern Function |
|------------------|-----------------|
| Tetrachord = building block | Scale = primary unit |
| Modal species by tetrachord type | Modal species by scale pattern |
| Genus (diatonic/chromatic/enharmonic) | Scale type (major/minor/etc.) |

### Riemann's Analytical Use

Riemann applied tetrachordal analysis to:
- Gregorian chant
- Celtic folk songs
- Ancient Greek reconstructions
- Scottish/Irish melodies

This synchronic approach ignored cultural specificity in favor of structural universals.

## Common Confusions

1. **Tetrachord naming**: Riemann (following Boeckh) names tetrachords differently than some other systems; the tetrachord names describe the *internal structure*, not the scale starting on that note
2. **Tetrachord vs. Mode**: A tetrachord is a four-note segment; a mode is typically an octave species built from tetrachords
3. **Historical accuracy**: Riemann's derivation of tetrachords from pentatonic trichords is structural/heuristic, not historically verified
4. **Pien universality**: Applying Chinese *pien* concept to Greek/Celtic music is Riemann's theoretical move, not a historical claim
5. **Symmetry**: Unlike pentatonic structures, tetrachords in Riemann's system lack inherent symmetry - this marks a departure from his usual dualistic preferences
