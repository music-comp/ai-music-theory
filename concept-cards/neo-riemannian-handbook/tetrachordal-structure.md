# Quick Definition

A four-note scalar fragment spanning a perfect fourth, serving as the intermediate building block between pentatonicism and diatonicism. Riemann derived three types (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian) from pentatonic trichords by adding *pien* tones.

# Core Definition

A **tetrachord** is a four-note scale segment spanning a perfect fourth. In *Folkloristische Tonalitätsstudien*, Riemann posited tetrachords as an intermediate evolutionary stage between pentatonicism and full diatonicism, derived when *pien* tones fill the gaps in pentatonic trichords.

Following Boeckh's nomenclature, Riemann identified three tetrachord types by semitone position (Ch. 4, pp. 153-154):

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|------|---------|---------|
| Dorian | semitone + whole + whole | E-F-G-A |
| Phrygian | whole + semitone + whole | D-E-F-G |
| Lydian | whole + whole + semitone | C-D-E-F |

Riemann's key theoretical claim: "Tetrachords are three-note fragments of pentatonic scales with infixed *pien*" (Ch. 4, abstract). A pentatonic trichord (e.g., D-E-G) can become a tetrachord by adding a *pien* tone in the minor-third gap. Which *pien* is added determines the tetrachord type.

This explanation is "historically doubtful" (Ch. 4, p. 154), but it allowed Riemann to "look at all subsequent scalar systems — hexachords and diatonic scales as combinations of these two principles" (Ch. 4, abstract).

# Prerequisites

- **pentatonicism** — Tetrachords derive from pentatonic trichords in Riemann's theory
- **pien** — The "filling tones" that convert trichords to tetrachords

# Key Properties

1. Spans a perfect fourth (ratio 3:4)
2. Contains exactly four notes with three internal intervals
3. The critical difference between types is semitone placement
4. Each diatonic mode can be built from two identical tetrachords
5. Dorian: semitone at bottom; Phrygian: semitone in middle; Lydian: semitone at top
6. Unlike pentatonic structures, tetrachords lack inherent symmetry — a departure from Riemann's usual dualistic preferences
7. Metric positioning of the *pien* determines whether it is ornamental or structural

# Construction / Recognition

## Deriving Tetrachords from Pentatonic Trichords
1. Start with a pentatonic trichord spanning a fourth (e.g., D-E-G around *mese* A)
2. Add *pien* below: F → D-E-F-G = Phrygian tetrachord
3. Add *pien* above (natural): F# → D-E-F#-G = tendency toward Dorian
4. Different *pien* placements yield different tetrachord types

## The Hexachord as Synthesis
1. The hexachord C-D-E-F-G-A can be analyzed as three overlapping tetrachords:
   - Lydian: C-D-E-F
   - Phrygian: D-E-F-G
   - Dorian: E-F-G-A
2. Or as two overlapping pentatonic scales with appropriate *pien* tones
3. "Here again we have another bridge from the melody of primordial times to the rarefied system of the middle ages!" (Ch. 4, p. 155)

# Context & Application

Riemann's treatment of tetrachords is significant for several reasons. First, the terms *mese* and *pyknon* are drawn from Greek tetrachordal theory, so applying them to pentatonic structures implies "an integral connection between those two disparate structural models — and as we shall see, this point greatly mattered to Riemann" (Ch. 4, p. 148).

Second, the metric placement of the *pien* within the tetrachord is analytically crucial: a *pien* on a weak beat remains ornamental and preserves the original tonal center, while a *pien* on a strong beat becomes structural and may induce a "quasi-modulatory shift to a fifth-related pyknon" (Ch. 4, pp. 154-155). This mechanism explains how tonal centers shift even in monophonic music.

Third, the tetrachordal analysis enables Riemann to derive the *subsemitonium modi* (leading tone) in minor — a critical analytical problem since the leading tone must be figured as an alteration of the natural scale (Ch. 4, pp. 156-157).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Confusing tetrachord naming (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian) with the full-octave modes
  **Correction**: Riemann (following Boeckh) applies these names to four-note segments, not octave species. The tetrachord name describes the internal interval structure only.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking Riemann's derivation of tetrachords from pentatonic trichords is historically verified
  **Clarification**: This derivation is "historically doubtful" (Ch. 4, p. 154); it is a structural/heuristic model, not a historical claim

- **Confusion**: Believing the three tetrachord names were traditional
  **Clarification**: Riemann acknowledged "there is no historical precedent for calling the tetrachords (as opposed to the whole modes) by these names" but argued for them based on "rigorous and systematic clarity" (Ch. 4, p. 153)
