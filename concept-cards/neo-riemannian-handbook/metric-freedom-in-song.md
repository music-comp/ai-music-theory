# Quick Definition

Metric freedom in song refers to passages where the perceived metric organization diverges from the notated meter, a phenomenon Riemann identifies in Brahms's late songs and traces historically to Caccini's seventeenth-century monodies.

# Core Definition

In "Die Taktfreiheiten in Brahms' Liedern" (1912), Riemann identifies metric freedoms as passages where the underlying musical logic — evidenced by harmonic rhythm, text declamation, and melodic structure — implies a metric organization different from the notated time signature and barlines. Riemann insists that "all of the freer forms are to be measured, today as in all times, according to the standard of the strictly regular; they obtain their individual quality and their individual effect through their deviation from the norm" (Berry, p. 464). The freedoms are not lawlessness but departures from an implied regular structure (Berry, pp. 463-464).

# Prerequisites

- **Expressed meter** — Metric freedom presupposes a distinction between notated and perceived meter
- **Normative analysis** — Freedoms are identified as deviations from Riemann's eight-measure periodic norm
- **Phrasing theory** — Riemann's approach to phrase structure provides the normative framework against which freedoms are measured

# Key Properties

1. Notated barlines may conceal the true metric organization of vocal music
2. Evidence for the "true" meter comes from harmonic motion, text accent, and melodic contour
3. Metric freedoms are measured against a normative periodic structure (powers of 2)
4. The phenomenon spans centuries: Riemann draws parallels between Caccini (c. 1600) and Brahms (c. 1886)
5. Brahms's allabreve mark and other notational details provide clues to the intended metric hearing
6. Changes between duple and triple meter may occur within a single song without being notated

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Metric Freedom in a Song

1. Note the notated meter and barline positions
2. Identify the harmonic rhythm — do chord changes align with notated strong beats?
3. Examine text accent — do verbal stresses coincide with notated downbeats?
4. Look for melodic groupings that span notated barlines
5. Consider whether re-barring the passage (possibly with a different time signature) produces a more natural reading
6. Compare strophes to see if the same passage receives different metric treatment

# Context & Application

Riemann's essay occupies an unusual position in his output: it applies his systematic metric theory to vocal music, a repertoire he generally considered inferior to absolute music. The essay is "the most detailed and penetrating music-analytic study that Hugo Riemann devoted exclusively to the music of Johannes Brahms" (Berry, p. 474). Berry notes that Riemann's approach yields a "fascinating but highly abstract" account, in which passages from a single song are reconceived in multiple meters "without stretching the concept of meter beyond the limits of audibility" (p. 476).

# Examples

**Brahms, "Immer leiser," Op. 105/2** (pp. 465-468): The opening melody takes three notated measures per line despite the eight-syllable verse. Riemann re-bars the passage, showing how the harmonic stasis across multiple notated measures suggests a different underlying meter. The consequent of the second period involves "bold harmonic progression based on the succession of multiple motions by minor third, a sequential structure of the most daring kind" (p. 467).

**Brahms, "Das Madchen spricht," Op. 107/3** (pp. 469-472): The prelude's meter "is deeply problematic" despite its "charming" character. The antecedent of the first period adheres to one meter while "the consequent flits quite clearly over" to another "in virtue of its motivic construction" (p. 470). Riemann's re-barring reveals "the structural simplicity" concealed by the notation.

**Historical parallel: Caccini** (p. 463): Caccini "introduced the sophisticated innovation" of "scrupulous responsiveness to the accentual demands and expressive meaning of individual words" but notated "almost all of his melodies entirely without metric shifts," leading later critics to misunderstand his declamation.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Re-barring passages without harmonic or textual evidence
  **Correction**: Riemann uses multiple converging criteria (harmony, text, melody) to justify re-barring

- **Error**: Concluding that Brahms's measures "are not proper measures" without further investigation
  **Correction**: Riemann insists one should "attempt to explore thoroughly what lies hidden beneath the misleading notation" (p. 472)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing metric freedom means absence of meter
  **Clarification**: Riemann argues the opposite — "the downright abolishment of normative concepts is of no use to anyone" (p. 464); freedoms are meaningful only in relation to norms

- **Confusion**: Assuming Riemann's re-barring is purely speculative
  **Clarification**: Berry shows that Brahms's own annotations of Lingg's poem text demonstrate a "process of normative structural comparison no less deliberate than Riemann's" (p. 479)
