# Quick Definition

The four basic symmetry operations implicit in Rameau's and Weber's theories of tonal harmony -- reordering, octave shift, note duplication, and transposition -- to which Riemann attempted to add a fifth: inversion.

# Core Definition

Tymoczko identifies four symmetry operations that Rameau's and Weber's theoretical innovations implicitly embody (Ch. 8, Section 1):

1. **Reordering**: Permuting the order of notes in a chord (C-E-G = E-G-C = G-C-E)
2. **Octave shift**: Moving individual notes between octaves without changing chord identity
3. **Note duplication**: Adding octave doublings without changing chord identity
4. **Transposition**: Shifting all notes by the same interval (I-V in C = I-V in G)

These operations define what "stays the same" in traditional harmonic analysis. Roman numerals are invariant under transposition; chord labels ignore voicing (octave symmetry, reordering); figured bass ignores doublings (note duplication).

Riemann's dualism attempted to add **inversion** as a fifth symmetry: his terminology (Schritte, Wechsel) was designed to be invariant under both transposition and inversion.

# Key Properties

1. Operations 1-3 are "individual" symmetries: applicable independently to each chord
2. Operation 4 (transposition) is a "uniform" symmetry: must be applied identically to all chords
3. Riemann's proposed operation 5 (inversion) is also uniform
4. Traditional tonal theory embodies operations 1-4 without Riemann's addition
5. The crucial question is whether operation 5 (inversion) legitimately describes tonal music

# Construction / Recognition

## Individual Symmetries (Apply per-chord)
- Reordering: C major = {C, E, G} regardless of vertical arrangement
- Octave shift: C4-E4-G4 and C3-E5-G4 are "the same chord"
- Note duplication: C-E-G and C-E-G-C are "the same chord"

## Uniform Symmetries (Apply to entire progression)
- Transposition: I-IV-V-I in C major and I-IV-V-I in G major are "the same progression"
- Inversion (Riemann's addition): Major T→D and Minor T→D are "the same progression"

## The Test
- Transposing C-F-G-C by fifth gives G-C-D-G: preserves the progression (I-IV-V-I)
- Transposing each chord by DIFFERENT amounts destroys the progression
- Similarly, inversion must be applied uniformly to preserve relationships

# Context & Application

The Rameau-Weber symmetries show what traditional theory takes for granted: that chord identity survives reordering, octave displacement, and doubling, and that harmonic patterns survive transposition. Weber's Roman numeral notation "develops and fulfills Rameau's ideas" by making transpositional invariance explicit.

Understanding these pre-existing symmetries clarifies what Riemann attempted: to extend the collection by one more symmetry. The question is whether this extension is justified by musical practice.

# Examples

**Roman numerals as transposition invariance**: I-V-I describes "the same" progression in every key. This shows transposition is already an implicit symmetry in standard tonal theory.

**Chord labels as individual symmetries**: "C major chord" ignores whether it is in root position, first inversion, or second inversion (reordering); whether it spans one octave or three (octave shift); and whether C is doubled (note duplication).

**Riemann's addition**: Gegenquintschritt describes "the same" transformation whether applied to C major → F major or C minor → G minor. This makes the label invariant under inversion.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating the four Rameau-Weber symmetries as explicit theoretical choices
  **Correction**: They are implicit in standard theoretical notation; Tymoczko makes them explicit

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: All five symmetries (including inversion) apply to tonal music
  **Clarification**: Operations 1-4 are well-supported by tonal practice; operation 5 (inversion) is not -- tonal syntax is not inversionally symmetric
