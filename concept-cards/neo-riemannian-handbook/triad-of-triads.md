# Quick Definition

Hauptmann's model of tonal structure as a three-stage dialectical process: the tonic (thesis) is opposed by its dominant and subdominant functions (antithesis), and synthesis is achieved when the tonic is reinstated as the central mediating element between both.

# Core Definition

The **triad of triads** extends Hauptmann's dialectical thinking from the individual triad to the tonal system. Just as the triad consists of three tones in a specific relationship, tonality consists of three functional triads (T, D, S) in a dialectical relationship (Ch. 7, pp. 224-226):

- **Stage I (Thesis)**: Unmediated tonic -- the key is stated without challenge
- **Stage II (Antithesis)**: The tonic "splits up within itself" -- is it the dominant of its subdominant, or the subdominant of its dominant? The opposition creates ambiguity
- **Stage III (Synthesis)**: The tonic is reinstated as center, simultaneously "being" dominant (to subdominant) and "having" a dominant -- its centrality is confirmed

Hauptmann's diatonic chain of thirds illustrates the spatial arrangement: F-A-C-E-G-B-D, with the tonic (C major) at center, subdominant side to the left, dominant side to the right.

# Prerequisites

- **Harmonic dualism** -- The triad of triads operates within a dualist framework

# Key Properties

1. Three stages: thesis (tonic alone), antithesis (opposition with D and S), synthesis (tonic as center)
2. The tonic's centrality is only confirmed when BOTH sides (D and S) have been presented
3. The chain of thirds F-A-C-E-G-B-D maps the diatonic system symmetrically around the tonic
4. Adjacent triads share two common tones (Klang-representation)
5. Hauptmann ducked the question of a "triad of triads" for the minor system, retaining major dominant

# Construction / Recognition

## For Major (C major)
1. Tonic: C-E-G (center)
2. Dominant side: G-B-D (right)
3. Subdominant side: F-A-C (left)
4. Synthesis: C is confirmed as center when both G major and F major have been heard

## For Minor (Rehding's Extension)
1. Tonic: °a (A minor, with E as dualist prime)
2. Dominant: °e (E minor) or A+ (major dominant in mixed minor)
3. Subdominant: °d (D minor)
4. Hauptmann was more cautious here: he retained the major dominant, making pure minor's triad of triads incomplete

## Formal Application
- Exposition = Stage I→II (tonic challenged by dominant-side motion)
- Recapitulation/Coda = completing Stage II→III (subdominant-side motion restores centrality)

# Context & Application

Rehding uses the triad of triads as the theoretical basis for reconstructing a dualistic theory of form. The model explains why the coda -- often featuring subdominant emphasis -- is structurally significant in dualist hearing: it completes the dialectical synthesis that the recapitulation alone cannot achieve.

The model also explains why Riemann considered the major subdominant "impossible" in the minor mode: a major chord built on the subdominant of minor would have its dualist root more than one fifth away from the tonic, exceeding the first degree of relationship.

# Examples

**The chain of thirds** (p. 224):
```
F - A - C - E - G - B - D
IV       I       V
Left     Center  Right
```

Each adjacent pair shares two tones. The tonic sits at center; moving right = dominant territory, moving left = subdominant territory.

**Stage II ambiguity**: After G major (dominant) appears, is C major the tonic or merely the subdominant of G? Only when F major (subdominant) also appears is C's centrality confirmed.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Applying the triad of triads literally as a prescription for key areas in a piece
  **Correction**: It is an abstract model of tonal relationships, not a form-template

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: The triad of triads is about three literal chords
  **Clarification**: It is about three functional relationships (T, D, S) in a dialectical process; the "triad" metaphor applies to the three-element structure
