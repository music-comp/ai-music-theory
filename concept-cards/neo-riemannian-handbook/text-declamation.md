# Quick Definition

Text declamation is the alignment of verbal stress patterns with musical accent in vocal music, which Riemann uses both as a criterion for evaluating songs and as evidence for determining the "true" metric organization underlying Brahms's notated scores.

# Core Definition

In Riemann's essay "Metric Freedoms in Brahms's Songs" (1912), text declamation (the placement of verbal accents relative to musical accent) becomes a central analytical tool. Riemann counters accusations of "poor declamation" against Brahms by arguing that what appears as faulty text-setting is actually precise notation of rhythmic flexibility, analogous to Caccini's monodic innovations: "scrupulous responsiveness to the accentual demands and expressive meaning of individual words" (Berry, p. 463). The apparent conflict between verbal accent and notated meter reveals that the notated barlines conceal a different, "true" metric organization that better serves the text (Berry, pp. 463-464).

# Prerequisites

- **Expressed meter** — Understanding the difference between notated and perceived meter is essential, since Riemann argues the "true" meter of Brahms's songs often differs from what is notated
- **Normative analysis** — Riemann's declamation argument depends on measuring deviations from regular metric patterns

# Key Properties

1. Verbal accent, musical accent, and notated accent are three distinct phenomena that may or may not align
2. Apparent declamation problems may indicate the analyst's failure to perceive the true metric organization, not compositional error
3. Riemann uses declamation as evidence for re-barring passages according to their expressed rather than notated meter
4. Historical parallels (Caccini's monody) show that sophisticated text-setting has been misunderstood across centuries
5. Brahms's notation sometimes expresses ritardando through longer note values rather than performance indications

# Construction / Recognition

## To Analyze Text Declamation in a Brahms Song

1. Identify the natural verbal stress pattern of the poem
2. Compare verbal stresses against notated barline placement
3. Look for harmonic evidence of the "true" metric organization (e.g., static harmonies spanning multiple notated measures)
4. Consider whether re-barring the passage would better align verbal and musical accent
5. In modified strophic songs, compare the declamation across strophes to identify text-motivated deviations

# Context & Application

Declamation issues in Brahms's songs became a live critical debate in the years surrounding the composer's death. Hermann Levi attacked the declamation of five Brahms songs in letters to Julius Stockhausen (1899), and partisans of Hugo Wolf joined in criticizing Brahms's text-setting (Berry, p. 478). Riemann's essay was designed to counteract such negative assessments, though Berry notes it "actually recapitulates the problem of scansion in Brahms's songs rather than addressing it head on" (p. 478).

# Examples

**Brahms, "Immer leiser wird mein Schlummer," Op. 105/2** (pp. 465-466): The opening two melodic lines each take up three notated measures despite eight-syllable normal verse. Riemann argues the harmonic motion projects "a single C-sharp-minor chord that remains undisturbed until the line-ending rhyme," so the true structure is not what the barlines indicate. "Whoever is not looking at the score will hear only the two identically constructed melodic lines."

**Second stanza variation** (p. 467): "The words 'Ja, ich werde sterben mussen' are transferred to the higher register of the second line of the first strophe" because "the intensification of the second line would substitute a jealous emotion in place of poignant resignation." The words "Wenn ich bleich und kalt" are displaced by a half note so that "the primary accent falls on 'bleich,' not on 'kalt'" — declamation follows expressive intention.

**Brahms, "Das Madchen spricht," Op. 107/3** (p. 470): "The verbal stresses reveal that the barlines may not entirely fulfill their obligations." Riemann demonstrates that the harmonic motion is the deciding factor in determining the true metric organization.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Judging declamation solely by notated barlines
  **Correction**: Consider harmonic motion, melodic structure, and verbal accent as evidence for the "true" metric organization

- **Error**: Assuming all syllable-barline misalignments are compositional errors
  **Correction**: Brahms often notates performance-like rhythmic flexibility (ritardando via longer note values, etc.) that creates deliberate misalignment

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing that "poor declamation" in Brahms reflects carelessness
  **Clarification**: Riemann and Berry argue it reflects sophisticated text-setting that requires analytical effort to understand, paralleling how Caccini's monodies were similarly misunderstood

- **Confusion**: Thinking verbal accent and musical accent must always coincide
  **Clarification**: Expressive text-setting may deliberately displace verbal accent for interpretive purposes, as when Brahms emphasizes "bleich" over "kalt"
