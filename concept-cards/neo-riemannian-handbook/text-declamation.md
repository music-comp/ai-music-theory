# Text Declamation

## Quick Definition

The alignment of verbal stress patterns with musical accent in vocal music, which in Brahms's songs often involves complex relationships between poetic meter, notated music meter, and expressed musical accent—relationships that superficial analysis may misinterpret as "poor declamation."

## Formal Definition

**Text declamation** concerns how the natural stress patterns of words and poetic lines align with musical accentuation. In the context of Riemann's analysis of Brahms, declamation becomes a criterion for both evaluating songs AND for determining the "true" metric organization.

**The Problem**: Critics accused Brahms of "frequently making errors in declamation." Riemann counters:
> "This is certainly a severe allegation against the master whom universal opinion considers the greatest proponent of the *Lied* after Schumann."

**Riemann's Defense**: What appears as poor declamation is actually:
1. Precise notation of performance-like rhythmic flexibility
2. Sophisticated metric organization that differs from notated meter
3. Text-sensitive responsiveness that prioritizes expressive meaning over mechanical stress

**Historical Parallel**: Caccini's "sophisticated innovation"—"scrupulous responsiveness to the accentual demands and expressive meaning of individual words"—was similarly misunderstood by later critics who no longer understood his intentions.

**Analytical Method**: Rather than judging declamation by notated bar lines, one should:
1. Identify where verbal accents fall
2. Determine the expressed meter from multiple criteria
3. Recognize that proper declamation may conflict with notated meter

## Musical Context

Declamation issues in Brahms's songs reflect broader tensions in Romantic *Lieder*:

- **Strophic vs. through-composed**: Strophic songs may sacrifice textual nuance; through-composed songs can respond to each word
- **Modified strophic**: Brahms's preferred form allows text-specific variation within strophic framework
- **Performance indication vs. notation**: Brahms often notates what might elsewhere be indicated as *ritardando*, etc.

**Compositional Priorities**: Brahms considers it his "overriding objective to do justice to the poem in fullest measure"—apparent declamation problems may reflect this deeper fidelity to textual expression.

## Examples

### Basic

**Apparent vs. actual declamation**:
```
Text:        IM-mer  LEI-ser   wird mein SCHLUM-mer
Notated:     |  x     X    x   |  x    X      x   |
Expressed:   x  X     x    X      x    X      X
```
Verbal stress on "Immer," "leiser," "Schlummer"
Notated bar lines may place different syllables on strong beats
Expressed meter (via Riemann's analysis) may better align with verbal accent

**Displacement serving expression**:
Brahms shifts "bleich" to receive primary accent rather than "kalt" ("Wenn ich bleich und kalt")—the expressive weight on "pale" rather than "cold" serves poetic interpretation.

### From Repertoire

**Brahms, "Immer leiser," Op. 105/2**: The harmonic motion places "a new chord in conjunction with the first barline" which gives certain words "almost too much accentuation." The smooth stepwise motion actually projects a single harmony that "remains undisturbed until the line-ending rhyme."

**Second stanza variation**: "Ja, ich werde sterben mussen" transfers to the higher register because "the intensification of the second line would substitute a jealous emotion in place of poignant resignation." Declamation follows expressive intention.

**Brahms, "Das Madchen spricht," Op. 107/3**: "The verbal stresses reveal that the barlines may not entirely fulfill their obligations." Riemann demonstrates that harmonically and textually motivated re-barring produces better alignment of verbal and musical accent.

**Reception history**: Hermann Levi attacked declamation in five Brahms songs including "Wie bist du meine Konigin" in letters to Julius Stockhausen (1899). Such criticisms motivated Riemann's defense.

## Common Confusions

- "Poor declamation" may reflect analytical failure, not compositional error
- Notated meter and expressed meter can legitimately differ
- Brahms's sophisticated text-setting can look like mistakes to superficial analysis
- Historical distance affects our ability to perceive intended declamation (as with Caccini)
- Verbal accent, musical accent, and notated accent are three distinct phenomena
- Strophic and modified-strophic forms create different declamation challenges
- Expert singers may intuitively project correct declamation despite misleading notation
