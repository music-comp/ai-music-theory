# Quick Definition

The conceptual transformation of the Tonnetz from Oettingen's static multiplication table for calculating acoustic frequencies into Riemann's dynamic space where pathways between tones carry meaning independent of absolute pitch values.

# Core Definition

The **traversable landscape** is Gollin's characterization of Riemann's mature understanding of the Tonnetz as a dynamic navigable space, in contrast to Oettingen's original static multiplication table. This fundamental epistemological shift involved moving from an "acoustical" to a "metaphorical" conception: where Oettingen's table entries were calculation results determined by their row and column indices (exponents in 5^m x 3^n), Riemann's landscape treats entries as waypoints whose meaning derives from the paths connecting them. Gollin argues that "the transition from a literal to a metaphorical understanding of the Tonnetz did not simply mirror Riemann's shift from an acoustical to a psychological view of the foundation of harmony, but rather made possible the transition, providing Riemann a mechanism to mediate between the phenomenal world of musical practice and the unbounded noumenal realm of musical meanings" (Ch. 9, abstract).

# Prerequisites

- **Tonnetz**: The spatial framework that underwent this conceptual transformation
- **Oettingen's acoustical matrix**: The static starting point from which the traversable landscape developed

# Key Properties

1. **Paths carry meaning**: Tone identity derives from the pathway of derivation, not from absolute frequency
2. **Internal relations primary**: The relationships between entries are the primary content, rather than external row/column indices
3. **Navigation metaphor**: Composers and listeners "traverse" the space; harmonic motion is spatial movement
4. **Distance is meaningful**: Verwandtschaftsgrad (degree of relatedness) measures path length
5. **Mediating function**: The landscape mediates between phenomenal sound and noumenal musical meaning

# Construction / Recognition

Gollin traces five historical stages of this transformation (Ch. 9):

1. **Oettingen's table (1866)**: Static frequency calculator; entries are calculation results
2. **Riemann's adoption (1873-77)**: Inherited acoustic function but began noting geometric patterns (triangular triads)
3. **Drobisch notation (1880s)**: Adoption of Q and T as path symbols; shift from ratio-based to step-based thinking
4. **Path combinatorics (1890s)**: Introduction of Verwandtschaftsgrad; distance measured in path steps, not frequency ratios
5. **Psychological foundation (1914-15)**: Complete shift in "Ideen"; the Tonnetz mediates between phenomenal and noumenal

# Context & Application

Understanding this transformation is central to Gollin's argument in Chapter 9 and to grasping why neo-Riemannian theory works the way it does. The shift from matrix to landscape enabled: transformational thinking (Lewin), graph-theoretic models (Douthett, Steinbach), and geometric voice-leading theory (Tymoczko). It also explains the increasingly spatial language in Riemannian theory: harmonic motion as "steps" (Schritte), "degrees of relationship" (Verwandtschaftsgrad), and modulation as "wandering" through tonal space.

# Examples

Gollin's central example (Ch. 9): Riemann increasingly used Drobisch's symbolic notation where Q represents a fifth step and T represents a third step. In this notation, C to D can be written as Q^2 (two fifths), making the path the primary descriptor rather than the frequency ratio. The same physical pitch D can have different meanings depending on whether it was reached as Q^2 (double fifth from C) or as TQ^-1 (up a third then down a fifth), even though in equal temperament they sound identical.

Riemann's quote from "Ideen" (1914-15): Tones "assume distinct meanings based on their derivation as pathways" on the Tonnetz. Enharmonic identification becomes "absolutely indispensable for our hearing of music" — the landscape mediates between the tempered sounds we hear and the unbounded tonal meanings we imagine.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming Riemann simply replaced acoustics with psychology overnight
  **Correction**: The transition occurred gradually over several decades (1870s-1910s) through multiple intermediate stages, with acoustic and spatial-metaphorical aspects coexisting for extended periods

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: The matrix and landscape views are incompatible
  **Clarification**: Both views preserve the same geometric relationships; only their interpretation differs. The matrix model remains valid for historical study and microtonal theory.

- **Confusion**: The traversable landscape is a neo-Riemannian innovation
  **Clarification**: It was Riemann's own mature conception (by the 1890s-1910s); neo-Riemannian theorists inherited and extended it
