# Leittonwechsel / Leading-Tone Change (L)

## Quick Definition

A neo-Riemannian transformation connecting a major triad to a minor triad whose root is a major third higher, achieved by moving the root by semitone while holding the third and fifth fixed - the transformation that preserves the most "leading-tone" character.

## Formal Definition

The **Leittonwechsel (L)** is one of the three fundamental neo-Riemannian operations (alongside P and R). It maps:

- C major (C-E-G) <-> E minor (E-G-B)
- Any major triad <-> the minor triad whose root is a major third above

**Voice-leading specification**:
- From C major to E minor: C moves up to B (semitone)
- Third and fifth are preserved: E and G remain
- Two common tones, one semitone motion

**In Riemann's terminology**: *Leittonwechsel* means "leading-tone exchange" - the root steps down to become the leading tone of the new chord (or vice versa).

**Formal properties**:
- L is an involution: L(L(X)) = X
- L preserves two common tones
- L involves semitonal motion (1 semitone) - "maximally smooth"

## Historical Context

**Riemann's Conception**:
For Riemann, the Leittonwechsel represented a characteristic chromatic relationship:
- The root of a major chord descends by semitone to become the leading tone
- In dualist terms: the prime of a major Klang exchanges with the leading tone of the related minor Klang

**Functional Designation**:
Riemann labeled L-related chords as *Leittonwechselklange*:
- *Tl* = Tonic Leittonwechsel (E minor relative to C major)
- *Sl* = Subdominant Leittonwechsel
- *Dl* = Dominant Leittonwechsel

These are another type of *Scheinkonsonanz* (apparent consonance) - chords sharing two tones with a primary function.

**The "Terzschritt" Connection**:
L is equivalent to Riemann's major-third *Schritt*:
- C+ to e° is *Terzschritt* (third-step)
- Combined with P, it produces the compound LP = *Gegenterzschritt*

## Theoretical Framework

**Position in PLR Group**:

| Operation | Common Tones | Moving Voice | Distance |
|-----------|-------------|--------------|----------|
| P | 2 (root, fifth) | Third | 1 semitone |
| L | 2 (third, fifth) | Root | 1 semitone |
| R | 2 (root, third) | Fifth | 2 semitones |

L and P are both "maximally smooth" - they involve only semitone motion.

**Major-Third Relations**:
L connects triads whose roots are a major third apart:
- C major <-> E minor (roots: C and E, a major third)
- This is central to chromatic third-relations

The combined LP or PL produces pure major-third motion between same-quality triads:
- C major -> C minor -> Ab major (LP)
- C major -> E minor -> E major (PL)

## Musical Context

**The "LP" Transformation** (Tymoczko/Lewin):
The compound LP is particularly significant:
- C major -> C minor -> Ab major
- Moves major third down while holding one common tone
- Wagner's "Tarnhelm" uses this pattern

Lewin's analysis: The voice leading from G# minor to E minor (in Tarnhelm) is "LP" - Leittonwechsel followed by Parallel.

**In Brahms's Clarinet Trio** (Rehding):
The relationship between C major (second subject) and E minor (closing theme) can be understood as L:
- C major shares E and G with E minor
- The "dual function" of the second subject: Tp to tonic AND L to closing theme (Dl)

**Chromatic vs. Diatonic L**:
Unlike R, L is inherently chromatic in most contexts:
- C major and E minor share different key signatures
- L creates chromatic third-relations characteristic of 19th-century music

### Compound Transformations
- **LP**: C major -> C minor -> Ab major (major third down, same quality)
- **PL**: C major -> E minor -> E major (major third up, same quality)
- **LPL**: C major -> E minor -> E major -> G# minor (tritone relation)

## Common Confusions

1. **L vs. "relative"**: L connects C major to E minor, not A minor (that's R). The relationship involves major-third root motion.

2. **Leading-tone direction**: The name refers to the semitone motion, which acts like a leading tone whether ascending or descending.

3. **LP vs. PL**: These are different compound transformations:
   - LP: major -> parallel minor -> L of that minor = major third DOWN
   - PL: major -> L of that major -> parallel = major third UP

4. **L in different contexts**: L always produces a minor-third relation between chord roots when applied to major, or a major-third relation when applied to minor.

## Significance for Analysis

L is essential for understanding:
- **Hexatonic cycles**: PLPLPL or LPLPLP creates complete hexatonic system
- **Chromatic third relations**: Characteristic of Romantic harmony
- **"Maximally smooth" voice leading**: L uses only semitone motion
- **Wagner and Liszt**: Frequent use of L and compound L-transformations

The major-third relations enabled by L are among the most characteristic sounds of 19th-century chromatic harmony.
