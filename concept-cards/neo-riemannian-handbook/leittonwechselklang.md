# Quick Definition

A subsidiary chord derived from a primary function by replacing its root with the leading tone (semitone below for major, semitone above for minor), sharing two common tones with the primary Klang. In C major, E minor (Tl) is the Leittonwechselklang of the tonic.

# Core Definition

The **Leittonwechselklang** ("leading-tone change chord") is one of three principal tone-relations connecting subsidiary chords to primary functions. It is derived by replacing the root (Grundton) of a major Klang with the leading tone below it, or the root of a minor Klang with the tone a semitone above.

From Riemann's *Musik-Lexikon* entry on "Dissonanz" (cited in Ch. 3, n. 43): "The leading tone (for major, from below; for minor, from above) replacing the root yields the Leittonwechselklang, likewise qualified for representation and providing even greater stimulus to harmonic motion. In C major: E minor [Tl], A minor [Sl?], B minor [Dl?]... in A minor: F major, C major, B-flat major."

The Leittonwechselklang shares two common tones with its parent Klang (the third and fifth are retained; only the root changes). The semitone motion of the root creates a particularly strong connection because the leading-tone relationship implies resolution.

Hyer notes that these tone-relations — Parallele, Variante, and Leittonwechsel — share the property of being operations that change one tone while retaining two (Ch. 3, pp. 107-108). The Leittonwechsel is the most characteristically chromatic of the three.

# Key Properties

1. Derived by semitone substitution of the root (leading-tone exchange)
2. Shares two common tones (third and fifth) with the parent Klang
3. The semitone motion gives it stronger "stimulus to harmonic motion" than the Parallelklang
4. Creates a Scheinkonsonanz with characteristically chromatic coloring
5. In neo-Riemannian theory, this becomes the L (Leittonwechsel) transformation
6. Inherently chromatic in most contexts (unlike the Parallelklang, which is diatonic)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Find the Leittonwechselklang of a Major Klang
1. Start with the major triad (e.g., C-E-G)
2. Replace the root (C) with the leading tone below (B)
3. Result: B-E-G = E minor = Tl in C major

## To Find the Leittonwechselklang of a Minor Klang (Dualistic)
1. Start with the minor triad (e.g., A-C-E)
2. Replace the root/prime with the tone a semitone above (in dualistic terms)
3. Result: F major (F-A-C) = Tl in A minor

# Context & Application

The Leittonwechselklang is central to understanding the connection between Riemann's function theory and neo-Riemannian transformational theory. In Riemann's system, it is a *functional representative* — E minor in C major is Tl, a modified form of the tonic. In neo-Riemannian theory, the same chord relationship becomes the L transformation, treated not as a functional representative but as an autonomous voice-leading operation.

This transformation is "maximally smooth" — involving only semitone motion in one voice. Combined with P (Parallel), it generates major-third relations: LP maps C major to Ab major; PL maps C major to E major. These compound transformations are characteristic of 19th-century chromatic harmony (Wagner, Liszt).

# Examples

**Example 1** (Ch. 3, n. 43): In C major: Tl = E minor, Sl = A minor(?), Dl = B minor(?). In A minor: Tl = F major, Sl = C major, Dl = Bb major.

**Example 2**: The relationship C major ↔ E minor exemplifies the Leittonwechsel: C moves to B (semitone), while E and G are retained.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Confusing the Leittonwechselklang (a functional representative in Riemann) with the L transformation (a contextual operation in neo-Riemannian theory)
  **Correction**: In Riemann, Tl represents tonic function; in neo-Riemannian theory, L is a transformational relation between equal nodes without functional hierarchy

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: The "leading-tone" in the name refers to the departing or arriving tone
  **Clarification**: The root of the original Klang is replaced by the leading tone — the new chord includes what would be the leading tone of the original root
