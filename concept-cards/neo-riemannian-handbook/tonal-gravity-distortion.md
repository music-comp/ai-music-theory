# Quick Definition

Harrison's metaphor for how the presence of functional tonics creates "gravitational distortions" in the otherwise equally-tempered, centerless space of neo-Riemannian theory, making transformational labels sensuously indifferent where functional labels carry qualitative weight.

# Core Definition

Drawing on Brian Hyer's image, Harrison argues that "the presence of functional tonics — at any level of structure in which they can be heard — creates what Brian Hyer imagined as a gravitational distortion of equally tempered Tonnetz space" (Harrison, p. 564). Neo-Riemannian theory "seems happiest operating in an apparently tonic-free, zero-gravity state, as its analytic products are transformational labels that have no sensuous-functional significance" (p. 564). Under conditions of tonal gravity, "neo-Riemannian analysis takes on a certain dreamlike quality. It works unconstrained by the laws of gravity or of nature — just as we do when in our dreams we fly" (p. 565). Such analysis "may also be a window into a compositional unconscious...But, like dreams, it does not touch a substantial and consequential reality, which can be addressed only while awake and, in obedience to gravity, with feet on the ground" (p. 565).

# Prerequisites

- **Tonnetz** — The geometric space that tonal gravity distorts
- **Harmonic function** — T, D, S generate the gravitational field

# Key Properties

1. Functional tonics create gravitational distortion of equal-tempered transformational space
2. Neo-Riemannian theory operates best in "zero gravity" (no functional tonic)
3. Under tonal gravity, neo-Riemannian analysis is dreamlike — valid but not touching "substantial reality"
4. Pieces with "unusual and even dreamlike" gravitation (like the Vaughan Williams) occupy a middle ground
5. The same space can have variable gravity: some passages functional, others absolute
6. Hyer's move to eliminate tonic from the Tonnetz was theoretically productive but sensuously impoverishing

# Construction / Recognition

## To Assess Tonal Gravity in a Passage

1. Determine whether a single chord functions as a clear tonic (strong gravity)
2. Assess whether multiple chords compete for tonic status (weak/distorted gravity)
3. Check whether no chord asserts tonic status (zero gravity / tonic overcrowding)
4. Strong gravity: use functional analysis. Zero gravity: use transformational analysis. Variable: use both.

# Context & Application

The gravity metaphor frames Harrison's third essay, where the Vaughan Williams *Tallis Fantasia* presents "functional gravitation, but of a kind that is unusual and even dreamlike" (p. 565). The piece mixes passages of recognizable (if confused) tonal gravity with passages of absolute progression, requiring both analytical modes. Harrison's conclusion is that transformational analysis is specifically appropriate for "absolute progressions" — passages where gravity has effectively zero effect because too many local tonics crowd the space.

# Examples

**Vaughan Williams, *Tallis Fantasia*** (pp. 564-573): The Tallis theme has recognizable but bifurcated functional gravity (G-Phrygian vs. C-minor systemic hearing). Vaughan Williams's own material ranges from "light, G-ish tonal gravity" in the introduction to near-zero gravity in the planed organum passages.

**The dreamlike quality** (p. 565): Neo-Riemannian analysis under tonal gravity "works unconstrained by the laws of gravity or of nature — just as we do when in our dreams we fly, or converse with friends and family members from long ago."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming all chromatic music is "zero gravity"
  **Correction**: Harrison distinguishes between chromatic passages with clear tonal gravity (where functional analysis works) and genuine absolute progressions (where it fails)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking "dreamlike" means invalid
  **Clarification**: Harrison acknowledges transformational analysis may reveal genuine compositional structure ("a window into a compositional unconscious") even when it does not model the sensuous experience of tonal gravity
