# Quick Definition

"Filling tones" borrowed from Chinese music theory that Riemann uses to explain how pentatonic scales evolve toward diatonicism. Pien tones fill the minor-third gaps in the pentatonic scale, converting three-note trichords into four-note tetrachords.

# Core Definition

**Pien** (from Chinese music theory) are tones that fill the wide gaps between non-adjacent degrees in the pentatonic scale. Riemann asserts that these tones "are rarely employed (usually as changing or neighbor notes)" and "do not have fixed intonation" (Ch. 4, p. 151). Despite these caveats, Riemann uses the concept systematically to explain how pentatonic scales transform into heptatonic (seven-note) scales.

The concept works as follows: a pentatonic scale has two gaps (minor thirds) where the 4th and 7th scale degrees would be in a diatonic scale. *Pien* tones fill these gaps. Which *pien* is used and its metric placement determines the resulting tetrachord type and, by extension, the mode (Ch. 4, pp. 154-155).

Riemann "continues to apply the term, culled from its Chinese theoretical context, in all situations to denote the upper or lower third around the *mese*" (Ch. 4, p. 151). His cross-cultural application of this Chinese concept to Greek, Celtic, and medieval European music is tendentious but central to his argument that pentatonic and tetrachordal structures are connected.

# Prerequisites

# Key Properties

1. Fill the minor-third gaps in the pentatonic scale
2. Originally ornamental (variable intonation, weak-beat placement)
3. Become structural when placed on strong beats (enabling "modulation")
4. Strong-beat *pien* can shift the perceived tonal center (the *mese*)
5. Different *pien* placements yield different tetrachord types (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian)
6. Borrowed from Chinese theory but applied universally by Riemann

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply Pien to a Pentatonic Trichord
1. Identify a three-note fragment spanning a fourth (e.g., D-E-G)
2. The minor-third gap is between E and G
3. Add *pien* F → D-E-F-G = Phrygian tetrachord
4. Add *pien* F# → D-E-F#-G = tendency toward different tetrachord
5. The *pien*'s metric placement determines structural vs. ornamental status

## Metric Weight Rule
- **Weak beat**: *Pien* is ornamental, does not change tonal center
- **Strong beat**: *Pien* becomes structural, may induce shift to new *pyknon*

# Context & Application

The *pien* concept allows Riemann to bridge pentatonic and tetrachordal theory — two structurally disparate models. "Riemann did not explain how exactly this principle of Chinese music theory, which manages to function as the historical lubricant that provides such smooth passage from Greek antiquity to the European Middle Ages, entered the Western frame of mind" (Ch. 4, p. 153).

The concept was not entirely Riemann's innovation: "the modulation between different pentatonic modes or gamuts via the introduction of infixes as pivot tones was well-established" in earlier scholarship (Ch. 4, n. 51). But Riemann systematized the concept within his evolutionary framework.

# Examples

**Example 1** (Ch. 4, ex. 4.5): A *pyknon* filled to a tetrachord by adding *pien* above or below the central cluster.

**Example 2** (Ch. 4, ex. 4.6a-b): The metric placement of *pien* — on a weak beat it remains ornamental; on a strong beat it shifts the perceived tonal center.

**Example 3** (Ch. 4, ex. 4.7): Different *pien* create different tetrachords from the same trichord.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating pien as fixed, structural scale degrees from the start
  **Correction**: Riemann asserts they begin as ornamental tones with variable intonation, becoming structural only in later evolutionary stages

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the Chinese concept of pien applies identically in all cultural contexts
  **Clarification**: Riemann's application of Chinese *pien* to Greek, Celtic, and medieval European music is his own theoretical move, not a historical claim about cultural contact
