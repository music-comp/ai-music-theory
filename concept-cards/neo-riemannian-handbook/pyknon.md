# Quick Definition

The central three-note cluster around the *mese* in Riemann's pentatonic theory. The "major-third pyknon" (two whole-tone steps) characterizes pure pentatonicism; the "minor-third pyknon" (semitone + whole tone) marks the transition toward diatonic and harmonic consciousness.

# Core Definition

The **pyknon** (from Greek *pyknon*, originally referring to the pair of smaller intervals in the chromatic and enharmonic genera of Greek tetrachordal theory) is repurposed by Riemann to describe the three consecutive notes at the center of his pentatonic formations, separated by whole-tone steps.

Two types exist:

1. **Major-third pyknon**: Three notes separated by two whole-tone steps (e.g., D-E-G, spanning a major third). This characterizes the anhemitonic pentatonic scale.

2. **Minor-third pyknon**: Three notes separated by a semitone and a whole tone (e.g., D-Eb-F, spanning a minor third). This marks the evolutionary transition from pentatonic to diatonic consciousness.

Riemann's use of the Greek term is "rather liberal" (Ch. 4, p. 148): in standard Greek theory, *pyknon* refers to the pair of smaller intervals of a tetrachord, not to a three-note cluster within a pentatonic scale. "The conclusion that Riemann's use of these Greek terms is tendentious is difficult to deny; it is, however, anything but arbitrary: the Greek terms lend gravitas and legitimacy to Riemann's theory" (Ch. 4, p. 148).

The minor-third pyknon is analytically crucial for Riemann: "The fact that the minor-third pyknon came to the fore instead of the major-third pyknon as the center indicates an emergent understanding of the essence of harmony, a recognition of the third as a part of the sonority, and the separation into major and minor" (Ch. 4, p. 157, quoting Riemann).

# Key Properties

1. Three consecutive tones around the *mese*
2. Major-third pyknon: whole + whole (pentatonic)
3. Minor-third pyknon: semi + whole or whole + semi (transitional to diatonicism)
4. The pyknon identifies the central structural unit of the pentatonic scale
5. Shift of pyknon indicates "modulation" between fifth-related pentatonic centers
6. The minor-third pyknon introduces the semitone that enables leading-tone cadences

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify the Major-Third Pyknon
1. In a pentatonic scale, locate the *mese*
2. The *mese* plus its upper and lower whole-tone neighbors form the pyknon
3. Example: In the pentatonic A-C-D-F-G (mese=D), the pyknon is C-D-F

## To Recognize a Minor-Third Pyknon
1. When a *pien* tone replaces the whole-tone step with a semitone
2. The three notes now span a minor third instead of a major third
3. Example: C-D-Eb (semitone + whole tone) or C-Db-Eb (semitone + whole tone)

# Context & Application

The pyknon serves two analytical purposes in *Folkloristische Tonalitätsstudien*:

First, it enables Riemann to track "modulations" within pentatonic music. "Many melodies with pentatonic tendencies exhibit — for our ears — a noticeable cadential oscillation between the two relative keys to which the central pyknon is common" (Ch. 4, p. 152, quoting Riemann). Depending on whether the pyknon appears with the upper or lower fourth, the closing gesture can be heard in different scalar contexts.

Second, the shift from major-third to minor-third pyknon represents the crucial evolutionary moment when harmonic consciousness emerged: the recognition of the third as a chord component and the differentiation of major from minor (Ch. 4, pp. 156-157).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Equating Riemann's pyknon with the standard Greek pyknon
  **Correction**: Riemann's usage is non-standard; the Greek pyknon refers to tetrachordal intervals, not three-note clusters within pentatonic scales

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the pyknon shift from major-third to minor-third is a historical claim
  **Clarification**: It is a structural/heuristic model for explaining how pentatonic systems transition to diatonic ones
