# Quick Definition

Rings's comparative analysis of Schubert's Impromptu in Gb, D. 899, no. 3, juxtaposing Riemann's own 1877 functional analysis with a neo-Riemannian regional analysis to demonstrate how divergent analytical values produce radically different readings of the same music.

# Core Definition

Rings uses Schubert's Gb-major Impromptu as a sustained case study, comparing Riemann's analysis from *Musikalische Syntaxis* (1877) with a neo-Riemannian approach inspired by Cohn. The piece is ideal because "Riemann himself devoted a five-page analysis" to it, providing direct paleo-Riemannian comparison material (Rings, p. 490). The analysis centers on the coda passage at mm. 78-82, where the music progresses from Gb major through B minor to G minor — "the tonal bottom seems to drop out of the music" (p. 488). Riemann hears tonal confirmation; neo-Riemannian analysis hears regional voice-leading coherence; Rings's tonal-intention framework hears accumulated flatward "shading" (pp. 488-506).

# Key Properties

1. Three distinct analyses of the same passage yield three different kinds of insight
2. Riemann's analysis: the piece is "a formally quite lucidly organized composition" (p. 491)
3. Neo-Riemannian analysis: mm. 78-82 fall within a Weitzmann region with consistent AVLS
4. Rings's tonal-intention analysis: the G-minor chord acquires extraordinary "chiaroscuro quality" through accumulated flatward shading
5. The subdominant bias of the piece — explored at the expense of dominant key areas — is significant for all three analyses
6. Riemann analyzed the piece in G major (from an early edition), not the correct Gb

1. Apply Riemannian function labels: the G-minor chord requires a triply-modified S label

5. Note the "fact/value gap": the neo-Riemannian analysis reveals coherence but not the remarkable sound

# Context & Application

Riemann's 1877 analysis came early in his career; the piece was one of very few Schubert works he analyzed in detail. The analysis reflects his early function-theory vocabulary and his conviction that all music confirms tonal law. Curiously, Riemann analyzed the piece in G major (from the Haslinger edition) rather than the correct Gb major, and continued to use G major even in his 1917 *Handbuch der Harmonielehre* (Rings, p. 494, n. 18).

# Examples

**Riemann's reading of mm. 78-82** (pp. 491-492): The passage demonstrates "wholly excellent effect" in "the final consolidation of the primary key." The G-minor chord (heard as Ab minor in Riemann's G-major version) is an excursion to the "antilogic-antinomic Terztonart" — among the most distant harmonies from the tonic.

**Neo-Riemannian reading** (pp. 494-496): The triads around m. 80 belong to a Weitzmann region centered on the augmented triad {Db, F, A}. Operations within the region (N, P, L, R, SLIDE) provide consistent AVLS values. The hexagonal network reveals voice-leading coherence.

**Tonal-intention reading** (pp. 502-505): The path from G minor back to Gb tonic (P-L-P-S) traverses four edges in the darkest region of the LRP map. Each step adds flatward "shading." The "menacing" bass trill on C (m. 79) points intentionally toward the coming G minor via P-D.
