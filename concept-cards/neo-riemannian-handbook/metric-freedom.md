# Metric Freedom

## Quick Definition

The compositional practice of departing from strictly regular meter through elongations, accelerations, and metric shifts while maintaining an underlying periodic logic, as exemplified in Brahms's songs and traced historically to Caccini's monodic innovations.

## Formal Definition

**Metric freedom** (German: *Taktfreiheit*) refers to the compositional flexibility in metric organization that creates apparent irregularities in relation to notated meter while serving expressive and text-sensitive purposes.

**Riemann's Definition** (from "Die Taktfreiheiten in Brahms' Lieder," 1912):
Brahms "writes exactly what should be sung" including elongations and accelerations that might elsewhere be indicated by performance markings (*ritenuto*, *stringendo*), but which he notates with actual rhythmic values "in conflict with the prevailing metric organization."

**Historical Lineage**: Riemann connects Brahms's practice to Giulio Caccini (early 17th century), who similarly:
- Wrote "exactly as it is sung" (*scrivo giustamente come si canta*)
- Notated performance-like rhythmic flexibility
- Was misunderstood by later critics who judged his declamation "abysmal"

**Key Principle**: "All of the freer forms are to be measured, today as in all times, according to the standard of the strictly regular; they obtain their individual quality and their individual effect through their deviation from the norm."

**NOT Implied**: Metric freedom does NOT mean:
- Abandonment of normative meter
- That "no strict time need prevail"
- License for arbitrary rhythmic treatment

## Musical Context

Metric freedom in Brahms's songs serves multiple expressive purposes:

- **Text sensitivity**: "Scrupulous responsiveness to the accentual demands and expressive meaning of individual words"
- **Differentiation**: Distinguishes "the higher art song from the strophic song"
- **Compositional refinement**: Reflects sophisticated manipulation of metric proportions

**Analytical Challenge**: The "abstruseness of the formal structure" requires analysis to uncover the "inner regularity" that makes the music "artistically well-motivated."

**Reception Problem**: Critics who cannot perceive the underlying logic may unfairly accuse Brahms of "poor declamation"—a charge Riemann works to refute.

## Examples

### Basic

**Notated elongation serving text expression**:
```
Standard meter: | quarter quarter quarter quarter |
Brahms's notation: | quarter quarter HALF |
```
The half note expresses a *ritardando* effect on an expressive word without requiring a performance indication.

**Metric shift within a song**:
```
Opening period: Triple meter (3/4 feel)
Consequent phrase: Duple meter (2/2 feel)
```
Riemann argues both sections conform to an underlying eight-measure periodic structure despite the apparent metric change.

### From Repertoire

**Brahms, "Immer leiser wird mein Schlummer," Op. 105/2**: The opening melodic lines take three measures despite eight-syllable normal verse. Riemann reanalyzes the meter:
- Brahms's notation suggests metric irregularity
- Riemann hears an underlying periodic structure with elongated line-endings
- The allabreve (cut-time) indication suggests half-note beats, not quarter-note

**Brahms, "Schwalbe, sag' mir an" (Das Madchen spricht), Op. 107/3**: The prelude's meter is "deeply problematic." Riemann argues:
- The notated 3/4 does not represent the heard meter
- An intelligent accompanist would "hold back somewhat" at phrase boundaries
- The underlying structure shows regular periodic organization

**Cross-strophic comparison**: In both songs, Riemann compares strophe settings to reveal how metric freedom serves text-specific expression while maintaining formal coherence.

## Common Confusions

- Metric freedom does NOT mean absence of metric organization—there is always an underlying norm
- Brahms's notation is NOT "incorrect"—it represents performance-like detail
- Riemann does NOT advocate abandoning bar lines—he reinterprets them
- Historical precedent (Caccini) shows this is an established compositional tradition
- "Poor declamation" accusations may reflect analytical failure, not compositional error
- Metric freedom and strict meter are not opposites—freedom is measured against the norm
- Analysis should reveal the "hidden" regular structure beneath apparent irregularity
