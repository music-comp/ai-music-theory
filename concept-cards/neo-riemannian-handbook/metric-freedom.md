# Quick Definition

The compositional practice of departing from strictly regular meter through elongations, accelerations, and metric shifts while maintaining an underlying periodic logic, as exemplified in Brahms's songs and traced historically to Caccini's monodic innovations.

# Core Definition

**Metric freedom** (Taktfreiheit) refers to compositional flexibility in metric organization creating apparent irregularities while serving expressive and text-sensitive purposes. Berry translates Riemann's essay "Die Taktfreiheiten in Brahms' Lieder" (1912), where Riemann states that Brahms "writes exactly what should be sung" including elongations and accelerations notated with actual rhythmic values "in conflict with the prevailing metric organization" (p. 463).

The key principle: "All of the freer forms are to be measured, today as in all times, according to the standard of the strictly regular; they obtain their individual quality and their individual effect through their deviation from the norm" (p. 464). Metric freedom does NOT mean abandonment of meter -- there is always an underlying norm against which deviations are measured.

# Prerequisites

- **Auftakttheorie**: Riemann's end-accented metric model provides the norm from which freedom departs.

# Key Properties

1. **Norm-relative**: Freedom is meaningful only in relation to strict metric regularity
2. **Text-motivated**: Serves "scrupulous responsiveness to the accentual demands and expressive meaning of individual words" (p. 463)
3. **Historically grounded**: Riemann traces Brahms's practice to Caccini (early 17th century)
4. **Notated precision**: Brahms notates what should be sung, incorporating ritardando-like effects as actual note values
5. **Inner regularity**: Apparent irregularity conceals underlying periodic structure

# Construction / Recognition

Riemann identifies metric freedom through: elongations of note values at expressive points; metric shifts between duple and triple within a song; deviations from the eight-measure periodic norm that serve text-specific expression. Analysis must uncover the "inner regularity" beneath "misleading notation."

# Context & Application

Berry (Ch. 17) presents Riemann's comparative analysis of two Brahms songs as rare examples of Riemann engaging with vocal music (which he generally considered inferior to absolute music). The essay is notable for presenting an "almost contemporary account of Brahms's music" and for its dissimilarity from Riemann's more systematic theories.

# Examples

**Brahms, "Immer leiser wird mein Schlummer," Op. 105/2** (Berry, Ch. 17, p. 465): The opening melodic lines each take three measures despite eight-syllable normal verse. Riemann reanalyzes the meter, arguing the allabreve indication suggests half-note beats and the structure is more regular than notation suggests. Comparing strophes reveals that only four measures return identically in the same formal position.

**Brahms, "Schwalbe, sag' mir an," Op. 107/3** (Berry, Ch. 17, p. 469): The prelude's meter is "deeply problematic." Riemann argues the notated 3/4 does not represent the heard meter and "every intelligent accompanist would hold back somewhat" at phrase boundaries.

**Caccini connection** (p. 463): Riemann connects Brahms to Caccini, who "writes exactly as it is sung" (scrivo giustamente come si canta) and was "misunderstood by later critics who judged his declamation 'abysmal.'" A "wholly analogous situation" befell Brahms.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Concluding that Brahms's notation contains "errors in declamation."
  **Correction**: Riemann argues such accusations reflect analytical failure, not compositional error.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking metric freedom means absence of metric organization.
  **Clarification**: There is always an underlying norm; "the downright abolishment of normative concepts is of no use to anyone" (p. 464).

- **Confusion**: Assuming metric freedom and strict meter are opposites.
  **Clarification**: Freedom is measured against the norm; they are complementary, not contradictory.
