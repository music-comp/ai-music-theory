# Quick Definition

Tymoczko's model of 19th-century chromaticism as combining a "first practice" of diatonic functional harmony with a "second practice" where virtually any voice leading between familiar chords is acceptable as long as it is efficient, explaining the chromatic adventurousness of composers like Wagner and Brahms.

# Core Definition

Tymoczko proposes that 19th-century chromatic music operates with two concurrent systems (Ch. 8):

1. **First practice**: Diatonic chord progressions following traditional tonal syntax (I-IV-V-I patterns)
2. **Second practice**: Chromatic connections using efficient voice leading, where "virtually any voice leading between familiar chords may be used, as long as it is efficient"

The second practice sets remarkably few constraints -- it "places very few constraints on chord-to-chord progressions." The only requirements are that sonorities be "familiar" (typically triads and seventh chords) and that voice leading be efficient.

Three principles characterize this practice:
- **P1**: If a sonority is acceptable, so is its inversion
- **P2**: Efficient voice leadings are desirable
- **P3**: Voice-leading prohibitions apply equally to ascending and descending motion

When all three hold, inversional symmetry emerges naturally.

# Prerequisites

- **Voice leading** -- The second practice is defined by voice-leading properties
- **Voice-leading efficiency** -- The key constraint on the second practice

# Key Properties

1. The second practice coexists with traditional functional harmony, not replacing it
2. Almost any chord connection is acceptable if voice leading is efficient
3. Efficient voice leading is inherently "centrifugal" -- it pulls music away from tonal centers
4. Inversional symmetry appears as a natural by-product (P1 + P2 + P3)
5. Compositional skill involves controlling centrifugal chromatic forces

# Construction / Recognition

## Identifying Second-Practice Passages
1. Look for chromatic progressions that defy functional explanation
2. Check whether adjacent chords connect via efficient voice leading (small total motion)
3. Verify that the sonorities are "familiar" (triads, seventh chords)
4. If efficient voice leading connects familiar sonorities without functional logic: second practice

## Constraints vs. Freedom
- First practice: Constrained by functional syntax (limited chord progressions)
- Second practice: Constrained only by efficiency (wide freedom in chord choice)
- Most chromatic music mixes both practices

# Context & Application

The second-practice model explains several features of 19th-century chromatic music:
- **Major-third relations** appear frequently because they involve the most efficient voice leading between same-quality triads
- **"Tristan" chord** resolutions systematically explore efficient voice leadings
- **Chromatic sequences** follow efficient voice-leading patterns
- **Dualistic patterns** emerge because inversionally related voice leadings are equally efficient

The model also explains why Riemann's dualistic terminology is analytically useful for chromatic music even though composers were not thinking dualistically: the terminology captures patterns that arise from voice-leading efficiency.

# Examples

**Brahms Intermezzo Op. 76 No. 4** (Ch. 8): The piece "systematically explores the voice-leading possibilities of a few characteristic sonorities." The Tristan chord resolves three different ways via efficient voice leading; the Eb minor triad resolves three ways. Motivic connections prevent "musical anarchy."

**Wagner's Tristan Prelude** (Ch. 8): The prelude can be described as exploring voice-leading possibilities of the Tristan chord sonority. Various resolutions are efficient chromatic connections to familiar chords.

**Schubert's modulations** (Ch. 8, n. 43): The pivot-chord modulation in Brahms where iv of Bb major becomes iii of Cb major is related to Schubert's modulation in "Die junge Nonne" -- dualistically equivalent modulations arising from efficient voice leading in different modes.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating the "second practice" as replacing functional harmony
  **Correction**: The two practices coexist and interact; most chromatic music mixes both

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: The term "second practice" refers to Monteverdi's *seconda prattica*
  **Clarification**: Tymoczko borrows the concept but applies it to a different historical context; the parallel is in the idea of a freer practice coexisting with a stricter one
