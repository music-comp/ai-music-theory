# Quick Definition

The principle that transformational analyses are necessarily incomplete by design, since the construction of any contextual model requires excluding significant aspects of musical experience — a feature, not a bug, suited to music that operates "in the seams between traditional diatonic hierarchies and a more fluid syntax."

# Core Definition

Cook argues that "an analytical approach built on such principles will produce incomplete analyses because the building of such a model requires that a significant amount of the musical experience be excluded" (Cook, p. 531). This incompleteness is a principled feature of transformational theory, which is "built on the premises that an analysis seeks to answer particular questions in a particular musical context (not surprising, given its origins in atonal analysis), that in a given piece there may be multiple, even contradictory contexts, that the analyst gives a *performance* of a piece (in a sense, at a certain level of abstraction), which can only ever be a partial realization of the piece" (p. 532).

# Prerequisites

- **Contextual harmony analysis** — Incompleteness is a direct consequence of the contextual approach

# Key Properties

1. Every transformational model excludes some aspects of musical experience
2. Multiple, even contradictory analytical contexts may coexist for a single piece
3. The analyst's account is a "performance" — partial by nature
4. Traditional analyses (Schenkerian, functional) remain "part of the analytical picture"
5. Incompleteness is especially suited to chromatic-yet-triadic music that works "in the seams" between diatonic and chromatic syntax
6. Contrasts with Schenkerian analysis, which "dispatches" what does not fit the model as unimportant or antithetical

# Construction / Recognition

## To Recognize Analytical Incompleteness

1. Identify what the transformational model captures (e.g., voice-leading patterns, major-third relations)
2. Identify what it excludes (e.g., phrase structure, tonal function, contrapuntal detail)
3. Note whether the excluded elements are addressed by complementary analytical approaches
4. Assess whether the model is epistemologically transparent about its limitations

# Context & Application

Cook's discussion of incompleteness follows directly from his comparison of contrapuntal, functional, and contextual analyses of Franck's *Le chasseur maudit*. He emphasizes that the Schenkerian and Riemannian hearings "are as much part of the analytical picture we paint of example 19.1b as the transformational analysis" (p. 531). The contextual model succeeds precisely because it does not try to account for everything.

# Examples

**Franck, *Le chasseur maudit*** (p. 531): The contrapuntal analysis revealed the importance of F# as a bass-line pivot; the functional analysis showed parallel between local third pairs and global endpoints. These insights are not captured by the transformational model but remain part of the complete analytical picture.

**Comparison with Schenkerian approach** (p. 532): Unlike Schenker's approach, "in which that which does not fit the model is dispatched as either unimportant or antithetical to true Art," transformational theory acknowledges multiple valid contexts.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating incompleteness as a weakness to be overcome
  **Correction**: It is a principled feature that enables epistemological transparency

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing incomplete analysis means inadequate analysis
  **Clarification**: An analysis that honestly models specific aspects of a piece may be more valuable than one that claims comprehensive coverage but masks its assumptions
