# Quick Definition

The numerical designation (1-8) that Riemann assigns to each measure within a period, indicating its metrical weight and position within the eight-measure prototype, where the "8" function is invariant and every actual period realizes, expands, or transforms the prototype.

# Core Definition

Periodic functions are the numbered positions (1 through 8) that Riemann assigns to measures within his prototypical eight-measure period. Burnham explains that "Riemann's prototype is an abstraction based on what he believes to be the first fact of musical rhythm: the fundamental impulse of upbeat to downbeat" (p. 442). Each function carries specific metrical weight: even-numbered functions (2, 4, 6, 8) are strong, odd-numbered (1, 3, 5, 7) are weak. The "8" function is absolutely invariant — "every Riemann period must contain an 8 function, just as every tree must have a trunk" (p. 443). Burnham characterizes the structure as "left-branching" — everything branches from the final term. Riemann also allows several non-standard designations: elisions (8=1), expansions (3a, 4a), multiple cadential arrivals (8, 8a, 8b), back-relating functions (5 relating to 4 rather than forward to 6), and *Vorhang* (curtain) for introductory material.

# Key Properties

1. Eight numbered functions (1-8) with consistent strong/weak alternation
2. Even-numbered functions (2, 4, 6, 8) are strong; odd-numbered (1, 3, 5, 7) are weak
3. The "8" function is invariant — every period must end with it
4. The first sounding measure is NEVER function "1" if it is strong — Riemann posits an imaginary preceding upbeat
5. Periods can be incomplete (versions of 5-8, never 1-4)
6. Expanded periods use alphabetic suffixes: 3a, 4a, 8a, 8b, etc.
7. Back-relating functions (e.g., "9" echoing "8") allow flexible parsing
8. Harmonic function and periodic function are linked: even measures carry *Harmoniewirkung*

| Function | Weight | Role | Harmonic Tendency |
|----------|--------|------|-------------------|
| 1 | Weak | Opening upbeat | Often tonic presentation |
| 2 | Strong | First accent | Harmonic change expected |
| 3 | Weak | Upbeat to 4 | Transitional |
| 4 | Strong | Half-phrase end | Often dominant or secondary |
| 5 | Weak | Second half begins | Upbeat to 6 |
| 6 | Strong | Antepenultimate | Often cadential preparation |
| 7 | Weak | Upbeat to 8 | Dominant expected |
| 8 | Strong | INVARIANT goal | Cadential resolution |

# Construction / Recognition

## To Assign Periodic Functions

1. Identify the cadential goal (the "8" function) — this is the starting point
2. Count backward from the cadence, assigning alternating strong/weak functions
3. If the first sounding measure is strong, posit an imaginary preceding upbeat (function 1)
4. For expanded passages, label inserted material with alphabetic suffixes
5. For multiple cadential arrivals, label as 8, 8a, 8b, etc.
6. When a function relates backward rather than forward, mark it as back-relating

# Context & Application

Burnham uses periodic functions as the primary lens for his analysis of Beethoven's Op. 31 sonatas. The analytical power lies in what the functions reveal: "Puzzling out the motivations for his functional designations forces one to ask different kinds of questions of these themes, preventing us from simply classifying them as straightforward 4 + 4 constructions" (p. 445). The functions enable tracking of compositional strategies — such as the "proliferation of 8 functions" that becomes a "comic tic" of Op. 31, No. 1, or the dramatic delay of the 8 function in the Tempest sonata.

**Analytical utility**: The distinction between "essential stations" and "inserted or appended" material is key. "Harmonic progression represents the evolving musical argument and thus helps determine the primary stations of his prototypical construction; patches of harmonic stasis serve as confirmation or filler" (p. 446).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 442, Ex. 16.1): Beethoven, Op. 31, No. 1, Rondo theme. An unproblematic realization: "Each even-numbered downbeat is a harmonic resolution, and it is easy to hear the various answering downbeats of the prototype."

**Example 2** (pp. 443-444, Ex. 16.2): Beethoven, Op. 31, No. 3, Second theme. Because it starts strong, Riemann designates the opening as function 2, leading to a doubled 8 function (8-8a). The analysis lays out as 2-3-4-5/6-7-8-8a.

**Example 3** (pp. 444-445, Ex. 16.3): Beethoven, Op. 31, No. 1, Second theme. Riemann gives function 4 to the opening, reinterprets the 8 as 5, creating 4-5-6-7/8=5-6-7-8 — "a radical reinterpretation" emphasizing "two separate motions to an 8 function."

**Example 4** (pp. 447-448): Beethoven, Op. 31, No. 2 ("Tempest"), opening. The Largo is designated 3-4 and also as *Vorhang*, stitching the two contrasting sections into a single period.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming function numbers equal measure numbers in the score
  **Correction**: Functions may differ from score measure numbers due to insertions, elisions, and reinterpretations

- **Error**: Treating multiple "8" functions as errors in the analysis
  **Correction**: Multiple 8s are analytically meaningful — they reveal compositional strategies around closure

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing Riemann's functional assignments are purely objective
  **Clarification**: Burnham notes they "mix empirical and theoretical evidence" — Riemann makes ad hoc adjustments (like allowing Tp to function as T, or recasting 4-5 as 5-6) that reveal tensions in the system

- **Confusion**: Thinking incomplete periods can begin from function 1
  **Clarification**: "A period left incomplete will be some version of 5-8, never 1-4" — the trunk (8) cannot be removed
