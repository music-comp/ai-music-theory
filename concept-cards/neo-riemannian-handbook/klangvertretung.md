# Quick Definition

The principle that individual tones and subsidiary chords are understood not in isolation but as representatives of complete Klange (triads) and primary functions (T, D, S), operating at three hierarchical levels: tone-to-Klang, chord-to-function, and key-to-key.

# Core Definition

**Klangvertretung** ("Klang representation") is the concept that any tone, interval, or chord acquires meaning by representing a complete Klang. Riemann's definition from the *Musik-Lexikon*: "Klangvertretung is the particular meaning that a tone or interval acquires depending on which Klang it is understood to represent" (Ch. 3, n. 40).

The concept operates at three hierarchical levels, as Riemann explains in "Die Natur der Harmonik" (Ch. 2, pp. 82-86):

1. **Tone-to-Klang**: Each tone can represent six possible Klange (three major, three minor), depending on whether it functions as root, third, or fifth in either direction
2. **Chord-to-Function**: Secondary chords (parallels, Leittonwechselklange) represent the principal functions (T, D, S) to which they relate
3. **Key-to-Key**: Related keys represent the principal key at a larger formal level — "Just as the root of a Klang relates to its subsidiary tones... the principal key relates to the subsidiary keys" (Ch. 2, p. 85)

The concept was developed historically by Helmholtz (who first stated that "we conceive tones as representatives of Klange," though limited to major), then extended by Oettingen to include minor Klange, giving it "unparalleled breadth" (Ch. 2, p. 82). Riemann integrated it into his comprehensive function theory. Crucially, Riemann argued that "the principle of Klang representation no longer belongs within physics, nor within physiology, but rather in psychology" (Ch. 2, p. 83).

# Key Properties

1. Any single tone can represent six possible Klange (root, third, or fifth in major or minor)
2. Context determines which Klang is represented
3. Representative chords create "apparent consonances" (Scheinkonsonanzen) — they sound consonant but function as dissonances relative to the governing tonic
4. Even the dominant triad "is not a perfect consonance" within a key because its mental representation includes its relation to the tonic (Ch. 2, p. 84)
5. The representation is psychological, not acoustic — it operates in the perceiving mind
6. Klangvertretung enables analytical reduction: all harmony reducible to T, D, S representations

# Construction / Recognition

## To Determine What a Tone Represents
1. Identify the musical context (key, prevailing harmony)
2. Determine which Klang the tone most naturally belongs to in context
3. The tone functions as root, third, or fifth of that Klang
4. Example: E in C major context → third of C over-Klang (c+)

## To Identify Scheinkonsonanz
1. The chord sounds consonant in isolation (it is a major or minor triad)
2. Within the key, it functions as a representative of a primary function, not as the tonic itself
3. Therefore it is "apparently consonant" but functionally dissonant — containing "an element of unrest, the desire for a progression to the tonic" (Ch. 2, p. 84)

# Context & Application

Riemann's "Die Natur der Harmonik" (Ch. 2) develops Klangvertretung as the bridge between acoustics and musical meaning. The key passage describes how hearing the G-major chord in C major is fundamentally different from hearing C major as tonic: "The C-major chord itself enters the mental representation as the Klang by which the significance of the G-major chord is determined... the center of the mental representation lies outside it, so to speak; that is, an element of unrest occurs in it" (Ch. 2, pp. 84-85).

This reconceptualization transforms consonance and dissonance from acoustic properties into relational ones: "even a chord which is, according to physical and physiological formulations, the most undisturbed and euphonious can be a dissonance musically (for example, the six-four chord)" (Ch. 2, p. 83).

In analytical practice, Holtmeier (Ch. 1) shows how Schreyer's concept of *Scheinharmonie* ("apparent harmony") extended Klangvertretung: chords that assume "a different significance from the expected one" within a structural context (Ch. 1, p. 20).

# Examples

**Example 1** (Ch. 2, pp. 82-83): The note E can represent six Klange: "It can be the root of its under-Klang (A-C-E), or the fifth of a B under-Klang (E-G-B), or the third of a G# under-Klang (C#-E-G#), as it is to comprehend it as the root of an E over-Klang (E-G#-B), or the fifth of an A over-Klang (A-C#-E), or finally as the third of a C over-Klang (C-E-G)."

**Example 2** (Ch. 2, p. 84): The G-major chord in C major: "If I think to myself of a G-major chord in the context of the key of C major, then I think of it as the Klang of the over-fifth of the C-major Klang... the center of the mental representation lies outside it."

**Example 3** (Ch. 3, n. 41): "The side-triads (*Nebendreiklange*) of a key are simultaneous representations of two of the three principal harmonies, of which one is always understood as the principal content (consonance), the other as a foreign addition (dissonance)."

**Example 4** (Ch. 1, pp. 19-24): Schreyer's analysis of Beethoven's Fifth Symphony transition: 50 measures of apparent harmonic activity reduced to a single prolonged dominant Klang, with intermediate harmonies as "parenthetical" representations.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating Klangvertretung as chord substitution (replacing one chord with another)
  **Correction**: Klangvertretung means the representative *is* the function in modified form — the A minor chord in C major is the tonic in a different guise, not a substitute for it

- **Error**: Analyzing tones as representatives of Klange without establishing context
  **Correction**: The same tone represents different Klange depending on the tonal context; the context must be established first

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking Klangvertretung is an acoustic phenomenon
  **Clarification**: Riemann explicitly placed it in psychology: "The principle of Klang representation no longer belongs within physics, nor within physiology, but rather in psychology"

- **Confusion**: Believing all representations are equal in hierarchical status
  **Clarification**: Primary functions (T, D, S) remain conceptually central; representatives derive their meaning from these

- **Confusion**: Equating Scheinkonsonanz with dissonance in the traditional sense
  **Clarification**: Scheinkonsonanz sounds consonant but is functionally unstable — it is a relational rather than acoustic dissonance
