# Chromatic Tonality

## Quick Definition

An expanded conception of tonality that embraces chromatic as well as diatonic relations to a tonic, where harmonic coherence is maintained through common-tone relationships, voice-leading connections, and tonal reference rather than exclusively through fifth-based functional progressions.

## Formal Definition

**Chromatic tonality** (German: *Tonalitat*) represents Riemann's conception of an expanded tonal system that incorporates chromatic relations as fundamental tonal relationships, as distinguished from *Tonart* (the diatonic conception of key).

**Core Principles**:
- Tonal coherence can be maintained through chromatic as well as diatonic means
- Chromatic mediants possess independent functional status analogous to dominant/subdominant
- Voice-leading efficiency and common-tone connections can substitute for fifth-based progression
- Tonic reference persists even through extended chromatic passages
- Harmonic meaning derives from relation to tonic, not just local progression

**Functional Expansion**: In chromatic tonality, the functional categories (T, S, D) expand to include chromatic variants:
- Mediants can function as alternatives to dominants (e.g., USM resolving like a functional dominant)
- Common-tone diminished sevenths and chromatic passing chords gain structural significance
- Enharmonic relationships create multiple tonal interpretations simultaneously

**Contrast with Neo-Riemannian "Atonality"**: Where neo-Riemannian theory often posits voice-leading operations as "extratonal" or independent of key, chromatic tonality maintains that chromatic progressions typically occur *within* an expanded tonal framework that listeners of the nineteenth century understood as fundamentally tonal.

## Musical Context

Chromatic tonality emerged as a theoretical and practical response to nineteenth-century compositional developments:

- Composers from Beethoven onward increasingly employed chromatic third relations
- Traditional Roman-numeral analysis struggled to account for these relationships adequately
- Riemann's system sought to theorize these practices as extensions of tonality, not departures from it
- The distinction matters for analytical interpretation: passages are heard as tonally related, not floating free

**Historical Significance**: Chromatic tonality represents the nineteenth-century view that chromaticism *expands* tonality rather than negating it—a view somewhat at odds with later modernist narratives of tonal dissolution.

## Examples

### Basic

**Riemann's exemplary cadence** (*Musik-Lexikon*, 1882):
```
E major -> C major -> Ab major -> C major
```
This progression exemplifies *Tonalitat* by juxtaposing the tonic with its two major-third chromatic mediants rather than its two dominants. The cadence demonstrates tonal coherence through chromatic means.

**Tonal vs. "Nontonal" Interpretation**:
- Neo-Riemannian: This is a hexatonic cycle (PL cycle), operating outside tonal organization
- Chromatic Tonality: Each chord has distinct meaning relative to tonic C—E major is USM, Ab major is LFM

### From Repertoire

**Beethoven, "Emperor" Concerto, III**: The C -> Ab -> Fb major-third cycle occurs at a point of formal distance from the tonic, but each harmony maintains orientation to Eb major: C major hovers above the tonic, Ab falls within the tonic sphere, and Fb dips deeply below.

**Schubert, Piano Sonata D. 850**: Chromatic mediants (F major, C# major) function within D major tonality—F major as upper flat mediant, C# major as upper sharp mediant of the dominant—rather than as "nontonal" hexatonic elements.

## Common Confusions

- Chromatic tonality is NOT atonality—it maintains tonic reference throughout
- *Tonalitat* vs. *Tonart*: The former is Riemann's expanded chromatic conception; the latter is traditional diatonic key
- Voice-leading efficiency doesn't negate tonal meaning—it can coexist with functional interpretation
- Not all chromatic music is "nontonal" even if it can be modeled by transformational processes
- Chromatic tonality was the *contemporary* understanding—retroactive "atonal" interpretations are historically presentist
- Hexatonic and chromatic tonal interpretations of the same passage need not be mutually exclusive
