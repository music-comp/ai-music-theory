# Quick Definition

An expanded conception of tonality, corresponding to Riemann's *Tonalität*, that embraces chromatic as well as diatonic relations to a tonic, where harmonic coherence is maintained through common-tone relationships and voice-leading connections rather than exclusively through fifth-based functional progressions.

# Core Definition

Chromatic tonality represents Riemann's conception of *Tonalität* — defined in his *Musik-Lexikon* (1882) "expressly to embrace chromatic as well as diatonic relations to a tonic, in contrast to *Tonart*, the familiar diatonic conception of key" (Kopp, p. 401). In chromatic tonality, chromatic mediants possess functional status analogous to dominants and subdominants, defined by intervallic relations, voice-leading content, and qualitative harmonic change. Kopp argues that neo-Riemannian theory, by positing "mechanisms of structural coherence to which harmonic fifth relations and even the presence of a tonic are extraneous" (p. 401), has ironically "drawn these boundaries tightly back" from Riemann's own expansive view. Chromatic tonality insists that chromatic passages in nineteenth-century music remain fundamentally tonal, with "each chord having a separate and distinct harmonic meaning in relation to the key" (p. 403).

# Prerequisites

- **Tonality** — Chromatic tonality is an expansion of the basic concept of tonal organization around a tonic
- **Chromatic mediant** — The primary harmonic relationships that define chromatic tonality beyond fifth relations

# Key Properties

1. Tonal coherence is maintained through chromatic as well as diatonic means
2. Chromatic mediants possess independent functional status analogous to dominant/subdominant
3. Voice-leading efficiency and common-tone connections can operate alongside fifth-based progressions
4. Tonic reference persists even through extended chromatic passages
5. Harmonic meaning derives from each chord's relation to the tonic, not just from local voice-leading
6. The concept was Riemann's own — chromatic tonality represents the contemporary nineteenth-century understanding of this music

# Construction / Recognition

## To Analyze Within Chromatic Tonality

1. Identify the tonic (which may only be confirmed late in a passage)
2. Classify each chord's relationship to the tonic (diatonic function, chromatic mediant type, or other)
3. Note common-tone connections between successive chords
4. Distinguish direct relations (to tonic) from indirect ones (between non-tonic chords)
5. Consider how cumulative tonal memory orients the listener even through chromatic passages

# Context & Application

Kopp presents chromatic tonality as a corrective to the neo-Riemannian tendency to label chromatic passages as "extratonal" or "nontonal." He identifies an irony: "Where Riemann sought to expand the theoretical boundaries of tonality to accommodate broad aspects of nineteenth-century chromaticism, this modern model, developed a century after his and in his name, has drawn these boundaries tightly back" (p. 401). The concept is particularly relevant for analyzing music by Beethoven, Schubert, and Wolf where chromatic mediant cycles occur within a tonal framework.

**Historical note**: Kopp invokes Thomas Christensen's concept of "presentism" — the tendency to find evidence in earlier music of concepts not articulated until later (such as nontonality) — as a cautionary framework for neo-Riemannian analysis of nineteenth-century music (p. 401, n. 3).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 401, Ex. 14.1): Riemann's *Musik-Lexikon* cadence: E major - C major - Ab major - C major. This exemplifies *Tonalität* by substituting chromatic mediants for dominants in a cadential progression.

**Example 2** (pp. 402-404): Beethoven, "Emperor" Concerto, III. The descending major-third cycle (C - Ab - Fb) "derives a necessary portion of its meaning from the heard relation of each element to the tonic, even if those relations are not direct" (p. 403). The passage is "profoundly tonal."

**Example 3** (pp. 404-407): Schubert, Piano Sonata D. 850, I. F major and C# major are understood as chromatic mediants functioning within D major tonality, not as "nontonal" hexatonic elements.

**Example 4** (pp. 407-412): Wolf, "Und schläfst du, mein Mädchen." Eb major "can be seen to organize aspects of the entire song, although this does not necessarily mean that it is always present as its key" (p. 412). The tonic "exerts an influence while the music veers between various harmonic states."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Labeling a chromatic mediant progression as "nontonal" simply because it cannot be explained by fifth-based functional analysis
  **Correction**: Chromatic mediants can function tonally; the inability of traditional Roman-numeral analysis does not imply nontonality

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing chromatic tonality is the same as atonality or nontonality
  **Clarification**: Chromatic tonality explicitly maintains tonic reference; it is an *expansion* of tonality, not its negation

- **Confusion**: Thinking hexatonic and chromatic-tonal interpretations are mutually exclusive
  **Clarification**: The same passage can be modeled both ways; the question is which better captures the musical experience. Kopp argues for the tonal interpretation when tonic reference is perceptible

- **Confusion**: Assuming that "extratonal transformational processes" prove music is nontonal
  **Clarification**: "Just because the elements of the local progression are capable of being described by a mechanism independent of a tonic, we need not conclude that the progression itself is not tonal" (Kopp, p. 403)
