# Quick Definition

Passages where the tonal framework is stretched or torn, creating a "harmonic surplus" that overflows familiar tonal categories — the very passages that neo-Riemannian analysis typically takes as starting points but that Riemann sought to contain within his functional system.

# Core Definition

Tonal crisis describes moments when "the tonal bottom seems to drop out of the music" (Rings, p. 488), harmonic events overflow the bounds of familiar tonal categories, and it takes cognitive effort to maintain tonal hearing. These passages exhibit what Rings calls "harmonic surplus" or "extratonal surplus" — energy exceeding what can be contained by traditional functional labeling (pp. 488-489, 494-497). The concept is paradigmatic for understanding the divergence between Riemannian and neo-Riemannian analysis: Riemann treats such passages as confirmations of tonal law, while neo-Riemannians treat them as the analytical starting point.

# Prerequisites

- **Harmonic function** — Understanding T, S, D is necessary to see how functional labels strain at tonal crises
- **PLR transformations** — Neo-Riemannian tools provide the alternative analytical framework for these passages

# Key Properties

1. Harmonic distance: the chord is very remote from the tonic
2. Cognitive strain: effort is required to maintain tonal hearing
3. Notational disturbance: enharmonic respellings, key signature changes fracture the notation
4. Affective intensity: the passage sounds extraordinary
5. Analytical resistance: traditional functional labels become awkward and multiply modified
6. The crisis is the limit case for both paradigms: the boundary Riemann defends, the phenomenon that justifies neo-Riemannian methods

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify a Tonal Crisis

1. Look for passages where notation fractures (key signature cancellation, enharmonic respelling)
2. Test whether functional labels require multiple modifications (e.g., variant of the leading-tone change of the minor subdominant)
3. Assess cognitive effort: can you hold the tonic in your ears through the passage?
4. Check for affective intensity disproportionate to the analytical label
5. Consider whether the passage serves as a starting point for neo-Riemannian investigation

# Context & Application

Neo-Riemannian analysis characteristically begins with tonal crises rather than with normative passages. Rings notes: "One thinks here of the many analytical forays into *Parsifal* that have begun not at the work's outset, but with the most chromatically distorted version of the Grail motive, very near the end of act III" (p. 495). This starting-point choice reveals the neo-Riemannian valorization of disruption. For Riemann, by contrast, crisis passages confirm the power of the tonal system to absorb extremity.

# Examples

**Schubert Impromptu in Gb, mm. 78-82** (pp. 488-489): In just over two measures, the music progresses from tonic Gb major through B minor to G minor. "A menacing bass trill on C in measure 79 announces the imminent arrival of the latter — we hear the G-minor chord coming before it sounds." Notation fractures: the six-flat key signature is cancelled and then reinstated. Correctly spelled as Ab minor, the chord would require 14 flats.

**Riemann's response** (p. 492): The passage serves the "final consolidation of the primary key" with "wholly excellent effect." His functional label requires three modifications to basic S. This "straining of the system betrays an anxiety, an attempt to contain the harmonic extravagance."

**Neo-Riemannian response** (pp. 494-496): The passage falls within a Weitzmann region sharing common tones with an augmented triad. Voice-leading coherence is revealed, but the analysis captures coherence rather than the remarkable sound.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating tonal crisis as purely a property of the music
  **Correction**: Crisis involves the listener's cognitive relationship to the tonic — the same passage may or may not produce crisis depending on context and listening strategy

- **Error**: Assuming neo-Riemannian analysis of a crisis passage captures its remarkable sound
  **Correction**: The analysis typically reveals coherence (voice-leading logic), not the disruption that motivated the analysis

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing tonal crisis means the music has left tonality entirely
  **Clarification**: Rings emphasizes that "we can indeed hold the Gb tonic in our ears throughout the passage — but it takes some effort to do so" (p. 504). Crisis is a matter of strain, not absence.

- **Confusion**: Thinking crisis and anomaly are the same thing
  **Clarification**: Anomaly is a structural observation (the passage resists functional labeling); crisis is the experiential consequence (cognitive strain, affective intensity)
