# Quick Definition

The mental process by which a listener organizes heard tones into the representation of a Klang (major or minor triad), even when the Klang is incomplete, with consonance defined as successful unitary representation and dissonance as competing representations.

# Core Definition

**Klang-representation** (*Klangvertretung*) is the cognitive act of assigning heard tones to a single Klang -- the mental image of a complete triad. In Riemann's mature theory, this process is foundational to both consonance and dissonance (Ch. 5):

- **Consonance**: Tones that combine into a single Klang-representation
- **Dissonance**: Tones that force competing Klang-representations (Oettingen: "the simultaneous occurrence of two or more Klange," p. 184)
- **Incomplete Klangs**: The mind "completes" missing tones -- a diminished triad B-D-F is heard as representing G7 (the mind supplies the absent G as prime)

This concept bridges the Tonvorstellungen turn and practical harmony: the listener's active mental representation determines how chords are classified, not acoustic properties.

# Prerequisites

- **Harmonic dualism** -- Klang-representation operates within the dualist framework
- **Klang** -- The triadic unit being mentally represented

# Key Properties

1. Klang-representation is a mental act, not an acoustic phenomenon
2. The mind can represent a complete Klang even from partial information
3. A single Klang-representation = consonance; competing representations = dissonance
4. The diminished triad and other "incomplete" chords are heard through Klang-representation
5. Dominant seventh is an aggregate of two Klang-representations (dominant + subdominant element)

# Construction / Recognition

## How Klang-Representation Works
1. Tones are heard and the mind assigns them to a Klang (major or minor triad)
2. If all tones fit a single Klang: consonant perception
3. If tones fit multiple Klangs: dissonant perception (competing representations)
4. Missing chord tones may be "supplied" by the mind (especially if reinforced by overtones)

## Examples of Representation
- C-E-G → Klang: C major (single representation, consonant)
- C-E-G# → Competing: C major OR E major OR Ab minor (dissonant despite consonant intervals)
- B-D-F → Klang: G dominant seventh minus root (mind supplies G as prime)

# Context & Application

Klang-representation was Riemann's key concept for connecting psychological hearing to practical harmony. It explains how listeners maintain harmonic orientation despite incomplete or ambiguous chords, and it provides the cognitive basis for function theory: a chord's function depends on which Klang-representation the listener assigns.

In Rehding's Brahms analysis (Ch. 7), Klang-representation explains how different key areas "share" tones: the pitch E is prolonged through the Trio's exposition because it belongs to the Klang-representations of A minor, C major, and E minor simultaneously.

# Examples

**The augmented triad paradox** (Ch. 5, p. 186): C-E-G# contains only consonant intervals but is dissonant because no single Klang-representation can encompass all three tones.

**The fifth can be omitted in minor** (Ch. 5, p. 190): In A minor with three As in the bass, the mind represents the complete Klang (A-C-E) even without the explicit E, because the overtones of A reinforce it.

**Dominant seventh as aggregate** (Ch. 6, p. 212): The dominant seventh (e.g., A-C#-E-G in D minor) represents an aggregation of two Klangs: a+ (dominant) and the presence of G as subdominant representative.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Identifying a chord's function purely by its notes without considering Klang-representation
  **Correction**: The same collection of notes can represent different Klangs in different contexts

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Klang-representation is merely "chord identification"
  **Clarification**: It is a cognitive process that includes completion of missing tones and resolution of competing representations -- more than simple labeling
