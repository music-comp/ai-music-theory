# Harmonic Dualism

## Quick Definition

A theoretical framework asserting that major and minor triads are equal, opposite, and symmetrically constructed entities generated by inverse principles, rather than the minor being derived from or subordinate to the major.

## Formal Definition

**Harmonic dualism** is a music-theoretical perspective with two characteristic ideas:

1. **Equality**: Major and minor triads arise as two distinct but equal expressions of a single structuring principle, together forming the totality of harmonic possibilities
2. **Inversion**: Major and minor triads appear as mirror or inverted expressions of one another

**Soft dualism** exhibits only the first characteristic (equality without explicit inversion). **Hard dualism** exhibits both characteristics, viewing the minor triad as structurally inverted from the major.

In hard dualist systems, the major triad is constructed upward from its root (1:3:5 as frequencies), while the minor triad is constructed downward from its "primary tone" at the fifth (the undertone series principle). Thus C-E-G (major) mirrors as G-Eb-C (minor), with G as the generative tone.

## Historical Development

**Evolution of Riemann's position**:
- **1873-1875**: Acoustic foundation - argued for "objective existence of undertones"
- **1880s-1890s**: Increasing prevarication about acoustic basis
- **1905 "Das Problem"**: Abandoned acoustic undertones as "pseudo-logic"
- **Late period**: Psychological foundation through *Tonvorstellungen* (tone imaginations)

Key quote from Riemann's 1905 article: "The two types of relationship... the series of simple multiples with respect to frequencies (over-series), and the series of simple multiples with respect to string-lengths (under-series). These series do not require verification by way of acoustical phenomena."

**Key figures and contributions**:
- **Zarlino (1558)**: First articulated the logical opposition between arithmetic and harmonic divisions
- **Hauptmann (1853)**: Dialectical approach; major "has" intervals, minor "is had" by intervals
- **Oettingen (1866)**: Systematized dual notation; introduced "tonic" and "phonic" fundamentals
- **Riemann (1873-1919)**: Most extensive development; shift from acoustic to psychological basis

## Musical Context

**The "Problem" of Dualism**:
The central challenge: how to justify the minor triad as equally consonant with the major when:
- The overtone series generates major triads naturally
- The undertone series has no clear acoustic reality
- Practical voice leading treats major and minor similarly (both with bass as root)

**Riemann's Solutions**:
1. **Physical abandonment**: Rejected overtones as basis for *either* chord's consonance
2. **Psychological turn**: Musical hearing is "active, selective" - the mind organizes tone relationships
3. **Mathematical elegance**: Both modes reduce to the same simple ratios (4:5:6 vs. 15:12:10)

**Major = "growing intensity"** (frequencies increase)
**Minor = "accumulating mass"** (wavelengths/string-lengths increase)

## Theoretical Implications

**For chord structure**:
- Major: root at bottom (C-E-G from C)
- Minor: "root" at top in pure dualism (C minor = Eb-G-C from G)
- The fifth becomes omissible in minor because overtones reinforce it

**For functional harmony**:
- Riemann's function labels (T, S, D) work identically in both modes
- This creates apparent contradiction with pure dualism (Belinfante's critique)
- Defense: Different *Schritte* (Quintschritt vs. Gegenquintschritt) preserve inversional logic

**For form** (Rehding):
- Dualistic synthesis requires exploring both "sides" of the tonic
- Dominant side (exposition) + Subdominant side (recapitulation/coda) = complete form

## Examples

### Klumpenhouwer's Defense

The critique that Riemann's function labels contradict dualism is refuted by using proper dualist transformations:
- In C major: T to D = Quintschritt (Q)
- In C minor: T to D = Quintschritt (Q) - same transformation, different direction
- The apparent parallelism is actually inversional when properly formalized

### Rehding's Brahms Analysis

In Brahms's Clarinet Trio Op. 114:
- Exposition explores "right" (dominant) side of tonic
- Recapitulation/coda explores "left" (subdominant) side
- Dualistic synthesis achieved when both sides are traversed

## Common Confusions

1. **Dualism is not about undertones**: Riemann abandoned acoustic justification; the principle is mathematical/psychological
2. **Minor "root" confusion**: Dualists acknowledge the bass functions as practical root but maintain the fifth as the generative principle
3. **Function labels aren't anti-dualist**: Belinfante's critique fails because it applies monist metrics to dualist structures
4. **Soft vs. hard dualism**: Not all dualist theories require full inversional structure

## Contemporary Relevance

Modern neo-Riemannian theory draws on dualism for:
- **Voice-leading parsimony**: Inversional relationships reveal efficient voice leadings
- **Transformational theory**: *Schritte* and *Wechsel* preserve distance under inversion
- **Set-theoretical analysis**: Inversion as a fundamental operation alongside transposition

As Tymoczko notes: "Dualism is useful for cataloging voice-leading possibilities" even if 19th-century composers weren't explicitly thinking dualistically.
