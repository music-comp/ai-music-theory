# Quick Definition

The fundamental unresolved tension in Riemann's metric theory between his two perspectives — the a priori periodic model (notated meter) that prescribes accent patterns, and the expressed-meter criteria that derive accent from musical content — which Riemann never fully acknowledged as distinct and inconsistently prioritized.

# Core Definition

Caplin identifies a central tension in Riemann's metric theory: the coexistence of two fundamentally different approaches to determining metrical accent that Riemann "never fully acknowledged as distinct" (p. 418). The notated-meter perspective applies the eight-measure periodic model as an a priori scheme, assigning accent based on position within the prototype (even = strong, odd = weak). The expressed-meter perspective derives accent from musical content (Harmoniewirkung, durational accent, Schlusswirkung, etc.). When these perspectives agree, the analysis is unproblematic. When they conflict, Riemann inconsistently privileges one over the other — sometimes invoking expressed-meter criteria to justify rebarring, other times abandoning expressed-meter findings to preserve his a priori model. Caplin notes that "while Riemann failed to realize the full potential of his own skepticism on the status of notation, his attempt to account for the origin of accent on the basis of musical content alone remains a significant achievement in the history of metrical theory" (abstract, p. 415).

# Prerequisites

- **Expressed meter** — One of the two perspectives in tension
- **Notated meter** — The other perspective in tension

# Key Properties

1. Riemann never explicitly acknowledged the two perspectives as distinct
2. The a priori model (notated meter) prescribes accent independent of content
3. The expressed-meter criteria derive accent from musical content
4. When they conflict, Riemann inconsistently prioritizes one or the other
5. The tension reveals the difficulty of reconciling prescriptive and descriptive approaches to meter
6. Modern metric theory has generally separated these concerns (e.g., Lerdahl/Jackendoff's well-formedness rules vs. preference rules)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify the Tension in Analysis

1. Apply the eight-measure periodic model to assign functions
2. Apply expressed-meter criteria to determine accent from musical content
3. Note where the two approaches agree and where they conflict
4. Observe which perspective Riemann privileges at each conflict point
5. The inconsistency in his choices reveals the underlying theoretical tension

# Context & Application

The tension is not merely a historical curiosity but touches on fundamental questions in metric theory: Is meter an abstract organizational scheme applied to music (a priori), or does it emerge from the music's content (a posteriori)? Riemann's inability to resolve this tension foreshadows debates in modern metric theory between "projection" approaches (where accent is content-derived) and "entrainment" approaches (where accent comes from an internalized schema).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 430): Beethoven, Op. 31, No. 1, iii. Harmoniewirkung (expressed meter) suggests accent on measure 3, but Riemann overrides this to preserve the a priori model, using melodic imitation to justify accent on measure 2 instead.

**Example 2** (p. 429): Beethoven, Op. 27, No. 1, i. Riemann invokes durational accent (expressed meter) to justify rebarring, contradicting the original notation (notated meter). Here expressed meter trumps notation.

**Example 3** (p. 431): Mozart, K. 283, ii. Riemann uses Schlusswirkung to argue that bar lines are in the wrong position. Here expressed meter is privileged over the composer's notation.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming Riemann consistently privileges one perspective
  **Correction**: He inconsistently switches between them, sometimes even within a single analysis

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the tension invalidates Riemann's theory
  **Clarification**: Caplin argues the expressed-meter dimension, despite its inconsistent application, represents "a significant achievement in the history of metrical theory"
