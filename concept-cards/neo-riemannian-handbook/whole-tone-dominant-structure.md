# Quick Definition

The [02468] pentachord formed by combining two [026] antitonic trichords from a nonatonic system, which Harrison identifies as an "agglomeration" of antitonic energy equivalent to the whole-tone dominant structures discussed by early twentieth-century theorists.

# Core Definition

Harrison shows that when two [026] antitonic trichords from the same nonatonic system are combined, the resulting [02468] pentachord is "an antitonic discord of mass and energy greater than the sum of its constituent [026] elements, because it embeds four other forms of [026] besides the originating pair" (Harrison, p. 561). This structure connects to the "whole-tone dominant" structures that Horace Alden Miller devoted an entire chapter to in his 1930 treatise on "modern harmonic problems" (p. 561). Harrison argues it is "fruitful to conceive of them...as agglomerations of [026] antitonic trichords that could be potentially useful in some extended tonal context" (p. 561), reviving "an interesting and useful branch of harmonic theory that seemed to dead-end after the work of Persichetti and Ulehla" (p. 562).

# Key Properties

1. Formed by combining two [026] trichords from the same nonatonic system
2. Contains six forms of [026] (the two originals plus four secondary)
3. Greater antitonic energy than the sum of its parts
4. In whole-tone space, [026] is fully parsimonious — the [02468] pentachord is its natural habitat
5. Can serve as a pivot chord between sum classes (contains secondary [026]s from different classes)
6. Corresponds to the "altered dominant" of jazz/extended tonal theory

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct a Whole-Tone Dominant Structure

1. Take two [026] trichords from the same nonatonic sum class
2. Combine them: the result is a [02468] pentachord
3. Identify the six embedded [026] trichords
4. Note which sum classes the secondary trichords belong to
5. In practice: take a V7 chord and add both raised and lowered fifths

# Context & Application

Harrison uses the [02468] pentachord to connect neo-Riemannian theory with early twentieth-century harmonic speculation (Miller, Hull, Persichetti, Ulehla) about extended sonorities. The structure also connects to Kurth's "intensive alteration style" and to jazz theory's treatment of altered dominants. By showing these chords as [026] agglomerations, Harrison provides a functional account of sonorities usually analyzed as pitch-class sets.

# Examples

**Example 20.9** (pp. 560-561): Paired combinations of [026] trichords voiced as normally spaced chords, resolving to [037] triads. The two trichords are distinguished by open and filled noteheads.

**Pivot chord function** (p. 563): "The pentachord can serve as a pivot chord since it can be built from two antitonic trichords from either [sum class], with 0 voice-leading displacement in both."

**Chromatic intensification** (p. 555): Harrison's example 20.3c replaces Santa's whole-tone bass with a chromatic one, showing that "the 'new' notes of my chromatic scale create other forms of the [026] trichord...the proliferation of these [026] trichords endows the 'altered' dominant tetrachords with their characteristic functional energy gain."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating [02468] as a single undifferentiated sonority
  **Correction**: It is an agglomeration of six [026] trichords with specific functional implications

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking whole-tone dominants are purely a jazz phenomenon
  **Clarification**: Harrison traces them to Rameau's fundamental bass theory and early twentieth-century harmonic speculation, connecting them to Riemannian function theory
