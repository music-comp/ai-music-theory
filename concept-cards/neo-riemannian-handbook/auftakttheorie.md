# Quick Definition

Riemann's fundamental principle that all musical meter is end-accented at every hierarchical level, with unaccented events functioning as upbeats (*Auftakte*) directed toward accented goals, making the eighth measure of a period the invariant metrical goal.

# Core Definition

Auftakttheorie ("upbeat theory") is Riemann's foundational metric principle holding that in all cases and at every level of metrical organization, the accented event is an *end* — a goal of musical motion — not a beginning. Caplin identifies this as flowing from Riemann's concept of "active hearing" (*aktives Hören*), in which "the listener will not passively relate an unaccent back to a preceding accent but instead will feel the unaccent impelling forward to the following accent" (p. 418). The principle applies consistently from the beat level through the measure, two-measure, four-measure, and eight-measure levels. Even-numbered measures are always "heavy" (*schwere Zeiten*), and the final measure 8 is the invariant goal of every period. As Caplin summarizes, Riemann's theory holds that "the 'naturally given dynamic form' of the upbeat to downbeat impels all levels of metrical organization" (p. 418).

# Prerequisites

- **Expressed meter** — Auftakttheorie provides the theoretical framework that expressed-meter criteria support
- **Hierarchical metric organization** — The multi-level structure within which upbeat-to-downbeat impulses operate

# Key Properties

1. Every rhythmic grouping at every hierarchical level is end-accented
2. Even-numbered measures (2, 4, 6, 8) are metrically strong ("schwere Zeiten")
3. The 8-function is invariant — every period must conclude with this terminal accent
4. A period can never end on measures 1-4; incomplete periods are always versions of 5-8
5. Local upbeats are more dispensable than local downbeats
6. There can be downbeats without upbeats, but never upbeats without downbeats
7. First measures of periods are NEVER metrically strong in Riemann's mature theory

# Construction / Recognition

## The Hierarchical Upbeat-Downbeat Pattern

```
Measures:   1   2   3   4   5   6   7   8
            U   A   U   A   U   A   U   A    (measure level)
            U---A       U---A                  (2-measure level)
            U-----------A                      (4-measure level)
            U-----------------------A          (8-measure level: goal)
```

Where U = unaccent (upbeat), A = accent (downbeat)

# Context & Application

Auftakttheorie reflects Riemann's broader aesthetic philosophy of goal-directed musical motion. Caplin notes that "Riemann brings to culmination the tendencies of Hauptmann and others to view the relationship of 'heavy' and 'light' measures as essentially equivalent to that of heavy and light beats" (p. 417). The theory is directly linked to Riemann's harmonic function theory: even-numbered measures carry *Harmoniewirkung* (harmonic effect), with harmonic changes typically falling on strong measures.

**Critique**: The theory conflicts fundamentally with "initial accentuation" (Hauptmann), which holds that the first presentation of something is metrically stronger. Caplin notes this as a significant theoretical tension, as does Dahlhaus. Most modern theorists reject universal end-accentuation.

**Analytical implication**: Riemann's controversial "rebarrings" of classical works — moving bar lines to align notation with his theory — are direct consequences of Auftakttheorie.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 421, Ex. 15.1, referenced from Ch. 15): Riemann's prototypical eight-measure period construction showing upbeat/downbeat impulse at multiple levels, with measures 1-2, 3-4, 5-6, 7-8 forming upbeat-downbeat pairs at the two-measure level.

**Example 2** (referenced via Burnham, p. 442, Ex. 16.1): Beethoven, Op. 31, No. 1, Rondo theme. Each even-numbered downbeat is a harmonic resolution; the answering relationships are "audible at three different levels: 2 answers 1; 3-4 answers 1-2; 5-8 answers 1-4."

**Example 3** (p. 442): Burnham notes that if a sounding first measure is strong, Riemann "posits a 1 function that precedes it as an imaginary upbeat" — preserving the principle that first measures cannot be strong.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Applying Auftakttheorie only to anacrusis (pickup notes)
  **Correction**: The theory applies at ALL hierarchical levels, not just to literal upbeat notes before bar lines

- **Error**: Assuming Riemann's "even = strong" means even measures are dynamically louder
  **Correction**: "Strong" refers to metrical weight and goal-directedness, not loudness

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking Auftakttheorie is derived empirically from individual pieces
  **Clarification**: It is an a priori scheme — a theoretical principle applied to all music, though expressed-meter criteria can be invoked to support it

- **Confusion**: Believing Auftakttheorie and initial accentuation can be reconciled
  **Clarification**: Caplin and Dahlhaus agree these principles are fundamentally incompatible — one must choose

- **Confusion**: Assuming all music literally begins with an upbeat note
  **Clarification**: The theory holds that first *measures* function as upbeats to second measures, regardless of whether there is a literal anacrusis
