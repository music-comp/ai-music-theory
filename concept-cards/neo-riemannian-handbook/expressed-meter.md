# Quick Definition

The metric interpretation that arises from the musical content itself — through parameters like harmonic change, duration, melodic contour, and cadential function — independent of how the music is notated, forming one of Riemann's two fundamental perspectives on meter alongside notated meter.

# Core Definition

Expressed meter is Caplin's term for the perspective in which "the musical events themselves can express, so to speak, their own metrical interpretation independent of the notation" (p. 418). In this view, "the interaction of certain musical parameters (such as duration, motivic contour, harmonic change) can engender a sense of meter in a listener who is unaware of how the music may be actually notated" (abstract, p. 415). Caplin identifies Riemann as "perhaps the first theorist to appreciate fully the need to explain how accents arise from the music itself" (p. 426), making this a foundational moment in the history of metric theory. Riemann develops five criteria for expressed meter: Harmoniewirkung, melodic imitation, durational accent, tonal accent, and Schlusswirkung.

# Prerequisites

- **Notated meter** — Understanding expressed meter requires understanding what it contrasts with: meter determined by notation conventions

# Key Properties

1. Meter is derived from musical content, not from notation
2. Five criteria generate expressed meter: Harmoniewirkung, melodic imitation, durational accent, tonal accent, and Schlusswirkung
3. A listener without access to the score can perceive metric organization through these criteria
4. Expressed meter may conflict with notated meter, leading to Riemann's controversial "rebarrings"
5. Harmoniewirkung (harmonic change) is the primary criterion, with others serving as "assistants"
6. The criteria can yield contradictory results, requiring analytical judgment

# Construction / Recognition

## Riemann's Five Criteria for Expressed Meter

1. **Harmoniewirkung** (Effect of Harmony): Moments of harmonic change are metrically accented
2. **Melodic imitation**: Repetition or response creates accent on the "answer"
3. **Durational accent**: Longer note values receive greater metrical weight
4. **Tonal accent**: Return to tonic is associated with metrical emphasis
5. **Schlusswirkung** (Cadential effect): Points of closure create accent

## To Apply Expressed-Meter Analysis

1. Identify harmonic changes — these are the primary accent indicators
2. Look for melodic repetitions where the "answer" receives accent
3. Note relatively long note values as durational accent indicators
4. Identify tonic returns and cadential arrivals
5. Synthesize the criteria to determine the expressed metric pattern
6. Compare with notated meter to identify agreements or discrepancies

# Context & Application

Caplin's chapter frames Riemann's metric theory through the distinction between expressed and notated meter, which he identifies as "two general perspectives" that Riemann "never fully acknowledged as distinct" (p. 418). While Riemann's attempt to account for accent on the basis of musical content alone is "a significant achievement in the history of metrical theory" (abstract), he "failed to realize the full potential of his own skepticism on the status of notation" — often abandoning expressed-meter analysis when results conflicted with his a priori Auftakttheorie model.

**Analytical implication**: When notation seems to place bar lines incorrectly relative to musical content, expressed meter provides justification for "rebarring" — though this practice is controversial.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 426): Beethoven, Piano Sonata Op. 27, No. 1, i. Riemann argues the notation places long values on upbeats, creating "incessantly hindering" syncopation effects. His rebarring aligns durational accents with metrical downbeats.

**Example 2** (p. 427): Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 283, ii. Riemann claims all Schlusswirkungen fall "in the middle of notated measures," leading him to conclude Mozart "left out the bar lines of the accented measures."

**Example 3** (p. 430): Beethoven, "Leonore Overture" No. 2, mm. 57-65. Melodic imitation (through repetition and directional inversion) creates expressed accent in the second and fourth double-measures, confirming the Auftakttheorie model.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming expressed meter always reveals the "correct" meter while notation is "wrong"
  **Correction**: Both perspectives are analytically valid; expressed meter provides an alternative lens, not a correction

- **Error**: Treating the five criteria as equally weighted
  **Correction**: Harmoniewirkung is the primary criterion; the others are "assistants" invoked when harmonic evidence is ambiguous

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking Riemann consistently applied expressed-meter principles
  **Clarification**: Riemann often deferred to his a priori Auftakttheorie model when expressed-meter criteria contradicted it, revealing "an inconsistency or at least a tension in his work" (Caplin)

- **Confusion**: Believing expressed-meter criteria always agree with each other
  **Clarification**: The criteria can yield contradictory results (e.g., Harmoniewirkung suggesting accent on measure 3 while melodic imitation suggests measure 2), requiring analytical judgment
