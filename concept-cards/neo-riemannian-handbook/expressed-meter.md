# Expressed Meter

## Quick Definition

The metric interpretation that arises from the musical content itself—through parameters like pitch, duration, harmonic change, and motivic contour—independent of how the music is notated, as distinguished from notated meter determined by time signatures and bar lines.

## Formal Definition

**Expressed meter** is a theoretical perspective in which "the musical events themselves can express, so to speak, their own metrical interpretation independent of the notation" (Caplin). This stands in contrast to **notated meter**, where metrical interpretation derives from notational conventions like time signatures and bar lines.

**Key Distinction**:
- **Notated meter**: Metrical accent determined by notation mechanics (bar lines, time signature)
- **Expressed meter**: Metrical accent arising from musical content parameters

**Riemann's Five Criteria for Expressed Meter**:
1. **Harmoniewirkung** (Effect of Harmony): Harmonic change creates accent
2. **Melodic Imitation**: Repetition/response relationships create accent
3. **Durational Accent**: Longer note values receive greater metrical weight
4. **Tonal Accent**: Return to tonic associated with metrical emphasis
5. **Schlusswirkung** (Cadential Effect): Points of closure create accent

**Historical Significance**: Caplin identifies Riemann as "perhaps the first theorist to appreciate fully the need to explain how accents arise from the music itself," making this a foundational moment in metric theory.

## Musical Context

The expressed vs. notated meter distinction addresses fundamental questions about metric perception:

- How do listeners perceive meter without seeing the score?
- Can composers' notation sometimes misrepresent the "true" metric organization?
- What makes certain beats feel accented regardless of bar line placement?

**Analytical Implications**:
- Performers may need to project expressed meter even when it conflicts with notation
- Analysis should account for discrepancies between notated and expressed meter
- Riemann's controversial "rebarrings" of classical works reflect expressed-meter analysis

**Limitation**: Riemann often abandoned expressed-meter analysis when results conflicted with his a priori *Auftakttheorie* model, revealing tension between his two theoretical commitments.

## Examples

### Basic

**Expressed vs. Notated meter in practice**:

Consider a phrase where harmony changes on beat 3 of each measure:
- Notated meter suggests beat 1 is accented (bar line convention)
- Expressed meter (via Harmoniewirkung) suggests beat 3 carries the accent
- A listener without the score might perceive a different metric organization

**Duration creating expressed accent**:
```
  quarter  quarter  HALF
    weak     weak   STRONG (expressed)
```
The half note receives expressed accent through durational differentiation.

### From Repertoire

**Beethoven, Piano Sonata Op. 27, No. 1, i**: Riemann argues that the correct expressed meter places bar lines differently than Beethoven's notation, based on where longer note values fall. His rebarring aligns durational accents with metrical downbeats.

**Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 283, ii**: Riemann claims all *Schlusswirkungen* (cadential points) fall in the middle of notated measures, suggesting Mozart "left out the bar lines of the accented measures" rather than the unaccented ones.

**Beethoven, "Leonore Overture" No. 2**: Melodic imitation (through repetition and directional inversion) creates expressed accent in the second and fourth double-measures.

## Common Confusions

- Expressed meter is not always "correct" vs. notated meter being "wrong"—both represent valid analytical perspectives
- Riemann did NOT consistently apply expressed-meter principles—he often deferred to his notated-meter model
- Not all musical parameters create accent equally—they can conflict with each other
- Expressed meter requires recognizing that listeners without scores still perceive metric organization
- The criteria for expressed meter can yield contradictory results (e.g., Harmoniewirkung vs. melodic imitation)
- Rebarring music is controversial—it may reveal expressed meter but can also impose theoretical bias
