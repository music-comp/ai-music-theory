# Quick Definition

The minor mode without the raised leading tone — using a minor dominant rather than a major one — which Riemann sought to rehabilitate as an equal partner to major, arguing that folk music of the European fringes (Scandinavia, Celtic lands) preserved this mode in its "pure" form.

# Core Definition

The **pure minor mode** (German: *reines Moll*) uses only the natural minor scale without raising the seventh degree. This means the dominant chord is minor (not major with leading tone), resulting in a distinctly different tonal character from common-practice minor.

In *Folkloristische Tonalitätsstudien*, Riemann argued that "newer music prefers the major scale somewhat — so much so that it has also affected the minor mode by adding alien elements to it that actually belong to the major mode, with the effect that the specifically minor melody and harmony had almost disappeared from consciousness" (Ch. 4, p. 152, quoting Riemann). "It was only the rise of a national Nordic music (Hartmann, Gade, Grieg) that drew attention to the peculiar effects of pure minor again."

The pure minor was an integral part of Riemann's dualistic project: if major and minor are polar opposites of equal theoretical standing, then minor should have its own authentic dominant (minor, with whole-tone approach from below) rather than borrowing the leading-tone from major. Oettingen had previously suggested reharmonizing Beethoven's Scottish folk-song settings to demonstrate how pure minor might work; Riemann offered his own versions in *Folkloristische Tonalitätsstudien* (Ch. 4, n. 53).

# Key Properties

1. Natural minor scale without raised seventh degree
2. Minor dominant chord (no leading tone)
3. Distinct cadential character: whole-tone approach to tonic from below, not semitone
4. Equal theoretical status with major in dualistic theory
5. Preserved in folk musics of Scandinavia, Celtic lands, and other "peripheral" European traditions
6. Common-practice minor (with raised seventh) is, in this view, a "corruption" by major-mode elements

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Pure Minor
1. The seventh degree is NOT raised (no leading tone)
2. The dominant chord is minor (e.g., in A minor: E-G-B, not E-G#-B)
3. Cadences use whole-tone approach (G→A) rather than semitone (G#→A)
4. No harmonic minor or melodic minor alterations

# Context & Application

Riemann's interest in pure minor was connected to his broader agenda in *Folkloristische Tonalitätsstudien*: proving that the major-minor system evolved equally from pentatonic underpinnings, demonstrating "not only that the major-minor system had evolved from pentatonic underpinnings but also that the two modern modes were equal" (Ch. 4, p. 153).

This was supported by his interest in Scandinavian and Celtic music, where he hoped "that the minor mode may have remained in a purer state in these 'less civilized' parts" of Europe (Ch. 4, p. 153).

Louis's five tonal "genders" (Ch. 1) formalized the concept: "pure minor" was one of five tonal types, using minor tonic, minor subdominant, and minor dominant.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Equating pure minor with the natural minor scale as used in common-practice tonal music
  **Correction**: In common practice, natural minor is typically mixed with harmonic minor (raised 7th). Riemann's "pure minor" deliberately excludes all major-mode borrowings.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking pure minor is Riemann's invention
  **Clarification**: The mode exists in folk music traditions; Riemann theorized it as the authentic minor, arguing that common-practice minor is a hybrid
