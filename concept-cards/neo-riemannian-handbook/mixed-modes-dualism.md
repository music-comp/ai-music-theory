# Quick Definition

In Hauptmann's and Riemann's dualist terminology, the distinction between "pure" modes (where all primary triads share the same quality) and "mixed" modes (*Dur-Moll* and *Moll-Dur*), corresponding to harmonic minor and harmonic major respectively, where one primary triad is borrowed from the opposite mode.

# Core Definition

Klumpenhouwer's analysis of Beethoven's First Symphony (Ch. 6) distinguishes between:

- **Pure minor** (*natürliches Moll*): All primary triads are minor (°a, °e, °b in A minor) -- corresponds to natural minor
- **Mixed minor** (*Dur-Moll Tonartsystem*): Minor tonic and subdominant with major dominant (°a, a+, °d) -- corresponds to harmonic minor
- **Pure major**: All primary triads are major (c+, g+, f+) -- standard major
- **Mixed major** (*Moll-Dur*): Major tonic and dominant with minor subdominant (c+, g+, °c) -- corresponds to harmonic major (less common)

The "dual" of mixed minor is mixed major: "the characteristic transformations linking the primary triads of one tonality are reversed in the other" (Ch. 6, Examples 6.9-6.10).

# Prerequisites

- **Harmonic dualism** -- Mixed modes are analyzed within the dualist framework

# Key Properties

1. Pure modes use only Quintschritt/Gegenquintschritt to connect primary triads
2. Mixed modes introduce Seitenwechsel (mode exchange) for one primary triad
3. D mixed minor and C mixed major are "dual" tonalities
4. The major dominant in minor (harmonic minor) is Riemann's mixed mode, not an anomaly
5. The "impossible" major subdominant in the minor mode is excluded because its dualist root would be too distant

# Construction / Recognition

## Mapping Mixed Minor (D minor)
1. Tonic: °a (A minor)
2. Dominant: a+ (A major -- borrowed from opposite mode via Seitenwechsel)
3. Subdominant: °d (D minor -- natural)
4. The progression °a → a+ involves Seitenwechsel, not Quintschritt

## Mapping Mixed Major (C major with minor subdominant)
1. Tonic: c+ (C major)
2. Dominant: g+ (G major -- natural)
3. Subdominant: °c (C minor -- borrowed from opposite mode via Seitenwechsel)
4. The progression c+ → °c involves Seitenwechsel

# Context & Application

The mixed-mode distinction is central to Klumpenhouwer's Beethoven analysis (Ch. 6, pp. 211-213). The extension of Ged establishes D mixed minor; the extension of Ged' establishes C mixed major. Their structural duality -- where "the Ged account was generated from the Ged' account by replacing the words" systematically -- demonstrates how dualist analysis reveals harmonic symmetries.

Rehding (Ch. 7) notes that Riemann considered the major subdominant in minor "impossible" because it would place two primary triads more than a fifth apart in dualist space -- exceeding the first degree of relationship.

# Examples

**D mixed minor** (Example 6.9): °a (tonic) connected to a+ (dominant, via Seitenwechsel) and °d (subdominant, via Gegenquintschritt).

**C mixed major** (Example 6.10): c+ (tonic) connected to g+ (dominant, via Quintschritt) and °c (subdominant, via Seitenwechsel).

**The "impossible" D major in A minor** (Ch. 7, n. 50): Riemann's explanation: d+ as subdominant would have its dualist root two fifths from the tonic's root, exceeding first-degree relationship.

  **Correction**: It is systematically accounted for as "mixed minor" with a clearly defined topological structure
