# Quick Definition
Third substitution replaces a root position chord with a first-inversion chord a third below (or vice versa), preserving the bass note while subtly altering the harmonic content — the diatonic analogue of chromatic major-third substitution.

# Core Definition
In third substitution, a root position chord is replaced with a first-inversion chord on the same bass note, or vice versa. For example, root position IV (F major, with F in the bass) can be replaced by ii6 (D minor in first inversion, also with F in the bass). The substitute chord shares the bass note and one upper note, while altering one pitch by step. This explains why so many third-related chord pairs can play similar functional roles: I/vi6, vi/IV6, IV/ii6, vii°/V6. The challenge in describing functional harmony is to make room for both the root-functional (Roman numeral) and figured-bass perspectives; the thirds-based model does so by placing third-related triads adjacent to one another, asserting that they can typically substitute for each other in the rightward progression from tonic to dominant.

# Prerequisites
- Common tones and harmonic similarity between third-related chords
- The circle of thirds as both contrapuntal and harmonic structure

# Key Properties
1. Preserves the bass note while changing one upper voice by step
2. Creates functionally equivalent pairs: I/vi6, vi/IV6, IV/ii6, vii°/V6
3. Geometrically represented as moving to a nearest neighbor on the circle of thirds
4. Explains the relationship between descending-fifths and "down a third, up a step" sequences
5. Bridges the Roman numeral and figured-bass analytical traditions

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Take a root position chord (e.g., IV = F-A-C)
2. Replace it with the first-inversion triad a third below (ii6 = D-F-A, bass F)
3. The bass and one upper note are preserved; one note changes by step

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for a chord substitution that preserves the bass note
2. Check if the substitute is a first-inversion chord whose root is a third below the original
3. In sequences, look for alternation between root position and first-inversion versions of the same pattern

# Context & Application
Third substitution is a powerful analytical tool for understanding the fluidity with which composers move between related sequences. Bach and Mozart, for instance, seem to view the descending-fifths and "down a third, up a step" sequences as fundamentally similar — they are related by third substitution. The concept also explains how Riemannian function theory groups third-related chords under the same "function" (though Tymoczko's model is more nuanced than Riemann's).

# Examples
**Example 1** (Fig. 7.3.5c, p. 260): In Mozart's first Piano Sonata K. 279, the development section alters the "down a third, up a step" sequence to descending fifths — a minimal perturbation (third substitution) on the circle of thirds.

**Example 2** (Fig. 7.3.6, p. 261): In Bach's Ab major fugue (WTC II), the "down a third, up a step" sequence (mm. 6-7) and descending-fifths sequence (mm. 13-15) are related by third substitution.

**Example 3** (Fig. 7.3.8, p. 262): Bach's Art of the Fugue, Contrapunctus X, mm. 62-66 — root substitution over a sequential bass changes one sequence type into the other.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming third substitution always produces an identical functional effect
  **Correction**: The substitute chord is *similar* but not identical; the substitution subtly alters harmonic content

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating diatonic third substitution (Chapter 7) with chromatic major-third substitution (Chapter 8)
  **Clarification**: Diatonic third substitution replaces a chord with its diatonic third-neighbor; chromatic major-third substitution replaces a chord with its chromatic major-third transposition — much more disruptive
