# Quick Definition
The ii-V-I progression is the fundamental harmonic schema of jazz, deriving from a basic nineteenth-century voice-leading pattern connecting fifth-related diatonic seventh chords by descending stepwise motion in the upper voices.

# Core Definition
Basic jazz harmony derives from an elementary voice-leading schema where fifth-related diatonic seventh chords are connected by descending stepwise voice leading. The upper voices of a series of ninth chords (ii9-V13-Imaj9) move by step while the bass leaps by fifth. The essential voice-leading skeleton consists of: introducing scale degree 4 moving from tonic to predominant, resolving 1->7 from predominant to dominant, and resolving 4->3 coupled with the return of scale degree 1 from dominant to tonic. Jazz musicians use this schema almost to the exclusion of other progressions, preventing confusion between chords with closely related pitch content.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of diatonic seventh chords
- Familiarity with descending-fifth voice leading

# Key Properties
1. Derived from nineteenth-century descending-fifth voice leading
2. Upper voices move by step; bass leaps by fifth
3. Three essential voice-leading motions define harmonic function
4. Used almost to the exclusion of other progressions in jazz
5. Jazz tunes repeat the same progressions, giving listeners many hearings
6. Many ostensibly distinct tunes share the same progression (rhythm changes, blues)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Start with diatonic seventh chords on the second, fifth, and first scale degrees
2. Voice the upper parts so that they descend by step
3. The bass leaps by descending fifth
4. Embellish with ninths, thirteenths, and other extensions
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Listen for root motion by descending fifth (or ascending fourth)
2. Identify the three chord qualities: minor seventh, dominant seventh, major seventh
3. The progression typically occupies two to four measures

# Context & Application
The ii-V-I is so fundamental that a "substantial fraction of the jazz repertoire" is based on either it, the blues progression, or "rhythm changes" (George Gershwin's "I Got Rhythm"). Classical anticipations appear in Ravel, Grieg, and Scriabin.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating jazz harmony as fundamentally different from classical harmony
  **Correction**: The ii-V-I derives directly from nineteenth-century voice-leading practices

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the ii-V-I requires specific voicings
  **Clarification**: As long as the essential voice-leading skeleton is clear, notes can be freely added
