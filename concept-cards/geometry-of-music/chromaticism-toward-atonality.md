# Quick Definition
Incessant chromatic voice leading produces saturated pitch-class space without tonal center, making the transition from late Romantic chromaticism to second Viennese school atonality a relatively small step — though musicians who rejected this path needed to devise entirely new approaches to tonality.

# Core Definition
When chromatic techniques are used incessantly (as in Strauss's Salome), they produce textures whose tonality can be difficult to discern: chromatic macroharmony, rapid pitch-class circulation, and absence of centricity. Chapter 5 already noted that chromatic tonality and free atonality share similar statistical profiles. From here it is a small step to the more robustly atonal works of Schoenberg and the second Viennese school. The claim of historical inevitability is "not obviously wrong" from this perspective. However, musicians who recoiled from post-Wagnerian extremes but did not want to write traditional music needed to go "back to the drawing board, devising new approaches that could coexist more peacefully with the five basic components of tonality." This sets up Chapter 9's discussion of twentieth-century scalar techniques.

# Key Properties
1. Increasing chromaticism produces saturated pitch-class space
2. Chromatic tonality and free atonality share statistical profiles
3. The transition to atonality is small once chromaticism overwhelms function
4. Alternative paths existed: new scalar approaches (Chapter 9)
5. The trajectory was historically contingent, not strictly inevitable
6. It explains both the push toward atonality and the pull toward new tonal approaches

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Saturated macroharmony approaching all 12 pitch classes
2. Absence of clear tonal center despite triadic/seventh-chord surface
3. Rapid pitch-class circulation
4. Chromaticism no longer decorating function but replacing it

# Context & Application
This trajectory frames the entire subsequent discussion of twentieth-century music (Chapters 9-10). Understanding how chromaticism naturally leads toward atonality helps explain both why atonality emerged and why other composers (Debussy, Ravel, Shostakovich, Reich) sought alternative paths through scalar techniques.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking the progression to atonality was the only possible outcome
  **Correction**: Many composers found alternative paths (scalar techniques, jazz harmony, etc.)

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Equating "chromatic" with "atonal"
  **Clarification**: Chromaticism can coexist with tonality (Chopin, Wagner); it approaches atonality only when it saturates pitch-class space and eliminates centricity
