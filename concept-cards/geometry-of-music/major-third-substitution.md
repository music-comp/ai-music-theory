# Quick Definition
Chromatic major-third substitution replaces a triad with its major-third transposition in a sequence, producing harmonic variety while preserving the descending voice-leading motion — the chromatic analogue of diatonic third substitution, but significantly more disruptive.

# Core Definition
Just as diatonic third substitution replaces a chord with its third-related neighbor (Chapter 7), chromatic major-third substitution replaces a triad with a triad whose root is a major third away. Since major-third-related triads are adjacent in three-note chord space and connected by efficient voice leading, this substitution preserves the descending contrapuntal motion while dramatically altering the harmonic content. Starting from a sequence of semitonally descending triads, one can derive six different two-chord sequences using only major chords, and nine using major and minor chords, all by applying major-third substitution. The descending voice leading creates a "palpable sinking feeling" that contrasts with the buoyant harmony — an effect used in both classical and popular music.

# Prerequisites
- The major-third system and cubic lattice geometry
- Efficient voice leading between major-third-related triads

# Key Properties
1. Much more disruptive than diatonic third substitution (preserving fewer notes)
2. Preserves the overall descending voice-leading trajectory
3. Generates multiple sequence types from one basic chromatic descent
4. Creates a characteristic contrast between sinking voice leading and rising harmony
5. Found in Schubert, pop music, and rock

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Write a chromatic descending sequence of triads (semitone by semitone)
2. Replace alternate chords with their major-third transposition
3. The voice leading remains largely descending while root motion changes
4. Different choices of which chords to substitute produce different sequence types

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. A triadic progression with root motion by major third or unusual chromatic intervals
2. Underlying voice leading that predominantly descends
3. Sequences that can be derived from a simpler chromatic descent

# Context & Application
Schubert's Quartett-Satz contains two sequences using different paths through the cubic lattice — one with I-V progressions descending by major second, another moving upward by fifths between major and minor. Both can be derived by major-third substitution from simpler chromatic sequences. In popular music, similar effects appear: "Natural Woman" uses the sinking voice leading to convey anomie, while "Hey Joe" mirrors the narrator's descent into depravity.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming major-third substitution preserves harmonic similarity
  **Correction**: Unlike diatonic third substitution, it dramatically changes the harmony; the similarity lies in voice-leading motion, not harmonic content

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the descending voice leading requires descending root motion
  **Clarification**: Root motion can ascend (by fifth, for example) while voices still descend
