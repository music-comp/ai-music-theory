# Quick Definition
A compositional technique where efficient voice leading between chords determines the musical progression, with scales arising as secondary byproducts that flesh out the chordal skeleton.

# Core Definition
In chord-first composition, the chordal level predominates: efficient voice leadings connect a sequence of chords, and these chords give rise to a series of scales at the musical surface. The scales need not have any independent logic -- they are related only by virtue of the chords they contain. For example, a semitonal voice leading like (E, G#, B, D) -> (F, Ab, Bb, D) might generate an E acoustic scale followed by a Bb mixolydian scale. The technique augments nineteenth-century chordal procedures with additional scales that "piggyback" on the chordal level.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of chord-scale compatibility
- Familiarity with efficient voice leading between chords

# Key Properties
1. Chords determine the musical logic; scales are secondary
2. Exploits efficient (often semitonal) voice leading between chords
3. Scales on their own may seem arbitrary without the chordal logic
4. Extends nineteenth-century chromatic voice leading with scalar awareness
5. The technique has nineteenth-century antecedents (e.g., Schubert modulations)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Begin with a sequence of chords connected by efficient voice leading
2. Assign a compatible scale to each chord
3. The scales need not be closely related to each other -- their connection is through the chords
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for passages with clear chordal voice-leading logic
2. Check whether scales change with each chord but lack independent scalar logic
3. The chordal progression should be imaginable as composed prior to the scales

# Context & Application
Chord-first composition appears across twentieth-century styles from Russian modernism to American minimalism to film music. The major-third system (juxtaposing triads related by major third) is a particularly common chord-first pattern, passing from Schubert's Vienna through Prokofiev and Shostakovich to contemporary composers like Glass and Reich.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 327, Fig. 9.1.3): Scriabin's Prelude Op. 48 No. 2 -- three upper voices contain standard nineteenth-century semitonal voice leading connecting D minor, Bb minor, and an incomplete C major seventh. Each chord is accompanied by a different scale (F, Db, and G diatonic). Chords clearly lead the scales.

**Example 2** (p. 332-334): Grieg's "Drommesyn" Op. 62 No. 5 -- descending semitonal voice leading connects familiar seventh chords, with each chord fleshed out by a diatonic scale. The scales alone seem arbitrary; the chordal logic makes them coherent.

**Example 3** (p. 334-336): Debussy's "Fetes" -- the central section exploits all different types of crossing-free voice leadings between half-diminished and dominant seventh chords, the same technique as Wagner's Tristan but now accompanied by diatonic modes.

**Example 4** (p. 337-338): Nyman's "The Mood That Passes Through You" -- Bb minor, D major, and E major triads connected by voice leading, each accompanied by different scales. Demonstrates chord-first composition in film music.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the scales in chord-first passages have independent logical relationships
  **Correction**: In chord-first composition, the scales are related only through the chords they contain

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating chord-first composition with purely chordal music without scalar awareness
  **Clarification**: Chord-first composition specifically involves the generation of recognizable scales from chordal voice leading -- the scalar level exists but is subordinate
