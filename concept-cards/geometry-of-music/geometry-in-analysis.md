# Quick Definition
The practice of plotting musical passages in chord space to reveal structural patterns — such as triangles, mirror images, and repeated shapes — that are difficult to see in conventional notation.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko demonstrates that translating musical passages into sequences of line segments in chord space can reveal patterns that are virtually invisible in standard notation. The geometric approach leverages our visual system's ability to recognize shapes (triangles, symmetries, parallel lines) and applies it to musical analysis. Standard musical notation evolved for performers rather than analysts and contains redundancies that obscure structural relationships. Chord space strips away these details, allowing direct perception of harmonic and contrapuntal relationships.

# Prerequisites
- Two-note chord space and generalized line segments
- Ability to plot voice leadings on the Mobius strip

# Key Properties
1. Geometric patterns (triangles, symmetries) correspond to musical structures
2. Our visual pattern-matching abilities reveal relationships obscured in notation
3. Mirror images in chord space correspond to inversionally related passages
4. Standard notation is optimized for performance, not analysis

# Construction / Recognition
## To Apply:
1. Identify the voice leadings in a passage
2. Plot each as a generalized line segment on the Mobius strip
3. Look for geometric patterns: triangles, parallelisms, symmetries, repetitions

# Context & Application
This section demonstrates the analytical payoff of the geometric construction. It bridges the abstract geometry of Sections 3.1-3.4 with concrete musical analysis, showing that the geometric model is not merely theoretically elegant but practically useful. The examples here are relatively simple (two-voice); later chapters apply the same principles to three- and four-voice textures.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 94-95): The eighth phrase of the Allelujia Justus et Palma traces a pair of triangles in chord space, revealing a four-chord unit that returns to its starting point (Figure 3.5.1).
**Example 2** (p. 95-97): Two passages from Brahms' Intermezzo Op. 116 No. 5 form mirror images on the Mobius strip (Figure 3.5.2). The geometric representation reveals that the second passage is the mirror image of the first — a relationship that "Brahms, with all his love for invertible counterpoint, would no doubt have enjoyed."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Expecting every geometric pattern to have deep musical significance
  **Correction**: Not all geometric patterns correspond to aurally perceptible musical relationships; judgment is needed

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking geometric analysis replaces traditional analysis
  **Clarification**: Geometric analysis complements traditional approaches by making certain relationships visible that are otherwise hard to describe
