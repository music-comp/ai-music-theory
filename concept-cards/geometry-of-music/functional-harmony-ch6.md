# Quick Definition
Functional harmony adds two innovations to Renaissance practice: purely harmonic conventions governing chord-to-chord progression (such as ii moving to V but not vice versa), and systematic modulation creating meaningful progressions among keys — producing a hierarchically self-similar style where the same procedures operate at both chord and key levels.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko analyzes Bach's chorale "Herr Christ, der ein'ge Gott'ssohn" as exemplifying functional harmony. Where Renaissance progressions are "extremely free," Bach's can be summarized by a simple map showing which chords may follow which. This represents genuinely new harmonic conventions — not derivable from voice-leading principles alone. The music still uses the 3+1 schema with upper voices moving crossing-free, but adds constraints on which chord follows which. Tymoczko argues this represents two overlapping common practices: a contrapuntal practice (Renaissance through present, including voice-leading rules and parallel-fifth avoidance) and a harmonic practice (Baroque through present, including functional progressions). The "common practice period" is their intersection. Crucially, twentieth-century tonal music (jazz, rock) often maintains harmonic conventions while relaxing contrapuntal ones, belonging to the harmonic but not contrapuntal common practice.

# Key Properties
1. Adds purely harmonic conventions to Renaissance voice-leading practice
2. Chord progressions governed by maps (e.g., ii goes to V, not vice versa)
3. Stronger centricity through cadential formulas (V-I)
4. Systematic modulation: keys participate in their own meaningful progressions
5. Hierarchically self-similar: same procedures at chord and key levels
6. The "common practice period" = intersection of contrapuntal and harmonic common practices
7. Modulation may be the most significant and enduring legacy of the functional tradition

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Use the 3+1 voice-leading schema
2. Follow functional harmonic conventions (maps of permitted progressions)
3. Articulate clear tonal centers through cadences
4. Create modulations through specific voice leadings between scales
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Check for functional progression patterns (ii-V-I, etc.)
2. Look for the 3+1 texture
3. Identify modulations as coordinated scale changes
4. Verify that progressions are not free (as in Renaissance) but follow directional patterns

# Context & Application
Tymoczko emphasizes that functional harmony is controversial in theory: Schenkerians claim that apparent harmonic conventions can be derived from voice-leading principles. Tymoczko disagrees, pointing to historical evidence: Renaissance music has efficient voice leading but free progressions; classical music adds harmonic rules; twentieth-century tonal music often maintains harmonic schemas while relaxing voice-leading constraints. These distinct combinations are best explained by recognizing separate contrapuntal and harmonic dimensions. Tymoczko also argues that systematic modulation is at least as important as chord-to-chord syntax, since modulation continues to feature in twentieth-century music even after chord-progression conventions are relaxed.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating functional harmonic rules as extensions of voice-leading rules
  **Correction**: Tymoczko argues they are genuinely independent constraints, as shown by the existence of music with voice-leading rules but free progressions (Renaissance) and music with functional progressions but relaxed voice-leading (jazz/rock)

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Treating modulation as less important than chord-to-chord syntax
  **Clarification**: Tymoczko argues modulation is "at least as important" and may be the most significant and enduring legacy of the functional tradition
