# Quick Definition
Schoenberg's song "Erwartung" Op. 2 No. 1 (not the atonal monodrama) is analyzed as structurally parallel to Brahms Op. 76 No. 4: delicately balanced between functional and chromatic "practices," exploring multiple resolutions of opening sonorities, with a middle section featuring an ascending-fifths sequence using descending stepwise voice leading.

# Core Definition
Like Brahms' Intermezzo, Schoenberg's song explores multiple resolutions of its opening chromatic sonority — an "augmented sixth" (enharmonically {B, D, F#, A} over an Eb pedal) that resolves semitonally as a neighbor to Eb major while also acting as a passing chord to IV7. The reduction shows a mix of familiar and unfamiliar progressions: Ab7 resolves as a German sixth to {Eb, G, C}, while a standard German sixth "normalizes" the song's unusual opening sonority. The middle section is an embellished ascending-fifths sequence of dominant sevenths, where each unit hints at a I-six-four to V-flat-9 progression. This sequence exploits descending stepwise voice leading producing ascending-fifths root progressions — the same technique first discussed in Section 3.8, linking Schoenberg to Haydn across a century.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of generalized augmented sixths and two-practice chromaticism

# Key Properties
1. Balanced between functional and chromatic passages (like Brahms)
2. Opening sonority receives multiple resolutions
3. Middle section: ascending-fifths sequence via descending stepwise voice leading
4. The sequence connects to a very similar one in Haydn's late piano sonatas
5. The ending evokes the opening of Wagner's Tristan (C#ø7-Bb7)
6. German sixth chords appear in both standard and non-standard forms

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. The opening "augmented sixth" and its multiple resolutions
2. The ascending-fifths sequence in the middle section
3. Functional passages (open noteheads) vs chromatic passages (closed noteheads)
4. The Tristan-like ending progression

# Context & Application
The analysis demonstrates continuity between Brahms and early Schoenberg, challenging narratives that portray Schoenberg as a revolutionary break from tradition. Both composers explore multiple resolutions of chromatic sonorities and balance functional and chromatic procedures. The connection to Haydn further emphasizes the deep continuities in voice-leading practice across centuries.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Analyzing the song as purely functional or purely chromatic
  **Correction**: It operates in both registers simultaneously, requiring a two-practice analytical approach

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating this "Erwartung" with Schoenberg's atonal monodrama of the same name
  **Clarification**: This is Op. 2 No. 1, an early tonal song; the monodrama is Op. 17
