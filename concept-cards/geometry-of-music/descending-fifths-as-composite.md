# Quick Definition
Falling fifths are "composite" progressions that can be factored into pairs of falling thirds (e.g., I-IV becomes I-vi-IV), whereas thirds cannot generally be factored into fifths — making thirds more fundamental even though fifths are more common.

# Core Definition
In Tymoczko's model, a descending-fifth progression corresponds to a two-step rightward motion on the chain of thirds. Since each fifth can typically be "factored" into a pair of adjacent thirds — I-IV can become I-vi-IV, vi-ii can become vi-IV-ii, and so on — fifths are composite rather than primitive. By contrast, thirds correspond to single steps on the chain and cannot generally be decomposed into fifths. This distinction between the *fundamental* (thirds) and the *common* (fifths) is a key insight of the model. Tonal composers sometimes exploit the full circle of diatonic thirds, interposing a mediant triad between dominant and tonic, where the engine of falling-thirds harmony temporarily overcomes the V-I paradigm.

# Key Properties
1. Every descending fifth on the chain spans exactly two descending thirds
2. I-IV = I-vi-IV; vi-ii = vi-IV-ii; IV-vii° = IV-ii-vii°; ii-V = ii-vii°-V
3. Thirds are single steps and cannot be factored into fifths
4. Falling fifths may be more common but are less fundamental
5. The distinction between fundamental and common applies to permissible vs probable progressions

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Take any falling-fifth progression (e.g., I-IV)
2. Insert the chord that lies between them on the thirds chain (e.g., vi)
3. Result: I-vi-IV — two falling thirds replacing one falling fifth

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. A progression that skips one chord on the thirds chain is a falling fifth
2. If a mediant chord is interposed, the fifth has been factored into thirds

# Context & Application
This principle underlies the organization of diatonic sequences and explains why the "down a third, up a step" sequence is closely related to the descending-fifths sequence (they are related by third substitution). It also explains the rhetorical effect when composers traverse the full circle of thirds: the descending-thirds engine temporarily overrides the dominant-tonic attraction.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Concluding that descending fifths are unimportant because they are composite
  **Correction**: Fifths are more *common* than thirds; the claim is about fundamentality (decomposability), not importance

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking that all fifths must be factored in analysis
  **Clarification**: Factoring is a theoretical possibility showing structural relationships; in practice, unfactored fifths are perfectly normal
