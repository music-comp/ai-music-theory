# Quick Definition
A generalized theory of keys uses macroharmony, centricity, and pitch-class profiles to conceptualize the full spectrum between complete atonality and traditional scale-based tonality, extending the notion of "key" to encompass any combination of macroharmony and tonal center.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko proposes that the analytical tools of Chapter 5 — pitch-class circulation graphs, global macroharmonic profiles, and pitch-class profiles — together constitute a "generalized theory of keys" that allows us to describe any point on the continuum between complete atonality and traditional major/minor tonality. Traditional theory recognizes 24 keys (12 major + 12 minor). The generalized framework replaces this with a vastly expanded space: combining the eight primary scale types (pentatonic, whole-tone, hexatonic, diatonic, acoustic, harmonic minor, harmonic major, octatonic) with their modes and transpositions yields over 450 possible "key areas" (Figure 5.8.6). But the theory goes further: pitch-class profiles with continuous values allow for "keys" that do not correspond to any standard scale, existing in the no-man's-land between traditional tonality and atonality.

## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose a macroharmony (scale/collection)
2. Choose a mode (which note is the center)
3. Construct a pitch-class profile representing the intended hierarchy
4. Use the profile to guide composition or improvisation
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Identify the operative macroharmony
2. Determine the tonal center
3. Assess the pitch-class hierarchy
4. Locate the result in the expanded space of possible keys

# Context & Application
The expanded key space helps explain the richness of twentieth-century tonal music. Instead of 24 keys, scalar composers have 450+ options, combining change of tonal center, change of scale type, and chromatic transposition — three operations represented geometrically in the three-dimensional modulatory space of Figure 5.8.5. This framework also provides a basis for Tymoczko's "tonality space" (Section 5.8.3), where the five components of tonality serve as conceptual vectors spanning the space of all possible tonal organizations, from traditional tonality to outright atonality.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 205-206, Figure 5.8.6): The inventory of scales and modes: 60 pentatonic modes + 12 whole-tone + 24 hexatonic + 84 diatonic + 84 acoustic + 84 harmonic minor + 84 harmonic major + 24 octatonic + 12 chromatic = 468 total tonal areas.

**Example 2** (p. 205, Figure 5.8.5): The three-dimensional modulatory space: one plane represents diatonic modes (horizontal = scalar transposition, vertical = chromatic transposition), with motion between planes representing change of scale type.

**Example 3** (p. 194-195): Tymoczko's own compositions using pitch-class profiles as compositional guides — creating music in the "no-man's-land between traditional tonality and full-on atonality."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the 450+ tonal areas as rigidly defined categories
  **Correction**: The generalized theory is a continuum; the 450+ areas are anchor points in a continuous space that also includes non-standard pitch-class profiles

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the generalized theory replaces traditional key concepts
  **Clarification**: It extends them — the 24 traditional major/minor keys are a subset of the generalized framework
