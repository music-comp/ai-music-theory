# Quick Definition
The overdetermination principle is the observation that the basic chords of Western tonal music are optimal for two independent reasons: they are acoustically consonant, and they also permit efficient voice leading between transpositions.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko identifies a "remarkable coincidence" at the heart of Western tonal music: the chords that sound best acoustically (perfect fifths, major/minor triads, dominant sevenths, etc.) are the same chords that are most suitable for contrapuntal writing. Acoustic consonance requires chords whose notes are related by simple frequency ratios, which implies near-even division of the pitch-class circle. Near evenness in turn implies proximity to transpositionally symmetrical chords, which guarantees efficient voice leading between transpositions. Thus the same chords are independently optimal from both acoustic and contrapuntal perspectives — they are "overdetermined" by two separate lines of reasoning.

# Key Properties
1. Consonant chords are nearly even in pitch-class space
2. Nearly even chords are near transpositionally symmetrical chords
3. Nearness to symmetrical chords guarantees efficient voice leading to transpositions
4. Therefore: consonance implies contrapuntal flexibility
5. This is a mathematical fact, not a cultural convention

# Context & Application
The overdetermination principle explains why Western counterpoint developed as it did. Musicians originally chose consonant chords for their pleasant sound, but these same chords turned out to be ideal for polyphonic writing. This "amazing accident" (as Tymoczko calls it) enabled the flowering of Western counterpoint from the medieval period onward. The principle also explains why certain non-Western traditions that value consonance can develop sophisticated polyphonic techniques.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the coincidence as culturally determined rather than mathematical
  **Correction**: The connection between consonance and voice-leading efficiency follows from the geometry of pitch-class space

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking that all nearly even chords are consonant
  **Clarification**: Consonance implies near evenness, but near evenness does not strictly imply consonance (though in 12-TET the most nearly even chords tend to be reasonably consonant)
