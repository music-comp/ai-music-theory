# Quick Definition
A scale is fundamentally a large chord, and a chord is a small scale. Both participate in efficient voice leading, both can be represented in the same geometric spaces, and both support scalar/interscalar transpositions. This duality is the broader moral of Chapter 4.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko's central insight in Chapter 4 is that chords and scales are not fundamentally different kinds of objects. A scale is a large chord (a collection of pitch classes that happens to serve as a distance metric), and a chord is a small scale (a collection of pitch classes that can serve as the basis for "scalar" transposition). Both inhabit the same geometric chord spaces, both participate in efficient voice leading, and both support (inter)scalar transposition. Composers develop motives by transposing along chords (treating them as scales, as in Shostakovich's "bugle fugue") just as they transpose along traditional scales. The decomposition of voice leadings into scalar and chromatic transpositions closely parallels the decomposition into parallel and contrary components. This duality provides "significant theoretical advantages" for a unified perspective on tonal music.

# Key Properties
1. A scale = a large chord; a chord = a small scale
2. Both inhabit the same chord spaces
3. Both participate in efficient voice leading
4. Both support scalar/interscalar transposition
5. Scalar/chromatic decomposition parallels parallel/contrary decomposition
6. The distinction is one of function, not structure

# Context & Application
This duality is the theoretical foundation for the book's unified treatment of harmony and counterpoint at multiple structural levels. It allows the same geometric tools (chord spaces, lattices, voice-leading graphs) to be applied to both chord progressions and modulations. It also connects to the notion that the "five components of tonality" operate at multiple scales simultaneously.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 161): Shostakovich's A major fugue treats triads as three-note scales, with the subject transposed along the A major "scale" of three notes (Figure 4.8.6c).
**Example 2** (p. 140): Figure 4.3.1b shows {C, E, G} as a "small but nearly even scale" whose transpositions preserve gesture shape.
**Example 3** (p. 172): "A scale is a large chord, and a chord is just a small scale: both participate in efficient voice leadings, and both can be represented using the same basic geometries."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking chords and scales require fundamentally different theoretical frameworks
  **Correction**: They are instances of the same mathematical structures, differing primarily in size and musical function

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Does this mean every chord should be thought of as a scale?
  **Clarification**: Not in all contexts — the duality is a theoretical insight, not a practical prescription. In many contexts the traditional distinction remains useful.
