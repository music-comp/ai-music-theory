# Quick Definition
Diatonic seventh chords arranged in a circle of thirds produce descending stepwise voice leading for strong progressions — the opposite of triads, where strong progressions use ascending voice leading. This makes seventh-chord harmony more straightforward in certain respects.

# Core Definition
Diatonic seventh chords can be arranged in a circle of thirds analogous to the triadic circle, but with a crucial difference: descending-thirds progressions produce descending voice leading. To move Cmaj7 down by third to Amin7, one lowers B to A; by contrast, to move the C major triad down by third to A minor, one must raise G to A. This means any "strong" seventh-chord progression can be realized by stepwise descending voice leading. The association between seventh chords, strong progressions, and descending stepwise voice leading plays an increasingly important role in late nineteenth-century harmony and is fundamental to pieces like Chopin's E minor Prelude and Wagner's Tristan. It also provides the nucleus of jazz voice leading.

# Key Properties
1. Seventh-chord strong progressions use descending voice leading (opposite of triads)
2. Any strong seventh-chord progression can be realized by maximal efficiency with descending motion
3. Harmonic cycles and descending-fifths sequences can be realized with maximally efficient descending voice leading
4. This property becomes increasingly important in late Romantic harmony
5. It provides the foundation for jazz voice-leading practices

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Arrange diatonic seventh chords by descending third: Cmaj7 - Amin7 - Fmaj7 - Dmin7 - Bø7 - G7 - Emin7
2. To move between adjacent chords, lower one note by step
3. For descending-fifths sequences, each chord connects to its neighbor by descending stepwise motion

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. A series of seventh chords with voices consistently descending by step
2. Root motion by descending thirds or fifths (strong progressions)
3. Each voice moves to the nearest lower pitch in the next chord

# Context & Application
The contrast between triadic and seventh-chord voice leading is a key theoretical insight. Triadic harmony requires at least one non-minimal voice leading per cycle (since descending melodies conflict with the ascending voice leading of strong triadic progressions). Seventh-chord harmony eliminates this conflict: strong progressions, efficient voice leading, and descending melodic motion all align. This is why the increasing prominence of seventh chords in late Romantic music went hand-in-hand with more pervasive chromatic voice leading.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming triadic and seventh-chord voice leading work the same way
  **Correction**: They are opposite — triadic strong progressions ascend, seventh-chord strong progressions descend

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking seventh chords are merely triads with an added note
  **Clarification**: The fourth note fundamentally changes the voice-leading geometry, reversing the direction of efficient motion
