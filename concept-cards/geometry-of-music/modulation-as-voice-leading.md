# Quick Definition
Modulation — changing from one scale to another — can be understood as a specific voice leading between scales, where individual scale degrees move by small amounts (typically semitones). Sequential passages force this interpretation: to maintain a consistent scalar analysis, we must postulate that specific notes have "moved."

# Core Definition
Tymoczko argues that modulation is not merely a change of key but a specific voice leading between scale collections. In a sequential passage (such as the Mozart K. 283 transition), the requirement to analyze repeating patterns as "the same" at different scalar levels forces us to postulate that particular notes have moved — e.g., F moves up by semitone to F#, creating a voice leading from C diatonic to G diatonic. This is not just notation: it reflects the listener's experience of a scale degree shifting position, analogous to "a frog hopping along a circle of gently shifting lily pads." Classical modulation is typically a two-stage process: first, a voice leading between scales (raising the fourth degree, etc.), then a shift of tonal center. Twentieth-century music frequently uses the first stage without the second (changing mode without changing tonic).

# Key Properties
1. Modulation = specific voice leading between scale collections
2. Sequential structure forces specific voice-leading interpretations
3. Classical modulation: two stages (scale change, then tonic change)
4. Twentieth-century: often scale change without tonic change (modal shift)
5. Standard notation reflects scalar voice leading: accidental changes show which notes move
6. Scale degrees are "abstract addresses" that can point to different chromatic locations

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify:
1. Find sequential or scalar patterns in the music
2. Determine which notes must be considered "the same" scale degree across the sequence
3. The accidental changes between these notes reveal the voice leading between scales

# Context & Application
This concept bridges the gap between chord-level voice leading (Chapters 2-3) and large-scale tonal structure. It allows modulation to be analyzed using the same geometric tools as chord progression, since scales are simply large chords and the scale lattice represents voice-leading distances among them. The concept is essential for Chapters 6 and 9, where it is applied to analyze modulation in music from Mozart through Shostakovich.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 147-148): In the transition of Mozart's K. 283, the sequential pattern requires F to "move" to F# and then C to "move" to C#, producing specific voice leadings between G major, D major, and A major scales (Figure 4.5.1).
**Example 2** (p. 149): Figure 4.5.3 shows classical modulation as a two-stage process: first the scale shifts (D -> D#), then the tonal center shifts (A -> E).
**Example 3** (p. 149): The Who's "I Can't Explain" shifts from E mixolydian to E major — a change of scale without change of tonic.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking modulation is just "changing the key signature"
  **Correction**: Modulation involves specific voice leadings between scales; different modulations between the same two keys may use different voice leadings

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Does this analysis depend on notation?
  **Clarification**: No — the analysis depends on hearing sequential patterns as repetitions of the same scalar gesture. Notation can help identify the voice leadings but is not essential.
