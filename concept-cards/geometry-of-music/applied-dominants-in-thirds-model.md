# Quick Definition
In the thirds-based model, any chord other than vii° can be preceded by its own applied dominant, extending the basic diatonic grammar to accommodate common chromatic intensifications.

# Core Definition
Applied (secondary) dominants are incorporated into the thirds-based grammar as a simple addendum: any diatonic chord except vii° may be preceded by its own applied dominant. This means V/V can precede V, V/ii can precede ii, V/vi can precede vi, and so on. Combined with the cadential I-six-four before V, these additions account for the most common chromatic elaborations within otherwise diatonic harmonic cycles. The exclusion of vii° reflects its diminished quality, which makes it an unlikely target for tonicization. Statistical data from Mozart's piano sonatas confirm that applied dominants (particularly V/IV and V/ii) appear among the most common harmonic cycles.

# Prerequisites
- The thirds-based harmonic grammar and its directional rules

# Key Properties
1. Any chord except vii° can be preceded by its applied dominant
2. Applied dominants do not disrupt the basic rightward/leftward cycle structure
3. V/IV and V/ii are among the most frequently used applied dominants
4. The cadential I-six-four before V is a related intensification device
5. Applied dominants represent the simplest chromatic extension of the diatonic model

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Identify the target chord in a harmonic cycle
2. Insert V or V7 of that chord immediately before it
3. The applied dominant does not count as a separate step on the thirds chain

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for chromatic alterations that create a temporary dominant-tonic relationship
2. The target chord should be a standard diatonic chord within the harmonic cycle

# Context & Application
Applied dominants represent a bridge between purely diatonic functional harmony (Chapter 7) and the more elaborate chromaticism discussed in Chapter 8. They are the most basic and common form of chromatic embellishment in classical music.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Fig. 7.1.7b, p. 248): In Mozart's sonatas, I-V/IV-IV-I appears 23 times and I-V/IV-IV-vii°-I appears 19 times among the most common harmonic cycles.

**Example 2** (Fig. 7.1.7b, p. 248): I-V/ii-ii-V-I appears 15 times in Mozart's sonatas.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating applied dominants as violations of the harmonic grammar
  **Correction**: They are explicitly part of the model as permitted chromatic additions

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating applied dominants with augmented sixth chords
  **Clarification**: Applied dominants are standard V or V7 chords of a secondary key; augmented sixths are chromatic alterations of a different sort (discussed in Chapter 8)
