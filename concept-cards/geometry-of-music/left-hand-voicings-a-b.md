# Quick Definition
The A and B voicings are two specific four-note closed-position left-hand chord shapes for jazz piano, derived from the upper voices of ninth chords in the ii-V-I schema, leaving the right hand free to improvise.

# Core Definition
The A and B voicings arise from moving the basic ii-V-I schema up by diatonic third, so it connects the four upper voices of ninth chords. A further embellishment suspends the ninth of the ii9 chord into the dominant (forming a thirteenth) and replaces the seventh of the tonic with the sixth, so all four upper voices descend by step from V to I. The resulting closed-position voicings contain the third, seventh (or sixth), and extensions of each chord, but not the root (which the bass player provides). Since each voicing contains all but two notes of the diatonic scale, jazz pedagogues describe them negatively by identifying the "avoid notes" -- the diatonic notes the voicing should not contain.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of the ii-V-I jazz progression
- Familiarity with extended chord tones (ninths, thirteenths)

# Key Properties
1. Four-note closed-position voicings for left hand
2. Derived from upper voices of ninth chords
3. Do not include the root (bass player provides it)
4. A and B forms alternate through descending-fifth progressions
5. Each contains all but two diatonic notes
6. Leave the right hand free for improvisation
7. Classical anticipations in Ravel, Grieg, and Scriabin

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. For the A voicing of ii7 (e.g., Dm9): start with the 3rd and stack diatonic thirds: F-A-C-E (3rd, 5th, 7th, 9th)
2. For the B voicing of V7 (e.g., G13): the ninth of ii suspends to become the 13th: E-A-B-D -> E-F-A-B (7th, root, 3rd... variants exist)
3. Voicings move down by step from V to I
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for four-note rootless voicings in the piano left hand
2. The voicings contain thirds, sevenths, and extensions but not roots
3. A and B forms alternate across descending-fifth progressions

# Context & Application
These voicings are fundamental to modern jazz piano and are taught in virtually every jazz piano method. They also appear in classical music: Ravel's "Forlane" presents A voicings on V and IV, Grieg's "Salon" outlines the dominant B voicing, and Scriabin's Etude Op. 65 No. 3 outlines an A voicing in its highest notes.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Including the root in left-hand voicings when a bass player is present
  **Correction**: The root is provided by the bass; including it creates a muddy sound

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking A and B refer to specific notes or keys
  **Clarification**: A and B are names for two voicing types that alternate through the ii-V-I progression
