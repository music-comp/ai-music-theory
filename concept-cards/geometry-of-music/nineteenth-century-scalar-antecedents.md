# Quick Definition
All three twentieth-century scalar techniques (chord-first, scale-first, subset) have clear antecedents in nineteenth-century music, but what is new in the twentieth century is the willingness to apply them more consistently, more frequently, and to a much wider range of scales and modes.

# Core Definition
Each scalar technique has nineteenth-century precedents. Chord-first composition occurs in Schubert's D. 960 Bb major sonata, where semitonal voice leading between F major and C# minor chords drags the entire tonal context along. Scale-first composition operates in ordinary diatonic modulation between closely related keys. The subset technique appears in Chopin's "black key" Etude Op. 10 No. 5, where the right hand plays only black keys as a fixed subset within both tonic and dominant scales. The Chopin F minor Mazurka shows rare nineteenth-century use of non-standard modes (C# phrygian returning as the fifth mode of F# harmonic minor). The twentieth century's innovation is systematic application to the full range of nondiatonic scales.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of the three scalar techniques

# Key Properties
1. Chord-first: Schubert's modulations via chromatic chord voice leading
2. Scale-first: traditional modulation generalized beyond diatonic
3. Subset: Chopin's pentatonic fixed-subset technique
4. What is new is scope, not technique
5. The continuity undermines narratives of radical twentieth-century rupture

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. In nineteenth-century music, look for non-functional scale/chord relationships
2. Identify whether the antecedent uses diatonic or non-diatonic scales
3. Compare the scale/mode range with twentieth-century examples

# Context & Application
This historical continuity is central to Tymoczko's argument against narratives of radical rupture between the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The extended common practice is an evolution, not a revolution.
