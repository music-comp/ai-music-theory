# Quick Definition
"Strong" progressions (descending thirds, descending fifths, ascending steps) correspond to rightward motion on the thirds chain and are more common than "weak" progressions (ascending thirds, ascending fifths, descending steps) in functional tonality.

# Core Definition
The thirds-based model's spatial layout creates an asymmetry: since rightward motion is always permitted, every chord except V can progress by descending third, every chord except vii° by descending fifth, and every chord except ii by ascending step. By contrast, only two chords can progress by ascending fifth, only two by descending step, and only one by ascending third. The model predicts that chords closer to the dominant side should be more likely to move "strongly" — a prediction confirmed by statistical analysis showing the asymmetry increases steadily from I (53% strong in Bach, 32% in Mozart) through vi, IV, ii, vii°, to V (95% strong in Bach, 97% in Mozart).

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of the thirds-based harmonic grammar's directional rules

# Key Properties
1. Strong = descending third, descending fifth, or ascending step
2. Weak = ascending third, ascending fifth, or descending step
3. The strong/weak asymmetry increases monotonically from I to V on the chain
4. In Bach: I (53%), vi (53%), IV (76%), ii (100%), vii° (96%), V (95%) move strongly
5. In Mozart: I (32%), vi (67%), IV (66%), ii (97%), vii° (99%), V (97%) move strongly

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Determine the root motion interval between two chords
2. Classify: descending third, descending fifth, or ascending step = strong
3. All other motions = weak
4. Strong progressions correspond to rightward motion on the thirds chain

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Calculate the root motion between adjacent chords
2. Check against the strong/weak classification
3. In functional passages, expect predominantly strong progressions, especially from ii onward

# Context & Application
The strong/weak distinction derives from Rameau (1722), Schoenberg (1969), Sadai (1980), and Meeus (2000). Tymoczko's contribution is showing that the asymmetry arises naturally from the thirds chain's geometry and is confirmed by large-scale statistical analysis. The distinction is particularly relevant to sequences, where the preference for strong progressions is especially clear since tonic and dominant lose their gravitational pull.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking "weak" means "forbidden" in tonal music
  **Correction**: Weak progressions are permitted (some are very common, like V-vi deceptive cadence), just less frequent overall

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating the strong/weak distinction here with Tymoczko's other uses in Tonality: An Owner's Manual
  **Clarification**: In Geometry of Music, strong = rightward on thirds chain; the concept overlaps but is not identical with other formulations
