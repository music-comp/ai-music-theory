# Quick Definition
The technique of connecting two scales by changing a single note by one semitone, creating the smallest possible voice leading between seven-note collections.

# Core Definition
Single-semitone scale voice leading extends the concept of efficient voice leading from chords to scales. Just as traditional modulation connects closely related keys by changing one note (e.g., C major to G major by raising F to F#), twentieth-century composers connect a broader range of scales by the same mechanism. On the scale lattice, scales connected by single-semitone voice leading are adjacent. This technique is central to scale-first composition and can be tracked on two- and three-dimensional scale lattices.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of efficient voice leading
- Familiarity with the scale lattice

# Key Properties
1. The smallest possible voice leading between seven-note scales
2. Adjacent scales on the scale lattice are connected by single-semitone voice leading
3. Generalizes traditional modulation to nondiatonic scales
4. Creates audibly smooth transitions between scalar collections
5. Multiple single-semitone steps can be chained for larger modulatory journeys

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose a starting scale
2. Raise or lower one note by semitone
3. Verify the result is a recognizable scale type
4. Chain multiple single-semitone changes for extended passages
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Compare consecutive scales in a passage
2. If they differ by exactly one note moving one semitone, this is single-semitone scale voice leading
3. Plot on the scale lattice to confirm adjacency

# Context & Application
This technique pervades twentieth-century tonal music from Debussy through Reich. The scale lattice shows that diatonic, acoustic, harmonic major, harmonic minor, whole-tone, and octatonic scales form a connected network through single-semitone voice leadings.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 328-329): Ravel's "Ondine" -- Cs mixolydian b6 to Cs harmonic major to Cs diatonic to Gs acoustic, each connected by a single-semitone change.

**Example 2** (p. 340-342): Debussy's "Des pas sur la neige" -- a tonic region of five closely related scales all connected by single-semitone voice leading on the scale lattice.

**Example 3** (p. 344-347): Janacek's "On an Overgrown Path" -- scales arranged in hub-and-spoke and railroad-car networks, all connected by single-semitone displacements.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming all scale changes in a piece use single-semitone voice leading
  **Correction**: Some scale changes involve multiple semitones; single-semitone changes are one specific technique

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing scale voice leading with chordal voice leading
  **Clarification**: Scale voice leading operates on seven-note collections, not triads or seventh chords
