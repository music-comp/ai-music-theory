# Quick Definition
A 3-gap macroharmony is a pitch-class collection with gaps of up to three semitones between successive notes, offering more collections than 2-gap macroharmonies but restricting which chord types and root positions are available.

# Core Definition
A macroharmony whose largest gap between consecutive pitch classes is three semitones. While 3-gap collections still guarantee that out-of-scale notes can be corrected by semitone, the direction of correction is constrained — there may be only one choice (up or down), unlike 2-gap collections where both directions work. The hexatonic scale is a paradigmatic example: its three-semitone gaps prevent certain chord types from appearing (no fourth chords exist in the hexatonic collection), and only three of the twelve possible root positions for major/minor triads are available. The 3-gap category encompasses many more collections than the 2-gap category — 4 five-note, 17 six-note, 19 seven-note, and 16 eight-note collections.

# Key Properties
1. Maximum gap between consecutive pitch classes is 3 semitones
2. Out-of-scale notes can still be corrected by semitone, but direction may be forced
3. Not all chord types are available above every note
4. Many more 3-gap collections exist than 2-gap collections
5. Includes the pentatonic scale as a 5-note 3-gap macroharmony

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose pitch classes ensuring no consecutive gap exceeds 3 semitones
2. Accept that some chord types will be unavailable above certain roots
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Arrange pitch classes in order and check all gaps
2. If the maximum gap is exactly 3 semitones, it is a 3-gap (but not 2-gap) collection

# Context & Application
The 3-gap category captures many familiar scales that are useful but harmonically more constrained than diatonic or acoustic scales. The pentatonic scale — the most common 3-gap macroharmony — has been the basis of enormous repertoire in Western, African, and Asian musical traditions, but its large gaps prevent certain harmonic constructions. The distinction between 2-gap and 3-gap helps explain why certain scales support richer harmonic vocabularies than others.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 174): The hexatonic scale (A minor + F major + Db major) has three-semitone gaps. Only triads rooted on F, A, and Db are available; no fourth chords exist in the collection because both possible fourths above Db fall in the gap.

**Example 2** (p. 176, Figure 5.2.4): Five-note 3-gap macroharmonies include the pentatonic scale, the "dominant ninth" chord, the "diminished seventh plus one" chord, and C-C#-E-F#-A.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming 3-gap macroharmonies are unusable for tonal music
  **Correction**: The pentatonic scale is a 3-gap macroharmony that supports vast repertoire; the limitation is in harmonic variety, not usability

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the "gap" refers to scale steps rather than semitones
  **Clarification**: Gap size is measured in chromatic semitones, not scale steps. A pentatonic scale has 2- and 3-semitone gaps, but all its scale steps are consecutive by definition.
