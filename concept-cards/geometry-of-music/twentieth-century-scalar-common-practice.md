# Quick Definition
At least three major twentieth-century tonal styles -- impressionism, jazz, and postminimalism -- share the same basic collection of nondiatonic scales (acoustic, octatonic, whole-tone) and the same three compositional techniques, constituting a genuine common practice larger than any particular style.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko argues that the convergence of multiple styles on the same scales is not coincidental but inevitable: the familiar scales of Western tradition are "overdetermined," with a variety of independent considerations (even octave division, consonance content, step sizes, third sizes) all pointing toward the same collections. Different composers independently found their way to these scales "much in the way that different mountain climbers tend to follow the most obvious routes up a particular cliff." Combined with the three scalar techniques (chord-first, scale-first, subset), these scales constitute the twentieth century's "most interesting example of a tonal common practice -- one that results not just from conscious imitation, but also from a simultaneous convergence on an intrinsically fertile territory."

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of the three scalar techniques
- Familiarity with the four central scales

# Key Properties
1. Same scales appear in impressionism, jazz, and postminimalism
2. Scales are mathematically overdetermined (multiple optimality criteria converge)
3. Three shared techniques: chord-first, scale-first, subset
4. The practice is larger than any particular style
5. Challenges the narrative of twentieth-century stylistic fragmentation
6. Some composers (Messiaen, neoclassicists) operate outside this common practice

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for acoustic, whole-tone, and octatonic scales in any twentieth-century tonal music
2. Identify which of the three scalar techniques is operative
3. Compare with usages in other styles to find commonalities

# Context & Application
This claim is perhaps the central historical argument of the book: the history of twentieth-century tonality runs through jazz, and the major tonal styles share more than they differ. This challenges both the narrative of atonality as the "main line" of twentieth-century music and the view that twentieth-century tonality is static.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 367-370): Miles Davis Group's "Freedom Jazz Dance" -- improvisers play Bb natural minor, Bb melodic minor ascending, Bb mixolydian, Bb acoustic, Ab harmonic minor, and Bb-Cb octatonic over a static Bb harmony, demonstrating the extended scalar palette.

**Example 2**: The same acoustic scale appears in Debussy, Stravinsky, Steve Reich, and jazz improvisation.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating twentieth-century tonal music as merely backward-looking
  **Correction**: The scalar common practice represents genuine innovation, not stasis

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking all twentieth-century tonal music belongs to this common practice
  **Clarification**: Messiaen, neoclassicists, and polytonal composers operate differently
