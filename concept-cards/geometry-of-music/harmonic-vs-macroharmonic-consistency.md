# Quick Definition
Harmonic consistency (using similar-sounding chords) and macroharmonic consistency (confining pitch classes to a recognizable collection) are independent properties that constrain each other: combining both requires special macroharmonies like 2-gap collections.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko distinguishes two kinds of consistency: harmonic consistency requires that chords within a passage be structurally similar (e.g., all triads), while macroharmonic consistency requires that the total pitch-class content articulate a recognizable collection smaller than the chromatic scale. These are logically independent — a passage can exhibit one without the other — but combining both imposes nontrivial constraints. Specifically, confining music to a small macroharmony while maintaining harmonic consistency requires the macroharmony to have small enough gaps to accommodate the desired chord types. This is why collections like the diatonic and acoustic scales (2-gap macroharmonies) are so prevalent: they allow both harmonic and macroharmonic consistency simultaneously.

# Prerequisites
- **macroharmony** — Understanding the concept of aggregate pitch-class collections

# Key Properties
1. The two forms of consistency are logically independent
2. Combining them constrains the available macroharmonies to those with small gaps
3. Traditional tonal music achieves both; pandiatonic music achieves only macroharmonic consistency
4. Atonal music typically achieves neither; "triadic atonality" (Gesualdo, Reger) achieves only harmonic consistency
5. The interaction creates a 2x2 space of musical possibilities

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose chord types (harmonic consistency target)
2. Choose a macroharmony that can contain those chord types above each of its notes
3. Verify that the macroharmony's gap size accommodates the chord vocabulary
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Check whether the passage uses structurally similar chords (harmonic consistency)
2. Check whether the aggregate pitch classes form a recognizable, limited collection (macroharmonic consistency)
3. Place the passage in the 2x2 grid of possibilities

# Context & Application
The four-way classification (Figure 5.8.7) maps major musical traditions: traditional scale-based tonality (Debussy, jazz) achieves both; "triadic atonality" (Gesualdo, Reger) achieves harmonic but not macroharmonic consistency; pandiatonicism achieves macroharmonic but not harmonic consistency; and complete atonality (Schoenberg, Babbitt) achieves neither. This framework explains why certain scales (diatonic, acoustic, whole-tone, octatonic) recur so frequently — they are among the few collections that permit both kinds of consistency.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming that harmonically consistent music is automatically macroharmonically consistent
  **Correction**: Reger's music uses consistent triads while cycling through all twelve pitch classes rapidly, achieving harmonic but not macroharmonic consistency

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating "chromatic" with "lacking harmonic consistency"
  **Clarification**: Chromaticism involves two independent variables: rate of pitch-class circulation and degree of macroharmonic consistency. High circulation with clear macroharmonies (Coltrane's "Giant Steps") is quite different from high circulation without them (Schoenberg)
