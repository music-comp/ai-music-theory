# Quick Definition
The acoustic scale occupies a unique structural position between whole-tone and diatonic collections, sharing five of six notes with the whole-tone scale and six of seven with the diatonic, making it an ideal modulatory intermediary.

# Core Definition
The acoustic scale (also called the lydian dominant scale in jazz) serves as a crucial intermediary in twentieth-century modulation because of its position on the scale lattice. It shares five of the whole-tone scale's six notes while also sharing six of the diatonic scale's seven notes, making it a hybrid that smoothes transitions between these very different collections. This bridging function is one of Debussy's characteristic modulatory devices and appears throughout twentieth-century tonal music.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of the scale lattice
- Familiarity with acoustic, diatonic, and whole-tone scales

# Key Properties
1. Shares 5 of 6 notes with the whole-tone scale
2. Shares 6 of 7 notes with the diatonic scale
3. Occupies a middle position on the scale lattice between whole-tone and diatonic
4. Functions as a modulatory bridge in both classical and jazz contexts
5. One of the most important nondiatonic collections in twentieth-century tonal music

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Take a diatonic scale and raise the fourth degree (producing lydian dominant)
2. Or take a whole-tone scale, add one note, and adjust to produce the acoustic collection
3. Use the acoustic scale to transition between whole-tone and diatonic passages
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for a seven-note scale with whole-tone lower tetrachord and diatonic upper tetrachord
2. Check for the characteristic raised fourth and lowered seventh relative to major
3. Often appears at modulatory transitions between whole-tone and diatonic passages

# Context & Application
This bridging function is characteristic of Debussy and appears in Ravel, Stravinsky, Reich, and jazz. In jazz, the acoustic scale (as "lydian dominant") is the default scale for dominant seventh chords when avoid notes are raised by semitone.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 341-342): Debussy's "Des pas sur la neige" -- the transition back from the C whole-tone scale to D natural minor occurs through Bb acoustic, which shares five notes with the whole-tone and six notes with the diatonic.

**Example 2** (p. 338): Nyman's "The Mood That Passes Through You" -- G acoustic appears over E minor, serving as one of the most important nondiatonic collections in twentieth-century tonal music.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the acoustic scale as exotic or rare
  **Correction**: It is one of the most common nondiatonic scales in twentieth-century music, appearing in impressionism, jazz, minimalism, and film music

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating the acoustic scale with the lydian mode
  **Clarification**: The acoustic scale has both a raised fourth AND a lowered seventh; lydian has only the raised fourth
