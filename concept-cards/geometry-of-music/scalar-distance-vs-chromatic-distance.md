# Quick Definition
Chromatic distance measures intervals in semitones (log-frequency); scalar distance measures intervals in scale steps. Listeners are typically aware of both simultaneously, and tonal music exploits the productive tension between them.

# Core Definition
Any scale defines two simultaneous measures of musical distance. Chromatic distance is the absolute measure in semitones (continuous log-frequency). Scalar distance counts the number of scale steps between two notes. The two metrics generally disagree: in C major, C-D and E-F are both one scale step (scalar distance 1) but differ in chromatic distance (2 semitones vs. 1 semitone). Traditional terminology captures both: "second" indicates scalar distance while "major" vs. "minor" indicates the chromatic discrepancy. Listeners perceive both metrics simultaneously — they recognize that D-E-F is "the same" as C-D-E (scalar distance) while also noticing that it sounds slightly different (chromatic distance). This doubleness is what makes scalar music interesting.

# Key Properties
1. Two independent metrics exist simultaneously
2. Chromatic distance: semitones (continuous, absolute)
3. Scalar distance: scale steps (discrete, scale-relative)
4. Traditional terminology encodes both (e.g., "minor third" = 2 steps + 3 semitones)
5. Listeners perceive both at once
6. The "crumpled" appearance of chord space drawn with chromatic distance reflects the discrepancy

# Construction / Recognition
## To Compare:
1. Count scale steps between two notes (scalar distance)
2. Count semitones between two notes (chromatic distance)
3. The difference between these measures reflects the scale's unevenness

# Context & Application
The coexistence of two distance metrics is what makes scalar music aesthetically rich. When a pattern is transposed along a scale, it retains its scalar identity while acquiring chromatic variation. This is the "pleasing mixture of identity and difference" that Tymoczko identifies as central to the appeal of scalar music. The concept also underlies the Goldilocks Principle: too much evenness eliminates the distinction between the metrics, while too little makes scalar distance useless.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking chromatic distance is always more "real" than scalar distance
  **Correction**: Both metrics are psychologically real; scalar distance often better captures musical relationships

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Is "chromatic" distance the same as distance along a chromatic scale?
  **Clarification**: In this book, "chromatic" refers to continuous log-frequency distance, not to any particular scale. It happens to coincide with chromatic scale distances only in equal temperament.
