# Quick Definition
In medieval theory, consonances are divided into "perfect" (unisons, fourths, fifths, octaves) usable anywhere, and "imperfect" (thirds, sixths) permitted only mid-phrase — a distinction that gradually evolved as thirds and sixths gained prominence and fourths became treated as quasi-dissonances.

# Core Definition
The Montpelier organum treatise classifies intervals into three categories: forbidden dissonances (steps, sevenths, tritones), imperfect consonances (thirds and sixths, usable only mid-phrase), and perfect consonances (unisons, fourths, fifths, usable anywhere including at beginnings and endings of phrases). This classification accurately describes the practice in Ad Organum Faciendum. The evolution of this classification is a crucial thread in Western music history: by the Renaissance, thirds and sixths had become essential (supporting triadic harmony), while the fourth over the bass was reclassified as a dissonance requiring resolution to a third. The fourth's ambiguous status — consonant between upper voices, dissonant over the bass — was addressed by early fifteenth-century theorists and reflects the emergence of a bass-oriented harmonic conception.

# Key Properties
1. Medieval: perfect consonances (unison, 4th, 5th, 8ve) usable anywhere; imperfect (3rd, 6th) only mid-phrase
2. Forbidden: steps, sevenths, tritones
3. In the Allelujia Justus et Palma, perfect consonances account for ~90% of intervals
4. By the Renaissance, thirds/sixths become essential for triadic harmony
5. The fourth's status shifts from perfect consonance to quasi-dissonance over the bass
6. Compound intervals follow the same classification (octave+third = imperfect, octave+fifth = perfect)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. In medieval style: begin and end phrases on perfect consonances; use imperfect consonances only internally
2. In Renaissance style: use thirds and sixths freely; treat fourths over the bass as dissonances resolving to thirds
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Perfect consonances produce a more "hollow" or "austere" sound
2. Imperfect consonances sound "warmer" or "richer"
3. Context determines whether a fourth functions as consonance (between upper voices) or dissonance (over bass)

# Context & Application
The gradual elevation of thirds and sixths from secondary to primary consonances drove the development of triadic harmony. When only perfect consonances are available for three-voice writing, there are almost no smooth voice leadings (Figure 6.3.3), making elegant three-voice counterpoint nearly impossible. The inclusion of imperfect consonances is virtually necessary for fluent polyphony, and once included, they naturally generate triads — the only three-note consonances without doubled notes. This combinatorial fact suggests that the rise of triadic harmony was a logical consequence of valuing three-voice polyphony.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 215-216): The Allelujia Justus et Palma uses perfect consonances ~90% of the time, with imperfect consonances appearing only in passing, mid-phrase positions.

**Example 2** (p. 219, Figure 6.3.2): In Josquin's music, fourths typically resolve to thirds (reclassified as dissonances over the bass), while diminished triads appear in first inversion despite containing a tritone.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the fourth has always been treated as a dissonance
  **Correction**: In medieval music the fourth is a perfect consonance; its reclassification as dissonance over the bass occurs gradually during the 15th century

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking "perfect" means "better sounding"
  **Clarification**: "Perfect" refers to the stability and static quality of these intervals; imperfect consonances are actually richer in sound and more useful for polyphonic writing
