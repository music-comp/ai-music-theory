# Quick Definition
Nineteenth-century chromaticism combines two systems: a functional "first practice" (traditional tonal syntax) and a chromatic "second practice" (efficient voice leading operating independently of tonal function) — coexisting like bilingual speakers switching between languages within a single sentence.

# Core Definition
Early nineteenth-century chromaticism tends to embellish traditional tonal progressions (decorative chromaticism). Over the following decades, chromatic procedures gain autonomy until they compete with or supersede traditional principles. By the time of Wagner's Tristan, chromaticism and diatonic function are in "extremely delicate balance." Tymoczko's analytical method separates the two systems visually: open noteheads for functionally harmonic chords, closed noteheads for chromatic voice leading. Cohn's analogy to bilingual communities captures the effect: speakers (composers) switch rapidly between languages (systems), sometimes within a single sentence (phrase). This Janus-faced quality characterizes much late Romantic music. Beyond Tristan, Strauss's Salome shows where this leads: incessant chromatic voice leading producing a fully chromatic macroharmony without tonal center, making the transition to atonality a relatively small step.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of decorative chromaticism and generalized augmented sixths

# Key Properties
1. First practice: functional tonal syntax (Chapter 7)
2. Second practice: efficient chromatic voice leading operating independently
3. Early Romantic: chromaticism decorates function
4. Late Romantic: chromaticism competes with function
5. Both can coexist within a single piece, even a single phrase
6. Analytical method: distinguish with open/closed noteheads

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Some progressions follow functional harmonic grammar (Chapter 7)
2. Others make sense only as efficient voice leading (no functional label fits)
3. The two systems interleave, sometimes switching within a single phrase
4. Use the open/closed notehead distinction in reductions

# Context & Application
The two-practice model provides a balanced alternative to analyses that force all late Romantic chromaticism into either functional or non-functional frameworks. It explains why theorists have long debated whether passages in Tristan, Brahms, and Schoenberg are "really" tonal — they are and they are not, simultaneously operating in both systems. The model also explains the historical trajectory toward atonality: as the second practice gains autonomy, tonal functionality becomes increasingly vestigial.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Trying to assign every chord in late Romantic music a single functional label
  **Correction**: Some chords operate in the chromatic second practice and resist functional categorization

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking "two practices" means the music is incoherent
  **Clarification**: Both practices have their own logic; the music is coherent within each system
