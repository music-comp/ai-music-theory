# Quick Definition
Crossing-free voice leadings from four-note chords (sevenths) to three-note chords (triads) are categorized by which pair of notes in the seventh chord converges onto which note in the triad, yielding twelve abstract schemas (4 converging pairs x 3 targets).

# Core Definition
When a four-voice texture moves from a seventh chord to a triad, the crossing-avoidance principle requires that two adjacent notes in the seventh chord converge on a single note in the triad, while the other two voices connect independently to the remaining triad notes. The voice leading can be categorized by two pieces of information: (1) which pair of adjacent notes converges (root+third, third+fifth, fifth+seventh, or seventh+root), and (2) which triad note they converge upon (root, third, or fifth). This yields 4 x 3 = 12 abstract possibilities. The generalized augmented sixth chords are the subset where the converging pair is separated by ten semitones. This systematic framework allows composers and analysts to survey all efficient voice-leading possibilities between any seventh chord and any triad.

# Prerequisites
- Efficient voice leading and the crossing-avoidance principle

# Key Properties
1. Twelve abstract schemas = 4 converging pairs x 3 target notes
2. Voice leadings within the same schema are individually T-related
3. The augmented sixth subset requires 10-semitone separation of converging pair
4. Not all twelve schemas are equally useful musically
5. The framework applies to any seventh chord resolving to any triad

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose a seventh chord and a target triad
2. Determine which pair of adjacent seventh-chord notes will converge
3. Determine which triad note they converge upon
4. Connect the remaining voices efficiently
5. Evaluate the musical utility of the result

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. In any seventh-chord-to-triad progression, identify which two voices converge
2. Identify the target note
3. Classify according to the 12-schema grid

# Context & Application
This framework is the theoretical backbone of Chapter 8's approach to chromaticism. Rather than memorizing individual chromatic chords and their resolutions, musicians can think systematically about all voice-leading possibilities, discovering both familiar and unfamiliar resolutions. Nineteenth-century composers developed this understanding intuitively through hours of keyboard experimentation; twenty-first-century musicians can use the geometric framework to conceptualize these abstract relationships more efficiently.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming only some of the twelve schemas appear in real music
  **Correction**: While not all are equally common, all twelve appear; the uncommon ones may represent untapped compositional resources

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the convergent pair must always resolve semitonally
  **Clarification**: Augmented sixth resolutions are semitonal, but other schemas may involve larger intervals
