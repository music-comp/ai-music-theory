# Quick Definition
The traditional "common practice period" is the intersection of two independent common practices: a contrapuntal practice (Renaissance through present, featuring efficient voice leading and avoidance of parallel fifths) and a harmonic practice (Baroque through present, featuring functional chord progressions) — each extending beyond the 18th-19th century core.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko argues that what is conventionally called the "common practice period" (roughly 1700-1900) is actually the intersection of two distinct sets of shared practices. The contrapuntal common practice includes efficient voice leading, the 3+1 schema, and the avoidance of parallel perfect intervals — originating in the Renaissance and persisting in much music through the present. The harmonic common practice includes functional progression patterns (ii-V-I, etc.), strong centricity through cadences, and systematic modulation — originating in the Baroque and also persisting to the present (in jazz, rock, etc.). Renaissance music belongs to the contrapuntal but not the harmonic practice (it uses efficient voice leading but has free progressions). Jazz and rock often belong to the harmonic but not the contrapuntal practice (they use functional progressions but permit parallel fifths). This Venn diagram (Figure 6.4.2) clarifies that "the common practice period" is more accurately described as the period when these two practices overlapped most completely.

# Context & Application
This framework resolves a persistent theoretical debate. Schenkerians deny that functional harmony involves independent harmonic conventions, claiming apparent harmonic laws derive from voice-leading. Tymoczko's Venn diagram provides a strong counterargument: if harmonic rules were just voice-leading rules, then any music with efficient voice leading would automatically have functional progressions — but Renaissance music has the former without the latter. Conversely, if there were no independent harmonic rules, jazz/rock could not maintain functional patterns while relaxing voice-leading constraints — but they do. The two practices are genuinely independent.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating all rules of common-practice music as a single unified system
  **Correction**: Voice-leading rules and harmonic progression rules are independent and can be combined or separated independently

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming music must follow both practices to be "tonal"
  **Clarification**: Music in either practice can be tonal; the extended common practice includes music following only one of the two sub-practices
