# Quick Definition
Root progressions by perfect fourth or fifth become increasingly prevalent from Josquin through Bach because they are uniquely effective at harmonizing any stepwise melody in a 3+1 voice-leading arrangement without creating forbidden parallels.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko traces the increasing dominance of fourth/fifth root progressions: evenly distributed among steps, thirds, and fourths in Dufay and Ockeghem, fourths reach >50% in Josquin, ~60% in Palestrina, and >70% in the Bach chorales (Figure 6.3.11). This dominance has a structural explanation: starting from any triad with any soprano note, a fourth/fifth root progression can harmonize ANY stepwise soprano motion using the 3+1 schema. By contrast, step progressions harmonize fewer stepwise melodies and easily create parallel fifths/octaves, while third progressions can harmonize only two of the possible stepwise motions from a given starting chord. Fourth progressions are thus the most flexible tool in the composer's arsenal for combining smooth melody with consistent harmony.

# Key Properties
1. Fourth progressions increase from ~33% (Dufay) to >70% (Bach) of root motions
2. From any triad with any soprano note, a fourth progression can harmonize any stepwise motion
3. Step progressions risk parallel fifths/octaves; third progressions are less flexible
4. Fourth progressions have exactly one common tone with the preceding chord (Goldilocks amount)
5. Renaissance distinguishes two types: within-phrase (3+1 schema, no tension-release) and cadential (converging voices, tension-release)
6. The gradual fusion of these two types may have produced functional harmony

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Given a triad and a stepwise soprano motion, choose the fourth/fifth-related triad that contains the target soprano note
2. Arrange upper voices in the most efficient crossing-free configuration
3. Move bass from root to root
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Determine root motion between successive chords
2. Check whether roots relate by fourth or fifth (including compounds)
3. Note that fourth progressions account for the majority of progressions in most tonal music

# Context & Application
The structural advantage of fourth progressions helps explain their dominance across the extended common practice. It also sheds light on the emergence of functional harmony: the two types of fourth progression in Renaissance music (within-phrase and cadential) gradually merged, with within-phrase progressions acquiring the tension-release quality of cadential formulas. This produced the characteristic "harmonic cycles" of functional tonality, each concluding with a V-I cadence.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming fourth progressions are a purely harmonic convention
  **Correction**: Their dominance has a contrapuntal explanation — they are the most flexible option for harmonizing stepwise melodies in a 3+1 framework

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Equating all fourth progressions with functional V-I
  **Clarification**: Many Renaissance fourth progressions have no cadential function and do not create tension-release; the functional interpretation develops gradually
