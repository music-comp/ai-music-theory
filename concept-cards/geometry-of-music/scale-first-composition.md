# Quick Definition
A compositional technique where music progresses by efficient voice leading between scales themselves, generalizing traditional modulation to a broader range of scales and modes.

# Core Definition
In scale-first composition, efficient voice leading connects scales rather than chords, creating a distinctively scalar logic. Single-semitone changes transform one scale into another -- for example, raising one note of C diatonic to produce C acoustic. This generalizes traditional modulation (moving between closely related diatonic keys by changing one note) to the full range of twentieth-century scales including acoustic, harmonic, whole-tone, and octatonic collections. The technique extends nineteenth-century chordal voice-leading practices to a new scalar domain.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of chord-scale compatibility
- Familiarity with the scale lattice and relationships between scale types

# Key Properties
1. Scales rather than chords determine the musical logic
2. Adjacent scales are connected by single-semitone voice leading
3. Generalizes traditional diatonic modulation to nondiatonic scales
4. Can be tracked on the scale lattice, where nearby scales are connected by small voice leadings
5. Has entered the musical vernacular across styles (classical, jazz, rock, minimalism)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose a starting scale
2. Alter one or two notes by semitone to produce a nearby scale on the scale lattice
3. Continue moving through closely related scales
4. The resulting path traces a compact region on the scale lattice
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for passages where scales change gradually by one or two notes
2. Plot the scales on the scale lattice -- if they form a compact region, scale-first composition is likely
3. Check whether the scalar logic seems primary (scales were composed before chords)

# Context & Application
Scale-first composition appears prominently in Debussy, Janacek, Shostakovich, Reich, rock music (The Who, Bob Seger), and jazz. It is now "an accepted part of our contemporary tonal language" -- totally unremarkable to find musicians exploiting efficient voice leadings between the full range of diatonic modes.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 328-329, Fig. 9.3.4): Ravel's "Ondine" -- a series of single-semitone changes creates Cs mixolydian b6, Cs harmonic major, Cs diatonic, and Gs acoustic. The passage explores a small region of scale space.

**Example 2** (p. 340-344): Debussy's "Des pas sur la neige" -- explores five closely related scales in a tonic region (C diatonic, F diatonic, A harmonic minor, D harmonic minor, Bb acoustic) and a contrasting region (Ab mixolydian, Ab dorian, C whole tone). The acoustic scale serves as intermediary between whole-tone and diatonic.

**Example 3** (p. 348-350): Shostakovich's F# minor Prelude and Fugue Op. 87 -- progressive lowering of scale degrees creates a "hyperminor" tonality. The sixteen possible scales from combining four lowerings form a four-dimensional cube.

**Example 4** (p. 350-354): Reich's New York Counterpoint -- scales linked by single-semitone voice leading demarcate compact regions in scale space.

**Example 5** (p. 358-359): The Who's "I Can't Explain" and Bob Seger's "Turn the Page" -- single-semitone shifts between verse and chorus modes (mixolydian to ionian, dorian to natural minor).

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming all scale changes are examples of scale-first composition
  **Correction**: Scale-first composition requires that scalar voice leading is the primary logic, not a byproduct of chordal motion

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing scale-first composition with the subset technique
  **Clarification**: When two scales share a large subset and are linked by efficient voice leading, both labels may apply; the distinction is one of emphasis
