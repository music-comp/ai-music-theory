# Quick Definition
The use of melodic intervals of a perfect fourth in jazz improvisation, facilitated by selecting notes from small stacks-of-fourths collections or pentatonic scales, which ensures a high proportion of fourth intervals regardless of note order.

# Core Definition
Melodic fourths became central to jazz improvisation largely through the influence of the John Coltrane Quartet in the early 1960s. One practical approach: select notes from a small stack-of-fourths collection (e.g., {F, G, Bb, C}) and play them in any order, which guarantees a high proportion of fourths. A four-note stack yields 50% fourths/fifths among its intervals; a five-note pentatonic scale yields 40%. The pentatonic scale's role as a "minimal perturbation of a pure stack of fourths" (returning to its starting point without passing through all twelve pitch classes) explains why similar considerations govern scale design, instrument tuning, and improvisational choices.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of quartal voicings and pentatonic scales

# Key Properties
1. Became prominent in 1960s jazz through Coltrane's influence
2. Stack-of-fourths collections guarantee high proportion of melodic fourths
3. Pentatonic scales naturally produce quartal melodies
4. Same mathematical considerations govern scale design and guitar tuning
5. Extended fourth sequences without a "near fourth" generate notes foreign to the harmony

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose a stack of fourths from the prevailing scale (e.g., F-Bb-Eb or G-C-F)
2. Play notes from this collection in any order
3. Optionally expand to pentatonic scale for more variety
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Listen for prominent perfect fourth intervals in melodic lines
2. Check whether the notes form a subset of a pentatonic or stack-of-fourths collection

# Context & Application
Melodic fourths connect jazz improvisation to the abstract mathematics of scale design and instrument tuning, illustrating how "the same fundamental facts can help explain such disparate musical phenomena."

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 377, Fig. 10.2.4): Keith Jarrett's solo on "You and the Night and the Music" -- four-note stack of fourths {F, G, Bb, C} expanding to pentatonic.

**Example 2** (p. 377, Fig. 10.2.5): The opening of "Freedom Jazz Dance" -- pentatonic arpeggio augmented by D to form a six-note stack of fifths.
