# Quick Definition
A compositional technique that juxtaposes scales sharing some contextually salient set of notes, dividing pitch material into "fixed" degrees that remain constant and "mobile" degrees that change to form different scalar collections.

# Core Definition
The subset technique links scales by way of common chords or common chord types rather than primarily through voice leading. A composer holds certain notes fixed while varying others to create different scalar contexts for the same melodic material. When the shared subsets are small and chord-like, the technique resembles chord-first composition; when they are large, it more closely resembles scale-first composition. The distinction between the subset technique and the other two is one of emphasis: when composers highlight the fixed subset (e.g., by using it in important themes), subset relationships are more important than voice leading.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of chord-scale compatibility
- Familiarity with scale construction

# Key Properties
1. Divides pitch material into "fixed" and "mobile" degrees
2. Intermediate between chord-first and scale-first composition
3. Fixed subsets are often stacked in fifths (D-A-E, G-D-A, etc.)
4. Can operate at concrete level (same pitches) or abstract level (same interval patterns)
5. Shared subsets often correspond to pentatonic or other familiar collections

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose a fixed subset of pitches (often a pentatonic collection or stack of fifths)
2. Vary the remaining "mobile" degrees to create different scalar contexts
3. The fixed pitches may appear in themes, ostinati, or drones
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for prominent melodic material that remains constant across scale changes
2. Identify which pitches are "fixed" and which are "mobile"
3. Check whether the fixed subset is compositionally emphasized (themes, ostinati)

# Context & Application
The subset technique appears in music from Grieg through Stravinsky to Steve Reich and the Beatles, crossing boundaries between art music, jazz, rock, and minimalism. The resemblances among these composers are not coincidental but reflect shared musical concerns and the natural properties of familiar scales.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 329-330): Debussy's "Les collines d'Anacapri" -- the pentatonic theme B-F#-C#-E-G#-B appears in three scalar contexts: B diatonic, E diatonic, and E acoustic. Five "fixed" scale degrees and two "mobile" degrees.

**Example 2** (p. 359-362): Grieg's "Klokkeklang" Op. 54 No. 6 -- three fixed notes (D, A, E) and four mobile notes create five different scales (C diatonic, D diatonic, A diatonic, F diatonic, D harmonic minor).

**Example 3** (p. 362-363): Stravinsky's "Petit airs" from Histoire du soldat -- fixed notes G-D-A-E-B with mobile F/F# and C/C# create four scales on a square face of the scale lattice.

**Example 4** (p. 364-365): The Beatles' "Help!" -- lead vocal confined to first five pitches of A major scale, contextualized within A major and A mixolydian. Different timbres reinforce the fixed/mobile distinction.

**Example 5** (p. 365-367): Stravinsky's "Dance of the Adolescents" -- abstract version where fixed intervallic patterns (major pentachord 2-2-1-2) appear in different modal contexts (aeolian, dorian, locrian #2).

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the subset technique is always distinct from the other two techniques
  **Correction**: The three techniques form a continuum; the subset technique is intermediate and may overlap with either

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing the concrete and abstract forms of the technique
  **Clarification**: The concrete form holds specific pitches fixed; the abstract form holds interval patterns fixed while allowing transposition
