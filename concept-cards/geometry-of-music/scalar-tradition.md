# Quick Definition
The scalar tradition encompasses twentieth-century tonal music that makes extensive use of familiar scales and modes — including impressionism, neoclassicism, jazz, rock, minimalism, and neo-Romanticism — united by the systematic combination of tonal center change, scale type change, and chromatic transposition.

# Core Definition
Diametrically opposed to the chromatic tradition, the scalar tradition maintains limited macroharmonies (5-8 note scales) as a primary organizational feature. Originating in late-nineteenth-century experiments by Chopin, Liszt, Mussorgsky, Rimsky-Korsakov, Grieg, and Faure with modes, whole-tone, octatonic, and melodic/harmonic minor scales, the scalar tradition became the dominant approach in impressionism, neoclassicism, jazz, rock, minimalism, and neo-Romanticism. Its distinctive innovation is the systematic combination of three operations: change of tonal center (modal transposition), change of scale type (moving between diatonic, acoustic, octatonic, etc.), and chromatic transposition. Within the scalar tradition, Tymoczko further distinguishes "chordal" approaches (preserving two-tiered chord-within-scale organization) from "pandiatonic" or "panscalar" approaches (effacing the role of chords as scales assert a more central harmonic role).

# Key Properties
1. Uses familiar 5-8 note scales as primary macroharmonies
2. Encompasses at least six major twentieth-century movements
3. Combines three operations: center change, scale-type change, chromatic transposition
4. Offers 450+ possible tonal areas (vs. 24 in classical tonality)
5. Contains both chordal and pandiatonic sub-approaches
6. The "chordal vs. pandiatonic" distinction mirrors the "Reger vs. Schoenberg" split in the chromatic tradition

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose from the primary scale types (diatonic, acoustic, harmonic minor/major, octatonic, whole-tone, etc.)
2. Establish a tonal center within the chosen scale
3. Modulate by changing center, scale type, or both
4. Maintain macroharmonic clarity (identifiable collections at any given point)
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Check for identifiable scale-based macroharmonies
2. Look for systematic modulation between scale types
3. Note whether chord-within-scale organization is maintained (chordal) or effaced (pandiatonic)

# Context & Application
The scalar tradition represents the dominant musical approach of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries when measured by sheer volume of repertoire. Tymoczko argues it should not be seen merely as a conservative rejection of atonality but as a genuine expansion of tonal resources — the first systematic exploration of nondiatonic scales alongside diatonic ones. The late-twentieth-century explosion of diatonicism (Shostakovich, Riley, Reich, Glass, Part, Gorecki, Adams, plus jazz and rock) demonstrates that the scalar tradition is not a relic but a living practice.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the scalar tradition as stylistically backward or conservative
  **Correction**: The tradition represents a genuine expansion of tonal resources — 450+ key areas vs. 24 — and continues to develop new techniques

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Equating "scalar" with "diatonic"
  **Clarification**: The scalar tradition includes extensive use of non-diatonic scales (whole-tone, octatonic, acoustic, etc.), particularly in impressionism and jazz
