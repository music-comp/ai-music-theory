# Quick Definition
Tymoczko advocates a "process-based" approach to chromatic harmony — teaching the general principle of chromatic gap-filling rather than memorizing individual chord types ("one chord per chapter"), so students can confront unfamiliar sonorities both analytically and compositionally.

# Core Definition
Traditional textbooks present chromatic harmony as a series of disconnected idioms, often in a "one chord per chapter" format: Italian sixth in one chapter, French sixth in the next, Neapolitan in another. This "object-based" approach is diametrically opposed to Tymoczko's "process-based" approach, which emphasizes the general structural principle linking all these chords: diatonic tones are modified to create efficient chromatic voice leading, smoothing the transition from chord to chord. The process-based approach is more powerful because: (1) not all useful chromatic chords have standard names (the "Till Sixth," the "Ecstatic Sixth"); (2) students who understand the process can invent and analyze new chromatic sonorities; (3) it reveals deep connections between superficially different chromatic techniques across centuries and styles. Mastering chromaticism requires developing a flexible grasp of all voice-leading possibilities between familiar chords — knowledge that nineteenth-century composers developed intuitively through keyboard experimentation.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of decorative chromaticism as chromatic gap-filling

# Key Properties
1. Object-based: memorize individual chord types and their resolutions
2. Process-based: understand the general principle of chromatic voice leading
3. Process-based approach handles unnamed chords and unfamiliar contexts
4. It reveals connections between superficially different chromatic techniques
5. Nineteenth-century composers developed this knowledge through keyboard experimentation
6. Twenty-first-century musicians can use geometric tools to conceptualize abstract relationships

# Construction / Recognition
## To Apply:
1. When encountering an unfamiliar chromatic chord, ask: "What diatonic chord does this resolve to?"
2. Identify which notes are chromatically altered and in which direction
3. Check if the alteration creates semitonal voice leading to the resolution chord
4. Classify the voice leading using the schemas (four-to-three-note, etc.)
5. Do not panic if the chord has no standard name

# Context & Application
This pedagogical philosophy underlies the entire chapter. It explains why Tymoczko begins with general principles (Section 8.1) before moving to specific analyses (Sections 8.3-8.6), and why the specific analyses repeatedly emphasize connections between different chromatic techniques.
