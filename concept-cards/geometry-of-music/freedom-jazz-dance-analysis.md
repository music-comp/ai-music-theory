# Quick Definition
The Miles Davis Group's "Freedom Jazz Dance" (1966) demonstrates the subset technique in improvisation: soloists interpret a static Bb harmony through a variety of scales (natural minor, melodic minor, mixolydian, acoustic, harmonic minor, octatonic), showing that "being in Bb" encompasses an extended twentieth-century scalar system.

# Core Definition
The piece consists of improvisations over a static Bb harmony. The bass line features fourths-based riffs (F-Eb-Bb); Herbie Hancock's accompaniment uses blues-scale chords (E-Bb-Eb). The soloists "color" the Bb drone differently: Miles Davis favors traditional Bb minor scales, Wayne Shorter makes heavy use of Bb acoustic, and Herbie Hancock uses octatonic and harmonic minor. The relevant conception of tonality is "not the major-minor system of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, but rather the extended scalar system of the twentieth." The piece parallels Debussy's notion of "being in D" in "Des pas sur la neige," encompassing natural minor, dorian, acoustic, and octatonic.

# Key Properties
1. Static Bb harmony with multiple scalar interpretations
2. Different soloists choose different scales
3. Miles Davis: traditional Bb minor scales
4. Wayne Shorter: heavy Bb acoustic usage
5. Herbie Hancock: octatonic and harmonic minor
6. "Being in Bb" = the extended twentieth-century scalar system
7. Parallels Debussy's multi-scalar approach to a single tonic

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Identify the static tonic harmony
2. Extract the scales used by each soloist
3. Note the variety of scalar interpretations of a single tonic

# Context & Application
This analysis demonstrates that jazz's "being in a key" is as expansive as Debussy's, encompassing the full range of twentieth-century scales over a single tonal center.
