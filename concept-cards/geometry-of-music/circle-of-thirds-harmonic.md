# Quick Definition
A circular arrangement of diatonic triads by descending thirds that simultaneously models single-step voice-leading relationships (contrapuntal role) and permissible harmonic progressions (harmonic role).

# Core Definition
The circle of thirds arranges the seven diatonic triads so that adjacent chords are related by third: I - vi - IV - ii - vii° - V - iii - (I). In Chapter 6, this circle was introduced as a purely contrapuntal structure showing single-step voice leadings among diatonic triads. In Chapter 7, the same circle reappears as a model of harmonic syntax: rightward motion along it defines the permissible progressions from tonic to dominant. This double significance means that a passage moving along the descending thirds chain is simultaneously a sequence of chords linked by maximally efficient voice leading and a complete statement of all potential intermediaries between tonic and dominant. Harmony and counterpoint work hand in hand.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of diatonic triads and their pitch content
- Concept of voice-leading efficiency between chords

# Key Properties
1. Adjacent chords share two of three notes (maximum common tones for triads)
2. Short rightward motions produce pitch classes that descend by third
3. The circle plays a dual role: contrapuntal (voice-leading proximity) and harmonic (functional progression)
4. For triads, strong progressions are realized by ascending voice leading (notes move up by step)
5. For seventh chords, an analogous circle exists where strong progressions use descending voice leading

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Start with any diatonic triad (e.g., C major)
2. Move to the triad whose root is a diatonic third below
3. Continue: C - Am - F - Dm - B° - G - Em - (C)
4. Write out the pitch classes in each chord to verify single-step voice-leading connections

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. A sequence of triads where each shares two common tones with its neighbor
2. Root motion consistently by descending third
3. Individual voices move by single diatonic step between adjacent chords

# Context & Application
The dual role of the circle of thirds is remarkable: it explains why functional tonality can satisfy both harmonic and voice-leading constraints simultaneously. The fact that the same circular arrangement governs both voice-leading proximity and harmonic syntax is not coincidental — the psychologically robust similarity created by efficient voice leading may have influenced the developing conventions of functional harmony. The circle also underpins the analysis of sequences in Section 7.3.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the circle of thirds is purely a contrapuntal device
  **Correction**: It simultaneously models both voice-leading proximity and harmonic function

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating the triadic circle of thirds with the seventh-chord circle of thirds
  **Clarification**: Both exist, but they have opposite voice-leading directions — triadic strong progressions use ascending voice leading, while seventh-chord strong progressions use descending voice leading
