# Quick Definition
Pandiatonicism (or "panscalar" music) effaces the role of chords as scales themselves become the primary harmonic entities, exhibiting macroharmonic consistency without harmonic consistency — the scalar counterpart to what Schoenberg's atonality is to chromatic music.

# Core Definition
Within the scalar tradition, Tymoczko distinguishes between chordal approaches (which preserve the two-tiered "chord within scale" organization of traditional music) and pandiatonic/panscalar approaches (in which the distinction between chord and scale becomes blurred, with scales asserting a more central harmonic role). Pandiatonicism was coined by Nicolas Slonimsky and has been applied both to diatonic music lacking harmonic consistency and to diatonic music lacking centricity. Tymoczko uses it in the former sense: pandiatonic music stays within a recognizable scale (macroharmonic consistency) but does not organize its pitch content into structurally similar chords (no harmonic consistency). Debussy's "Voiles" is an early example. This creates a parallel within the scalar tradition to the chromatic tradition's internal distinction: just as Reger preserves harmonic consistency while Schoenberg abandons it, chordal scalar music preserves it while pandiatonicism abandons it.

# Key Properties
1. Limited macroharmony (identifiable scales) — YES
2. Harmonic consistency (structurally similar chords) — NO
3. Scales become the primary harmonic entities rather than chords
4. The scalar counterpart to atonality's abandonment of harmonic consistency
5. Debussy's "Voiles" is a paradigmatic early example
6. Creates sonic "washes" where macroharmonic change is more salient than chord change

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose a scale as the macroharmony
2. Use notes from the scale without organizing them into consistent chord types
3. Let the scale itself create the harmonic color
4. Change scales to create large-scale harmonic motion
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. The music stays within a recognizable scale (macroharmonic consistency)
2. Individual harmonies are diverse — not consistently triadic or any other chord type
3. Harmonic motion comes from macroharmonic change rather than chord progression

# Context & Application
Pandiatonicism appears in Figure 5.8.7 as one of four quadrants: limited macroharmony + no harmonic consistency. It parallels "complete atonality" (no macroharmony + no harmonic consistency) and contrasts with "traditional scale-based tonality" (limited macroharmony + harmonic consistency). In practice, pandiatonicism often creates atmospheric or impressionistic textures where the overall sonic color matters more than individual chord identities. The concept extends beyond diatonicism: any scale can serve as the basis, hence "panscalar."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming pandiatonic music is formless or random
  **Correction**: It is organized by macroharmony (scale choice and change) even though it lacks chord-level consistency

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Equating pandiatonicism with atonality
  **Clarification**: Pandiatonicism maintains limited macroharmony (identifiable scales); atonality abandons both macroharmonic and harmonic consistency
