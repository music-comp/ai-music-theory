# Quick Definition
The ubiquitous descending-fifths sequence (C-F-b°-e-a-d-G-C) is the third-most compact sequence on the circle of thirds, consisting of two-step motions, and can be realized with maximally efficient descending voice leading when using seventh chords.

# Core Definition
The descending-fifths sequence is among the most common patterns in tonal music, appearing 33 times in Mozart's piano sonatas with zero instances of its ascending counterpart. On the circle of thirds, it consists of two-step motions (each fifth spans two thirds). When realized with seventh chords, it can be articulated entirely by maximally efficient descending voice leading — each voice moves down by one step to reach the next chord. This alignment of strong harmonic motion with efficient descending voice leading makes it an especially natural and satisfying pattern. Tymoczko views the descending-fifths sequence as a composite structure that can be factored into the more fundamental descending-thirds pattern via third substitution.

# Key Properties
1. Third-most compact on the circle of thirds (two-step motions)
2. 33 instances in Mozart sonatas; 0 for ascending-fifths counterpart
3. Can be realized with maximally efficient descending voice leading (seventh chords)
4. Related to "down a third, up a step" sequence by third substitution
5. Consists entirely of strong progressions

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Start on any diatonic chord
2. Move the root down by diatonic fifth (or up by diatonic fourth)
3. Repeat the melodic-rhythmic pattern at each new level
4. With seventh chords, lower each voice by step

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Root motion consistently by descending fifth (ascending fourth)
2. Pattern repeats at each new level
3. With seventh chords, look for pervasive descending stepwise motion

# Context & Application
The descending-fifths sequence is foundational to tonal music from Bach through jazz. Its special status comes from combining exclusively strong progressions with efficient voice leading. Chopin's E minor Prelude is a chromatic elaboration of a descending-fifths sequence of dominant sevenths, disguised by interpolated passing chords. The sequence bridges diatonic and chromatic practices.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating descending-fifths sequences as the most basic harmonic motion
  **Correction**: Tymoczko argues descending thirds are more fundamental; fifths are composite

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking all descending-fifths motion must be sequential
  **Clarification**: Descending fifths occur both in sequences and in nonsequential harmonic cycles (I-IV-V-I contains a descending fifth I-IV)
