# Quick Definition
The 3+1 voice-leading schema is the prevalent arrangement in which three upper voices articulate strongly crossing-free voice leadings between complete triads while a fourth voice (typically the bass) independently adds doublings, often leaping from root to root.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko identifies a "sizable proportion" of Renaissance voice leadings as conforming to the 3+1 schema: three voices form a strongly crossing-free voice leading between complete triads (as modeled on the circle of thirds), while a fourth voice — typically the bass — moves independently according to its own principles, often leaping between chord roots. The upper three voices are typically in close position and move as efficiently as possible. This schema allows four-voice music to be analyzed using three-note chord space, treating the bass as an "odd man out" subject to different rules. Historical data shows 3+1 voice leadings accounting for a significant and increasing proportion of four-voice triadic voice leadings from Dufay through the late Renaissance. The schema anticipates the figured-bass conception of harmony as "a bass line plus some relatively homogeneous upper voices."

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose successive triads
2. Arrange three upper voices in close position
3. Move upper voices by the most efficient crossing-free voice leading (via the circle of thirds)
4. Add the bass independently, typically on chord roots
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for a leaping bass with three smoother upper voices
2. Check whether the upper three voices form complete triads connected by single-step motion
3. Verify that the upper voice-leading is strongly crossing-free

# Context & Application
The 3+1 schema is remarkable for its longevity. It appears in Renaissance polyphony (Josquin), is standard practice in Bach chorales, and persists in contemporary guitar fingerings where the top three strings articulate efficient voice leadings while the bass strings provide roots. This continuity is central to the extended common practice thesis. The schema also has pedagogical value: by separating the bass from the upper voices, students can focus on smooth three-voice counterpoint (modeled on the circle of thirds) without the complications of four-dimensional chord space.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the bass always doubles the root
  **Correction**: While the bass often moves root-to-root, it can also provide other chord tones; the key point is that it moves independently of the upper voices

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking 3+1 means the bass is always a separate entity from the harmony
  **Clarification**: The bass contributes to the overall harmony but follows different voice-leading principles than the upper voices — it can leap where they typically step
