# Quick Definition
Familiar cadential formulas (including V-I) arise as relatively obvious solutions to the problem of harmonizing two converging stepwise voices, rather than being arbitrary conventions — and their evolution from medieval to Renaissance to functional practice shows increasing complexity built on the same basic principle.

# Core Definition
Medieval cadences feature two voices converging by stepwise contrary motion onto a unison or octave. When a third voice is added (with the bass sounding the pitch class of convergence), only a handful of harmonizations are available. These include: the double leading-tone cadence (with F# — a standard medieval form), the vii-degree-6-I progression (with F-natural), the phrygian cadence (with Bb and Db), and a progression closely resembling V-I. With four voices, the situation is even more constrained — virtually all options evoke familiar cadences. Tymoczko argues that these cadential formulas are not arbitrary conventions but emerge as natural solutions to a basic contrapuntal problem. The history of cadential practice shows the gradual dominance of the V-I form, which Randel attributes to two factors: the preference for concluding on open fifths (rather than complete triads) and the increasing preference for four-voice textures.

# Key Properties
1. Basic pattern: two voices converge stepwise in contrary motion onto unison/octave
2. Adding a third voice generates a small number of familiar cadential types
3. Adding a fourth voice further constrains options — almost all evoke known cadences
4. V-I gradually dominates due to preference for open fifths and four-voice textures
5. Medieval double leading-tone cadence, vii-6-I, phrygian cadence, and V-I all derive from the same basic schema
6. Functional harmony may have arisen from the fusion of cadential and non-cadential fourth progressions

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Begin with two voices converging by step onto a unison/octave (the basic medieval pattern)
2. Add a third voice that sounds the convergence pitch in the bass
3. Choose harmonization: with raised 7th (V-I or double leading tone), with natural 7th (vii-6-I), with lowered notes (phrygian)
4. Optionally add a fourth voice completing the dominant chord
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for stepwise convergence in at least two voices
2. Check whether the bass arrives at the chord root of the resolution
3. Identify the specific cadential type by the intervallic content of the penultimate chord

# Context & Application
The evolution of cadential formulas from medieval to functional practice illustrates how compositional solutions build on each other across centuries. The same basic principle — converging stepwise motion — generates different cadential types depending on the number of voices and the prevailing consonance rules. The gradual dominance of V-I is not arbitrary but reflects the intersection of multiple preferences (open-fifth conclusions, four-voice textures, bass on root). One interesting hypothesis is that functional tonality arose when within-phrase fourth progressions acquired the tension-release quality of cadential formulas, producing "harmonic cycles" each ending with V-I.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating cadential formulas as arbitrary conventions
  **Correction**: They arise as a small number of solutions to the problem of harmonizing converging stepwise voices — the options are structurally constrained

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming V-I has always been the primary cadential form
  **Clarification**: The double leading-tone cadence dominated medieval and early Renaissance practice; V-I gradually became dominant through the late Renaissance
