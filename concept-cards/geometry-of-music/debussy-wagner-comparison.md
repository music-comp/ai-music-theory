# Quick Definition
Debussy uses the same half-diminished to dominant seventh voice leadings as Wagner's Tristan but at a much slower rate, generating diatonic modes rather than chromatic macroharmony, creating a "classicist" quality in marked contrast to Wagner's "relentless churning."

# Core Definition
The central section of Debussy's "Fetes" exploits all different types of crossing-free voice leadings between half-diminished and dominant seventh chords, systematically exploring the four-dimensional lattice near the diminished seventh chord. This is "essentially the technique of Wagner's Tristan." The key difference lies in their attitude toward scales: for Wagner, chromatic voice leading is purely chromatic, bringing the chromatic scale into play at the melodic level. For Debussy, these chromatic voice leadings occur at a much slower rate and give rise to additional diatonic modes at the surface. By remaining in a single scale for a significant length of time, Debussy endows his music with a simple quality in marked contrast to Wagnerian chromaticism. The same procedures appear in the Prelude to "The Afternoon of a Faun."

# Key Properties
1. Same voice-leading types between half-diminished and dominant seventh chords
2. Debussy exploits all four crossing-free interscalar transpositions
3. Wagner: fast chromatic voice leading -> chromatic macroharmony
4. Debussy: slow chromatic voice leading -> diatonic modal macroharmony
5. Significant continuity between Wagnerism and impressionism
6. The four-dimensional lattice models both composers' chords

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Identify half-diminished to dominant seventh voice leadings
2. Note the rate at which chord changes occur
3. Check whether the surface is chromatic (Wagner) or modal (Debussy)

# Context & Application
This comparison reveals "significant continuity between Wagnerian Romanticism and Debussian impressionism," challenging the narrative of a radical break between the two.
