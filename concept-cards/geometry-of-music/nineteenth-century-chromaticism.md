# Quick Definition
Nineteenth-century chromaticism involves the increasing tendency to conceive of chords as objects in chromatic (rather than diatonic) space, using direct chromatic voice leading between triads and seventh chords — manifesting in both a pedestrian form that decorates functional progressions and a more radical form that bypasses them entirely.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko identifies two strands of nineteenth-century chromaticism. The "pedestrian" technique uses chromatic chords to embellish functional progressions without fundamentally altering them. The "radical" technique abandons functional norms in favor of direct chromatic voice leading, using chromatic passing chords, neighboring chords, and bridges to distant keys. The shift from diatonic to chromatic thinking is illustrated by filling in chromatic gaps: in the diatonic scale, G moves to A in one step; in the chromatic scale, there is room for Gs between them, generating augmented triads and borrowed chords through the same underlying process of "gap-filling." This perspective reveals that chords typically labeled differently ("borrowed chord," "augmented sixth," "augmented triad") are often products of the same chromatic voice-leading process. The major-third system (connecting triads whose roots are a major third apart) and minor-third system (connecting seventh chords whose roots relate by minor third or tritone) are the two primary "highways" through chromatic space.

# Key Properties
1. Two strands: pedestrian (decorating functional progressions) and radical (bypassing them)
2. Gap-filling: chromatic notes inserted between diatonic scale steps generate chromatic chords
3. The major-third system connects triads by efficient chromatic voice leading
4. The minor-third system connects seventh chords by efficient voice leading
5. A chord can be simultaneously "close" melodically (connected by semitone voice leading) and "distant" tonally (from a remote key)
6. Traditional labels ("borrowed," "augmented sixth") can obscure the common voice-leading origin
7. Geometry of chord space ensures that intuitive exploration naturally produces these systems

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Take a standard diatonic progression
2. Fill chromatic gaps between chord tones to create passing or neighboring chords
3. Use major-third-related triads or minor-third-related seventh chords for dramatic modulation
4. Combine pedestrian and radical techniques within a single passage
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for chromatic voice leading (semitone motion in multiple voices)
2. Check whether chromatic chords function as embellishments of functional progressions or as independent entities
3. Identify major-third or minor-third root relationships between consecutive chords

# Context & Application
Tymoczko's analysis of Schumann's "Chopin" (Section 6.5) exemplifies the dual nature of chromatic chords: the A7 chord is simultaneously a collection of chromatic passing tones (contrapuntally explicable) and a familiar dominant seventh with its own harmonic identity (vertically significant). This "twofold status" is characteristic of Romantic chromaticism. The Schubert C major Quintet scherzo (Section 6.6) shows chromatic modulation on a larger scale, with 9 of 11 modulations using the major- and minor-third systems. Understanding the geometry of chord space helps explain why composers' intuitive exploration of chromatic voice leading consistently produces these particular patterns.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 233, Figures 6.5.1-6.5.2): Schumann's "Chopin" — the A7 chord as both chromatic passing chord (four voices each moving by semitone) and harmonically significant dominant seventh, simultaneously "close" (by voice leading) and "distant" (tonally).

**Example 2** (p. 235-236, Figures 6.6.1-6.6.2): Gap-filling: diatonic C-Am-F becomes chromatic C-C+-F or C-fm-F by inserting notes between G and A. Chopin's E major prelude uses both major-third and minor-third systems.

**Example 3** (p. 237, Figure 6.6.3): Schubert's C major Quintet scherzo — 9 of 11 modulations use the major- or minor-third system.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating chromatic chords only as "borrowed" from other keys
  **Correction**: Many chromatic chords arise from voice-leading processes (gap-filling) rather than key-borrowing; the "borrowing" metaphor can obscure structural relationships

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking "pedestrian" and "radical" chromaticism are unrelated
  **Clarification**: Both involve the same underlying chromatic voice-leading processes; the difference is whether the result decorates a functional progression or replaces it
