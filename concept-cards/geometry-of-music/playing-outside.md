# Quick Definition
The jazz practice of deliberately departing from the underlying key or harmony to create tension, oscillating between states of being "together" (in key) and "apart" (out of key), adding a dimension of tension and release beyond traditional dominant-tonic function.

# Core Definition
Playing outside involves deliberately moving to notes or chords foreign to the underlying harmony, creating a polytonal clash between the improviser and the accompaniment. This technique traces back to the blues, where blue notes (b3, b5) create "delicious dissonance" with the underlying harmony. In more radical form, improvisers shift entire melodic passages to foreign keys ("sidestepping"), often by a semitone. The result is four kinds of harmonic tension in jazz: (1) motion within a key (I to V and back), (2) motion between keys (tonic to subsidiary regions), (3) passing and neighboring tones (fleeting dissonances), and (4) motion between states of being together and apart. The interaction of these four kinds can produce music of extraordinary subtlety.

# Key Properties
1. Oscillation between "home" (in key) and "away" (out of key)
2. Traceable to blues polytonal clashes
3. Sidestepping: shifting entire melodic figures by semitone
4. Four types of harmonic tension interact simultaneously
5. Wide registral and timbral separation between instruments ameliorates clashes
6. "Modal jazz" sometimes refers to this technique over static harmonies
7. Classical precedents in Chopin's semitonal key shifts

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Establish the home key clearly
2. Shift melodic material up or down by semitone to a foreign key
3. Return to the home key, creating resolution
4. Use registral separation to prevent excessive harshness
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Listen for melodic material that clashes with the underlying harmony
2. Check whether the "wrong" notes form a coherent foreign-key pattern
3. Note the oscillation between in-key and out-of-key states

# Context & Application
This technique produces some of jazz's most exciting moments. Warne Marsh's solo on "Smog Eyes" plays an E major figure over Eb harmony. McCoy Tyner's "Passion Dance" oscillates between in-key fourths (F-Bb-Eb) and out-of-key fourths (F#-B-E) over a static F7 drone. Bill Evans' "Oleo" features extended chromatic passages that depart entirely from the chord changes.
