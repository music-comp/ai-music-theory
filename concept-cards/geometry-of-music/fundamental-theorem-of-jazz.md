# Quick Definition
The "Fundamental Theorem of Jazz" states that in a 2-gap macroharmony, you can never be more than a semitone wrong — any out-of-scale note can be resolved by moving a semitone in either direction into the macroharmony.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko's playfully named principle captures a fundamental property of 2-gap macroharmonies: when trying to fit any chord into such a collection, one only needs to shift its notes by at most one semitone. Since jazz improvisers typically work within 2-gap macroharmonies (diatonic, acoustic, or other 7-8 note scales), any note they play either belongs to the macroharmony or functions as a chromatic neighbor to one of its notes. If a note falls outside the macroharmony, both of its chromatic neighbors are guaranteed to be inside it, allowing instant correction without having to calculate the exact direction of resolution. This property explains why experienced improvisers can incorporate "mistakes" fluidly — any accidental out-of-scale note can be reinterpreted as a chromatic approach tone.

# Key Properties
1. In a 2-gap macroharmony, out-of-scale notes are always one semitone from a scale tone
2. Both chromatic neighbors of an out-of-scale note are in the collection
3. The improviser can correct in either direction without calculating
4. In a 3-gap macroharmony, one can still never be more than a semitone wrong, but direction of correction may be forced
5. The principle applies to fitting any chord into the macroharmony, not just single notes

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Work within a 2-gap macroharmony (diatonic, acoustic, octatonic, etc.)
2. When playing a note outside the macroharmony, resolve by semitone in either direction
3. Reinterpret out-of-scale notes as chromatic neighbors or approach tones
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Identify the operative macroharmony
2. Note any out-of-scale tones
3. Check whether they resolve by semitone — if the macroharmony is 2-gap, they always can

# Context & Application
While the name is humorous, the principle genuinely underlies jazz improvisation pedagogy. Jazz theory teaches improvisers to associate scales with chords, and most of the recommended scales (diatonic modes, melodic minor modes, diminished scales) are 2-gap macroharmonies. The theorem explains why the "chord-scale" approach works so well practically: the improviser's target collection is forgiving enough that momentary deviations sound like intentional chromaticism rather than errors. More broadly, the theorem explains why 2-gap macroharmonies dominate tonal music — they accommodate the widest range of harmonic content with the least risk of irrecoverable clashes.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 174-175, Figure 5.2.2): In a diatonic scale (a 2-gap macroharmony), the note F# is out of scale but can be corrected to F (down) or G (up), both of which are in the collection. The improviser need not think about which direction to go.

**Example 2** (p. 174): Fitting an E minor triad into the C acoustic scale: the note B is not in the acoustic scale, but shifting it down one semitone to Bb places the chord entirely within the macroharmony.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking the theorem means jazz musicians play random notes
  **Correction**: The theorem provides a safety net for momentary deviations; skilled improvisers still target specific scale tones and chord tones

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating the theorem with near-evenness
  **Clarification**: Near-evenness means scalar transposition approximates chromatic transposition (chords stay similar when moved along the scale). The Fundamental Theorem concerns fitting external material INTO the scale. A nearly even collection can have large gaps (e.g., major triad), and a 2-gap collection can be quite uneven.
