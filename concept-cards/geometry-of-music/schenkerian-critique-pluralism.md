# Quick Definition
Tymoczko argues against Schenkerian monism (harmony reduces to counterpoint) and holism (harmony and counterpoint cannot be separated), advocating for pluralism: independent harmonic laws govern tonal music, and Schenkerian analysis augments but does not replace traditional harmonic theory.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko identifies four positions on the relationship between harmony and counterpoint: (1) Nihilism — denying harmonic regularities exist (refuted by statistical data); (2) Monism — harmonic regularities can be explained contrapuntally (refuted by showing that contrapuntally unobjectionable progressions like I-vi6-I and I-iii-V are virtually absent in classical music); (3) Holism — harmony and counterpoint are inseparable (refuted by the existence of a successful, largely independent harmonic theory); (4) Pluralism — independent harmonic laws exist, and Schenkerian analysis adds additional information without replacing harmonic grammar. Tymoczko advocates pluralism, noting that the project of constructing a harmonic grammar is totally independent of musical analysis, just as linguistics is independent of literary criticism. He offers the two-lattice model as a minimalist alternative to Schenkerian hierarchical self-similarity.

# Key Properties
1. Statistical evidence shows harmonic laws are independent of contrapuntal principles
2. Contrapuntally unobjectionable progressions (V-IV, I-iii-V) are rare in classical music
3. Very similar harmonic laws govern styles with different contrapuntal norms (baroque, classical, jazz)
4. The grammar project is independent of analysis, as linguistics is from literary criticism
5. Scale-to-scale voice leadings provide an alternative to Schenkerian recursive self-similarity

# Context & Application
This section addresses one of the most contentious debates in music theory. Tymoczko's position allows him to respect Schenkerian insights about musical coherence while maintaining the independence and utility of harmonic grammar. His alternative route to self-similarity (via the two lattices) avoids the problematic cognitive claims of Schenkerian recursion.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Fig. 7.6.3, p. 279): Replacing Mozart's I-IV-I with I-vi6-I — contrapuntally fine but harmonically unusual, demonstrating independent harmonic constraints.

**Example 2** (Fig. 7.6.4-7.6.5, pp. 280): V-IV in root position, I-iii-V, i-III-V — all contrapuntally unobjectionable but virtually absent in Mozart's sonatas.

**Example 3** (Fig. 7.6.6, p. 283): Beethoven Op. 10 No. 1 slow movement — five harmonic cycles "like beads on a string" (harmonic view) vs recursive nesting (Schenkerian view).

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking Tymoczko rejects all Schenkerian insights
  **Correction**: He explicitly praises Schenker's contributions and advocates pluralism, not anti-Schenkerianism

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming the harmony-counterpoint debate is merely academic
  **Clarification**: It has practical implications — if harmonic laws are independent, they can be taught and applied without reference to Schenkerian reduction
