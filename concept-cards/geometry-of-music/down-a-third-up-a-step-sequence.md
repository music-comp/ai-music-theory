# Quick Definition
A diatonic sequence alternating descending thirds with ascending steps (e.g., C-a-b°-G-a-F...), which can appear in four basic inversional forms and is related to the descending-fifths sequence by third substitution.

# Core Definition
The "down a third, up a step" sequence alternates one-step and three-step motions on the circle of thirds (descending third then ascending fourth, which is equivalent to descending third then ascending step in root motion). Although rarer than the descending-fifths sequence, it appears sporadically throughout the literature. Tymoczko argues that theorists have been reluctant to acknowledge its existence — as if it were an anomaly threatening the foundations of classical theory. From his perspective it is anything but inexplicable: it results from a simple third substitution applied to the descending-fifths sequence. Replacing first-inversion chords with root-position chords on the same bass (or vice versa) transforms one sequence into the other. It can appear in four basic forms depending on which chords are in inversion.

# Key Properties
1. Alternates descending third with ascending step (or equivalently, descending third with ascending fourth)
2. Related to descending-fifths sequence by third substitution
3. Can appear in four basic forms depending on inversions
4. Entirely composed of strong progressions
5. Repeats at the interval of a descending second
6. Rarer than descending fifths but appears throughout the literature

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Start on any diatonic chord
2. Move root down by third
3. Move root up by step
4. Repeat; the pattern descends by step with each two-chord unit
5. Four forms possible depending on which chords are root position vs first inversion

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Alternating root motions: down a third, up a step (or up a fourth)
2. Net motion descends by step
3. Often disguised by sixteenth-note figuration or inversional variety
4. Related to descending fifths by third substitution

# Context & Application
By providing a theoretical home for this sequence, Tymoczko's model helps musicians learn to hear it and resist the "almost overwhelming temptation to deny its existence." The fluidity with which Bach and Mozart move between descending-fifths and "down a third, up a step" versions testifies to their intimate relationship. Both composers treat them as fundamentally similar patterns.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Fig. 7.3.5, p. 260): Mozart's first Piano Sonata K. 279, third movement — the exposition uses "down a third, up a step," while the development substitutes descending fifths, a minimal perturbation on the circle of thirds.

**Example 2** (Fig. 7.3.6, p. 261): Bach's Ab major fugue (WTC II) uses "down a third, up a step" in mm. 6-7 and descending fifths in mm. 13-15, related by third substitution.

**Example 3** (Fig. 7.3.9, p. 263): Four forms with examples — Haydn's D major Sonata Hob. XVI/42, Bach's "Crucifixus" from B minor Mass, Brahms' F minor Piano Quintet Op. 34, and Bach's G major fugue WTC II.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Denying the existence of this sequence in classical music
  **Correction**: It appears in Bach, Mozart, Haydn, Brahms, and Fauré, among others

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the pattern sounds fundamentally different from descending fifths
  **Clarification**: The two are closely related; Bach and Mozart treat them as near-equivalent
