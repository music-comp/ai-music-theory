# Quick Definition
Steve Reich's characteristic scalar practice, featuring heavy use of diatonic collections, occasional acoustic scales, sporadic exotic scales like "whole-tone plus one," and a fascinating parallelism between pitch organization and rhythmic structure.

# Core Definition
Reich's music makes very heavy use of the diatonic collection, occasional use of the acoustic scale, and more sporadic use of exotic scales. In New York Counterpoint, scales linked by efficient voice leading demarcate compact regions in scale space: Ab diatonic connects by single-semitone voice leading to both Eb diatonic and Db acoustic, and Db acoustic connects to the "whole-tone plus one" scale. In The Desert Music, modulations move generally flatward through closely related scales. Remarkably, Reich's twelve-beat measures create rhythmic patterns structurally analogous to equal-tempered chords: the opening of the third movement of New York Counterpoint attacks on beats {0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11} -- the pitch-class analog of C diatonic. Canons that maximize common attack points parallel scales maximizing common tones.

# Key Properties
1. Heavy diatonic use, occasional acoustic, sporadic exotic scales
2. Scales linked by efficient voice leading in compact lattice regions
3. "Whole-tone plus one" scale appears as third lattice layer beyond diatonic/acoustic
4. Rhythm-pitch parallelism: rhythmic patterns = pitch-class structures
5. Canons maximize common attack points (analogous to common tones)
6. Generally flatward modulatory direction in The Desert Music
7. The "diatonic rhythm" {0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11} appears precisely when nondiatonic pitches enter

# Context & Application
Reich's practice demonstrates scale-first composition within postminimalism. His techniques connect directly to both impressionism and jazz, supporting the argument for a twentieth-century common practice.
