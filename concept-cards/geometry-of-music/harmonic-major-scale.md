# Quick Definition
The inversional partner of the harmonic minor — a major scale with lowered sixth degree (or equivalently, a harmonic minor with raised third). Like the harmonic minor, it has one three-semitone step and is not inversionally symmetrical.

# Core Definition
The harmonic major scale (e.g., C-D-E-F-G-Ab-B) is the inversion of the harmonic minor: where the harmonic minor has the augmented second between degrees 6 and 7, the harmonic major has it between degrees 5 and 6. It can be described as a major scale with lowered sixth degree or as a harmonic minor with raised third. It was investigated by nineteenth-century theorists including Weitzmann and Rimsky-Korsakov. Like the harmonic minor, it is one of the four seven-note scales constructible from two-octave stacks of thirds, and it sits between the diatonic and transpositionally symmetrical scales on the scale pyramid.

# Prerequisites
- Stacks of thirds construction
- Harmonic minor scale (as inversional partner)

# Key Properties
1. Steps: 2-2-1-2-1-3-1 (contains one 3-semitone step)
2. Inversion of the harmonic minor
3. Not inversionally symmetrical
4. Thirds are 3 or 4 semitones: supports tertian harmony
5. Less common than harmonic minor but important in chromatic harmony

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Take a major scale: C-D-E-F-G-A-B
2. Lower the sixth degree: C-D-E-F-G-Ab-B
3. Or: take harmonic minor and raise the third: C-D-Eb-F-G-Ab-B -> C-D-E-F-G-Ab-B

# Context & Application
The harmonic major is less familiar than the harmonic minor but plays an important role in nineteenth-century chromatic harmony and in Tymoczko's systematic account of scale relationships. Its inclusion completes the set of four seven-note scales that arise from two-octave thirds stacks.
