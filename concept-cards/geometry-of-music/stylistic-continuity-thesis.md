# Quick Definition
The stylistic continuity thesis is Tymoczko's argument that when we examine Western music at a sufficiently abstract level, we find nonobvious structural connections between very different styles — from medieval organum to Renaissance polyphony to jazz — because the constraints of the five tonal components limit the available solutions.

# Core Definition
The stylistic continuity thesis is the overarching argument of Chapter 6 and Part II of the book. Tymoczko claims that Western tonal music across eleven centuries shares deep structural commonalities that emerge when we abstract away from surface stylistic differences. The argument rests on two pillars: (1) the five components of tonality constrain each other in nonobvious ways, limiting the space of compositional solutions, and (2) composers across eras have wanted to combine these features, leading them to discover similar solutions independently. The thesis is deliberately provocative: Tymoczko acknowledges it abstracts away from "numerous historical particulars" that professional historians rightly value, and compares his approach to Jared Diamond's broad geographical explanations in human history. He does not deny the importance of historical specificity but insists that general structural facts have been under-emphasized.

1. Structural similarities exist across 1000 years of Western tonal music
2. These similarities arise because the five tonal components constrain available solutions
3. The thesis deliberately abstracts from historical particulars
4. It does not claim all tonal music is the same — only that deep structural patterns recur

6. Pedagogically fruitful: enables a more inclusive music theory curriculum

# Context & Application
Tymoczko links the thesis to pedagogy, describing a year-long introductory course that moves chronologically through the five styles: medieval two-voice counterpoint, Renaissance triadic counterpoint, classical functional harmony, Romantic chromaticism, and twentieth-century scalar music. Students learn that "musical styles change, that tonality is a living tradition, and that when you dig deep enough, you can find nonobvious connections between very different musical styles." The thesis also challenges the bifurcated pedagogy common in many music programs, which separates "musty Germanic classics" from "bracing twentieth-century atonality" without connecting them to jazz, rock, minimalism, or earlier music.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 225): Rock guitar fingerings articulate the same strongly crossing-free voice leadings as Josquin's Renaissance counterpoint — 500 years apart, the same structural principle.

**Example 2** (p. 241): Bill Evans' improvisations develop ideas found in Ravel's String Quartet — the chord-scale association technique bridging art music and jazz.

**Example 3** (p. 242-243): The five styles surveyed in Chapter 6 each add techniques while retaining earlier ones, forming a cumulative tradition.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Interpreting the thesis as claiming all Western music is the same
  **Correction**: Each era makes distinctive contributions; the thesis identifies structural commonalities beneath surface differences

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the thesis devalues detailed historical scholarship
  **Clarification**: Tymoczko explicitly states his approach is complementary to historical specificity — he focuses on general structural facts that have been under-emphasized, not on replacing detailed analysis
