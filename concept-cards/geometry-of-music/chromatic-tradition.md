# Quick Definition
The chromatic tradition is the lineage from post-Wagnerian chromaticism through Schoenberg's atonality, characterized by rejection of limited macroharmonies in favor of the complete chromatic scale, culminating in the abandonment of both harmonic consistency and macroharmonic consistency.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko traces a standard music-historical narrative: composers from Strauss and Reger made increasingly heavy use of chromatic voice leading, increasing pitch-class circulation to the point where traditional analytical concepts lost purchase. Schoenberg then took the radical step beyond mere chromaticism to atonality, abandoning not only macroharmonic consistency but also harmonic consistency — moving from "everything is permitted" to "everything is permitted at all times." Tymoczko argues this second step may have been an unintended byproduct: since harmonic consistency and conjunct melodic motion can be combined only with acoustically consonant chords (as shown in Part I), abandoning consonance may have forced the loss of harmonic consistency. The resulting music exhibits statistical properties remarkably similar to random notes — in pitch-class circulation, distribution of harmonies, and pitch-class distributions — which may explain its persistent unpopularity more than any failure of perceptual understanding.

# Key Properties
1. Originates in post-Wagnerian extended chromaticism
2. Reger's music already approaches atonal circulation rates while maintaining harmonic consistency
3. Schoenberg abandons both harmonic consistency and macroharmonic consistency
4. Atonal music exhibits statistical similarity to random notes
5. May represent an unintended consequence of abandoning consonance rather than a deliberate choice

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Use chromatic voice leading between any available sonorities
2. Do not restrict chord types or macroharmonic collections
3. (Twelve-tone method) Organize notes in ordered rows to impose alternative structure
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Check for flat macroharmonic profiles (no dominant collection)
2. Check for flat harmonic profiles (no dominant chord type)
3. Compare pitch-class circulation to random notes

# Context & Application
Tymoczko's analysis of the chromatic tradition has a polemical edge. He argues that atonal music's statistical similarity to random notes explains the common "naive" response that "it sounds random" — listeners perceive this fairly accurately. The twelve-tone method imposes order on pitch sequences but does not change the unordered statistical properties. Tymoczko suggests appreciating atonal music requires not just learning alternative organizational principles but learning "not to respond to those statistical features that the music shares with random sequences." He likens atonal appreciation to an acquired taste (like clam chowder ice cream) rather than a cognitive achievement.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the progression from tonality to atonality as inevitable
  **Correction**: Tymoczko shows it was one of two responses to late-Romantic chromaticism; the scalar tradition was equally viable

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming atonal music is highly structured because it uses twelve-tone methods
  **Clarification**: Twelve-tone methods organize the ORDER of pitches but do not change the unordered statistical properties, which remain similar to random notes
