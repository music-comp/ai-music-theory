# Quick Definition
The jazz practice of raising avoid notes by semitone to create altered chord tones, which in turn generate non-diatonic scales (acoustic, whole-tone, octatonic) that provide improvisers with complete sets of compatible notes for each chord in a progression.

# Core Definition
In jazz improvisation, high rates of harmonic change constrain improvisers' freedom, making scales invaluable guidance for which notes will sound good. When playing diatonically over a ii-V-I, avoid notes must be handled carefully. An alternative approach raises the avoid notes by semitone, creating altered chords with no avoid notes, freeing the improviser to treat all seven scale tones as harmonic. This generates three different scales for a ii-V-I: C diatonic over ii7, G acoustic over V7, and G diatonic (or lydian) over Imaj7. The simple ii-V-I becomes a fundamentally polyscalar construction, synthesizing functional harmony, chromatic voice leading, and impressionist scale use.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of chord-scale compatibility, avoid notes, and tritone substitution

# Key Properties
1. Raising avoid notes creates altered chords with no forbidden tones
2. The ii-V-I uses three different scales when avoid notes are altered
3. Four central scales (diatonic, acoustic, whole-tone, octatonic) suffice for most contexts
4. These scales contain all nonchromatic chords of five or fewer notes (with four rare exceptions)
5. Jazz pedagogues describe this as "chord-scale compatibility"
6. The conventions provide robust rules for associating chords and scales

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Identify the avoid note for each chord in the progression
2. Raise it by semitone, creating an altered chord tone
3. Determine which scale contains the resulting chord
4. Improvise using that scale over that chord
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for non-diatonic notes that correspond to raised avoid notes
2. Check whether the resulting collection matches acoustic, whole-tone, or octatonic
3. Note whether the passage uses a different scale for each chord

# Context & Application
This practice was first codified by George Russell's Lydian Chromatic Concept (1953) and is now taught in every jazz education program. The technique itself was used by Debussy and other early twentieth-century composers before being systematized in jazz.
