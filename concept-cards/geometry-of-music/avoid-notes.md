# Quick Definition
Diatonic notes that should not be sustained or emphasized over a particular chord because they prematurely introduce tones that define the next harmonic function, obscuring the voice-leading skeleton of the ii-V-I.

# Core Definition
Jazz pedagogues describe chord voicings negatively by identifying diatonic notes a particular chord should not contain. A ii voicing can contain any diatonic note except the leading tone; a V voicing can contain any diatonic note except the tonic; and an I voicing can contain any diatonic note except the fourth scale degree. These "avoid notes" obscure the essential voice-leading motions that define harmonic function: introducing scale degree 4 (tonic to predominant), resolving 1->7 (predominant to dominant), and resolving 4->3 with the return of 1 (dominant to tonic). Including the leading tone in the ii chord blurs the difference between predominant and dominant; including the tonic in V blurs the difference between dominant and tonic.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of the ii-V-I jazz progression

# Key Properties
1. Each chord has one primary avoid note (the note defining the NEXT function)
2. ii avoids the leading tone (scale degree 7)
3. V avoids the tonic (scale degree 1)
4. I avoids the fourth degree (scale degree 4)
5. The concept conveys the freedom inherent in jazz: as long as the skeleton is clear, notes can be freely added
6. The term is associated with Mark Levine (1989) but dates to Berklee in the 1950s

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Identify the chord's function (predominant, dominant, tonic)
2. Omit the note that would prematurely anticipate the next function
3. All other diatonic notes may be used freely
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. When a note clashes with a chord's function, it is likely an avoid note
2. Avoid notes create premature resolutions or functional ambiguity
3. They can still be used as passing or neighboring tones

# Context & Application
The concept applies not only to jazz but also to twentieth-century notated music. Debussy's "Sirenes," Ravel's Jeux d'eau, and Reich's Different Trains all use extended tonic voicings that omit the fourth above the root. Similarly, dominant voicings in these composers conspicuously omit the fourth above the root.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating avoid notes as absolutely forbidden
  **Correction**: They can appear as passing tones, neighbors, or brief ornaments -- they just should not be sustained or emphasized

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking avoid notes are a uniquely jazz invention
  **Clarification**: The same voice-leading principles operate in classical and twentieth-century notated music
