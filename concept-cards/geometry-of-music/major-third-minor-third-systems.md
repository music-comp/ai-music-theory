# Quick Definition
The two primary "highways" through chromatic space: the major-third system connects triads whose roots lie a major third apart via efficient voice leading, while the minor-third system connects seventh chords whose roots relate by minor third or tritone — both arising naturally from the near-symmetry of these chord types.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko identifies two systematic voice-leading patterns that dominate nineteenth-century chromaticism. The major-third system connects triads related by major third through efficient chromatic voice leading (e.g., C major to E major or Ab major, each requiring only one or two semitones of total motion). The minor-third system connects seventh chords related by minor third or tritone (e.g., C7 to Eb7, F#7, or A7) through similarly efficient voice leadings. These systems arise from the near-symmetry of the chords involved: major triads nearly divide the octave into three equal parts (major-third cycle), while dominant sevenths nearly divide it into four (diminished-seventh framework). The geometry of chord space ensures that any composer exploring chromatic voice leading intuitively will naturally discover these paths — which is why they appear consistently in Chopin, Schubert, Schumann, Wagner, Liszt, and later in jazz.

# Key Properties
1. Major-third system: triads connected by major-third root relationships via semitone voice leading
2. Minor-third system: seventh chords connected by minor-third or tritone root relationships
3. Both arise from near-symmetry of the chords (triads ~ augmented triad; dom7 ~ dim7)
4. Both produce voice leadings where each voice moves by at most one semitone
5. The geometry of chord space ensures these are the most efficient chromatic connections
6. Composers discover these systems intuitively through keyboard exploration

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Major-third system: start with any triad, move one voice by semitone to reach a major-third-related triad
2. Minor-third system: start with any seventh chord, move one or two voices by semitone to reach a minor-third or tritone-related seventh chord
3. Chain these moves to create longer chromatic progressions
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Check root relationships: if triads are major-third apart, likely the major-third system
2. If seventh chords are minor-third or tritone apart, likely the minor-third system
3. Verify that individual voices move by semitone or stay fixed

# Context & Application
In Chopin's E major prelude (Figure 6.6.2), the second phrase uses both systems: major-third connections move between V chords of E and C, while minor-third connections link dominants of F, D, and Ab. In Schubert's C major Quintet scherzo (Figure 6.6.3), 9 of 11 modulations use these two systems. A deep appreciation of chromatic music requires understanding these voice-leading possibilities as the two main "routes" through chromatic space — similar to how the circle of thirds models diatonic triadic voice leading.

# Examples

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking these systems are arbitrary theoretical constructions
  **Correction**: They arise naturally from the geometry of chord space; any composer exploring chromatic voice leading will discover them independently

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating major-third triadic connections with minor-third seventh-chord connections
  **Clarification**: They are distinct systems: triads connect most efficiently at major-third intervals (3 divisions of octave), seventh chords at minor-third/tritone intervals (4 divisions of octave)
