# Quick Definition
Minor-mode harmony uses virtually the same thirds-based grammar as major, with frequent digressions to the relative major treated as brief key changes — a remarkable symmetry given that modes in other tonal styles often have their own distinctive harmonic repertoires.

# Core Definition
If digressions to the relative major are treated as brief changes of key, the minor-mode harmonic grammar is virtually identical to the major-mode grammar: i-VI-iv-ii°-vii°-V, with the same directional rules. This symmetry is quite remarkable because in other tonal styles (such as contemporary popular music), modes often have distinctive harmonic vocabularies. For instance, the natural minor progression i-VII-VI-VII-i is common in pop music, but its major analogue I-vii°-vi-vii°-I is virtually unknown. Classical harmony is atypical in imposing rigorous consistency on all modes and keys. The payoff is that composers can present "the same" musical material in two dramatically different emotional contexts.

# Prerequisites

# Key Properties
1. Minor-mode grammar parallels major: i-VI-iv-ii°-vii°-V
2. Digressions to relative major are treated as brief modulations
3. The symmetry between modes is atypical when compared to other tonal styles
4. 95-97% accuracy of the model holds for both major and minor in Bach and Mozart
5. Modal asymmetry is the default situation; classical consistency is the exception

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Use the same chain structure: i-VI-iv-ii°-vii°-V
2. Allow rightward motion freely, leftward only via specified arrows
3. Treat passages in relative major as brief modulations rather than exceptions

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Apply the same rightward/leftward test as in major
2. Watch for brief excursions to relative major (III) that should be analyzed as local key changes

# Context & Application
The parallel between major and minor grammars enables the classical technique of presenting themes in contrasting modes. This consistency distinguishes classical harmonic practice from popular, folk, and Renaissance styles where modal differences are more pronounced.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming minor mode requires a fundamentally different harmonic grammar
  **Correction**: In classical music, the same grammar applies; modal differences are handled by treating relative-major passages as brief modulations

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Applying the classical minor grammar to pop or folk music
  **Clarification**: The symmetry between major and minor is specific to classical practice; other styles have mode-specific norms
