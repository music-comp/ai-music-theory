# Quick Definition
Brahms' Intermezzo Op. 76 No. 4 demonstrates nineteenth-century chromatic "second practice" by exploiting multiple resolutions of the Tristan chord and the Eb minor triad, propelling the music through distant keys while maintaining unity through recurring chromatic sonorities.

# Core Definition
The Intermezzo opens on an F7 dominant seventh that resolves only at the end, oscillating between F7 and the Tristan chord {F, G#, B, Eb}. The piece progresses by exploiting alternate resolutions of these chords: at alpha-2, the Tristan chord resolves to Ab major (F and Eb collapse onto Eb, B rises to C); the V7 of Ab then reinterprets as an augmented sixth of G minor, taking a "scenic detour in tonal space." The middle section uses individual T-relatedness to reach Cb major. The third section resolves the Tristan chord first to Ab major (as before), then to E major (a new resolution), initiating a descending chromatic sequence culminating in a ii-half-dim-7 to bII7 to I cadence (a "tritone substitution" in jazz terms). The multivalence of the Tristan chord and Eb minor triad propels the music while endowing it with harmonic unity.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of generalized augmented sixths and individual T-relatedness

# Key Properties
1. Opens and closes on the dominant F7, never establishing the tonic conventionally
2. Tristan chord and Eb minor triad serve as recurring pivot sonorities (labeled alpha, beta)
3. Multiple resolutions of the same chord propel harmonic motion
4. Individual T-relatedness connects sections
5. Mixes functional ("open noteheads") and chromatic ("closed noteheads") progressions
6. Concludes with a tritone-substitution cadence (bII7-I)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for recurring sonorities that resolve differently each time
2. Track the alpha (Tristan chord) and beta (Eb minor) labels on the reduction
3. Identify which resolution schema each instance uses
4. Note the interplay between functional and chromatic passages

# Context & Application
This analysis exemplifies Tymoczko's approach to late Romantic chromaticism: rather than forcing every chord into a functional framework, he distinguishes two "practices" operating simultaneously. The piece is delicately balanced between them, with chromatic voice leading providing an alternative language alongside traditional tonal syntax.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Trying to analyze every chord functionally
  **Correction**: Tymoczko explicitly separates functional and chromatic passages; forcing functional analysis on chromatic passages obscures the music's logic

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the Tristan chord in Brahms must function the same as in Wagner
  **Clarification**: The same sonority receives different resolutions and harmonic interpretations in different contexts
