# Quick Definition
Tritone substitution (jazz) and diatonic third substitution (classical) are structurally parallel: both preserve important tones of the original chord while moving remaining notes by small distances, but they exploit different near-symmetries of different chord types.

# Core Definition
In diatonic third substitution (e.g., replacing IV with ii6), two notes are kept fixed while the remaining note moves by diatonic step. In tritone substitution (replacing V7 with bII7), two notes (the tritone) are kept fixed while the remaining notes move by chromatic step. The underlying principle is identical: preserve something of the harmonic and contrapuntal character by keeping important tones fixed. The difference lies in the intrinsic geometry of the relevant chord spaces: three-note triads divide the octave into three nearly even parts, enabling efficient voice leading to third transpositions; four-note chords containing fourths and fifths divide the octave into two nearly even parts, enabling efficient voice leading to tritone transpositions.

# Key Properties
1. Both preserve important chord tones while moving others minimally
2. Third substitution: triads, diatonic step motion, preserves two of three notes
3. Tritone substitution: seventh chords, chromatic step motion, preserves two of four notes
4. Different chord types exploit different near-symmetries
5. The geometric approach reveals this connection across centuries

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Note when a chord is replaced by one sharing important tones
2. Check whether the remaining tones move by small distances
3. Identify whether the substitution exploits near-symmetry of thirds or fourths/fifths

# Context & Application
This comparison illustrates the power of the geometric approach: "were it not for our intensive exploration of the geometry of chord space, we might never think to associate the third substitutions of the eighteenth century with the tritone substitutions of the twentieth."
