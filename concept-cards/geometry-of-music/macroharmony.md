# Quick Definition
Macroharmony is the total collection of pitch classes used over a span of musical time, extending beyond the instantaneous harmony of individual chords to encompass the "harmonic penumbra" of recently heard notes that colors the listener's perception.

# Core Definition
The fourth of Tymoczko's five components of tonality. Macroharmony refers to the aggregate of pitch classes sounding within a temporal window larger than a single chord. Previously heard notes linger in memory, mixing with current sounds to create a harmonic penumbra. Tymoczko identifies four key questions about macroharmony: (1) Does the music articulate identifiable macroharmonies other than the total chromatic? (2) How fast do these macroharmonies change? (3) Are the various macroharmonies structurally similar (related by transposition)? (4) Are the macroharmonies consonant or dissonant? In some twentieth-century music (Debussy, Stravinsky, Reich), macroharmony becomes the primary bearer of harmonic significance, with motion between macroharmonies more salient than individual chord changes.

# Key Properties
1. Macroharmonies extend beyond instantaneous chords, aggregating pitch classes over time
2. They can be consonant or dissonant independently of the chords they contain
3. Sequences of macroharmonies can exhibit consistency just as chord progressions can
4. Macroharmonic states are intermediate between chord and key in duration and note count
5. The same chords can produce very different effects depending on macroharmonic context

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose a collection of 5-8 pitch classes as a target macroharmony
2. Select chords whose notes collectively stay within that collection
3. Vary the macroharmony over time to create large-scale harmonic motion
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Collect the pitch classes sounding over a moderate time window
2. Determine whether the aggregate forms a recognizable collection (diatonic, acoustic, etc.)
3. Track how this collection changes over the course of the piece

# Context & Application
Macroharmony is essential for understanding how pieces create their distinctive sonic character. Two sequences using identical chords can sound completely different if the macroharmonic organization differs — e.g., twelve C diatonic triads followed by twelve F-sharp diatonic triads sounds placid, while alternating between the two collections every two chords sounds angular and energetic. In twentieth-century music, macroharmony often takes on the organizational role formerly played by key, as in Debussy's "Voiles" where shifts between whole-tone and pentatonic scales create the primary harmonic narrative.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 172-173): Debussy's "Voiles" uses whole-tone and pentatonic scales as distinct macroharmonic regions, creating a large-scale progression from dissonance to consonance and back, analogous to a classical V7-I-V7 progression.

**Example 2** (p. 172): Two sequences built from the same triads but organized differently — one confining itself to C diatonic then F-sharp diatonic (placid), the other alternating between the two (angular) — demonstrate that macroharmony powerfully shapes musical character.

**Example 3** (p. 173): In Stravinsky's "Dance of the Adolescents" and Reich's Different Trains, mechanical or repetitive chord changes create a sonic "wash" where macroharmonic changes become more salient than individual chords.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming macroharmony is just another word for "key"
  **Correction**: A key implies centricity and a specific scale; macroharmony is the aggregate collection of pitch classes used, which may or may not correspond to a recognized key

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating macroharmonic consonance with harmonic (chord-level) consonance
  **Clarification**: A passage can use consonant chords while being macroharmonically dissonant (if the chords collectively span the chromatic scale), or vice versa
