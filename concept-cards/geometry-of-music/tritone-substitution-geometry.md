# Quick Definition
The practice of replacing a dominant seventh chord with the dominant seventh a tritone away, explained geometrically by the fact that tritone transposition corresponds to reflection across the center of the two-note Mobius strip, preserving tritones exactly and moving perfect fourths/fifths by only a semitone.

# Core Definition
Tritone substitution's effectiveness rests on two factors: it preserves the leading tone and seventh of the V7 chord (the "tendency tones" responsible for resolution toward tonic), and it moves the remaining voices by only a semitone, transforming diatonic steps into chromatic steps. This works because dominant seventh chords can be decomposed into a tritone and a perfect fifth. Tritones are unchanged by tritone transposition (they lie on the "mirror line" of the Mobius strip), while perfect fifths are very close to the mirror and move only a semitone. The substitution relies crucially on jazz's use of seventh chords rather than triads (triads cannot be decomposed into a fourth and a tritone). It is most naturally applied to dominant chords (preserving the all-important 3-7 tritone) and goes hand in hand with quartal voicings.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of the ii-V-I jazz progression
- Basic familiarity with the Mobius strip geometry

# Key Properties
1. Preserves the tritone (leading tone and seventh) of the dominant chord
2. Moves remaining voices by semitone
3. Corresponds to reflection across the center of the two-note Mobius strip
4. Requires seventh chords (triads don't have the right structure)
5. Most effective on dominant chords; less so on predominant; rarely on tonic
6. Structurally parallel to diatonic third substitution (both preserve important tones)
7. Historical roots in augmented sixth chords

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Take a dominant seventh chord (e.g., G7: G-B-D-F)
2. Replace it with the dominant a tritone away (Db7: Db-F-Ab-Cb)
3. The notes B-F (tritone) are common to both
4. G-D become Db-Ab (each moving by semitone)
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for dominant chords whose root is a tritone from the expected V
2. The tritone of the chord will be the same as the original dominant's
3. Bass motion by half step to the tonic instead of by fifth

# Context & Application
Tritone substitution "presumably originates with augmented sixth chords" (Chapter 8) and progresses through Wagnerian chromaticism to its universal acceptance in modern jazz. The German sixth chord can be understood as a tritone substitution for V7/V. The Tristan chord derives from tritone substitution applied to the first chord of a ii-V7 progression.
