# Quick Definition
Tonicity is the global form of centricity — the sense that a particular pitch class is more prominent, important, or stable than others over an extended passage of music, functioning as a tonal "center."

# Core Definition
Tonicity (or global centricity) refers to the prominence of a particular pitch class over longer stretches of musical time, as opposed to rootedness, which applies to individual chords. Tonicity is represented by pitch-class profiles: three-tiered profiles assign value 0 to notes outside the macroharmony, 1 to non-centric notes inside it, and 2 to the tonal center. More refined profiles use additional tiers — for example, distinguishing tonic, dominant, mediant, remaining diatonic notes, and chromatic notes. Local and global centricity can conflict: in C major, the tonic C can be locally unstable as a neighbor to B in a V7 chord, yet globally stable as the tonic. Tymoczko emphasizes that centricity is primarily an external, compositional achievement (created through repetition, duration, accent, register, stepwise convergence, and doubling) rather than an internal property of collections.

# Prerequisites
- **macroharmony** — Tonicity operates within a macroharmonic context
- **rootedness** — The local counterpart to tonicity

# Key Properties
1. Applies over longer time spans than rootedness
2. Represented by pitch-class profiles with multiple tiers of prominence
3. Can conflict with local rootedness (a note can be the tonic yet locally unstable)
4. Primarily achieved by external means (repetition, accent, duration) not internal structure
5. The line between rootedness and tonicity blurs in impressionist and minimalist music

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Emphasize the target tonal center through repetition, duration, and metric accent
2. Use stepwise convergence (especially contrary motion) toward the center
3. Double the center at the octave or pair it with its fifth
4. Place the center in registrally salient positions (melodic peaks and valleys)
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Listen for which pitch class feels most stable over the entire passage
2. Check pitch-class distributions — the tonic usually appears frequently
3. Look for cadential patterns converging on the center
4. Use pitch-class profiles to visualize the hierarchy

# Context & Application
Tymoczko draws from Krumhansl-Shepard experiments showing that pitch-class distributions in real music often correlate with listeners' intuitions about tonal hierarchies. He uses pitch-class profiles compositionally, treating them as targets or probability distributions to guide improvisation and computer composition. The concept extends naturally to non-diatonic music: Stravinsky's "Petit airs" from Histoire du soldat uses a four-tiered profile with five fixed scale degrees, two mobile degrees, and A as the clear tonal center despite the absence of a conventional key.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 189-190, Figure 5.6.6): Three-tiered pitch-class profiles for C lydian, G ionian, C phrygian, and G locrian — each has the same macroharmony but different tonal centers and internal hierarchies.

**Example 2** (p. 192, Figure 5.6.9): Stravinsky's "Petit airs" — a four-tiered profile with fixed pitches (G-A-B-D-E), mobile degrees (C/C# and F/F#), and A as tonic, representing music that is "locally scale-based" within each instrument despite macroharmonic ambiguity.

**Example 3** (p. 193, Figure 5.6.10): The pitch-class distribution of the opening of Mozart's Jupiter Symphony approximates a three-tiered profile with C and G most prominent and the remaining diatonic notes at a secondary level.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the tonic must be the most frequently occurring note
  **Correction**: While tonic often appears frequently, there are many mechanisms for establishing centricity; in Mozart's Jupiter Symphony, G appears more frequently than C, yet C is the tonic

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming that collections have inherent tonal centers
  **Clarification**: Tymoczko sides with the externalist view — composers create centricity through compositional means, and can make virtually any note of any collection sound like a tonic. Internal properties of collections (like having a fifth above a note) contribute only weakly.
