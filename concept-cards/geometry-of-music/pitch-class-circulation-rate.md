# Quick Definition
The rate of pitch-class circulation — how quickly new pitch classes are introduced over time — is itself a compositional variable that composers manipulate to create large-scale harmonic contrast and formal structure.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko observes that in most Western music, the rate of pitch-class circulation is not constant but varies as a harmonic parameter. Composers create large-scale form by juxtaposing passages of relative calm (fixed in a small macroharmony with slow circulation) with passages of aggressive chromaticism (rapid circulation through many pitch classes). This variability stands in stark contrast to atonal music, where the circulation rate tends to be consistently high. Chopin's Etudes, Debussy's Preludes, Stravinsky's Rite of Spring, and Shostakovich's Preludes and Fugues each display enormous variation in circulation rate — comparable to the range of the entire classical tradition — while Schoenberg's Op. 11 and Webern's Op. 27 show remarkably uniform rates across their movements.

# Key Properties
1. Circulation rate is a compositional variable, not a fixed property of a style
2. Tonal composers typically use a wide range of circulation rates for formal contrast
3. Atonal composers often restrict themselves to a narrow range of high circulation
4. Individual movements or sections within a work can have dramatically different rates
5. The homogeneity of atonal music's circulation rate may contribute to the perception of stasis

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Vary macroharmonic content across sections: diatonic passages for low circulation, chromatic passages for high
2. Use macroharmonic contrast to reinforce formal boundaries
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Compute circulation graphs for individual sections or movements
2. Compare the range of variation across sections
3. Wide variation suggests deliberate macroharmonic modulation; narrow variation suggests macroharmonic stasis

# Context & Application
Tymoczko notes that historians sometimes use stylistic categories to describe macroharmonic diversity — low-circulation passages are labeled "folk," "archaic," or "exotic," while high-circulation passages are called "modernist." This can lead to unfair characterizations of composers like Debussy, Stravinsky, and Shostakovich as "stylistic magpies" rather than recognizing their embrace of macroharmonic modulation as a legitimate formal tool. From Tymoczko's perspective, it is thoroughgoing chromaticism that is conservative, as it abandons macroharmonic change in favor of uniform textures, recalling the macroharmonic stasis of the earliest Western music.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Interpreting consistently high circulation as compositionally "advanced"
  **Correction**: Tymoczko argues that macroharmonic diversity (variable circulation) represents a richer compositional resource than uniformly high circulation

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Associating diatonic passages with regression and chromatic passages with progress
  **Clarification**: Low-circulation and high-circulation passages serve complementary formal functions; neither is inherently more "advanced"
