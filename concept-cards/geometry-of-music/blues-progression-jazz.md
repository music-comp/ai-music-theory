# Quick Definition
One of the two foundational chord progressions of jazz (alongside rhythm changes), the blues progression features polytonal clashes between an African American "blues scale" melody and European dominant seventh harmonies, accounting for a substantial fraction of the jazz repertoire.

# Core Definition
The blues is characterized by "blue notes" (b3, b5) that create a delicious dissonance with the underlying dominant seventh harmony, suggesting a kind of polytonality or clash between independent harmonic streams. The blues and rhythm changes together account for "a substantial fraction of the jazz repertoire," with many tunes being variants on the standard blues progression (e.g., "Au Privave," "Blue Monk," "Collard Greens and Black-Eyed Peas"). The repetitive nature of these progressions gives listeners many opportunities to identify the harmonic structure, allowing improvisers greater freedom in their embellishments. The blues scale (Bb-Db-Eb-En-F-Ab) provides melodic material that clashes productively with the harmony.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of the ii-V-I jazz progression

# Key Properties
1. One of two foundational jazz forms (with rhythm changes)
2. Features polytonal clash between blues scale and European harmony
3. Blue notes (b3, b5) create characteristic dissonance
4. Many ostensibly distinct tunes share the blues progression
5. Repetition allows listeners to internalize the form
6. The origins of "playing outside" can be traced to blues

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Twelve-bar form with I7-IV7-V7 structure
2. Melody emphasizes blue notes (b3, b5, b7) over dominant seventh harmonies
3. Polytonal clash between melody and harmony

# Context & Application
The blues represents the African American contribution to the polytonal dimension of jazz, creating a template for the "inside/outside" aesthetic that permeates all subsequent jazz styles.
