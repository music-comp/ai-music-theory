# Quick Definition
Modulation can be understood as the process that introduces new pitch classes into a piece, causing the gradual slope in pitch-class circulation graphs characteristic of tonal music — each key change supplies fresh pitch classes, preventing the graph from plateauing as sharply as in non-modulating music.

# Core Definition
In macroharmonically static music (Palestrina, Webern), the pitch-class circulation graph rises quickly then flattens sharply, because the piece systematically exhausts a fixed collection. In modulating tonal music (Mozart through Wagner), the graph rises quickly — reflecting the local exhaustion of a diatonic collection — but then continues to rise gradually, reflecting the slow but steady introduction of new pitch classes through modulation. This gradual slope is the quantitative signature of modulation. The rate of this secondary rise correlates with the frequency and distance of modulations: more frequent and more distant modulations produce steeper slopes. Tymoczko reframes modulation as a special case of manipulating the rate of pitch-class circulation, an approach that generalizes to non-traditional contexts where the concept of "key" may not apply.

# Key Properties
1. Modulation is the primary mechanism for the gradual slope after initial rise in circulation graphs
2. More frequent/distant modulations produce steeper secondary slopes
3. The gradual slope distinguishes tonal modulating music from both static diatonicism and atonality
4. A typical 10-note excerpt of classical music contains roughly half the pitch classes of a 100-note excerpt (log-linear relationship)
5. Macroharmonically static music (Palestrina, Webern) shows a sharp plateau; modulating music shows a gradual slope

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Begin in one macroharmony (key/scale)
2. Introduce new pitch classes by changing to a related macroharmony
3. Control the rate and distance of macroharmonic changes to shape large-scale form
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Examine the pitch-class circulation graph after the initial rise
2. A gradual slope indicates modulation; a sharp plateau indicates macroharmonic stasis
3. Compare secondary slope steepness to gauge modulation frequency/distance

# Context & Application
This perspective unifies modulation across historical periods: classical key changes, Romantic chromaticism, and twentieth-century scale changes are all mechanisms for introducing new pitch classes. It also explains why atonality can feel static despite rapid surface activity — the circulation graph plateaus near 12 almost immediately, leaving no room for the gradual introduction of new pitch content that characterizes modulating tonal music.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 178, Figure 5.3.2): Palestrina's Pope Marcellus Mass levels off near 7 pitch classes with a very gradual slope reflecting occasional accidentals. Pieces by Mozart through Wagner level off more gradually, reflecting progressively more frequent modulation.

**Example 2** (p. 178): Webern's Op. 27 reaches a completely horizontal plateau at 12 — the twelve-tone method ensures complete pitch-class circulation with no macroharmonic change possible.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating modulation only as a change of tonal center
  **Correction**: Tymoczko's framework treats modulation primarily as the introduction of new pitch classes into the macroharmony, which may or may not involve a clear change of tonal center

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking atonal music is maximally diverse in pitch content
  **Clarification**: While atonal music uses all 12 pitch classes, it is macroharmonically uniform — it lacks the temporal variation in pitch content that modulation provides
