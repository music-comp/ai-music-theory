# Quick Definition
Both scales and chords should be "not too even and not too uneven" — perfectly even structures produce boring parallelism, while very uneven ones create musical disorder. Near-evenness is the "just right" condition.

# Core Definition
The Goldilocks Principle states that both scales and chords benefit from being nearly but not perfectly even. For scales: perfectly even scales (like the whole-tone scale) make scalar transposition identical to chromatic transposition, eliminating the interesting variation that makes scalar music appealing. Very uneven scales distort patterns beyond recognition under transposition (the scale in Figure 4.3.1a transforms a semitone step into an eight-semitone leap). For chords: perfectly even chords can only be connected to their transpositions by parallel motion (all voices moving the same way), which sounds like doubling rather than counterpoint. Slightly uneven chords allow voices to move by different amounts, creating independent melodic lines. Near-evenness is therefore overdetermined — it is independently favored for three reasons: (1) efficient voice leading, (2) acoustic consonance (correlation with simple frequency ratios), and (3) useful scalar transposition with moderate variation.

# Key Properties
1. Perfect evenness: boring (parallelism, no variation)
2. Too much unevenness: chaotic (patterns unrecognizable after transposition)
3. Near-evenness: "just right" (moderate, musically interesting variation)
4. Applies to both scales (as distance metrics) and chords (as voice-leading objects)
5. Three independent reasons converge on near-evenness: voice leading, consonance, scalar transposition
6. This overdetermination limits the freedom composers have in choosing alternative musical languages

# Construction / Recognition
## To Evaluate:
1. Check the scale's step sizes: are they approximately equal?
2. Check what happens under scalar transposition: do patterns retain their general shape?
3. If steps are within 1-2 semitones of each other, the scale satisfies the Goldilocks Principle

# Context & Application
The Goldilocks Principle is one of Tymoczko's key explanatory claims. It explains why Western music gravitates toward a small number of scales (diatonic, acoustic, whole-tone, octatonic, etc.) and chord types (major/minor triads, seventh chords): these are the structures that are "just right" — near enough to even for efficient voice leading and usable scalar transposition, but uneven enough for musical interest. The principle suggests that the basic vocabulary of Western music is not arbitrary convention but is constrained by mathematical and perceptual considerations.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking the whole-tone scale is ideal because it is perfectly even
  **Correction**: Perfect evenness makes scalar transposition trivially equal to chromatic transposition, eliminating the musically interesting variation

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Doesn't overdetermination mean there is no creative freedom?
  **Clarification**: Overdetermination constrains but does not eliminate freedom. There are still multiple "just right" options (diatonic, acoustic, octatonic, etc.), each with different properties.
