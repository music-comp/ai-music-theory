# Quick Definition
Tritone substitution — replacing a dominant seventh with another a tritone away — evolved historically from augmented sixth chords (initially V-of-V alterations), becoming direct dominants in the early nineteenth century and virtually mandatory in twentieth-century jazz. Geometrically, it reflects the adjacency of tritone-related chords on the tesseract.

# Core Definition
The augmented sixth chords (Italian, French, German) first appeared as applied dominants (V-of-V chords). During the early nineteenth century, they began appearing as dominant sonorities in their own right. By the twentieth century, jazz musicians conceived them as "tritone substitutions" for V7. This historical development reflects the gradual recognition that tritone-related dominant sevenths share the same tritone interval (the guide tones) and are adjacent in four-note chord space. The connection between Chopin's Mazurka (descending by semitone) and Prelude (descending by fifth) is itself a tritone substitution: replacing every other chord in one sequence with its tritone transposition produces the other. This demonstrates that semitonal descent and fifth descent are geometrically related through the tesseract.

# Key Properties
1. Historical progression: secondary dominant -> direct dominant -> tritone substitution
2. Tritone-related dominant sevenths share the same tritone (guide tones)
3. They are adjacent on the tesseract (minor-third/tritone adjacency)
4. Chopin's Mazurka and Prelude are related by tritone substitution of alternating chords
5. Beethoven's Op. 54 alternates mazurka-like semitones and prelude-like fifths

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Take any dominant seventh (e.g., G7 = G-B-D-F)
2. Its tritone substitute is Db7 = Db-F-Ab-Cb
3. They share the tritone B-F (= Cb-F)
4. In a ii-V-I progression: Dm7-G7-Cmaj7 becomes Dm7-Db7-Cmaj7

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. A bII7 chord resolving to I (or to I by semitonal bass descent)
2. A chord that can be respelled as an augmented sixth (German sixth resolving to I)
3. In sequences, alternating tritone-related dominant sevenths

# Context & Application
Tritone substitution is a key link between nineteenth-century chromaticism and twentieth-century jazz. The connection is not merely historical but geometrical: the same four-dimensional structure (the tesseract) that explains Chopin's procedures also underlies jazz harmonic practice. Victor Herbert's operettas (1898) and Jelly Roll Morton's "Shreveport Stomp" demonstrate the transitional period.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking tritone substitution was invented by jazz musicians
  **Correction**: It evolved from augmented sixth chords over centuries; jazz musicians formalized and named it

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the bII7-I resolution is fundamentally different from the German sixth resolution
  **Clarification**: They are the same voice leading viewed from different analytical perspectives
