# Quick Definition
Rock music commonly uses single-semitone shifts between diatonic modes (e.g., mixolydian to ionian, dorian to natural minor), demonstrating that scale-first composition has entered the musical vernacular and is "an accepted part of our contemporary tonal language."

# Core Definition
The Who's "I Can't Explain" uses E mixolydian for the verse (I-VII-IV) and E major for the chorus (I-vi-IV-V), a single-semitone change (D->D#) over a fixed tonic, "exactly analogous to the opening of Debussy's 'Des pas sur la neige.'" Bob Seger's "Turn the Page" uses dorian for the verse (i-VII-IV) and natural minor for the chorus (VI-VII-i), another single-semitone shift (G#->G). These songs are "arbitrarily selected from among thousands" that use closely related modes. The procedures have entered the musical vernacular: "it is totally unremarkable to find musicians of virtually any stripe exploiting efficient voice leadings between the full range of diatonic modes."

# Key Properties
1. Single-semitone shift between verse and chorus modes
2. Fixed tonic with changing modal color
3. Analogous to Debussy's scalar techniques
4. The Who: mixolydian -> ionian (D->D#)
5. Seger: dorian -> natural minor (G#->G) -- "dual" to The Who
6. Part of the musical vernacular across all styles

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Compare the scales used in verse and chorus
2. Identify the single note that changes between modes
3. Note the fixed tonic with changing modal color

# Context & Application
These examples demonstrate that scale-first composition is not limited to "art music" but pervades rock, film music, jazz, and popular styles.
