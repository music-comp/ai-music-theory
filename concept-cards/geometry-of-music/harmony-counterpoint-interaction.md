# Quick Definition
Western music exhibits a two-dimensional coherence: vertically, chords should be harmonically similar (harmonic consistency); horizontally, voices should move by small steps (efficient voice leading). These two constraints can coexist only when chords are nearly symmetrical.

# Core Definition
Section 2.9 formalizes the central thesis: polyphonic music uses independent melodies to articulate efficient voice leadings between meaningful harmonies. The vertical dimension (harmony) demands that successive chords be structurally similar. The horizontal dimension (counterpoint) demands that individual voices move by small steps. These goals are in tension: there is no guarantee that structurally similar chords can be connected smoothly. The resolution comes from near-symmetry: efficient voice leading between transpositionally, inversionally, or permutationally related chords is possible if and only if those chords are nearly symmetrical under the corresponding transformation. This is the book's first major theoretical result.

# Key Properties
1. Two independent constraints: harmonic similarity (vertical) and smooth voice leading (horizontal)
2. These constraints are in tension — not all chord pairs admit efficient voice leading
3. Resolution: nearly symmetrical chords satisfy both constraints
4. Three types of symmetry: T (transposition), I (inversion), P (permutation)
5. These three are the ONLY symmetries needed — every case of efficient voice leading between similar chords involves one of these
6. Applies in any scale or tuning system

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose a nearly symmetrical chord type
2. Successive transpositions (or inversions) of this type will be connectable by efficient voice leading
3. The resulting passages exhibit both harmonic consistency and smooth counterpoint
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Check if a passage uses structurally similar chords (harmonic consistency)
2. Check if individual voices move smoothly (efficient voice leading)
3. If both, the chords are likely nearly symmetrical

# Context & Application
This interaction is the central theoretical thread of the book. Figure 2.9.1 demonstrates it across four vastly different styles: classical, jazz, Wagnerian, and atonal cluster music. In each case, harmonically similar chords are connected by smooth voice motion, and in each case the chords used are nearly symmetrical. The result explains not just common-practice music but also the voice-leading practices of jazz, chromatic Romanticism, and even some atonal music.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking all chords permit efficient voice leading to their transpositions
  **Correction**: Only nearly symmetrical chords do; arbitrary chords generally cannot

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking this interaction is specific to common-practice music
  **Clarification**: It applies across the extended common practice, including jazz, chromatic Romanticism, and cluster-based music
