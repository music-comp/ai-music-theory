# Quick Definition
The extended common practice is Tymoczko's thesis that Western tonal music from the eleventh century to the present constitutes a single broad tradition united by the technique of connecting harmonically significant sonorities through efficient voice leading.

# Core Definition
The traditional "common practice period" (roughly 1700-1900) is, in Tymoczko's view, too narrow. He argues for a much broader common practice encompassing eleven centuries: from medieval two-voice organum through Renaissance polyphony, baroque/classical functional harmony, nineteenth-century chromaticism, and twentieth-century scalar music (including jazz). The unifying technique is efficient voice leading between harmonically significant sonorities. Each era develops this differently: medieval music connects consonant intervals; Renaissance music connects triads; classical music adds functional harmonic conventions and systematic modulation; Romantic music extends voice leading into chromatic space; twentieth-century music applies scale-to-chord association and scalar voice leading. The five components of tonality constrain composers' choices so heavily that similar solutions recur across eras — the common practice exists because the problem (combining tonal features) has a limited number of solutions.

3. Maintain some subset of the five tonal components
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for efficient voice leading connecting structurally similar chords
2. Check for macroharmonic organization
3. Assess whether the music can be understood as extending or combining earlier practices

# Context & Application
The extended common practice thesis reframes music history. Instead of seeing Renaissance, Classical, Romantic, and twentieth-century music as fundamentally different languages, Tymoczko sees them as dialects of a single tradition. The "common practice period" is merely the intersection of a contrapuntal common practice (Renaissance through present) and a harmonic common practice (Baroque through present). Twentieth-century tonal music that permits parallel fifths while maintaining harmonic schemas belongs to the harmonic but not the contrapuntal common practice. Renaissance music that uses efficient voice leading but lacks functional progressions belongs to the contrapuntal but not the harmonic practice. Each style can be understood as exploiting opportunities latent in the constraints of the five tonal components, like a mountaineer ascending a cliff by taking advantage of the handholds the rock face offers.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Equating "extended common practice" with "common practice period"
  **Correction**: The extended common practice is much broader, spanning eleven centuries and including jazz, rock, and non-Western tonal traditions

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Interpreting the thesis as claiming all tonal music is the same
  **Clarification**: Each era makes distinctive contributions; the thesis identifies deep structural similarities beneath obvious surface differences. It is descriptive (structural similarities exist) not prescriptive (all music should follow these practices).
