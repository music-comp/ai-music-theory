# Quick Definition
Tonality space is a metaphorical multi-dimensional space spanned by the five components of tonality, in which any piece or genre can be located by answering a set of diagnostic questions about its use of harmony, voice leading, macroharmony, centricity, and consonance.

# Core Definition
The five components of tonality — conjunct melodic motion, acoustic consonance, harmonic consistency, macroharmony, and centricity — each function as a conceptual "vector" pointing into its own abstract dimension. Together they span a region called "tonality space," which encompasses the full range of possibilities from traditional tonality to outright atonality. Pieces and genres can be situated within this space by answering a series of questions (Figure 5.8.9): locally, does the music use similar-sounding chords, efficient voice leading, and consonant harmonies? Globally, how fast do pitch classes circulate, does the music articulate clear macroharmonies, and is there a sense of tonal center? This replaces the binary "tonal"/"atonal" opposition with a fine-grained set of categories appropriate to contemporary musical pluralism.

# Key Properties
1. Multi-dimensional space with five components as axes
2. Replaces the binary tonal/atonal classification
3. Pieces can traverse multiple regions of tonality space within a single work
4. Composers can inhabit a small region (specialization) or broad range (maximalism)
5. Questions about local properties (chords, voice leading) and global properties (macroharmony, centricity) define position in the space

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. For each of the five components, decide on a target level
2. Make deliberate choices about consonance, voice leading, harmonic consistency, macroharmony, and centricity
3. Navigate between regions of tonality space to create formal structure
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Answer the diagnostic questions in Figure 5.8.9
2. Place the music on each of the five axes
3. Compare to reference points (traditional tonality, atonality, pandiatonicism, etc.)

# Context & Application
Tonality space provides a framework for understanding maximalist composers like Stravinsky, Bartok, Shostakovich, Messiaen, Ligeti, and John Adams, who traverse broad regions of the space within single pieces. It also helps composers orient themselves among possibilities: some may prefer to specialize in one region (like a particular style), while others may embrace the full range. Tymoczko likens tonality space to the field in which contemporary music operates, just as the 24 major/minor keys bounded eighteenth-century exploration.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating tonality space as a literal geometric space like the chord spaces of Chapter 3
  **Correction**: Tonality space is a metaphorical framework for thinking about musical possibilities, not a mathematically defined geometric object

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking that position in tonality space determines aesthetic quality
  **Clarification**: Tonality space is descriptive, not evaluative; all regions contain both excellent and mediocre music
