# Quick Definition
Diatonic sequences repeat the same musical pattern at different pitch levels within a key; they are organized by compactness on the descending circle of thirds, with sequences featuring small descending motions being far more common than their ascending counterparts.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko catalogs all 18 diatonic sequences whose repeating unit contains at most two chords, ordered by compactness on the descending circle of thirds. Inversionally related sequences share the same row, with the descending-thirds-oriented version on the left and its ascending counterpart on the right. There is a marked asymmetry: in the first five rows, the leftward (descending-thirds) sequence is considerably more common than the rightward (ascending-thirds) counterpart. In sequential tonality, where tonic and dominant lose their gravitational pull, the preference for strong progressions becomes especially visible. The most compact sequence is descending thirds itself; the second-most compact alternates descending thirds with ascending fourths; the ubiquitous descending-fifth sequence is third-most compact (two-step motions on the circle).

# Prerequisites
- The circle of thirds as a metric for harmonic distance
- The strong/weak progression distinction

# Key Properties
1. 18 basic two-chord diatonic sequences exist (9 inversionally related pairs)
2. Sequences featuring strong progressions are far more common than their weak counterparts
3. Compactness on the circle of thirds predicts relative frequency
4. Sequences frequently use the iii chord, which is rare in nonsequential harmony
5. Related sequences (e.g., descending fifths and "down a third, up a step") are connected by third substitution

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose a root-motion pattern (e.g., down a fifth, down a third then up a step)
2. Apply the pattern starting from any diatonic chord
3. Transpose the melodic-rhythmic content at each step
4. The pattern repeats within the diatonic collection

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for repeated melodic-rhythmic patterns at different pitch levels
2. Identify the root motion between successive chords
3. Check whether the root motion is compact on the circle of thirds
4. Classify the sequence by its position in the 18-sequence catalog

# Context & Application
Sequences reveal the connection between Fétis's two components of tonal practice: "harmonic tonality" moves from tonic to dominant along the circle of thirds, forming strong progressions; "sequential tonality" loses tonic-dominant gravity, allowing the preference for strong progressions to shine forth more clearly. Both are organized by the descending circle of thirds, but in different ways.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Fig. 7.3.1, p. 257): Five common sequence types ordered by compactness: (a) descending thirds, (b) descending third + ascending fourth, (c) descending fifths, (d) descending third + ascending second, (e) two descending thirds + descending fifth.

**Example 2** (Fig. 7.3.4, p. 260): Two-unit sequences in Mozart's piano sonatas — 33 descending-fifth sequences vs 0 ascending-fifth sequences; 22 ascending-step/descending-fifth vs 0 of its inverse.

**Example 3** (Fig. 7.3.7, p. 262): Bach's F minor fugue (WTC I, mm. 16-18) — a sequence-like passage composed entirely of strong progressions, with descending-thirds melodies creating a sequential feel despite lacking exact transposition.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking all tonal sequences use descending fifths
  **Correction**: There are 18 basic types; descending fifths is the most common but not the only one

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming a passage must be exactly transposed to be a sequence
  **Clarification**: Tymoczko's analysis of Bach's F minor fugue shows that sequence-like passages with strong progressions and descending-thirds melodies can create a sequential feel without exact transposition
