# Quick Definition
A ubiquitous jazz idiom where upper-voice tritone substitution is applied to every other chord in a descending-fifth sequence, allowing the soloist to play chromatically descending voicings while the bass descends by fifths.

# Core Definition
A soloist can always play a descending-by-semitone melodic sequence over dominant harmonies descending by fifths by applying upper-voice tritone substitution to every other chord. The harmonic meaning of the notes alternates between chords: an augmented triad might represent the 7th, #11th, and 9th in one chord, then the 3rd, root, and #5th of the next. Similarly, melodic fourths alternate between lydian dominant and altered voicings. This idiom is "ubiquitous in jazz" and appears identically in Debussy's Six epigraphes antiques No. 4, where semitonally descending A voicings accompany descending fifths in the bass.

# Key Properties
1. Upper-voice tritone substitution applied to alternating chords
2. Produces chromatically descending upper voices over descending-fifth bass
3. Harmonic meaning alternates between chords (e.g., lydian dominant / altered)
4. Ubiquitous jazz idiom, easy way to handle fast descending-fifth sequences
5. Anticipated by Debussy in identical fashion
6. Works with augmented triads, fourths, and other voicing types

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Take a descending-fifth bass line
2. Choose a voicing for the first chord
3. Move the voicing down by semitone for each new chord
4. The effect: every other chord is a tritone substitution
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Listen for chromatically descending upper voices over descending-fifth bass
2. The upper voices move by semitone while the bass moves by fifth

# Context & Application
Bill Evans uses this technique in the B section of "Oleo" and throughout his playing. It provides a practical solution for handling fast harmonic rhythm over descending-fifth sequences.
