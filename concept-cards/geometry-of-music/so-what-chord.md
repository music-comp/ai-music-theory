# Quick Definition
A chord consisting of three perfect fourths and one major third, named for its prominent appearance in Bill Evans' playing on Miles Davis' "So What," functioning as a quartal predominant voicing and equivalent to a stack of pentatonic thirds.

# Core Definition
The "So What" chord is a five-note voicing built from three perfect fourths and one major third (e.g., D-G-C-F-A), often encountered with the lowest note doubled in the soprano (D-G-C-F-A-D). Guitar players recognize this as the open strings of the guitar tuned down a major second. The chord is a complete stack of two-step intervals in the pentatonic scale: beginning on different pentatonic notes produces all the various chords that insert a major third into a sequence of five perfect fourths. Each voicing exhausts the notes of a pentatonic scale. Combined with quartal V-I voicings, it produces a fourth-based ii-V-I schema.

# Key Properties
1. Three perfect fourths and one major third
2. Exhausts the notes of a pentatonic scale
3. Functions as predominant (ii) voicing in quartal ii-V-I
4. Named for Bill Evans' playing on Miles Davis' "So What"
5. Guitar open strings = "So What" voicing (transposed)
6. Each of five inversions has four perfect fourths and one near-fourth

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Take any pentatonic scale and stack its notes in consecutive thirds: D-F-G-A-C-D-F-G-A-C-D
2. Select five consecutive notes from this stack
3. Or: stack three perfect fourths and one major third from any starting note
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Five-note voicing with open, "fourthy" sound
2. All notes belong to a single pentatonic scale
3. Four intervals are perfect fourths/fifths, one is a major third

# Context & Application
The "So What" chord became iconic through Miles Davis' Kind of Blue (1959) and represents the aesthetic of modal jazz. It bridges classical quartal harmony (Stravinsky, Bartok, Hindemith) and jazz.
