# Quick Definition
The diatonic, acoustic, whole-tone, and octatonic scales form the four central collections of twentieth-century tonal music and jazz, connected through their shared tritone structures and ability to combine whole-tone and octatonic fragments.

# Core Definition
These four scales can be constructed by combining whole-tone and octatonic tritone fragments in different ways. Each dominant-compatible tritone (e.g., G to C#) can be filled with either a whole-tone fragment (G-A-B-C#) or an octatonic fragment (C#-D-E-F-G). Combining these two options for upper and lower tritones yields: whole-tone + whole-tone = whole-tone scale; octatonic + octatonic = octatonic scale; whole-tone lower + octatonic upper = acoustic (lydian dominant); octatonic lower + whole-tone upper = acoustic (altered mode). Together with the diatonic, these are the only scales with one-or-two semitone steps and three-or-four semitone thirds (Chapter 4), and they appear in impressionism, jazz, and postminimalism.

# Key Properties
1. Four scales constructed from combining whole-tone and octatonic tritone fragments
2. All contain the tritone G-C# (and hence work over both V7 and its tritone substitute)
3. The only scales with optimal step and third sizes (from Chapter 4)
4. Sufficient for virtually all nonchromatic altered dominant chords
5. Convergent evolution: discovered independently by impressionists, jazz musicians, and minimalists

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Take two tritones spanning a dominant chord (e.g., G-C# and C#-G)
2. Fill each with either whole-tone (2-2-2) or octatonic (1-2-1-2) fragments
3. The four combinations produce the four central scales
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for dominant-functioning passages using non-diatonic scales
2. Identify whether the scale's tritone spans are filled with whole-tone or octatonic patterns

# Context & Application
These four scales are highlighted in virtually every jazz textbook and appear across impressionism, jazz, and postminimalism. Jazz theorists were the first to isolate this collection of scales and assert their importance; more academic theorists did not begin to emphasize the group until much later.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating these four scales as arbitrary choices
  **Correction**: They are mathematically overdetermined, being the only scales satisfying multiple independent optimality criteria

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the acoustic and altered scales are different scale types
  **Clarification**: They are different modes of the same (acoustic) scale
