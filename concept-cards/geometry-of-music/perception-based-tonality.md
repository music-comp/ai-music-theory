# Quick Definition
Tymoczko grounds tonality in perceptual and cognitive facts about how humans hear music, rather than in purely formal or historical criteria.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko's approach to tonality is fundamentally perception-based. Rather than defining tonality through compositional rules (e.g., functional harmony) or historical period, he defines it through features that listeners can perceive: whether melodies move by small steps (conjunct motion), whether chords sound similar to each other (harmonic consistency), whether harmonies sound consonant (acoustic consonance), whether a limited set of pitch classes predominates (limited macroharmony), and whether one pitch feels central (centricity). This grounds the theory in the cognitive science of music perception and makes it applicable across cultures and historical periods.

# Key Properties
1. Tonality is defined in terms of listener-perceivable features, not compositional rules
2. Draws on empirical research in music cognition and psychoacoustics
3. Makes tonality applicable across cultures and time periods
4. Avoids circular definitions that define tonality by reference to specific historical practices

# Context & Application
The perception-based approach allows Tymoczko to make cross-cultural and cross-historical claims about tonality that would be impossible under narrower definitions. It also connects music theory to the empirical sciences of psychology and acoustics, moving the discipline toward testable claims rather than purely interpretive assertions.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 21): Tymoczko notes that the expansive usage of "tonal" accords with the intuition that Schubert, the Beatles, and Pärt share musical preoccupations, and argues that perception-based criteria explain what they share.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming perception-based means purely subjective
  **Correction**: The perceptual features Tymoczko identifies have strong cross-cultural agreement and are rooted in psychoacoustic facts

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking this approach denies the importance of cultural learning in music perception
  **Clarification**: Tymoczko acknowledges cultural variation but identifies features with sufficient cross-cultural stability to serve as a foundation
