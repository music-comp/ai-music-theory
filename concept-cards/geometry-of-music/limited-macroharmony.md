# Quick Definition
Limited macroharmony is the tendency for tonal music to use a relatively small number of pitch classes (typically 5-8) over moderate spans of time, rather than freely employing all twelve.

# Core Definition
The fourth of Tymoczko's five basic components of tonality. Macroharmony refers to the total collection of pitch classes used in a passage of music. The constraint of "limited macroharmony" means that tonal music typically restricts itself to a subset of available pitch classes over moderate time spans — most commonly the seven notes of a diatonic scale, but also pentatonic collections (5 notes), hexatonic collections (6 notes), or the octatonic scale (8 notes). This creates a background pitch-class "palette" against which individual chords and melodies are heard.

# Key Properties
1. Tonal music uses a small subset (typically 5-8) of the 12 available pitch classes at any given time
2. The macroharmony often corresponds to a familiar scale (diatonic, pentatonic, etc.)
3. Macroharmony can shift over time (modulation), but changes tend to be gradual
4. Limited macroharmony is distinct from the chords used at any given moment
5. Creates a sense of tonal "region" or "key area"

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Select a scale or collection of 5-8 pitch classes
2. Compose melodies and harmonies primarily using those pitch classes
3. Introduce chromatic alterations sparingly or as signals of modulation
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Collect all pitch classes sounding over a moderate passage of time
2. If they total roughly 5-8 and correspond to a recognizable scale, macroharmony is limited
3. Compare different sections to see whether the macroharmony changes

# Context & Application
Limited macroharmony provides the background scalar context against which melodic and harmonic events are perceived. In common-practice music, it typically corresponds to major or minor keys. In jazz, various modes and scales may define the macroharmony. The concept is important for understanding modulation (change of macroharmony) and for connecting local chord choices to broader tonal regions. Tymoczko devotes Chapter 5 to a thorough investigation of macroharmony and centricity.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 21-25): A passage in C major typically uses the seven pitch classes {C, D, E, F, G, A, B}, with chromatic notes appearing only as passing tones or as harbingers of modulation.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Equating macroharmony with key
  **Correction**: Macroharmony is the pitch-class content of a passage; key additionally implies a tonal center and functional relationships

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing macroharmony with the chord sounding at a given moment
  **Clarification**: Macroharmony is the aggregate of all pitch classes over a stretch of time, not the harmony at a single instant
