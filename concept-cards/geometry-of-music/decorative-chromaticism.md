# Quick Definition
Decorative chromaticism fills in diatonic gaps with chromatic alterations that intensify dominant-tonic progressions, producing familiar chords (diminished seventh, augmented sixth, dominant seventh with augmented fifth) as well as nameless but analogous sonorities — all manifestations of a single process.

# Core Definition
In a diatonic dominant-tonic progression, voice leading is as small as possible diatonically, but notes sometimes move by two semitones. Chromatic alterations can fill these gaps, creating heightened yearning for the tonic. Figure 8.1.1 shows that such alterations produce a variety of familiar chords: the diminished seventh, the dominant seventh with augmented fifth, the dominant seventh flat-five, the Italian/French/German augmented sixth chords, and nameless altered sonorities like the "Till Sixth" and the "Ecstatic Sixth." Tymoczko advocates a "process-based" approach: what matters is not memorizing each chord individually, but understanding the general principle of chromatic gap-filling. Students who grasp this principle can confront unfamiliar chromatic sonorities without panic, both analytically and in their own composition.

# Prerequisites
- Functional harmonic grammar and dominant-tonic progressions
- Efficient voice leading as a basic principle

# Key Properties
1. Chromatic alterations fill two-semitone gaps in diatonic voice leading
2. Produces familiar named chords (augmented sixths, diminished seventh, etc.)
3. Also produces unnamed but equally valid altered sonorities
4. Process-based understanding > object-based (one-chord-per-chapter) memorization
5. The augmented sixth first appeared as V-of-V, only later as a direct dominant
6. Similar principles apply to embellishing ii and IV progressions

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Write a standard diatonic dominant-tonic progression
2. Identify any voice that moves by two semitones
3. Fill in the gap with a chromatic passing tone or anticipation
4. The resulting vertical sonority is a chromatic embellishment
5. Apply to V-I, ii-V, IV-I, and other standard progressions

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. A chord that resolves semitonally to a diatonic triad
2. Often contains an augmented sixth interval resolving outward to an octave
3. May have a standard name (German sixth, etc.) or may be nameless
4. The key is the resolution pattern, not the chord's label

# Context & Application
Decorative chromaticism represents the first level of chromatic practice, where chromatic tones embellish a fundamentally diatonic progression. It is distinct from the "second practice" of autonomous chromaticism (Sections 8.2-8.6), where chromatic voice leading operates as an independent force. Historical development: augmented sixths appeared first as secondary dominants (V-of-V), then as primary dominants in early nineteenth century, then as mandatory "tritone substitutions" in twentieth-century jazz.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Panicking when encountering a chromatic chord without a standard textbook name
  **Correction**: Understand the process — any chromatic alteration that creates semitonal resolution to a diatonic chord is legitimate

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking augmented sixth chords are fundamentally different from altered dominants
  **Clarification**: They are produced by the same process of chromatic gap-filling; the distinctions are historical rather than structural
