# Quick Definition
The prohibition on parallel perfect fifths and octaves, established in Renaissance practice, may reflect not only auditory streaming (voices fusing into one) but also structural properties of the diatonic scale, where parallel fifths produce nearly identical chromatic intervals while parallel thirds do not.

# Core Definition
Many theorists explain the prohibition on parallel fifths/octaves through auditory streaming: parallel perfect intervals cause listeners to fuse two voices into one, destroying voice independence. Tymoczko is sympathetic but proposes an additional structural explanation rooted in the diatonic scale itself. Parallel diatonic fifths almost always produce parallel chromatic fifths (all either 7 semitones), creating maximal parallelism. By contrast, parallel diatonic thirds produce an uneven mix of major and minor thirds (3 and 4 semitones), creating less chromatic parallelism. The difference in chromatic parallelism is at least as striking as the difference in acoustic quality. Tymoczko illustrates this with Messiaen's nine-note "mode 3": three-step intervals (always 4 semitones) exhibit one degree of parallelism, while four-step intervals (alternating 6 and 7 semitones) exhibit another — and the musical result shows roughly similar voice independence despite containing perfect fifths.

# Key Properties
1. Prohibited: parallel fifths and parallel octaves between any pair of voices
2. Standard explanation: auditory streaming causes voice fusion
3. Tymoczko's additional explanation: diatonic fifths produce uniform chromatic intervals (all 7 semitones)
4. Diatonic thirds produce varied chromatic intervals (3 or 4 semitones) — less true parallelism
5. Parallel fourths are permitted in classical three-voice counterpoint (unlike fifths) — a distinction favoring the acoustic explanation for some aspects
6. Scale structure matters: in Messiaen's mode 3, interval uniformity differs from the diatonic scale

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. When writing parallel motion, use thirds or sixths rather than fifths or octaves
2. If voices must move in the same direction, ensure at least one moves by a different interval
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Check consecutive vertical intervals between any two voices
2. If both intervals are perfect fifths (or octaves) with both voices moving in the same direction, parallel fifths (octaves) are present

# Context & Application
The prohibition is central to the contrapuntal common practice from the Renaissance through the twentieth century. However, twentieth-century tonal music (jazz, rock) often relaxes it while maintaining harmonic conventions — this is why Tymoczko places such music in the "harmonic common practice" but not the "contrapuntal common practice." The structural explanation adds nuance: the prohibition may be as much about the specific properties of the diatonic scale as about universal acoustic principles.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the prohibition as a universal acoustic law
  **Correction**: Tymoczko argues it also reflects structural properties of the diatonic scale; occasional parallel fifths in complex textures may not significantly weaken voice independence

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking parallel fourths are always prohibited too
  **Clarification**: Parallel fourths are permitted in classical three-voice counterpoint, even though the fourth is an inversion of the fifth. This marks an interesting difference: octaves remain objectionable regardless of register arrangement, while fifths can always be converted to unobjectionable fourths by voice transposition.
