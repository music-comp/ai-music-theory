# Quick Definition
A perfectly even six-note scale with all steps equal to two semitones. It is transpositionally symmetrical (only two distinct forms exist), contains no perfect fifths, and represents the extreme case where scalar and chromatic transposition are identical.

# Core Definition
The whole-tone scale combines two augmented triads a major second apart, producing six equally spaced notes. It is the only commonly used scale that is perfectly even in twelve-tone equal temperament — all steps are exactly two semitones. This perfect evenness means that scalar transposition is identical to chromatic transposition (no variation is introduced), which can make it harmonically static. It has six-fold transpositional symmetry, so only two distinct whole-tone collections exist. It contains many major thirds but no perfect fifths. It can be transformed into an acoustic scale by "splitting" one note into its two chromatic neighbors (Figure 4.6.2a).

# Prerequisites
- Stacks of thirds (augmented triads)

# Key Properties
1. 6 notes, all steps exactly 2 semitones
2. Perfectly even — the extreme case of the Goldilocks Principle
3. Six-fold transpositional symmetry: only 2 distinct forms
4. No perfect fifths
5. Scalar transposition = chromatic transposition (no variation)
6. Can transform into acoustic by "splitting" one note

# Context & Application
The whole-tone scale is closely associated with Debussy and other Impressionist composers. In Tymoczko's Goldilocks framework, it illustrates the danger of too much evenness: while it is maximally regular, it can become boring because scalar transposition introduces no variation. It sits at the top of the scale pyramid (Figure 4.6.3), connected to six acoustic scales by single voice leadings.
