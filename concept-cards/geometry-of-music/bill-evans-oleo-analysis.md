# Quick Definition
A detailed four-chorus analysis of Bill Evans' improvisation on Sonny Rollins' "Oleo" (1958), demonstrating the full range of jazz techniques: blues inflection, sidestepping, tritone substitution, quartal voicings, polyrhythm, and motivic development within a spontaneous improvisation.

# Core Definition
Evans' solo is "unusual and austere, like Bach in its stripped-down rigor," with Evans often playing only with his right hand while the bass player improvises counterpoint. The four choruses trace an arc of increasing complexity and resolution. Chorus 1 opens with blues gestures and introduces sidestepping between semitone-related triads. Chorus 2 features linearized voicings and rhythmic displacement. Chorus 3 is the climax, with extended dotted-sixteenth polyrhythms that are largely chromatic and outside the tonal system. Chorus 4 develops quartal ideas and blues motives, bringing the solo to a satisfying close. The relationships across choruses -- recurrent motives, organic development, and formal arc -- demonstrate "a deep understanding of the fundamental principles of twentieth-century tonal composition" achieved spontaneously.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of rhythm changes, tritone substitution, and playing outside

# Key Properties
1. Four-chorus arc: introduction -> development -> climax -> resolution
2. Chorus 1: blues gestures, sidestepping, B-Bb triadic oscillation
3. Chorus 2: linearized voicings, rhythmic displacement, increasing complexity
4. Chorus 3: dotted-sixteenth polyrhythms, chromatic/outside playing, climax
5. Chorus 4: quartal development, blues return, formal closure
6. Spontaneous motivic development comparable to composed music
7. Ensemble coordination at 240 bpm with syncopation and polyrhythm

# Construction / Recognition
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Track the motivic connections across choruses
2. Note the increasing rhythmic complexity from Chorus 1 to 3
3. Observe the formal arc of tension and release
4. Identify the different techniques (blues, sidestepping, quartal, polyrhythm) as they appear

# Context & Application
This analysis demonstrates Tymoczko's argument that jazz improvisation can achieve the structural sophistication of composed music. It synthesizes all the techniques discussed in Chapter 10 into a single performance.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 397-398): Chorus 1 -- blues opening, octatonic passage over Eb7, sidestepping between B and Bb triads.

**Example 2** (p. 401-402): Chorus 3 -- extended dotted-sixteenth polyrhythm with chromatic motive F-Gb-F-D shifted up and down by semitone, independent of chord changes.

**Example 3** (p. 404-405): Chorus 4 -- augmented triads ascending by minor third (instead of descending by semitone), exploiting both tritone and major-third substitution simultaneously.
