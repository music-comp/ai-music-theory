# Quick Definition
A mobile scale degree is a pitch position in a macroharmony that exists in two alternative forms (e.g., Bb vs. B-natural) which do not intermix within a single voice or clashing between simultaneous voices, creating a "7.5 note" collection.

# Core Definition
The concept of a mobile scale degree appears first in Tymoczko's discussion of medieval organum, where the "enriched diatonic system" contains seven and a half notes: C, D, E, F, G, A, and Bb/B-natural. The seventh degree exists in both a "soft" (Bb) and "hard" (B-natural) form, with the two forms avoiding direct succession within a single line and simultaneous clashing between voices. This creates an asymmetric macroharmony larger than a standard diatonic scale but smaller than a chromatic segment. The concept recurs in Tymoczko's analysis of Stravinsky's "Petit airs" from Histoire du soldat, where five degrees are fixed (G-A-B-D-E) and two are mobile (C/C# and F/F#), creating music that is "locally scale-based" within each instrument while globally ambiguous. The mobile degree concept is represented by multi-tiered pitch-class profiles, with fixed degrees assigned higher values than mobile ones.

# Prerequisites
- **macroharmony** — Mobile degrees create a special type of macroharmony

# Key Properties
1. A pitch position with two chromatic alternatives (e.g., Bb and B-natural)
2. The two forms do not progress directly to each other within a single voice
3. The two forms avoid simultaneous clashing between voices
4. Creates a macroharmony between n and n+1 notes ("7.5 notes" for the medieval system)
5. Representable by multi-tiered pitch-class profiles (mobile degrees get lower weights)
6. Creates a mild form of polytonality when different forms appear in different voices

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Choose a scale with one or more chromatic alternatives for specific degrees
2. Allow the alternatives to appear in different contexts or voices
3. Avoid direct chromatic succession between forms in a single voice
4. Avoid simultaneous clashing between forms in different voices
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for scale degrees that appear in two chromatic variants
2. Check whether the variants are kept separate (no direct succession, no simultaneous clashes)
3. Use pitch-class profiles with intermediate values for mobile degrees

# Context & Application
Mobile degrees bridge the gap between strict scale-based composition and free chromaticism. They allow composers to introduce chromatic variety while maintaining a fundamentally scale-based macroharmony. The medieval Bb/B-natural system (designed to avoid the tritone E-Bb or F-B) is the earliest example. Stravinsky's use is more sophisticated, creating an effect of polytonal ambiguity. The concept also connects to jazz, where performers may freely alternate between modal variants of specific scale degrees.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 215-216): Medieval organum — the seventh degree exists as Bb (to avoid E-Bb tritone) or B-natural (to avoid F-B tritone), creating a "7.5 note" system.

**Example 2** (p. 191-192, Figures 5.6.9): Stravinsky's "Petit airs" — five fixed degrees (G-A-B-D-E) and two mobile (C/C# and F/F#), with a four-tiered pitch-class profile showing A as tonic, fixed degrees as stable, and mobile degrees as secondary.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Analyzing music with mobile degrees using a larger fixed scale
  **Correction**: Tymoczko explicitly notes that in Stravinsky's "Petit airs," the mobile degrees should be understood as two forms of the same scale degree, not as belonging to a nine-note scale

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking mobile degrees are the same as chromatic neighbor tones
  **Clarification**: Mobile degrees are structural alternatives within the macroharmony, not surface embellishments; they persist across phrases and voices
