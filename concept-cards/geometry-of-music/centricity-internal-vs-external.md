# Quick Definition
Internal explanations claim that a collection's structure inherently picks out a tonal center; external explanations hold that composers create centricity through compositional means such as repetition, accent, duration, and stepwise convergence.

# Core Definition
Tymoczko identifies two competing explanations for centricity. Internalists cite structural principles: a note is more prominent if it is the lower note of consonant intervals in the macroharmony (I1), or if it does not form sharp dissonances with other notes (I2). These principles suggest, for example, that C is the "natural" center of {C, E, G} and that ionian/aeolian are the "natural" diatonic modes. Externalists cite compositional strategies: notes become prominent by appearing more frequently (E1), being held longer (E2), being dynamically accented (E3), rhythmically accented (E4), registrally salient (E5), targets of stepwise convergence (E6), or doubled at the octave/paired with the fifth above (E7). Tymoczko strongly favors the external view, arguing that internal factors are weak and easily overridden — it is entirely possible to write diatonic music that is acentric, or chromatic music with a clear center.

# Key Properties
1. Internal factors are structural properties of collections (intervallic relationships)
2. External factors are compositional strategies (repetition, accent, duration, etc.)
3. Tymoczko argues internal factors are relatively weak and easily overridden
4. The debate had historical consequences: Schoenberg's internalism led to atonality; Debussy's externalism led to extended tonality
5. External factors include seven specific mechanisms (E1-E7)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create (externalist approach):
1. Choose any macroharmony and any target tonal center
2. Apply E1-E7: repeat the target note, hold it longer, accent it, place it at registral extremes
3. Use stepwise contrary motion converging on the target
4. Double the target at the octave or pair it with the note a fifth above
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Check whether the perceived center matches internal predictions (lowest note of consonant intervals)
2. Check whether external factors (repetition, accent) point to a different center
3. If external factors override internal predictions, the externalist view is supported

# Context & Application
The internal/external debate had profound consequences for twentieth-century music. Schoenberg, an internalist, believed the diatonic scale had an inherent center but the chromatic scale did not — so abandoning diatonicism required abandoning centricity entirely, leading to atonality. Debussy and other French/Russian composers, externalists, believed centricity was a compositional choice and could be established within any collection, including non-diatonic scales. This divergence explains the split between nonscalar atonality and scalar "extended tonality." Tymoczko's own computer experiments confirm the externalist view: emphasizing particular notes through repetition and motion creates centricity in otherwise random sequences.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Claiming certain modes (like phrygian or locrian) are inherently unusable as tonal centers
  **Correction**: Tymoczko argues these modes are fully viable when supported by external compositional means, as demonstrated by Renaissance, jazz, rock, and other repertoire

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the external view means centricity is arbitrary
  **Clarification**: External factors are systematic and predictable; they are compositional strategies, not arbitrary choices. The external view simply holds that centricity is something composers achieve, not something collections possess inherently.
