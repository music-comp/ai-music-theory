# Quick Definition
Common tones and efficient voice leading together produce a kind of harmonic similarity: chords that can be linked by single-step voice leading (like third-related triads sharing two of three notes) are psychologically similar and can often substitute for one another.

# Core Definition
A root position F major triad is very similar to a first-inversion D minor triad because the chords share two notes (F and A) and can be linked by single-step voice leading (C moves to D). The "substitute" chord preserves the bass note and upper third while substituting a consonant sixth for a perfect fifth. This voice-leading-based similarity may explain how the circle of thirds came to play its dual role: by linking chords according to efficient voice leading, the circle defines a psychologically robust notion of similarity that in turn influenced the developing conventions of functional harmony. The result is multiple pairs of chords that can play similar functional roles: I/vi6, vi/IV6, IV/ii6, vii°/V6, and even V/III+6.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of efficient voice leading between chords
- The circle of thirds as a voice-leading structure

# Key Properties
1. Third-related triads share two of three notes
2. They can be connected by single-step voice leading
3. A first-inversion chord can replace a root-position third-related chord on the same bass
4. This similarity is psychologically robust and influenced harmonic conventions
5. The principle generates pairs: I/vi6, vi/IV6, IV/ii6, vii°/V6

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Take any diatonic triad in root position
2. Find the triad a diatonic third below
3. Place it in first inversion — it shares the same bass note
4. The two chords are functionally similar

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for chords sharing two of three notes
2. Check if one is a root-position and the other a first-inversion triad on the same bass
3. These pairs can substitute for each other without disrupting the harmonic fabric

# Context & Application
This principle bridges the figured-bass and Roman numeral approaches to harmony. Figured-bass notation privileges the bass note (treating chords on the same bass as similar), while Roman numeral analysis privileges root relationships. Common-tone similarity explains why both approaches capture something real about functional harmony and why third-related chords can serve similar roles.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming any two chords sharing common tones are functionally interchangeable
  **Correction**: The substitution principle applies specifically to third-related triads in diatonic context

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking common tones alone determine harmonic similarity
  **Clarification**: It is the combination of common tones and efficient voice leading that creates similarity; two chords might share notes but not be voice-leading proximate
