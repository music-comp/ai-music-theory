# Quick Definition
The cubic lattice at the center of three-note chromatic chord space arranges major and minor triads so that major-third-related chords are adjacent on each cube, providing a three-dimensional map for chromatic triadic progressions.

# Core Definition
At the center of three-note chromatic chord space lies a lattice of cubes. Each cube contains chords belonging to a single hexatonic scale. On each cube, major triads related by major third are adjacent, connected by single-semitone voice leadings. The lattice extends through multiple levels, with different cubes sharing vertices. Descending stepwise voice leading produces paths through the lattice that move "downward" level by level. Schubert's sequences in the Quartett-Satz present different ways of moving through this lattice: one sequence descends by I-V pairs down by major second; another moves upward by fifths alternating major and minor. Both can be visualized as paths through the same cubic structure.

# Prerequisites
- The major-third system and its voice-leading basis

# Key Properties
1. Each cube contains chords from a single hexatonic scale
2. Major-third-related triads are adjacent on each cube
3. Descending stepwise voice leading moves downward through the lattice
4. The lattice is three-dimensional (compared to the tesseract's four dimensions for seventh chords)
5. Different paths through the lattice produce different chromatic sequences
6. The augmented triad lies at the center of each cube

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Place three major triads related by major third at alternate vertices of a cube
2. The remaining vertices hold minor triads (also major-third-related)
3. Connect cubes that share a vertex to build the lattice
4. Paths through the lattice correspond to chromatic triadic progressions

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. A chromatic triadic progression that can be mapped as a path through connected cubes
2. Voice leading that moves predominantly downward by step
3. Root motion that combines semitones, major thirds, and fifths

# Context & Application
The cubic lattice provides a visual "game board" for chromatic triadic composition, analogous to the tesseract for seventh chords. Understanding the lattice helps explain why certain chromatic progressions feel related despite different root motions — they traverse the same geometric space by different routes. The lattice also explains the connection between major-third substitution and hexatonic scales: the substitution is simply movement within a single cube.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking the cubic lattice only contains major triads
  **Correction**: Each cube contains both major and minor triads

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating the cubic lattice (three-note chromatic space) with the diatonic chord lattice (Section 7.5)
  **Clarification**: The diatonic lattice contains diatonic three-note chords; the cubic lattice contains chromatic three-note chords — different spaces entirely
