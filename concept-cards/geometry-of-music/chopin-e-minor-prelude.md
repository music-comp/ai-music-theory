# Quick Definition
Chopin's E minor Prelude Op. 28 No. 4 is a virtual rewriting of the F minor Mazurka Op. 68 No. 4, using the same voice-leading procedures to embellish a different harmonic sequence — descending fifths instead of descending semitones, achieved by lowering the voice containing the fifth (rather than the root) at each cycle boundary.

# Core Definition
The E minor Prelude uses the same "directions for improvisation" as the Mazurka: begin with a dominant seventh, lower third/fifth/seventh in any order to reach a diminished seventh, then lower one note to reach the next dominant seventh. The critical difference: in the Mazurka, the root descends to produce semitonal motion; in the Prelude, the voice containing the fifth descends, producing descending-fifth motion. This small change transforms the relationship between successive dominant sevenths from semitone to perfect fifth. Since the diminished seventh is completely symmetrical, there are four possible choices for which note to lower (corresponding to voices that contained the root, third, fifth, or seventh of the original dominant seventh), producing four sequence types: descending semitone, descending major third, descending fifth, and ascending major second.

# Key Properties
1. Same "directions for improvisation" as the Mazurka, different cycle-connection rule
2. Lowering the "fifth-voice" produces descending fifths (vs root-voice for semitones)
3. Four total possibilities: descend by semitone, major third, fifth, or ascend by major second
4. The liquid chromatic texture disguises the sequential descending-fifths skeleton
5. The Prelude displays one route through the hypercube; the Mazurka another
6. Together they demonstrate more profound understanding than either alone

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Follow the Mazurka's directions (lower third/fifth/seventh of dominant seventh in any order)
2. At the diminished seventh, lower the note that was the fifth (not the root)
3. Result: a new dominant seventh a perfect fifth below the original
4. Repeat; the sequence descends by fifths

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. A series of dominant seventh chords related by descending fifth
2. Chromatic passing chords (diminished sevenths, half-diminished sevenths, etc.) between them
3. Overall voice leading that descends stepwise

# Context & Application
The connection between the two pieces is one of the chapter's most remarkable findings. It demonstrates that Chopin had a sophisticated understanding of four-dimensional chord space — knowledge expressed through piano music rather than mathematical notation. The recomposition of the Prelude as the Mazurka at the end of his life suggests the earlier piece did not exhaust what he wanted to say about these voice-leading possibilities.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Analyzing the Prelude as a chain of first-inversion triads with suspensions
  **Correction**: Tymoczko interprets it as a four-voice texture exemplifying a descending-fifths sequence of dominant sevenths

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the four sequence types are unrelated
  **Clarification**: They are all produced by the same directions; only the cycle-connection rule differs
