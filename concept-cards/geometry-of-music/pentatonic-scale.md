# Quick Definition
A five-note nearly even scale constructed from a stack of five perfect fifths. Its "thirds" (two-step scalar intervals) are four or five semitones, giving pentatonic melodies a distinctive "fourthy" quality. It is a subset of the diatonic scale.

# Core Definition
The pentatonic scale (e.g., C-D-E-G-A) is constructed from a stack of five perfect fifths, which falls 0.9 semitones short of three octaves. Its "near fifth" is approximately a minor sixth. With steps of two and three semitones, it has one step larger than two semitones, placing it in Group 2 of Figure 4.4.5. Its most distinctive property is that pentatonic "thirds" (stacks of two scale steps) are four or five semitones — the five-semitone interval being a perfect fourth. This means that tertian constructions within the pentatonic scale tend to produce "fourth chords" rather than the more familiar triadic sonorities. Twentieth-century composers exploit this property to create music with a distinctively "fourthy" or open quality.

# Key Properties
1. 5 notes with steps of 2 and 3 semitones
2. Nearly even five-note scale
3. Constructed from 5 perfect fifths
4. A subset of the diatonic scale
5. Pentatonic "thirds" = 4 or 5 semitones (giving a "fourthy" quality)
6. Can be linked to its five-semitone transposition by single-semitone voice leading

# Context & Application
The pentatonic is often considered a "simpler" scale, but Tymoczko highlights its distinctive intervallic properties. The fact that pentatonic thirds sound like fourths gives it a unique harmonic color exploited by Debussy, Ligeti, Herbie Hancock, and many others. From the five-note chord space perspective, the pentatonic's efficient voice leading to its five-semitone transposition (parallel to the diatonic circle of fifths) is a key structural property.
