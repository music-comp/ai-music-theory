# Quick Definition
"Parasitic" scales are mere byproducts of chordal accidentals applied to a diatonic collection, with no independent scalar logic. "Virtuous" scales are familiar collections possessing desirable qualities -- even octave division, many consonances -- suggesting attentiveness to both chordal and scalar levels.

# Core Definition
The distinction between virtuous and parasitic scales identifies whether a composer treats scales as independent musical objects or merely as chordal byproducts. A parasitic scale arises when chordal accidentals are mechanically applied to an underlying diatonic collection, producing unfamiliar or awkward collections without regard for scalar properties. A virtuous scale, by contrast, is a recognizable collection (diatonic, acoustic, whole-tone, octatonic, etc.) that divides the octave relatively evenly and contains many consonant intervals. The presence of virtuous scales suggests a compositional mind attuned to both chordal and scalar levels -- the "musical twofoldness" central to twentieth-century tonal practice.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of chord-scale compatibility
- Knowledge of scale properties (evenness, consonance content)

# Key Properties
1. Parasitic scales: determined solely by chordal accidentals; no independent scalar logic
2. Virtuous scales: familiar, recognizable collections with desirable properties
3. Virtuous scales divide the octave relatively evenly
4. Virtuous scales contain a large number of consonant intervals
5. The distinction diagnoses the degree of scalar awareness in a composition

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. When harmonizing chords, choose scales that are familiar and possess good scalar properties rather than merely applying accidentals
2. Select from the standard repertoire: diatonic, acoustic, whole-tone, octatonic, harmonic, hexatonic
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Extract the complete collection of pitches sounding over a chord
2. If the collection is a recognized scale type, it is virtuous
3. If it is an unfamiliar collection arising only from chordal accidentals, it is parasitic

# Context & Application
This distinction is fundamental to analyzing twentieth-century tonal music. It separates composers who merely use chromatic chords within a diatonic framework from those who actively compose at both chordal and scalar levels simultaneously.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming all scales in twentieth-century music are virtuous
  **Correction**: Some passages use purely parasitic scales, especially in transitional or less scale-conscious contexts

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking "parasitic" is always negative
  **Clarification**: The term describes a structural relationship, not necessarily aesthetic quality; some effective passages use parasitic scales
