# Quick Definition
Shostakovich's technique of lowering notes of natural minor to create unfamiliar "hyperminor" scales with a sinister character, where four possible lowerings combine to form sixteen scales representable as a four-dimensional cube (tesseract).

# Core Definition
Unlike traditional composers who raise notes of natural minor to bring it closer to major, Shostakovich lowers notes to create darker tonalities. F# natural minor offers four candidates for semitonal lowering: the second degree (producing phrygian), the fourth (natural minor b4), the fifth (a mode of D acoustic), and the seventh (natural minor b7). These four lowerings can be combined to produce sixteen different seven-note scales, represented as a four-dimensional cube. In the F# minor Prelude and Fugue Op. 87, Shostakovich uses eight of these sixteen scales and no others, systematically exploring the "scale tesseract" as his modulatory space.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of scale-first composition
- Familiarity with natural minor and its alterations

# Key Properties
1. Four notes of F# natural minor can be lowered by semitone
2. Sixteen possible combinations form a four-dimensional cube
3. Shostakovich uses eight of the sixteen in Op. 87
4. Progressive lowering creates increasingly "hyperminor" tonality
5. The journey from natural minor to fully lowered corresponds to descending along the cube's edge
6. Analogous to Chopin's systematic chord descents along four-dimensional chord lattices

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Start with natural minor
2. Identify the four degrees that can be lowered without creating duplicates
3. Combine lowerings systematically to generate the scale tesseract
4. Compose by moving along edges of the cube (single lowerings)
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Look for progressively flattened scale degrees in a minor-key context
2. Identify which lowerings are active at each point
3. Map the scales onto the four-dimensional cube

# Context & Application
The analysis reveals Shostakovich's prelude as preparing the listener for the fugue's unfamiliar tonality by gradually acclimatizing through progressive lowerings -- fulfilling the traditional function of a prelude in an unfamiliar manner. It is "amazing that such similar geometries can represent both chords in Chopin and scales in Shostakovich."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating each scale as an ad hoc choice
  **Correction**: Shostakovich's scales form a systematic network; the tesseract reveals the underlying logic

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing "hyperminor" with harmonic or melodic minor
  **Clarification**: Hyperminor goes beyond natural minor by lowering additional degrees, creating scales darker than any standard minor mode
