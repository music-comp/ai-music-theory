# Quick Definition
Short rightward motions (strong progressions) along the circle of thirds produce pitch classes that descend by third, a fact that Bach and other tonal composers exploit by writing descending melodic thirds over familiar progressions.

# Core Definition
When chords progress rightward along the circle of thirds, each new note is a third below its predecessor. For example, the progression I-ii-V-I can be articulated by the pitch-class sequence (G, E, C)-(A, F, D)-(D, B, G)-(G, E, C), where each entering note is a third below the previous. Tonal composers exploit this by writing descending melodic thirds over familiar progressions — a device that was a particular favorite of Bach's. Sometimes bare sequences of falling thirds appear without articulating individual chords, presenting the raw material of functional harmony in an unusually pure form. This omnipresent "engine of descending thirds" creates a strong sense of tonal directionality even when exact sequential transposition is absent.

# Prerequisites
- The circle of thirds and strong progressions

# Key Properties
1. Strong progressions generate descending-thirds sequences of pitch classes
2. This is a consequence of the circle of thirds arrangement
3. Bach frequently exploits this with descending-thirds melodic lines
4. Bare descending thirds can appear without clear chord articulation
5. Creates the characteristic "falling" quality of tonal progressions

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Voice a strong progression (rightward on the circle of thirds)
2. In each new chord, the entering pitch class is a third below the departing one
3. Feature this entering pitch class prominently in the melody

## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Melodic lines consistently descending by thirds
2. Underlying strong harmonic progressions
3. Particularly characteristic of Bach

# Context & Application
The descending-thirds quality explains why even non-sequential passages in Bach can sound sequential — the falling thirds provide a quasi-sequential feel. This "engine" is typically encountered in a refined form, but occasionally appears in raw pitch-class sequences.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Fig. 7.2.5, p. 252): Bach's Brandenburg Concerto No. 3, III, mm. 11-12, and F major two-part invention, mm. 21-23.

**Example 2** (Fig. 7.2.6, p. 252): Bare descending thirds without clear harmonies in Bach's Goldberg Variation 14, mm. 9-10, and the opening of Brahms' Op. 119 No. 1.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming descending melodic thirds always indicate a sequence
  **Correction**: They can appear in nonsequential contexts as a byproduct of strong progressions

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating descending melodic thirds with root motion by third
  **Clarification**: Root motion by third generates descending thirds in the pitch-class stream, but the melody may be more elaborate
