# Quick Definition
Centricity is the tendency for one pitch class to be heard as more prominent or important than the others, functioning as a point of reference or "home base" for the music.

# Core Definition
The fifth of Tymoczko's five basic components of tonality. Centricity refers to the perceptual phenomenon whereby one pitch class in a passage serves as a gravitational center — a point of stability, rest, and reference around which other pitches are organized. This central pitch class is typically emphasized through repetition, metric placement, registral position, and resolution patterns. Centricity is related to but distinct from the traditional concept of "key," which implies specific functional relationships in addition to a tonal center.

# Key Properties
1. One pitch class is perceived as more important, stable, or central than others
2. Created through multiple reinforcing cues: repetition, metric emphasis, bass position, duration
3. Can be present even in music that lacks traditional functional harmony
4. Often works in conjunction with limited macroharmony (the center is typically a member of the macroharmony)
5. Different from "key" — centricity is a more general concept

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct/Create:
1. Emphasize a particular pitch class through repetition and metric stress
2. Place the central pitch in the bass and at phrase beginnings/endings
3. Resolve melodic and harmonic motion toward the central pitch class
## To Identify/Recognize:
1. Listen for the pitch class that feels like "home" or a point of rest
2. Check which pitch class appears most often in prominent positions
3. Identify the pitch to which phrases tend to resolve

# Context & Application
Centricity is one of the most broadly applicable features of tonal music. It is present in medieval and Renaissance music (as the "final" of a mode), in common-practice music (as the tonic), in jazz (as the key center), in rock (as the tonal center), and in much twentieth-century music that avoids traditional functional harmony but maintains a pitch-class center. Tymoczko argues that centricity, along with the other four components, defines the broad "extended common practice" of tonal music.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 21-25): In a piece in C major, the pitch class C functions as the center — it feels like the most stable note, appears at cadences, and serves as the point of resolution.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Equating centricity with major/minor key
  **Correction**: Centricity is a more general phenomenon; music can have a center without being in a traditional key

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing centricity with root of a chord
  **Clarification**: Centricity is a property of a passage or piece, referring to the pitch class that functions as a tonal center; chord root is a property of an individual chord
