# Quick Definition
Motion in which voices move along both chord and scale simultaneously, combining transposition along two hierarchically nested collections.

# Formal Definition
Doubly parallel motion combines transposition along the chord (t_x) and transposition along the enclosing scale (T_y), producing a series of harmonies that are broadly similar -- arranging the same intervals in the same abstract registral configuration. These two transpositions are the *only* operations that preserve both chord type and distance in chordal steps. They commute (t_x T_y = T_y t_x) and subscripts add (t_x T_y + t_a T_b = t_{x+a} T_{y+b}). For an n-note chord in an o-note scale, t_n = T_o (n chordal steps equals o scale steps).

# Musical Context
Doubly parallel motion is central to the quadruple hierarchy, appearing at every level: voice in chord, chord in scale, scale in chromatic aggregate. In rock harmony (Chapter 2), the model treats inner voices as moving in doubly parallel motion with the melody using the same combination t_x **T**_y, while the bass provides contrary motion by using the same chromatic transposition but a different chordal transposition. This simplification avoids the more complex hierarchical picture while capturing essential voice-leading relationships.

# Examples
- Scarlatti K.3, mm. 3-6: right hand moves t_1 (up by chordal step) while left hand moves T_2 (up by two scalar steps), the two producing very similar results since t_1 and T_2 are almost the same (Figure P2.3)
- In "Knockin' on Heaven's Door," background voices shadow the main melody at two chordal steps distance, producing a scalar interval of sixth or fifth depending on the chord (Section 2.3)
- The combination t_{-1} T_2 "does very little" since chordal step and two scalar steps nearly cancel

# Common Confusions
- Doubly parallel motion is not simple parallel motion (which moves all voices by the same interval) -- it involves *two different* levels of transposition
- The operations can include inversion as well as transposition (notated i_x I_y), though this is less common
- When measured in chordal steps the voices move exactly in parallel; measured in chromatic or diatonic steps they may diverge
