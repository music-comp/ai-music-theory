# Quick Definition

A canonic sequence combining five-step and seven-step patterns on the triadic circle, where each new arpeggio entry appears either a fifth above or at the same pitch level. Both sequence and canon, appearing from Josquin to Beethoven, often at moments of expressive urgency.

# Core Definition

On the 3-in-7 triadic circle, five-step motion produces ascending-fifth canonic sequences with ascending triadic arpeggios (typically filled with passing tones); seven-step motion embeds the arpeggio in a round moving by chordal step (p. 172). "These two patterns can be combined -- each new triadic arpeggio either a fifth above or at the same pitch level" (p. 172). Unison entries slow harmonic rhythm, avoid second-inversion triads, and extend melodic lines into longer scales (Figure 4.4.5, p. 172). The four-voice version takes two-step counterclockwise motion on the space of triads with doubled third (Figure 4.6.7, p. 182), generating ascending-fifth canons with 4-3-2 in the top voice against 6-7-1 in the bass. This was "a favorite of J. S. Bach's, permeating his four-voice music and likely internalized as part of his improvisational practice" (p. 182).

# Key Properties

1. Combines five-step (ascending fifth) and seven-step (chordal step) patterns
2. Each entry either a fifth above or at unison with previous
3. Unison entries slow harmonic rhythm and avoid 6-4 chords
4. Four-voice version: ascending-fifth canon with 4-3-2 vs. 6-7-1
5. Appears from Josquin to Beethoven -- virtually every Renaissance composer
6. Can be remarkably disguised (Bach chorale harmonizations)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct:
1. Start with an ascending triadic arpeggio in one voice
2. Bring in a second voice either a fifth above or at the same pitch level
3. Continue adding entries, filling arpeggios with passing tones
4. Use unison entries to extend scales and avoid 6-4 chords

## To Recognize:
1. Look for ascending arpeggios entering in imitation
2. Check for fifth or unison relationships between entries
3. In four voices, look for 4-3-2 / 6-7-1 scale degree pattern

# Context & Application

Orlando Gibbons uses the Chase schema to generate ascending fifths F-C-g-d in *In Nomine a 4* (Figure 4.4.6, p. 173). Josquin's "Benedicite, omnia opera" has seven entries (Figure 4.4.7, p. 173). Beethoven's Third Symphony scherzo, mm. 96-111, uses the Chase variant (Figure 4.4.8, p. 173). Bach's chorale "Was mein Gott will, das g'scheh allzeit" contains a remarkably disguised four-voice version (Figure 4.6.8, p. 182).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating unison entries as anomalies
  **Correction**: Unison entries serve specific purposes and are integral to the schema

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the Chase must be audibly canonic
  **Clarification**: It can be remarkably disguised, as in Bach's chorale harmonizations
