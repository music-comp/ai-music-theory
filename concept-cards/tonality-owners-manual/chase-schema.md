# Quick Definition
A canonic sequence combining the five-step and seven-step patterns on the triadic circle, where each new triadic arpeggio entry appears either a fifth above or at the same pitch level as the previous entry. Both sequence and canon, appearing from Josquin to Beethoven.

# Formal Definition
On the 3-in-7 triadic circle, five-step motion produces a fifth-sequence with ascending triadic arpeggios (typically filled with passing tones), while seven-step motion embeds the arpeggio within a round moving by chordal step. The Chase schema combines these two, each new entry either a fifth above or at unison with the previous. Unison entries slow harmonic rhythm, avoid second-inversion triads, and extend melodic lines into longer scales. The four-voice version takes two-step counterclockwise motion on the space of triads with doubled third, generating an ascending-fifth canon with scale degrees ^4-^3-^2 in the top voice against ^6-^7-^1 in the bass.

# Musical Context
The Chase schema appears in virtually every Renaissance composer's music and survives well into the nineteenth century. From the standpoint of the Prime Directive, it is essentially similar to the two-voice canon generated by the diatonic third's basic voice leading, only at the triadic level. It was a particular favorite of J. S. Bach, permeating his four-voice music and likely internalized as part of his improvisational practice.

# Common Confusions
- The Chase schema is simultaneously a sequence and a canon; it is not one or the other exclusively
- Unison entries are not "failed fifths" but serve specific purposes: slowing harmonic rhythm, avoiding 64 chords, extending scales
- The four-voice version can be remarkably disguised, as in Bach's chorale harmonizations
