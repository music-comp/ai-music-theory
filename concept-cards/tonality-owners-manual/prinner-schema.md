# Quick Definition

Gjerdingen's name for a melodic-harmonic schema featuring bass 4-3-2-1 with upper voice 6-5-4-3, combining I6 as the default harmonization of bass 3 with the vii-dim-6--I cadence. Grounded in the fifths-and-steps arrangement and present from the Renaissance through the eighteenth century.

# Core Definition

The Prinner schema (named by Gjerdingen) combines bass 4-3-2-1 with upper-voice 6-5-4-3 (p. 266, Fig. 6.2.5). It combines two standard ionian features: I6 as the default harmonization of 3 in the bass and the vii-dim-6--I cadence (p. 266). It is grounded in the fifths-and-steps arrangement (Fig. 6.1.4): 3-4-5-6 over I-IV-I-IV can be understood as a reversed Prinner (p. 262). The Prinner is "as characteristic of the Renaissance as of the eighteenth century" (p. 266).

# Key Properties

1. Bass: 4-3-2-1; upper voice: 6-5-4-3
2. Combines I6 over bass degree 3 with vii-dim-6--I cadence
3. Characteristic of both Renaissance and eighteenth-century music
4. Grounded in the fifths-and-steps defaults
5. A widespread triadic schema, not a localized eighteenth-century idiom (p. 298)
6. Appears in Marenzio's "Ahi dispietata morte" as a IV64-I alternation (p. 298)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify a Prinner:
1. Look for a descending bass line 4-3-2-1
2. Check for upper voice 6-5-4-3 in parallel tenths
3. Note harmonic progressions: typically IV-I6-vii-dim-6-I or variants
4. The schema may be varied or fragmented

# Context & Application

The Prinner appears across centuries and genres, demonstrating that galant schemas are grounded in basic triadic voice-leading patterns rather than being arbitrary conventions of a specific period.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 266, Fig. 6.2.5): The Prinner combines standard ionian features.

**Example 2** (p. 262, Fig. 6.1.5): Michaelis's "Passando per una rezzola" (1504), mm. 7-9, features a Prinner variant.

**Example 3** (p. 298): Marenzio's "Ahi dispietata morte," mm. 17-19, presents a Prinner variant as IV64-I alternation.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating the Prinner as unique to the eighteenth century
  **Correction**: It appears throughout the Renaissance as well

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking galant schemas are arbitrary period conventions
  **Clarification**: The Prinner is grounded in basic triadic geometry -- any style using fifths-and-steps defaults will produce similar patterns
