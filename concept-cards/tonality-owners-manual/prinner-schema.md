# Quick Definition
A galant schema identified by Gjerdingen featuring a descending melody 6^-5^-4^-3^ over a descending bass 4^-3^-2^-1^, combining the preference for I6 over iii with the vii-diminished-6--I cadence.

# Formal Definition
The Prinner schema, named by Robert Gjerdingen, combines an upper voice descending 6^-5^-4^-3^ with a bass descending 4^-3^-2^-1^. This produces a characteristic sequence of harmonies often including IV-I6-ii-vii-diminished-6-I. Tymoczko shows that the Prinner emerges naturally from two standard features of ionian-mode protofunctionality: the preference for I6 as the default harmonization of 3^ in the bass (rather than iii) and the Renaissance vii-diminished-6--I cadence. The Prinner can also be understood as a reversed form of the I-IV-I-IV (3^-4^-5^-6^) pattern from the fifths-and-steps arrangement.

# Musical Context
The Prinner is as characteristic of the Renaissance as of the eighteenth century. It appears in protofunctional music wherever composers default to I6 over iii and use the vii-diminished-6--I cadence. The schema represents a widespread triadic pattern rather than a localized eighteenth-century idiom. In Palestrina's Pope Marcellus Mass, the "up by fourth, down by step" bass motive generates Prinner figures (IV-I6-ii-vii-diminished-6-I).

# Common Confusions
- The Prinner is not just an eighteenth-century schema. It is grounded in the basic features of ionian-mode protofunctionality and appears throughout the Renaissance.
- The Prinner can be understood as a reversed form of the 3^-4^-5^-6^ pattern under I-IV-I-IV, which is itself a sequential transposition of the Fenaroli.
- The schema is a "widespread triadic schema rather than a localized eighteenth-century idiom."
