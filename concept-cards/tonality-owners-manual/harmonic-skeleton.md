# Quick Definition
The underlying sequence of chord tones that remains after all nonharmonic tones have been removed from a musical passage, revealing the structural harmonies.

# Formal Definition
The harmonic skeleton is the result of nonharmonic reduction: the consonant framework that carries the weight of the musical argument after nonharmonic tones have been replaced by their harmonic partners. In many styles, this produces a triadic skeleton that can be analyzed for harmonic progressions, voice leading, and parallel motion. The nonharmonic system allows theorists to play the role of structural engineers, distinguishing the "load-bearing and ornamental walls of the musical house."

# Musical Context
In Palestrina's music, the harmonic skeleton is nearly always recoverable through algorithmic reduction, producing a triadic framework exactly as described in textbooks. This represents perhaps the closest Western music has come to the theorist's dream of purely consonant harmony decorated by clearly nonharmonic embellishment. In later music, the relationship between surface and skeleton becomes more complex and interpretive.

# Common Confusions
- The harmonic skeleton is not always objectively determinable. In some passages, there is no way to reduce the surface to a well-behaved harmonic skeleton, and the "skeleton" involves interpretive choices.
- Suspensions can license parallel fifths at the skeleton level (Figure 5.1.10), creating an inconsistency: some parallels are allowed "on the surface" while others are allowed "in the skeleton."
- Palestrina's nonharmonic tones rarely obscure the entire duration of a consonance, meaning harmonies are usually "real sonic objects." In later music, harmonies are often entirely obscured and function as abstract entities.
