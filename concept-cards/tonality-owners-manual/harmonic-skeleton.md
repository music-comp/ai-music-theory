# Quick Definition

The underlying sequence of chord tones remaining after all nonharmonic tones have been removed, revealing the structural harmonies. The nonharmonic system allows theorists to distinguish the "load-bearing and ornamental walls of the musical house" (p. 210).

# Core Definition

The harmonic skeleton is the consonant framework produced by nonharmonic reduction: removing nonharmonic tones and replacing them with their harmonic partners reveals the structural harmonies carrying the weight of the musical argument (p. 210). In Palestrina's music, this skeleton is nearly always recoverable algorithmically, producing a triadic framework exactly as described in textbooks. This represents "perhaps the closest Western music has ever come to the theorist's dream of purely consonant harmony decorated by clearly nonharmonic embellishment" (p. 213). In later music, the relationship between surface and skeleton becomes more complex and interpretive.

# Prerequisites

- **Nonharmonic tone** — Understanding what is removed to reveal the skeleton

# Key Properties

1. Produced by removing all nonharmonic tones from the musical surface
2. In Palestrina, nearly always recoverable by algorithm
3. In later music, increasingly interpretive and contested
4. In Mahler and late Romantic music, the skeleton is "all but unrecoverable" (p. 245)
5. Suspensions can create inconsistencies -- licensing parallels at the skeleton level (p. 217)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Reveal the Harmonic Skeleton:
1. Identify all nonharmonic tones using SNAP classification
2. Replace each with the chord tone it embellishes
3. The remaining notes form the harmonic skeleton
4. Verify the skeleton produces acceptable harmonic progressions

# Context & Application

The concept becomes increasingly problematic in later music. In the Rondo-Burlesque from Mahler's Ninth Symphony, reduction to a harmonic skeleton is "no longer something broadly intersubjective or syntactical, but rather autobiographical and interpretive" (p. 246).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 213): In Palestrina's masses, a computer can extract the harmonic skeleton with high accuracy.

**Example 2** (p. 239, Fig. 5.4.7): A computer analysis of the opening Mozart piano sonata, correctly identifying about 75% of chords using nonharmonic penalties.

**Example 3** (p. 217, Fig. 5.1.10): Suspensions masking parallel fifths in the harmonic skeleton.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming the harmonic skeleton is always objectively determinable
  **Correction**: In later music, multiple valid skeletons may exist for the same passage

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking suspensions are purely decorative relative to the skeleton
  **Clarification**: Suspensions can license parallels at the skeleton level, creating inconsistency (p. 217)
