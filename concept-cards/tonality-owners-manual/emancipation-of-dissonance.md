# Quick Definition

The twentieth-century loosening of nonharmonic constraints, where notes outside the harmony are no longer required to resolve to harmonic notes according to rigid formulas. The very distinction between "inside" and "outside" the harmony becomes problematic.

# Core Definition

The emancipation of the dissonance, in Tymoczko's precise sense, means notes outside the harmony are no longer required to resolve to harmonic notes according to rigid formulas (p. 247). This should be distinguished from the more radical view that denies the distinction between consonance and dissonance altogether. In music by Shostakovich, jazz, and rock, there remains a fundamental sense of "inside" and "outside," of tension and release relative to the prevailing harmony (p. 248). This limited emancipation begins with Chopin, Mahler, and Debussy, who realized harmonies could acquire sixths and ninths without losing meaning (p. 248). In popular-music scholarship, the phenomenon is known as "the melodic-harmonic divorce" (p. 247).

# Key Properties

1. Notes outside the harmony no longer require formulaic resolution
2. Distinguished from denying consonance/dissonance distinction altogether
3. A sense of "inside" and "outside" remains
4. Begins with Chopin, Mahler, Debussy (added sixths and ninths)
5. Radicalized by Stravinsky (chromatic nonharmonic tones with secondary centricity)
6. A feature of virtually all twentieth-century music (notated and non-notated)
7. In jazz: theorized as "playing outside"

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Emancipated Dissonance:
1. Look for nonharmonic tones that do not resolve according to standard rules
2. Check whether a sense of tonal center or harmonic function persists
3. Note whether the "outside" notes create their own secondary logic
4. Distinguish from fully atonal music where inside/outside loses meaning

# Context & Application

This is the freedom Monteverdi advocated, realized only centuries after his death (p. 248). Sound recording played a crucial role, allowing musicians to bypass notation and formal education -- significant forces sustaining the nonharmonic system (p. 248). The blues was central: in "Rising Sun Blues," the G-natural clashing against E major is "essential to the blues but utterly foreign to the nonharmonic system" (p. 248).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Equating emancipation of dissonance with atonality
  **Correction**: Emancipated dissonance still operates within a framework of tonal reference and inside/outside distinction

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the emancipation of dissonance means "anything goes"
  **Clarification**: There is still a fundamental sense of tension and release relative to the prevailing harmony (p. 248)
