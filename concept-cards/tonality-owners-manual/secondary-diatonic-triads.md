# Quick Definition
The diatonic triads other than the three primary triads (I, IV, V) -- specifically ii, iii, vi, and vii-diminished -- which gradually acquired standardized functional roles over the course of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

# Formal Definition
Secondary diatonic triads are the chords built on scale degrees other than 1^, 4^, and 5^. In protofunctional music, these chords play relatively undifferentiated roles. Over time, they acquire specific functional identities: ii becomes the primary predominant (eventually eclipsing IV), vi serves as a pre-predominant, vii-diminished-6 acts as a dominant-function chord, and iii becomes vanishingly rare. The gradual differentiation of secondary triads represents one of several independent processes in the emergence of functional tonality.

# Musical Context
In Dalza's pavanes and the frottola collections, ii can move to I more often than to V or vii-diminished, and the submediant has no characteristic role. The four-chord cycle I-ii-V-I and eventually the five-chord cycle I-vi-(ii or IV)-V-I represent the increasing incorporation of secondary triads into functional routines. This process terminates with the baroque's seven-chord sequence of thirds: I-vi-IV-ii-vii-diminished-V-I.

# Common Confusions
- The functional roles of secondary triads are not fixed across all periods. The late sixteenth century is relatively IV-focused while the seventeenth century becomes more ii-oriented. We need to recognize multiple functionalities rather than generalizing about "functional harmony as such."
- The vii-diminished-6 chord has its functional role substantially settled even in Palestrina's music, unlike other secondary triads whose roles continue to evolve.
- vi gradually shifts from serving as a predominant (moving to V) to a pre-predominant (moving to ii or IV before V).
