# Quick Definition
Replacing a chord with one whose root is a tritone away; maximally distant on the diatonic distance scale but connected by specific voice-leading relationships.

# Formal Definition
Tritone-related triads are four steps apart on the diatonic distance scale -- the maximum possible distance. On the spiral diagram, they occupy diametrically opposed positions. Despite this harmonic remoteness, tritone substitution is a recognized technique in jazz harmony (replacing a dominant seventh chord with one a tritone away) because the two chords share the same tritone interval between their third and seventh. In the context of rock, tritone-related progressions are essentially absent from the repertoire, consistent with the model's prediction that diatonically distant progressions are rare.

# Musical Context
The near-total absence of tritone progressions in the Rolling Stone corpus (Figure 2.2.4) strongly supports the diatonic distance model. Heavy metal occasionally emphasizes diatonically distant progressions including tritones for their ominous quality -- what David Huron calls "contratonal" music. In jazz, tritone substitution is a learned convention that overrides the diatonic distance preference, enabling chromatic approaches to resolution.

# Examples
- Rolling Stone "500 Greatest Songs" data: not a single tritone progression between major triads appears in the corpus (Figure 2.2.4)
- C major and F# major: diatonic distance 4, the maximum, requiring four semitonal shifts between their closest parent scales
- In jazz, Db7 substitutes for G7 in a ii-V-I progression (Dm7-Db7-Cmaj7), exploiting shared guide tones

# Common Confusions
- Tritone substitution in jazz works for seventh chords (shared third/seventh) -- it is less natural for simple triads
- Maximum diatonic distance does not mean maximum voice-leading distance; tritone-related triads can still be connected by fairly efficient voice leading
- The rarity of tritone progressions in rock reflects a diatonic norm, not a voice-leading limitation
