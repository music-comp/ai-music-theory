# Quick Definition

An approach to music theory, deriving from Leonard Meyer via Robert Gjerdingen, proposing that composers recombine atomic patterns (schemas) rather than following general laws -- emphasizing the idiomatic and particular nature of musical practice.

# Core Definition

Schema theory proposes a "construction grammar" in which musical behavior is "not lawlike at its deepest level -- instead it is an unruly and messy enterprise whose basic objects are specific gestures, often tied to particular formal contexts" (p. 26). Gjerdingen "favors a return to a pre-Rameauian conception of musical structure, rooted in the inherently particular injunctions of seventeenth- and eighteenth-century pedagogy" (p. 27). Tymoczko argues schema theory is "best understood as augmenting rather than replacing the generalizations of familiar theory" (p. 29) -- composers learn "both specific idioms and also more general and systematic principles" (p. 29).

# Key Properties

1. Schemas are atomic patterns -- specific gestures tied to particular formal contexts
2. Schema theory shares with Schenkerian theory a focus on outer-voice contrapuntal patterns
3. It differs in favoring particular idioms over general principles
4. Many schemas originate in constraints inherent to musical materials -- the "rock face" of contrapuntal geometry
5. The "deep" version of a schema (general voice-leading pattern) must be distinguished from the "shallow" version (specific stylistic mannerism)
6. Schemas can take on different rhetorical roles in different styles

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify a Schema:
1. Recognize a recurring outer-voice contrapuntal pattern
2. Check whether it appears in a specific formal context (e.g., cadential, opening)
3. Compare instances across pieces and composers for consistent features
4. Distinguish deep (general voice-leading) from shallow (style-specific) aspects
5. Use corpus analysis to determine the scope of a schema's actual use

# Context & Application

Schema theory provides an important corrective to speculative overreach, offering an "antidote to two powerful music-theoretical temptations": (1) declaring unusual moments ungrammatical because they violate oversimplistic generalizations; (2) constructing ad hoc analyses to justify "violations of the rules" (p. 27). Tymoczko triangulates between schema theory, Schenkerian analysis, and Rameauian harmonic theory, aiming to describe "general principles governing the interdependence of harmony and counterpoint" (p. 33).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 27, Figure 1.5.1): Bach's cadential formula where V2 bass steps down to tonic without the seventh resolving -- an idiom unexplained by general rules.

**Example 2** (p. 29, Figure 1.5.3): Gjerdingen's "Romanesca" in sequential and nonsequential variants, with the galant preference for I6 over iii.

**Example 3** (pp. 31-32): The "deep Prinner" (general voice-leading schema used from Renaissance to present) vs. the "shallow Prinner" (specific galant mannerism).

**Example 4** (pp. 31-32, Figures 1.5.6-1.5.8): Contrary-motion schema alternating thirds and sixths, found in Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel, Meredith Monk, and film scores.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using schema theory to replace all general principles with specific patterns
  **Correction**: Without general principles, the schematic approach devolves into "mindless stitching-together of precomposed chunks" (p. 32)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Equating schemas with codified figure-skating moves that listeners track consciously
  **Clarification**: Music perception is more complex; composers navigate musical spaces without being able to say what they are doing, and listeners understand without describing it (p. 17)

- **Confusion**: Thinking all schemas are arbitrary conventions
  **Clarification**: Many schemas originate in constraints inherent to musical materials -- "responses to the contours of musical space -- a rock climber taking advantage of a particularly prominent outcropping" (p. 31)
