# Quick Definition
An approach to music theory proposing that composers recombine atomic patterns (schemas) rather than following general laws, emphasizing the idiomatic and particular nature of musical practice.

# Formal Definition
Schema theory, deriving from Leonard Meyer by way of Robert Gjerdingen, proposes a "construction grammar" in which musical behavior is not lawlike at its deepest level but rather an "unruly and messy enterprise whose basic objects are specific gestures, often tied to particular formal contexts." Gjerdingen favors a return to pre-Rameauian conceptions of musical structure rooted in the particular injunctions of seventeenth- and eighteenth-century pedagogy, including solfege and unfigured-bass traditions.

# Musical Context
Schema theory provides an important corrective to speculative overreach, emphasizing that musical style is idiomatic at its core. It shares with Schenkerian theory a focus on outer-voice contrapuntal patterns, privileging line and dyad over triad and seventh chord. Tymoczko argues that schema theory is best understood as *augmenting* rather than *replacing* the generalizations of familiar theory -- composers learn both specific idioms and more general systematic principles.

# Examples
- Bach's cadential formula where the V^2 bass steps down to tonic without the seventh resolving (Figure 1.5.1) -- an idiom not captured by general rules
- Gjerdingen's "Prinner" schema: a precadential "riposte" in galant music, a cadence in the Renaissance
- Gjerdingen's "Romanesca" schema in sequential and nonsequential variants (Figure 1.5.3)
- The contrary-motion schema alternating thirds and sixths: found in Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel, Meredith Monk, and film scores (Figures 1.5.6-1.5.8)

# Common Confusions
- Schema theory should not replace generalization entirely -- many schemas originate in constraints inherent to musical materials (the "rock face" of contrapuntal geometry)
- Without general principles, the schematic approach can devolve into "mindless stitching-together of precomposed chunks"
- The "deep" version of a schema (general voice-leading pattern) should be distinguished from the "shallow" version (specific stylistic mannerism)
