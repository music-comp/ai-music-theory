# Quick Definition
A model of functional harmony that arranges diatonic triads in a chain of descending thirds (I--vi--IV--ii--vii^o^--V), where chords move freely rightward but leftward only along specified arrows.

# Formal Definition
The descending-thirds arrangement organizes functional harmony around the observation that "strong" root progressions (descending third, descending fifth, ascending step) move rightward along the chain I--vi--IV--ii--vii^o^--V by one, two, or three positions respectively, while "weak" counterparts move leftward. The model places inversionally equivalent chords vertically (e.g., IV and ii^6^ share the same position), encoding functional categories by horizontal position. It is consistent with figured-bass theory: bass lines move along an abstract chain of descending thirds from ^3 to ^5, with 5/3 preceding 6/3 over the same bass note (Figure 7.1.4). At the most fundamental level, harmonic cycles generate complete chains of diatonic pitch classes descending by third through two abstract octaves (Figure 7.2.4).

# Musical Context
This arrangement is Tymoczko's central alternative to traditional "circle of fifths" models. It provides a unified framework that incorporates Riemannian functional categories (encoded by horizontal position), Rameauian root-motion preferences (encoded by directional constraints), and figured-bass practice (encoded by bass-degree organization). The model can be learned by example without explicit instruction, using only eleven chords.

# Common Confusions
- This is not simply a relabeling of the circle of fifths: while closely related, the descending-thirds model is more fundamental. Rameau's descending-fifth paradigm can be derived from the descending-third chain by treating triads as incomplete seventh chords (Figure 7.3.3--7.3.4).
- The arrangement does not mean that descending thirds are the most common root progression -- descending fifths are. Rather, the chain of thirds is the abstract structure underlying multiple types of root motion.
