# Quick Definition

A model of functional harmony that arranges diatonic triads in a chain of descending thirds (I--vi--IV--ii--vii^o^--V), where chords move freely rightward but leftward only along specified arrows.

# Core Definition

The descending-thirds arrangement organizes functional harmony by placing diatonic triads along a chain of descending thirds: I--vi--IV--ii--vii^o^--V. "Chords are completely free to move rightward and vertically, but leftward motion must proceed along one of the arrows" (p. 312, Figure 7.1.3). Inversionally equivalent chords are placed above one another (e.g., IV and ii^6^), encoding functional categories by horizontal position. The "strong" progressions (descending third, descending fifth, ascending step) move rightward by one, two, or three steps respectively, while their "weak" counterparts move leftward (p. 313).

At a deeper level, harmonic cycles generate complete chains of diatonic pitch classes descending by third through two abstract octaves (Figure 7.2.4). This pitch-class version shows that "mature functionality simply *is* the descending sequence of thirds---sometimes manifest on the musical surface and sometimes locked away in the musical subconscious" (p. 320).

# Prerequisites

- **Protofunctionality** -- The simple I--IV--V--I cycles provide the foundation that the descending-thirds model elaborates

# Key Properties

1. Triads are arranged I--vi--IV--ii--vii^o^--V in a chain of descending thirds
2. Rightward and vertical motion is unrestricted; leftward motion follows specified arrows
3. Inversionally equivalent chords share the same horizontal position (e.g., IV and ii^6^)
4. Strong progressions (descending third, descending fifth, ascending step) move rightward by 1, 2, or 3 positions
5. The model is consistent with figured-bass theory: bass lines move along descending thirds from 3-hat to 5-hat, with 5/3 preceding 6/3 over the same bass (Figure 7.1.4)
6. Can be reformulated at the pitch-class level, where harmonic cycles generate complete chains of descending diatonic thirds (Figure 7.2.4)
7. Contains only eleven chords and can be learned by example without explicit instruction

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Progressions on the Model:
1. Assign each chord its position on the chain I--vi--IV--ii--vii^o^--V
2. Check vertical equivalences: IV and ii^6^ share position; vii^o^ and V share position
3. Rightward moves are always permitted (toward dominant)
4. Leftward moves are permitted only along the arrows (primarily V/vii^o^ returning to I)
5. The root-functional version (Figure 7.1.5) allows approximate analysis using just root names, though some arrows require specific inversions

# Context & Application

The model incorporates a variety of earlier approaches: Riemannian functional categories (encoded by horizontal position), Rameauian root-motion preferences (directional constraints), and figured-bass practice (bass-degree organization). Where protofunctionality was "decidedly Riemannian, privileging primary triads as *locations*," this model is "more Rameauian, highlighting a specific *way of moving* from tonic to dominant" (p. 313).

The model can also be approximately reformulated using roots alone (Figure 7.1.5), though some progressions require specific inversions.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating the model as a circle of fifths in disguise
  **Correction**: While closely related, the descending-thirds chain is more fundamental; Rameau's descending-fifth paradigm can be derived from it by treating triads as incomplete seventh chords (Figures 7.3.3--7.3.4)

- **Error**: Assuming ascending thirds are always forbidden
  **Correction**: Ascending thirds are permissible when they support 5/3 to 6/3 progressions (root position to first inversion), as in the vertical dimension of the model

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking descending thirds are the most common root progression
  **Clarification**: Descending fifths are most common. The descending-third chain is the *abstract structure* underlying multiple types of root motion, not itself the dominant surface pattern.

- **Confusion**: Believing the model is a set of inviolable laws
  **Clarification**: It describes first-order defaults; a penumbra of second-order idioms provides additional options that evolve historically
