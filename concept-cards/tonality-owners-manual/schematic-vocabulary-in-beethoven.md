# Quick Definition

Beethoven's personal compositional vocabulary consisting of sequences, schemas, and contrary-motion patterns -- particularly the Ludwig, fauxbourdon, horn fifths, and ascending-step sequences -- deployed across pieces as recurring building blocks rather than unique melodic inventions.

# Core Definition

Tymoczko argues that Beethoven built "a personal vocabulary from ready-to-hand material -- drawing a life's worth of music out of theoretical relationships that other composers might dismiss as trivial" (p. 458). This vocabulary includes the Ludwig in all its variants, fauxbourdon patterns (descending-third, ROTO), ascending-step and ascending-fifth sequences, horn-fifths patterns, the Quiescenza, and various contrary-motion devices. What makes this a vocabulary rather than a collection of tricks is "its systematic recurrence across pieces and its role as the primary structural material rather than decorative surface" (synthesized from analysis). The "features that recur from piece to piece" matter more than "the motives distinguishing one piece from another" (p. 458). This vocabulary is "compatible with a wide range of expressive moods" -- from the violence of the Tempest to the placidity of the Pastorale.

# Prerequisites

- **The Ludwig** -- The central schema in Beethoven's vocabulary
- **Fauxbourdon** -- The parallel-motion counterpart to the Ludwig's contrary motion

# Key Properties

1. The vocabulary is not arbitrary but reflects the fundamental geometry of contrapuntal possibility
2. It functions as primary structural material, not surface decoration
3. Cross-piece recurrence distinguishes vocabulary from within-piece motivic unity
4. Virtually every passage in analyzed movements is schematic, sequential, or both
5. "Beethoven's incessant Ludwiging may represent a kind of musical understanding" (p. 472)
6. Compatible with diverse expressive moods and formal designs

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Beethoven's Schematic Vocabulary:
1. Look for Ludwig patterns (contrary motion through thirds and sixths)
2. Identify fauxbourdon passages (parallel first-inversion triads)
3. Check for ascending-step and ascending-fifth sequences
4. Note horn-fifths patterns and Quiescenza variants
5. Track which schemas recur across movements and pieces

# Context & Application

The three detailed analyses in Chapter 10 (Tempest, Fifth Symphony, Pastorale) demonstrate that virtually every passage is schematic, sequential, or both. The Tempest exposition consists of six sequential processes. The Pastorale's six expositional phrases are each based on fauxbourdon or contrary-motion schemas. This vocabulary encodes "implicit theoretical knowledge" (p. 472) about the geometry of functional counterpoint.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Confusing cross-piece vocabulary with within-piece motivic unity
  **Correction**: This is about patterns shared across pieces, not motives distinguishing one piece from another

- **Error**: Thinking the vocabulary is merely a set of surface ornaments
  **Correction**: It constitutes the primary structural material of Beethoven's compositions

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking Beethoven's schematic vocabulary makes his music formulaic
  **Clarification**: The vocabulary is a toolkit that can produce a vast range of expressive and formal outcomes

- **Confusion**: Assuming sequence-based reduction replaces harmonic analysis
  **Clarification**: Sequence-based reduction supplements it, providing "a sense of ownership that I do not get from Schenkerian analysis" (p. 480)
