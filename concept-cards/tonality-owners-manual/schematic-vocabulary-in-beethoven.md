# Quick Definition
Beethoven's personal compositional vocabulary consisting of sequences, schemas, and contrary-motion patterns -- particularly the Ludwig, fauxbourdon, horn fifths, and ascending-step sequences -- which he deployed across pieces as recurring building blocks rather than unique melodic inventions.

# Formal Definition
Tymoczko argues that Beethoven built "a personal vocabulary from ready-to-hand material -- drawing a life's worth of music out of theoretical relationships that other composers might dismiss as trivial" (p. 458). This vocabulary includes: the Ludwig in all its variants; fauxbourdon patterns (descending-third, ROTO); ascending-step and ascending-fifth sequences; horn-fifths patterns; the Quiescenza; and various contrary-motion devices. What makes this a vocabulary rather than a collection of tricks is its systematic recurrence across pieces and its role as the primary structural material rather than decorative surface. The "features that recur from piece to piece" matter more than "the motives distinguishing one piece from another" (p. 458).

# Musical Context
The three detailed analyses (Tempest, Fifth Symphony, Pastorale) demonstrate that virtually every passage in these movements is schematic, sequential, or both. The Pastorale's six expositional phrases are each sequential or schematic, with no extended passage of nonsequential, nonschematic, melodic music in the entire movement. This vocabulary is "compatible with a wide range of expressive moods" -- from the violence of the Tempest to the placidity of the Pastorale.

# Examples
- Tempest: six sequential processes constituting the entire exposition (Figure 10.3.9, pp. 479-480)
- Fifth Symphony: covert sequences throughout exposition, grand contrary-motion structure in development (Section 10.4)
- Pastorale: six phrases each based on fauxbourdon or contrary-motion schemas (Section 10.5)
- The Ludwig appearing across pieces from Op. 2 through Op. 130 (Sections 10.1-10.2)

# Common Confusions
- This is not a claim about motivic unity within pieces but about vocabulary shared across pieces
- The vocabulary is not arbitrary but reflects the fundamental geometry of contrapuntal possibility
- "Beethoven's incessant Ludwiging may represent a kind of musical understanding" -- the vocabulary encodes implicit theoretical knowledge (p. 472)
