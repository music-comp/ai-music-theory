# Quick Definition
Tymoczko's acronym for the four basic types of nonharmonic tones in the first practice: Suspensions, Neighbors, Anticipations, and Passing tones.

# Formal Definition
The SNAP system is Tymoczko's framework for categorizing the nonharmonic tones of the prima prattica. These four categories -- Suspensions, Neighbors, Anticipations, and Passing tones -- describe virtually all the nonharmonic tones found in Palestrina's music. The system stabilized in the music of Palestrina and contemporaries such as Lassus and Byrd, though it is often said to originate with Josquin. Earlier polyphony features additional types (incomplete neighbors, upward-resolving suspensions) that the Palestrina style largely eliminates, retaining only the cambiata as a surviving incomplete neighbor.

# Musical Context
The SNAP system represents the first great era of nonharmonic control. Palestrina's music is astonishing in its systematicity, almost completely free of the occasional glitches found in most human productions. This music is highly susceptible to computational analysis: it takes just a few hours of programming to produce a reasonably accurate survey of nonharmonic usage, with virtually all nonharmonic tones labelable and removable algorithmically.

# Common Confusions
- SNAP is Tymoczko's own acronym for organizing the traditional categories -- it is not a term found in older theory textbooks.
- While the SNAP system accounts for virtually all of Palestrina's nonharmonic tones, it does not capture every nuance: decorated anticipations, Renaissance sevenths, and certain 64 chords resist clean classification within the system.
- Anticipations in Palestrina almost always occur in the context of suspensions, suggesting a "suspension/anticipation system" rather than anticipations as a freestanding class.
