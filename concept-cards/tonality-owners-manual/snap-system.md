# Quick Definition

Tymoczko's acronym for the four fundamental categories of Renaissance nonharmonic tone: Suspensions, Neighbors, Anticipations, and Passing tones. These constitute the first practice (prima prattica) of nonharmonic control.

# Core Definition

The SNAP system is Tymoczko's framework for categorizing the nonharmonic tones of the prima prattica. These four categories -- Suspensions, Neighbors, Anticipations, and Passing tones -- describe virtually all the nonharmonic tones found in Palestrina's music. The system stabilized in the music of Palestrina and contemporaries such as Lassus and Byrd, though it is often said to originate with Josquin (p. 211). Earlier polyphony features additional types (incomplete neighbors, upward-resolving suspensions) that the Palestrina style largely eliminates, retaining only the cambiata as a surviving incomplete neighbor (p. 212).

# Prerequisites

- **Nonharmonic tone** — Understanding the general concept of notes outside the prevailing harmony is needed to classify them into species

# Key Properties

1. Four categories: Suspensions, Neighbors, Anticipations, Passing tones
2. Stabilized in Palestrina's practice (late sixteenth century)
3. The Palestrina style is "astonishing in its systematicity, almost completely free of the occasional glitches that mark most human productions" (p. 212)
4. Anticipations are the rarest species in Palestrina, comprising less than 1% of dissonances as freestanding events
5. Suspensions are unique in regularly licensing parallel fifths at the harmonic skeleton level
6. The cambiata is the sole surviving incomplete neighbor

# Construction / Recognition

## To Classify a Nonharmonic Tone in the SNAP System:
1. **Suspension**: Prepared (held over from previous chord), dissonant, resolves down by step
2. **Neighbor**: Departs from and returns to the same chord tone by step
3. **Anticipation**: Arrives at the next chord tone before the harmony changes
4. **Passing tone**: Connects two chord tones by stepwise motion in the same direction

# Context & Application

The SNAP system represents the first great era of nonharmonic control. Palestrina's music is highly susceptible to computational analysis because it contains relatively few changes of tonal center and little need for concepts such as nonharmonic consonance. Virtually all nonharmonic tones can be labeled and removed algorithmically, leaving a triadic skeleton. This is "perhaps the closest that Western music has ever come to the theorist's dream of purely consonant harmony decorated by clearly nonharmonic embellishment" (p. 213). SNAP is Tymoczko's own acronym -- it is not a term found in older theory textbooks.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 212): Palestrina's music almost completely conforms to SNAP categories, with the sole exception being the cambiata (an inherited incomplete neighbor).

**Example 2** (p. 214): Anticipations in Palestrina almost always occur in the context of suspensions, suggesting a "suspension/anticipation system" rather than anticipations as a freestanding class.

**Example 3** (p. 212, Fig. 5.1.1): Unusual nonharmonic tones in pre-Palestrina music -- Josquin's "Liber generationis" features both an incomplete neighbor and upward-resolving suspension.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating all four SNAP categories as equally "decorative"
  **Correction**: Suspensions are fundamentally different from the other three, having genuine harmonic significance and cadential function

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the SNAP system originated with Palestrina
  **Clarification**: The system is often said to originate with Josquin, but it stabilized somewhat later in Palestrina's generation (p. 211)

- **Confusion**: Believing the SNAP system perfectly describes all Renaissance dissonance
  **Clarification**: Even in Palestrina, there are idioms that resist classification -- Renaissance sevenths, 6/4 chords as harmonies, and 121 passing/neighboring tones that cut across beat divisions
