# Quick Definition
Contrary motion in which voices move by almost the same amount in opposite directions, produced by combining a basic voice leading with a voice exchange. Fifth-related thirds produce the most balanced antiparallel motion, with voices moving by a fourth and a fifth.

# Formal Definition
Antiparallel motion results from combining a single basic voice leading with a single voice exchange. For example, the basic voice leading (C4, E5) -> (A3, F5) combined with the two-step voice exchange (A3, F5) -> (F3, A5) produces (C4, E5) -> (F3, A5), with the root falling by fifth and the third ascending by fourth. The same basic voice leading combined with the five-step exchange produces (C4, E5) -> (F4, A4), root ascending by fourth and third descending by fifth. These are "contrapuntally balanced" -- moving voices by approximately equal distances (a fourth and a fifth). Moving further along the circle produces increasingly unbalanced motion.

# Musical Context
Antiparallel motion is fundamental to the *Pathetique*'s canonic sequence, where one voice ascends by fourth and the other descends by fifth, switching hands after every dyad. The diatonic fifth is contrapuntally privileged for antiparallel motion because it is the closest diatonic approximation to the half-octave, allowing the most balanced contrary motion. This explains why fifth-relations appear in diatonic contrary-motion passages from Monteverdi to Meredith Monk.

# Common Confusions
- Antiparallel is not the same as contrary motion generally; it specifically means nearly balanced contrary motion, a fourth against a fifth
- The "balance" of antiparallel motion is a geometrical fact about the diatonic scale, not a stylistic convention
