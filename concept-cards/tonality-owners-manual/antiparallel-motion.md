# Quick Definition

Contrary motion in which voices move by almost the same amount in opposite directions, produced by combining a basic voice leading with a voice exchange. Fifth-related thirds produce the most balanced antiparallel motion, with voices moving by a fourth and a fifth.

# Core Definition

Antiparallel motion results from combining a single basic voice leading with a single voice exchange. For example, the basic voice leading (C4, E5) -> (A3, F5) combined with the two-step voice exchange (A3, F5) -> (F3, A5) produces (C4, E5) -> (F3, A5), with the root falling by fifth and the third ascending by fourth. These voice leadings are "contrapuntally balanced in the sense that they move their voices by approximately the same distance -- a fourth and a fifth" (p. 104). Moving further along the circle produces "more and more unbalanced motion" (p. 104). The diatonic fifth is privileged because "fourth-related thirds, as geometrical neighbors, allow for antiparallel motion in which the voices are almost exactly balanced, and hence minimize the largest distance moved by either voice; other root motions produce less-balanced counterpoint, with larger leaps in one of the voices" (p. 104).

# Prerequisites

- **Basic Voice Leading** — Antiparallel motion combines the basic voice leading with a voice exchange
- **Voice Exchange** — The rearranging component of the antiparallel motion

# Key Properties

1. Produced by combining a basic voice leading with a voice exchange
2. Voices move in contrary motion by almost equal amounts (fourth and fifth)
3. Fifth-related intervals produce the most balanced motion
4. As root motion departs from the fifth, motion becomes increasingly unbalanced
5. The balance is a geometrical fact about the diatonic scale, not a stylistic convention

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct:
1. Start with a dyad (e.g., C-E third)
2. Apply the basic voice leading: (C, E) -> (A, F)
3. Apply a voice exchange to the result: (A, F) -> (F, A) [two-step] or (A, F) -> (F, A) [five-step]
4. The combined result moves voices in opposite directions by nearly equal intervals

## To Recognize:
1. Look for two voices moving in contrary motion by a fourth and a fifth
2. The intervals should switch hands from one dyad to the next
3. The harmonic motion should be by fifth

# Context & Application

Antiparallel motion is fundamental to the Pathetique's canonic sequence (Prelude to Ch. 4), where one voice ascends by fourth and the other descends by fifth, with the motions switching hands after every dyad. This explains "why we find fifth-relations in diatonic contrary-motion passages from Monteverdi to Meredith Monk" (p. 104). Beethoven's Op. 26 finale systematically deploys antiparallel motion alongside the basic voice leading and voice exchanges (Figure 3.2.4, p. 106).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming antiparallel motion requires exactly equal intervals
  **Correction**: The intervals are approximately equal (fourth and fifth), not exactly equal; exact equality would require an even division of the octave

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Equating antiparallel with contrary motion generally
  **Clarification**: Antiparallel specifically means nearly balanced contrary motion (fourth against fifth); unbalanced contrary motion is not antiparallel

- **Confusion**: Thinking the balance is a stylistic preference
  **Clarification**: The balance is a geometrical fact about the diatonic scale's structure
