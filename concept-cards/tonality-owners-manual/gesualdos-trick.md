# Quick Definition
A contrapuntal technique in which two voices move in parallel while a third alternates between prime and inverted forms of a sonority, producing consistent chord types with smooth voice leading.

# Formal Definition
Gesualdo's trick involves two voices moving in parallel (by step, in a given scale) while a remaining voice alternates position to create chord forms related by pitch-class inversion. In the original version, outer-voice tenths move downward by semitone, forming triads by alternately adding a minor third above and below this dyadic core. Generalized via the Prime Directive, the technique applies to any interval of parallel motion and any chordal/scalar environment, always producing close-position sonorities without voice crossings.

# Musical Context
The technique appears throughout the Western tradition, from the Renaissance to the present day. It connects chromatic triadic sequences to diatonic patterns like the Pachelbel sequence (Romanesca). It is fundamentally a *dyadic* logic -- analyzing triadic music through the lens of parallel voice pairs. The technique is a straightforward consequence of efficient voice leading and attention to chord voicing as measured in chordal steps.

# Common Confusions
- The technique is not unique to Gesualdo -- it is named after him but found throughout Western music
- It should not be analyzed as two separate harmonic moves (e.g., "hexatonic poles" + "standard iv^6-V") but as a unified contrapuntal pattern
- The diatonic version (Pachelbel sequence) is the same technique as the chromatic version, just measured in scale steps rather than semitones
