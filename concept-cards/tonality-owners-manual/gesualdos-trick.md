# Quick Definition

A contrapuntal technique in which two voices move in parallel while a third alternates between prime and inverted forms of a sonority, producing consistent chord types with smooth voice leading across a wide range of styles.

# Core Definition

Gesualdo's trick involves two voices moving in parallel (stepwise in a given scale) while a remaining voice alternates position to create chord forms related by pitch-class inversion. In the original version from "Moro Lasso" (1611), outer-voice tenths move downward by semitone, "forming triads by alternately adding a minor third above and below this dyadic core" (p. 4). Generalized via the Prime Directive, the technique applies to any interval of parallel motion and any chordal/scalar environment, always producing close-position sonorities without voice crossings. The technique is "a straightforward consequence of a concern for efficient voice leading, or attention to chord voicing as measured in chordal steps" (Prelude, p. 41).

# Key Properties

1. Two voices move in parallel (by step in the relevant scale)
2. A third voice alternates between two positions to produce inversionally related chord forms
3. All sonorities are in close position without voice crossings
4. The technique works in both chromatic and diatonic space
5. In chromatic space, it produces alternating major/minor triads; in diatonic space, the Pachelbel sequence
6. The parallel voices form the dyadic core that controls the contrapuntal logic

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct:
1. Choose two voices and move them in parallel by step (in any scale)
2. Add a third voice that alternates between being a fixed interval above and below the parallel pair
3. The result will produce inversionally related chord forms with smooth voice leading

## To Recognize:
1. Look for two voices moving in consistent parallel motion
2. Check if a third voice alternates between two positions relative to the parallel pair
3. Verify that the resulting chords are related by pitch-class inversion

# Context & Application

The technique appears throughout the Western tradition from Renaissance to contemporary music. It exemplifies Tymoczko's analytical approach: (1) composer-centered perspective using modern tools; (2) fluid understanding of stylistic boundaries; (3) identification of specific techniques spanning music history; (4) analyzing triadic music using dyadic logic; (5) an aesthetic attitude finding beauty in cross-historical connections (pp. 8-9).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 4, Figure 1.1.1): Gesualdo's "Moro Lasso" (1611) -- C# major to A minor to B major to G major, outer voices in parallel tenths descending by semitone.

**Example 2** (p. 5, Figure 1.1.2): Beethoven's Seventh Symphony, IV, mm. 136-146; Schubert's "Morgengruss," mm. 12-15 (four-voice version); Debussy's *Prelude to the Afternoon of a Faun*, m. 107.

**Example 3** (p. 6, Figure 1.1.3): Mozart K.309, I, mm. 73-76 -- parallel minor thirds instead of major.

**Example 4** (p. 7, Figure 1.1.4): Stravinsky's *Rite of Spring* at R114 -- parallel major sevenths with tritone alternation.

**Example 5** (p. 7, Figure 1.1.5): Rudresh Mahanthappa's "The Decider" (2006) -- linearized version with parallel perfect fourths.

**Example 6** (p. 7, Figure 1.1.6): Pachelbel sequence -- diatonic version with parallel thirds and alternating fifths/sixths.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Analyzing Gesualdo's passage as two separate harmonic moves (e.g., "hexatonic poles" + "standard iv6-V")
  **Correction**: It should be analyzed as a unified sequential pattern with consistent contrapuntal logic

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the technique is unique to Gesualdo or to chromatic music
  **Clarification**: It is named after Gesualdo but found throughout Western music; the diatonic version is the Pachelbel sequence (Romanesca)

- **Confusion**: Assuming the technique is "arcane contrapuntal wizardry"
  **Clarification**: It is a straightforward consequence of efficient voice leading and attention to chordal-step voicing
