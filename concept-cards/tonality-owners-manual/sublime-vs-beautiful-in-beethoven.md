# Quick Definition

The eighteenth-century aesthetic opposition between beauty (pleasant, well-formed, moderate) and sublimity (overwhelming, awesome, extreme), which illuminates Beethoven's distinctive formal innovations as provocations embracing excess rather than "inevitable" expressions of tasteful form.

# Core Definition

Eighteenth-century philosophers associated beauty with the decorous: "elegant major-key melodies stated at a moderate tempo and dynamic" (p. 452). The sublime was aesthetic pleasure associated with "the overwhelming, awesome, or incomprehensible: storms and mountain ranges, the ruins of a great city" (p. 452). Beethoven was recognized during his lifetime as "the great composer of the sublime -- wild and almost out-of-control, exploiting unprecedented contrasts to create extremes of emotional response" (p. 452-453). His formal innovations are "precisely not a matter of 'inevitability'" but rather "provocations, a willingness to tolerate excesses other composers avoided -- and in some cases, to embrace an obviousness that might seem naive" (p. 453).

# Key Properties

1. Beauty: pleasant, well-formed, moderate, decorous
2. Sublimity: overwhelming, awesome, extreme, out-of-control
3. Beethoven's rhythmic variation is more extreme than Mozart's, passing through a wider range of states
4. Beethoven's cycles of change are slower, with rhythm demarcating formal regions
5. Registral position oscillates dramatically rather than staying in a narrow range
6. Kant distinguished external sublimity (overwhelming force) from internal sublimity (human drive to transcend finitude)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Recognize the Sublime in Beethoven:
1. Note extreme contrasts in dynamics, register, rhythm, and texture
2. Look for parameters serving form-defining rather than merely expressive roles
3. Identify the slower cycles of change that demarcate formal regions
4. Listen for the tension between convention and disruption (e.g., elided medial caesura, omitted cadences)
5. Observe the deliberate simplicity of thematic material against the complexity of formal trajectory

# Context & Application

This framework reinterprets Bernstein's question about Beethoven's preeminence. Where Bernstein attributed it to "form" understood as inevitability, Tymoczko argues Beethoven's innovations are "closer to provocations" -- loosening formal schemas rather than perfecting them. This creates a conflict between "Beethoven's nascent Romanticism, his compositional self-reliance, and recent approaches to musical form" (p. 455). The Tempest's upper-voice pedal (m. 189) exemplifies Kantian sublimity: the piano's edge annihilates the music's physical aspect, pointing beyond music itself.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Equating sublimity with mere loudness or length
  **Correction**: Kant distinguished external sublimity (overwhelming force) from internal sublimity (the drive to transcend finitude)

- **Error**: Interpreting Beethoven's formal innovations as perfecting classical convention
  **Correction**: They are provocations that deliberately loosen convention

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking Beethoven aimed for "inevitability" in his formal choices
  **Clarification**: His innovations are closer to provocations than to tasteful inevitability; "paradoxically he is simultaneously eccentric and archetypal" (p. 454)

- **Confusion**: Assuming the beauty/sublimity distinction is merely historical
  **Clarification**: It provides genuine analytical insight into how Beethoven's music differs from Mozart's in its use of musical parameters
