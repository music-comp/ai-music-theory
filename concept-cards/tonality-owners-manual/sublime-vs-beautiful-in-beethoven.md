# Quick Definition
The eighteenth-century aesthetic opposition between beauty (pleasant, well-formed, moderate) and sublimity (overwhelming, awesome, extreme), which illuminates Beethoven's distinctive formal innovations as provocations embracing excess rather than "inevitable" expressions of tasteful form.

# Formal Definition
Eighteenth-century philosophers associated beauty with the decorous: elegant major-key melodies at moderate tempo and dynamic (Burke 1757). The sublime was aesthetic pleasure associated with the overwhelming or incomprehensible: storms, mountain ranges, distant galaxies. Beethoven was recognized during his lifetime as the great composer of the sublime -- wild, almost out-of-control, exploiting unprecedented contrasts. His formal innovations are "precisely not a matter of inevitability" but rather "provocations, a willingness to tolerate excesses other composers avoided" (p. 453).

# Musical Context
This framework reinterprets Beethoven's relationship to classical convention. Rather than perfecting sonata form through "inevitable" choices (as Bernstein argued), Beethoven loosened formal schemas, embracing obviousness that might seem naive while subordinating local melodic interest to long-term formal processes. His anti-melodic impulse and primary-triad harmony are inseparable from his focus on large-scale formal trajectories.

# Common Confusions
- Sublimity in Beethoven is not merely about loudness or length -- Kant distinguished external sublimity (overwhelming force) from internal sublimity (the human drive to transcend finitude)
- Beethoven's simplistic materials are features, not bugs -- they deliberately subordinate local interest to long-term narrative
- An atonal recomposition of the Waldstein preserves "a remarkable amount of its formal clarity" (p. 453), demonstrating the primacy of gesture over pitch detail
