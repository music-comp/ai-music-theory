# Quick Definition

Four-voice sequences can divide voices as two pairs, a triad plus independent voice (3 + 1), or a single four-voice canonic unit, each producing different sequential structures whose complexity exemplifies the "subterranean mathematics of Western music" (p. 183).

# Core Definition

Four voices can organize as: (1) two superimposed pairs each on their own spiral diagram; (2) three upper voices forming a canonic group with bass in contrary motion (3 + 1); (3) all four in a single canonic cycle (p. 179). Paired voices can move in contrary motion within a four-note chord, reproducing the complete collection at every step -- the "thinking within the chord" technique (p. 180). The "omnibus" has three upper voices (diminished triad) ascending canonically while the root-sounding bass descends (Figure 4.6.4, p. 181). The four-voice Chase schema takes two-step counterclockwise motion on the space of triads with doubled third (Figure 4.6.7, p. 182). These passages "exemplify what might be called the subterranean mathematics of Western music, a mathematics that becomes increasingly complex as we add voices" (p. 183).

# Key Properties

1. Three organizational types: 2+2, 3+1, or 4-voice unit
2. Paired contrary motion can reproduce complete collection at every step
3. The omnibus: three upper (diminished triad) + one bass
4. Four-voice Chase: two-step CCW on triads-with-doubled-third space
5. Complexity increases dramatically with voice count
6. Composers likely discovered patterns piecemeal without theoretical framework

# Construction / Recognition

## To Analyze:
1. Identify the grouping: 2+2, 3+1, or single unit
2. For pairs: check if moving in contrary motion within a four-note chord
3. For 3+1: identify the canonic group and independent voice
4. For single unit: track all four arrows (may be bewilderingly complex)

# Context & Application

Wagner's *Tristan* prelude mm. 43-44 has pairs moving in contrary motion within a four-note chord (Figure 4.6.1). Haydn's E minor sonata has a nearly inaudible 3+1 canon (Figure 4.6.5). Bach's "Was mein Gott will" contains a marvelously disguised four-voice Chase (Figure 4.6.8). Earlier composers were "proud of their sequential discoveries, rightly considering them novel solutions to ubiquitous compositional challenges" (p. 183).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating four-voice sequences as just doubled two-voice patterns
  **Correction**: Additional voices introduce qualitatively new organizational possibilities

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Expecting four-voice canons to be audibly canonic
  **Clarification**: The analytical situation can be "bewilderingly complex" and the canon may be completely inaudible
