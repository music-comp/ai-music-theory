# Quick Definition
Four-voice sequences can divide voices as two pairs, a triad plus independent voice, or a single four-voice unit, each producing different sequential structures. Complexity increases dramatically with four voices, exemplifying the "subterranean mathematics of Western music."

# Formal Definition
Four-voice sequences can be organized as: (1) two superimposed pairs each on their own spiral diagram; (2) three voices (often upper) forming a canonic group with bass in contrary motion (3 + 1); (3) all four voices in a single canonic cycle. Pairs can move in contrary motion within a four-note chord (reproducing the complete collection at every step), extended to triadic contexts by doubling a note. The "omnibus" sequence has three upper voices ascending canonically against a bass descending by minor third. The four-voice Chase schema uses a two-step counterclockwise path on the space of triads with doubled third.

# Common Confusions
- Four-voice sequences are not just "two-voice sequences with doublings"; the additional voices introduce qualitatively new organizational possibilities
- The analytical situation can become "bewilderingly complex" with four voices in a single canonic cycle; the same passage may permit multiple valid descriptions
- Earlier composers likely discovered these patterns piecemeal, without an overarching theoretical framework
