# Quick Definition
The sharpward application of the diatonic scale's basic voice leading, raising the fourth degree (^4) by semitone to produce a scale one step counterclockwise on the circle of keys.

# Formal Definition
Fourth-scale-degree raising is the sharpward (counterclockwise) direction of the diatonic scale's basic voice leading. It moves a diatonic scale to its sharp-side neighbor by raising a single pitch class -- the fourth degree -- by one chromatic semitone. For example, raising F to F-sharp transforms C major into G major. This corresponds to adding one sharp (or subtracting one flat). On the scalar spiral diagram, each counterclockwise step adds a sharp. A series of seven such steps maps C major's tonic to C-sharp major's tonic (Figure 8.1.7b).

# Musical Context
Fourth-scale-degree raising is the mechanism behind the most common classical modulation: from tonic to dominant. It can be heard as "the fourth scale degree has been raised by semitone to become the leading tone of the new scale" (cf. Figure 1.2.5). This is the standard way to understand tonic-to-dominant modulation at the level of pitch classes.

# Examples
- C major to G major: Raise F to F-sharp, keeping C, D, E, G, A, B fixed.
- Figure 8.1.7b: Seven counterclockwise steps from C major to C-sharp major, mapping tonic to tonic.
- Modal homogenization (Figure 8.6.1): The scale-degree-preserving model of mixolydian-to-ionian transformation raises the seventh degree, which is equivalent to fourth-degree raising applied to the key a fourth below.

# Common Confusions
- While it is natural to say "the fourth degree has been raised to become the leading tone," changes in scale degree do not imply determinate trajectories: pitch classes simply take on new roles without occupying intermediate points.
- Fourth-degree raising and leading-tone lowering are inverses of each other, producing opposite motion on the scalar spiral.
