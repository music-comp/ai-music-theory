# Quick Definition

The sharpward application of the diatonic scale's basic voice leading, raising the fourth degree by semitone to produce a scale one step counterclockwise on the circle of keys -- the mechanism behind the most common classical modulation from tonic to dominant.

# Core Definition

Fourth-scale-degree raising is the sharpward (counterclockwise) direction of the diatonic scale's basic voice leading. It moves a diatonic scale to its sharp-side neighbor by raising a single pitch class -- the fourth degree -- by one chromatic semitone. For example, raising F to F-sharp transforms C major into G major. This corresponds to adding one sharp (or subtracting one flat). On the scalar spiral diagram, each counterclockwise step adds a sharp. A series of seven such steps maps C major's tonic to C-sharp major's tonic (p. 364, Fig. 8.1.7b). It can be heard as "the fourth scale degree has been raised by semitone to become the leading tone of the new scale" (cf. Fig. 1.2.5).

# Key Properties

1. Raises the fourth degree (scale-degree 4) by one chromatic semitone
2. All other pitch classes remain fixed
3. Corresponds to adding one sharp or subtracting one flat
4. Counterclockwise motion on the scalar spiral diagram
5. Seven successive applications map C major tonic to C-sharp major tonic
6. The mechanism behind tonic-to-dominant modulation in classical music

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply Fourth-Scale-Degree Raising:
1. Identify the fourth scale degree of the current key
2. Raise it by one chromatic semitone
3. All other notes remain fixed
4. The raised note becomes the leading tone of the new key
5. The new tonic is a fifth above the old tonic

# Context & Application

Fourth-scale-degree raising is the mechanism behind the most common classical modulation: from tonic to dominant. It also relates to modal homogenization, where the scale-degree-preserving model of mixolydian-to-ionian transformation raises the seventh degree, which is equivalent to fourth-degree raising applied to the key a fourth below (discussed in Ch. 8, section 6). The concept is the inverse of leading-tone lowering, and together these two operations generate all movement along the circle of keys.

# Examples

**Example 1**: C major to G major -- raise F to F-sharp, keeping C, D, E, G, A, B fixed.

**Example 2** (p. 364, Fig. 8.1.7b): Seven counterclockwise steps from C major to C-sharp major, mapping tonic to tonic.

**Example 3** (Ch. 8, section 6, Fig. 8.6.1): The scale-degree-preserving model of mixolydian-to-ionian transformation -- raising the seventh degree of G mixolydian is equivalent to fourth-degree raising.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Thinking the fourth degree literally "moves" to become the leading tone through a continuous path
  **Correction**: Changes in scale degree do not imply determinate trajectories -- "pitch classes simply take on new roles without occupying intermediate points" (p. 365)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Conflating fourth-degree raising with simply adding a sharp to the key signature
  **Clarification**: While the result is the same (one more sharp), the concept emphasizes the voice-leading motion of a specific pitch class, connecting modulation to the same framework used for chord progressions
