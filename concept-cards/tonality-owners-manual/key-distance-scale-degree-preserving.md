# Quick Definition
A measure of key distance that minimizes the motion of scale degrees from tonic to tonic, supertonic to supertonic, and so on -- making parallel major/minor and semitonally related keys the closest neighbors.

# Formal Definition
Scale-degree-preserving key distance measures the total displacement when mapping each scale degree of one key to the corresponding scale degree of another. From this perspective, C major and G major are not close (their degrees are almost half an octave apart); instead, the nearest keys are parallel major/minor (e.g., C major and C minor) and semitonally related keys (e.g., C and D-flat major) -- Figure 8.1.1. On the spiral diagram, scale-degree-preserving voice leadings are represented as *slides* along the spiral. For mode-changing modulations, slides combine with the parallel modulation connecting keys that share a tonic (Figure 8.1.4).

# Musical Context
This notion of key distance is most relevant when instruments do the same thing before and after the modulation -- for example, one singer performing the same melody in both keys. It underlies modulatory practices such as "sidestepping" or "pumping-up," familiar in jazz and popular music.

# Common Confusions
- Scale-degree-preserving distance defines "close" keys very differently from the traditional circle-of-fifths model: C major and G major are distant rather than close.
- Changes in scale degree do not imply determinate trajectories through musical space: pitch classes simply take on new roles without occupying intermediate points.
