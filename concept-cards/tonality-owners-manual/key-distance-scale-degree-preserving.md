# Quick Definition

A measure of key distance that minimizes the motion of scale degrees from tonic to tonic, supertonic to supertonic, and so on -- making parallel major/minor and semitonally related keys the closest neighbors.

# Core Definition

Scale-degree-preserving key distance measures the total displacement when mapping each scale degree of one key to the corresponding degree of another. "From this point of view C major and G major are not close, since their scale degrees are almost half an octave apart; instead, the most proximate keys are the parallel major and minor---as well as semitonally related keys such as C and D-flat major" (p. 361, Figure 8.1.1). On the spiral diagram, these voice leadings are represented as *slides* along the spiral. For mode-changing modulations, slides combine with the parallel modulation connecting keys sharing a tonic (Figure 8.1.4).

# Prerequisites

- **Basic voice leading of scales** -- The concept of scales as collections of abstract voices

# Key Properties

1. Nearest keys: parallel major/minor (e.g., C major and C minor) and semitonally related keys (e.g., C and D-flat major)
2. Fifth-related keys are *distant* by this measure (scale degrees are nearly half an octave apart)
3. Represented as slides on the spiral diagram
4. Abstract voices never change scale-degree roles
5. Most relevant when instruments do the same thing before and after modulation (e.g., a singer performing the same melody)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Calculate Scale-Degree-Preserving Distance:
1. Map each scale degree of the first key to the corresponding degree of the second
2. Measure the chromatic distance each degree must move
3. Sum the total displacement
4. Smaller total = closer keys by this measure

# Context & Application

This notion of key distance underlies modulatory practices such as "sidestepping" or "pumping-up," familiar in jazz and popular music (e.g., a song modulating up by semitone for its final chorus). It is also found in classical music. The approach is most relevant "when instruments do the same thing before and after the modulation: one singer, for example, singing the same melody in both old key and new" (p. 361).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming this is the "standard" notion of key distance
  **Correction**: This is one of two valid measures; the pitch-class-changing measure (circle of fifths) is the classical norm

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking scale-degree-preserving distance means the keys share many notes
  **Clarification**: C major and D-flat major share only one note (F) but are close by this measure because each degree moves minimally
