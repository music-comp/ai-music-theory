# Quick Definition

A note that does not belong to the prevailing harmony and is subject to melodic constraints (stepwise approach, resolution, or other linear obligations). Tymoczko argues the concept is both useful and inherently problematic.

# Core Definition

A nonharmonic tone is a note melodically constrained in ways that harmonic tones are not: it must typically be approached and/or left by step according to specific rules governing its species (passing, neighboring, suspension, anticipation). The nonharmonic system ensures "teachability" by allowing analysts to distinguish load-bearing harmonic structure from ornamental embellishment. However, Tymoczko argues that nonharmonicity is "an inherently unstable notion, both useful and misleading, explanatory and yet false to the music it purports to describe" (p. 211). Composers sometimes used the nonharmonic system with irony, writing music in which purportedly nonharmonic notes had important harmonic effects (p. 211).

# Key Properties

1. Nonharmonic tones are melodically constrained in ways chord tones are not (p. 226)
2. They are governed by rules about approach and resolution (stepwise motion, metrical placement)
3. They can be classified into species: suspensions, neighbors, anticipations, passing tones (the SNAP system)
4. The system creates a distinction between "harmonic skeleton" and "surface decoration"
5. The distinction between harmonic and nonharmonic is sometimes blurry, especially with suspensions and in later styles
6. Nonharmonic tones can participate in genuinely harmonic narratives despite their classification

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify a Nonharmonic Tone:
1. Determine the prevailing harmony at the point where the tone sounds
2. Check whether the note belongs to the chord (if yes, it is a chord tone)
3. If not, classify by its melodic behavior: how it is approached and left
4. Assign to the appropriate species (passing, neighbor, suspension, anticipation, pedal, incomplete neighbor)
5. Verify that the tone obeys the rules of its species

# Context & Application

The nonharmonic system originated in the Renaissance first practice and was codified from Tinctoris onward. It reached its most systematic form in Palestrina's music, where virtually all dissonances can be labeled and removed algorithmically. The system was "standardized" anew in the baroque, incorporating seventh chords, cadential six-fours, incomplete neighbors, and pedals. It remained in force throughout much of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. In the twentieth century, the "emancipation of the dissonance" loosened nonharmonic constraints, making the distinction between harmonic and nonharmonic increasingly problematic.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 211): In Palestrina's music, nonharmonic tones rarely obscure the entire duration of a consonance, meaning consonances are "real sonic objects usually sounding at some point during their lifespan."

**Example 2** (p. 226): The safest understanding of nonharmonic tones is that they are "melodically constrained in ways that harmonic tones are not" -- even when harmonically significant, the fourth of a 6/4 chord is almost never doubled and almost always resolves downward.

**Example 3** (p. 237): In Bach's chorales, of more than ten thousand nonharmonic tones, all but about ten are well behaved according to standard rules.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating every nonharmonic tone as purely decorative
  **Correction**: Some nonharmonic tones (especially suspensions) have genuine harmonic significance and cannot be reduced to a consonant background

- **Error**: Assuming nonharmonic reduction is always analytically unproblematic
  **Correction**: Even when syntactically feasible, reduction can misrepresent the music's semantics

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing "nonharmonic" means the tone has nothing to do with harmony
  **Clarification**: Tymoczko shows that nonharmonic tones can be harmonically meaningful -- the term refers to melodic constraints, not harmonic irrelevance. As Schoenberg put it, "these are chords: not of the system, but of music" (p. 226)

- **Confusion**: Thinking the nonharmonic system was uniformly obeyed by all composers
  **Clarification**: Composers from Monteverdi to Mahler treated the system with varying degrees of irony, and the "true grammar" inferred from practice is more complex than textbook simplifications
