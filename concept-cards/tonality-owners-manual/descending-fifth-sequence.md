# Quick Definition
The most fundamental sequence in functional tonality, generated by iterating the basic voice leading clockwise on the dyadic or seventh-chord circles, producing root motions by descending fifth (or ascending fourth).

# Formal Definition
On the 2-in-7 dyadic circle, iterated clockwise steps produce a descending-fifth progression alternating thirds and sixths (Figure 3.1.3). On the 4-in-7 seventh-chord circle, two clockwise steps produce descending fifths (T_-4 t_2). In four-voice counterpoint, descending fifths arise from superimposing the basic voice leadings of thirds and fifths. The descending-fifth sequence can be realized as incomplete seventh chords, 7-6 fauxbourdon, full seventh-chord sequences, or the Fonte schema. Adding seventh chords converts retrofunctional triadic motion into functional harmony.

# Musical Context
The descending-fifth sequence pervades Western music from the Renaissance to jazz. Palestrina's masses contain abundant fifth progressions generated by the basic voice leading. Bach's fugues, Mozart's sonatas, and jazz standards like "All the Things You Are" and "Autumn Leaves" all exploit this geometry. The Chopin E minor prelude embellishes a descending-fifth middleground. The sequence appears in Beethoven's Op. 1 no. 3 third variation as pure sequential structure revealed through progressive analysis.

# Common Confusions
- Descending fifths can arise from purely contrapuntal logic (the geometry of diatonic thirds) without explicit harmonic intention
- The same descending-fifth pattern can be realized with very different textures (fauxbourdon, sequences, free composition) depending on voicing and embellishment
- In triadic space, descending fifths require ascending melodic motion, creating a tension; in seventh-chord space, descending melodies naturally produce descending fifths
