# Quick Definition

The most fundamental sequence in functional tonality, generated by iterating the basic voice leading clockwise on the dyadic or seventh-chord circles, producing root motions by descending fifth. It pervades Western music from Palestrina to jazz.

# Core Definition

On the 2-in-7 dyadic circle, iterated clockwise steps produce a descending-fifth progression alternating thirds and sixths (Figure 3.1.3, p. 98). On the 4-in-7 seventh-chord circle, two clockwise steps produce descending fifths (T_{-4}t_2). In four voices, descending fifths arise from superimposing basic voice leadings of thirds and fifths (Figure 3.3.2, p. 108). The sequence can be realized as incomplete seventh chords, 7-6 fauxbourdon, full seventh-chord sequences, or the Fonte schema (Figures 3.3.3, 3.1.10). Adding seventh chords converts retrofunctional triadic motion into functional harmony (p. 140). "The theory of voice leading and the theory of sequence are one and the same" (p. 163).

# Key Properties

1. Generated by iterating the basic voice leading clockwise
2. Two-voice form alternates thirds and sixths
3. Four-voice form superimposes thirds and fifths basic voice leadings
4. Seventh-chord form requires two clockwise steps per fifth
5. Can arise from purely contrapuntal logic without harmonic intention
6. Functional with dyads and seventh chords; retrofunctional with triads alone

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct:
1. Start with any dyad on the 2-in-7 circle
2. Apply the basic voice leading repeatedly (clockwise)
3. Root moves down by fifth at each step (with thirds and sixths alternating)
4. For four voices, superimpose with the fifth's basic voice leading

## To Recognize:
1. Look for root motion by descending fifth (or ascending fourth)
2. Check for stepwise voice leading between chords
3. Verify alternation of chord inversions (thirds and sixths in two voices)

# Context & Application

The descending-fifth sequence pervades Western music. Palestrina's masses contain abundant fifth progressions generated by the basic voice leading (Figure 3.1.5). Bach's fugues and chorales, Mozart's sonatas, and jazz standards like "All the Things You Are" (Figure 3.8.2) and "Autumn Leaves" all exploit this geometry. Chopin's E minor prelude embellishes a descending-fifth middleground (Figure 4.10.1, p. 198). Beethoven's Op. 1 no. 3 third variation reveals pure sequential structure through progressive analysis (Figure 4.10.4, p. 199).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming descending fifths always require harmonic motivation
  **Correction**: They can arise from purely contrapuntal geometry of diatonic thirds

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking all descending-fifth sequences have the same texture
  **Clarification**: The same pattern can appear as fauxbourdon, incomplete sevenths, full seventh chords, or free composition depending on voicing
