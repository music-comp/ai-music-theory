# Quick Definition

Contrapuntal patterns that recur across many musical styles, generalized beyond their original chordal and scalar environments through the Prime Directive to reveal a fundamental "language of counterpoint" grounded in the abstract structure of musical space.

# Core Definition

Generalized voice-leading patterns result from applying the Prime Directive to specific musical idioms, abstracting them to apply to "any chords and scales whatsoever" (p. 3). These form a "fundamental language of counterpoint grounded in the abstract structure of musical space, and encompassing both schematic routine and idiosyncratic innovation" (p. 9). The book describes "general principles governing the interdependence of harmony and counterpoint" (p. 33) using geometrical models that allow viewing passages under "a dyadic, triadic, seventh-chord, or scalar lens" (p. 33). This is "a generalized analogue of schema theory, combining linear ideas from Schenker with harmonic ideas from Rameau" (p. 33).

# Prerequisites

- **Prime Directive** -- The Prime Directive is the tool for generalizing specific patterns
- **Voice Leading** -- Understanding voice leading is needed to see the patterns

# Key Properties

1. Recur across many musical styles from Renaissance to present
2. Grounded in abstract structure of musical space, not arbitrary convention
3. Encompass both schematic routine and idiosyncratic innovation
4. Include "thinking within the chord," hierarchically nested transposition, and repeating contrapuntal patterns
5. Contrary-motion sequences are most balanced when moving by about half an octave
6. General principles are the source of the particular schemas that define specific styles

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Generalized Patterns:
1. Identify a specific voice-leading pattern in a piece
2. Abstract the contrapuntal logic from its specific harmonic context
3. Apply the Prime Directive to find instances in other environments
4. Check whether the pattern appears across stylistic boundaries
5. Distinguish the "deep" (general) pattern from its "shallow" (style-specific) instances

# Context & Application

These patterns allow connecting practices from Gesualdo to Stravinsky to Mahanthappa, from Mendelssohn Hensel to Meredith Monk. The contrary-motion schema enumerating diatonic consonances (fifth, third, octave, sixth) "appears in virtually all modal and functionally tonal styles" (p. 31). Without general principles, composition risks becoming "mindless stitching-together of precomposed chunks" (p. 32).

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 31-32, Figures 1.5.6-1.5.7): Contrary-motion sequences enumerating diatonic consonances, found in Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel's "Januar" and Klaus Badelt's *Pirates of the Caribbean*.

**Example 2** (p. 32, Figure 1.5.8): Meredith Monk's *Anthem* (2020) adapting the traditional contrary-motion schema to dissonant dyads.

**Example 3** (p. 30, Figure 1.5.5): Three ways to harmonize stepwise descending thirds with alternating 5/3 and 6/3 sonorities -- the first common in all triadic music, the second only in baroque, the third only in modal.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating generalized patterns as mechanical formulas to be applied without musical judgment
  **Correction**: "Genuine musical thinking requires novel solutions to high-level compositional problems" (p. 32)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking general patterns eliminate the need for style-specific knowledge
  **Clarification**: Both general and particular knowledge coexist; schemas have independent value
