# Quick Definition
Contrapuntal patterns that recur across many musical styles, generalized beyond their original chordal and scalar environments to reveal a fundamental language of counterpoint.

# Formal Definition
Generalized voice-leading patterns are the products of applying the Prime Directive to specific musical idioms, abstracting them so they apply to any chords and scales whatsoever. These include voice leading, counterpoint, sequence, motivic development, nonharmonic tones, and modulation. The book describes "general principles governing the interdependence of harmony and counterpoint" using a family of geometrical models that allow viewing any passage under a dyadic, triadic, seventh-chord, or scalar lens.

# Musical Context
These patterns form a fundamental "language of counterpoint" grounded in the abstract structure of musical space, encompassing both schematic routine and idiosyncratic innovation. They span the tradition from Renaissance polyphony to jazz, rock, and contemporary notated composition. Particularly important is the contrary-motion schema that enumerates diatonic consonances (fifth, third, octave, sixth), which appears in virtually all modal and functionally tonal styles and is most balanced when voices move by about half an octave.

- Contrary-motion sequences alternating thirds and sixths: Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel's "Januar" mm. 15-18 and Klaus Badelt's *Pirates of the Caribbean* (Figure 1.5.7)

# Common Confusions
- Generalized patterns are not mechanical formulas -- genuine musical thinking requires novel solutions to high-level compositional problems
- Not all patterns are equally common in all styles; the general possibility space is narrowed by stylistic conventions
- "Repeating contrapuntal patterns" (Chapter 4's term) are a specific subset of these generalized patterns
