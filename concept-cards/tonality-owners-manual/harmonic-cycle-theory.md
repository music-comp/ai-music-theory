# Quick Definition
A theory of functional tonality as built around repeating cycles of chord progressions that pass through tonic, subdominant, and dominant functions, governed by a descending-thirds arrangement of chords.

# Formal Definition
Tymoczko models functional harmony as a heterogeneous grammar combining three independent subsystems: harmonic cycles, fauxbourdon, and sequence. Harmonic cycles are the primary subsystem, organized around the principle that chords move along a chain of descending thirds from tonic to dominant. In the descending-thirds model (Figure 7.1.3), diatonic triads are arranged I--vi--IV--ii--vii^o^--V, with free rightward and vertical motion but constrained leftward motion (following specific arrows). More than 95% of progressions in typical functional pieces conform to this model (excluding sequences and fauxbourdon).

# Musical Context
The harmonic cycle is the core grammar of functional tonality from Corelli through the present day. It produces an aesthetic of near-sequential repetition: music cycles through the same small map of harmonic options, changing just enough to keep the ear intrigued. This cyclic quality is overt in baroque music, sublimated in the classical era, and returns in Beethoven and Chopin.

# Examples
- Figure 7.1.3: The descending-thirds arrangement I--vi--IV--ii--vii^o^--V, with symbol sizes representing frequency.
- Bach chorales and Mozart piano sonatas: Statistical analysis shows bass degrees ^1, ^3, ^5, and ^7 typically support tonic and dominant, while ^2, ^4, and ^6 provide harmonic variation (Figure 7.1.2).
- The simple protofunctional cycles I--IV--I, I--V--I, and I--IV--V--I are the foundation, with the dominant cycle growing in complexity over time.

# Common Confusions
- This is not a single-principle grammar: functional tonality is heterogeneous, combining harmonic cycles with fauxbourdon and sequence as independent subsystems.
- The model describes first-order defaults surrounded by a penumbra of second-order idioms -- it is not a set of inviolable laws.
- The descending-thirds arrangement is Tymoczko's alternative to the traditional circle-of-fifths model, though the two are closely related.
