# Quick Definition

A theory of functional tonality as built around repeating cycles of chord progressions that pass through tonic, subdominant, and dominant functions, governed by a heterogeneous grammar combining harmonic cycles, fauxbourdon, and sequence.

# Core Definition

Tymoczko models functional harmony as a "simple, approximately true, and *heterogeneous* grammar of chord-to-chord possibilities, combining the three independent subsystems of harmonic cycles, fauxbourdon, and sequence" (p. 311). Harmonic cycles are the primary subsystem, organized around the descending-thirds arrangement (Figure 7.1.3) where chords move freely rightward along the chain I--vi--IV--ii--vii^o^--V but leftward only along specified arrows. More than 95% of progressions in typical functional pieces conform to this model, excluding sequences and fauxbourdon (p. 312). The grammar produces an aesthetic of near-sequential repetition: music cycles through the same small map of harmonic options, changing just enough to keep the ear intrigued (p. 312).

# Prerequisites

- **Descending-thirds arrangement** -- The chain I--vi--IV--ii--vii^o^--V that organizes the harmonic grammar
- **Protofunctionality** -- The simpler I--IV--I, I--V--I, and I--IV--V--I cycles from which mature functionality develops

# Key Properties

1. Functional tonality is heterogeneous, combining three independent subsystems: harmonic cycles, fauxbourdon, and sequence
2. Harmonic cycles account for more than 95% of non-sequential, non-fauxbourdon progressions
3. Bass degrees 1, 3, 5, and 7 typically support tonic and dominant; 2, 4, and 6 provide harmonic variation (Figure 7.1.2)
4. The model privileges "strong" progressions (descending third, descending fifth, ascending step) over "weak" counterparts
5. The system is governed by first-order defaults surrounded by a penumbra of second-order idioms

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify a Harmonic Cycle:
1. Locate the tonic chord that begins and ends the progression
2. Map each chord to its position on the descending-thirds chain I--vi--IV--ii--vii^o^--V
3. Verify that motion is predominantly rightward (toward dominant)
4. Identify any leftward motions and check whether they follow permitted arrows (V/vii^o^ returning to I)
5. Check whether remaining anomalies can be attributed to fauxbourdon or sequence

# Context & Application

Functional harmony begins roughly with Corelli, dominates European music for almost two centuries, and survives to this day. The cyclic quality is overt in baroque music, somewhat sublimated in the classical era, and returns transformed in Beethoven and Chopin. By the twentieth century, repetition and sequence are viewed with suspicion, but the underlying cyclic grammar continues in popular music.

**Schenker denied** that chord-to-chord rules exist, asserting that harmonic events are byproducts of linear motion. Contemporary "microtheorists" have suggested functionality can be replaced by schemas. Tymoczko argues that neither extreme is adequate: the grammar is real but heterogeneous (p. 311).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 312, Figure 7.1.1): Distribution of major-mode sonorities in Bach chorales and Mozart piano sonatas shows the statistical basis for the model.

**Example 2** (p. 312, Figure 7.1.3): The full descending-thirds arrangement where rightward and vertical motion is unrestricted but leftward motion follows arrows.

**Example 3** (p. 319): Roy Orbison's "Oh, Pretty Woman" -- melody descends through one octave by thirds while harmony descends by third through two abstract octaves, demonstrating the system's reach into popular music.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating harmonic cycles as the *only* principle governing functional harmony
  **Correction**: Functional harmony is heterogeneous; fauxbourdon and sequence operate independently of the harmonic-cycle grammar

- **Error**: Expecting 100% conformity to the descending-thirds model
  **Correction**: The model accounts for 95%+ of non-sequential, non-fauxbourdon progressions; a penumbra of second-order idioms accounts for the rest

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing this is simply a relabeling of the circle of fifths
  **Clarification**: The descending-thirds model is more fundamental; Rameau's descending-fifth paradigm can be derived from it by treating triads as incomplete seventh chords

- **Confusion**: Thinking the model describes universal laws
  **Clarification**: It describes first-order defaults that vary historically, with Haydn/Mozart/Beethoven representing maximal regimentation (the hourglass's narrow point)
