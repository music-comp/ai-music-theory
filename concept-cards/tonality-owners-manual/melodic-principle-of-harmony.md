# Quick Definition

The idea that harmonic progressions can arise from fundamentally melodic aims -- particularly that stepwise melodic descent, when harmonized with root-position triads, preferentially generates "weak" (retrofunctional) root progressions rather than the "strong" progressions of classical functionality.

# Core Definition

The melodic principle proposes that "if a melody embellishes a stepwise descent from one tonic-triad note to another" and "we decide to harmonize each note with a root-position major triad," then certain root progressions are favored (p. 48). "Simple tabulation shows that descending steps preferentially give rise to what are sometimes called 'weak' root-progressions: ascending fifths rather than descending fifths, ascending minor thirds rather than descending minor thirds, and descending rather than ascending steps" (p. 48). The principle has "Schenkerian overtones in suggesting that harmonic patterns can arise out of fundamentally melodic aims" but "rather than trying to show that stepwise descending melody necessarily reproduces the conventions of functional harmony, I will suggest that it leads to different kinds of progressions in different harmonic contexts" (p. 48).

# Key Properties

1. Descending stepwise melodies generate retrofunctional progressions (ascending fifths, ascending minor thirds)
2. The model uses root-position major triads as harmonizations
3. On the spiral, descending melodies correspond to radial or short clockwise motion
4. The principle explains tendencies, not absolute rules
5. It applies to ascending and leaping melodies too, but stepwise descent is the focus
6. The goal is to model implicit knowledge of untrained musicians

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply:
1. Start with a stepwise descending melody
2. Harmonize each note with a major triad containing that note
3. Tabulate the resulting root progressions
4. Observe the preference for weak (retrofunctional) over strong (functional) progressions
5. Map the progressions onto the spiral diagram

# Context & Application

The principle motivates the entire model of rock harmony in Ch. 2. The "subversion of the tonic-subdominant-dominant paradigm" in rock "may not be a rebellion against classical harmonic practice, but rather a straightforward solution to a basic musical problem" (p. 47). # Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming the principle claims all harmony arises from melody
  **Correction**: It is one factor among several; harmonic proximity (diatonic distance) is another

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the principle reproduces classical functional harmony
  **Clarification**: It generates retrofunctional norms that reverse classical expectations
