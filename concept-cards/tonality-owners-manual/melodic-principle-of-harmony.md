# Quick Definition
The idea that harmonic progressions can arise from fundamentally melodic aims, particularly the tendency for stepwise melodic descent to generate specific chord-to-chord voice leadings.

# Formal Definition
The melodic principle proposes that if a melody embellishes a stepwise descent from one tonic-triad note to another, and each note is harmonized with a root-position major triad, then certain root progressions will be favored over others. Specifically, descending steps preferentially give rise to "weak" root progressions: ascending fifths rather than descending fifths, ascending minor thirds rather than descending minor thirds, and descending rather than ascending steps. This principle has Schenkerian overtones but does not attempt to reproduce functional harmony; instead, it produces different progressions in different contexts.

# Musical Context
The principle motivates the model of rock harmony developed in Chapter 2, emphasizing the melodic origin of harmonic idioms. The underlying techniques apply to ascending or leaping melodies as well, but the main focus is on stepwise descent. The goal is to understand the implicit knowledge of talented but possibly untrained musicians -- principles absorbed from thousands of hours of listening and playing, comparable to grammar in natural language.

# Common Confusions
- The melodic principle does not claim that *all* harmony arises from melody -- it is one factor among several
- It does not necessarily reproduce classical functional progressions -- it leads to *retrofunctional* norms that reverse classical expectations
- The principle explains tendencies, not absolute rules; rock musicians use both functional and retrofunctional progressions
