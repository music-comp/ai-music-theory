# Quick Definition

The distinction between concrete surface voices (perceptible melodies played by instruments) and abstract voices (scale degrees or "melodic slots" that exist in every octave and none, serving as potentialities rather than specific pitches).

# Core Definition

Surface or *concrete* voices "articulate perceptible melodies" localized in pitch and time. Abstract voices are "scale degrees or melodic slots" that are "localized neither in time nor in register" (p. 12). A scale degree is "a potentiality, a melodic location existing a step below every tonic and two steps above every dominant. It is in every octave and none, a pitch class rather than a pitch" (p. 12). This produces "a deep analogy between scale degrees and musical voices: at some level, scale degrees are voices, articulated not by concrete musical instruments but by abstract structures of melodic possibility" (p. 14).

# Prerequisites

- **Quadruple Hierarchy** -- The distinction is essential for understanding motion at different hierarchical levels

# Key Properties

1. Surface voices are concrete, localized in pitch and time
2. Abstract voices are scale degrees or melodic slots, existing in every octave
3. Scale degrees ARE voices at some level -- articulated by abstract melodic possibility
4. A single surface voice can move along multiple abstract voices
5. When reducing to a scalar background, there is no "obligatory register"
6. The distinction is functional rather than ontological

# Construction / Recognition

## To Distinguish:
1. Identify the perceptible melody (surface voice)
2. Determine which scale degree or chord tone the melody occupies at each point
3. Check whether the melody moves between different abstract voices (scale degrees)
4. Note cases where the same pitch corresponds to different scale degrees

# Context & Application

The distinction is crucial for understanding the quadruple hierarchy and Shepard-tone passacaglias. In Schenkerian theory, reductions deliver concrete pitches with octave shifts understood as "register transfer." When reducing to a scalar background, however, "there is no 'obligatory register' since the background object is a scale existing in every octave and none" (p. 13). This opens new analytical possibilities.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 10, Figure 1.2.1): Beethoven's Op. 54, II -- a single surface voice moves along three abstract voices (the omnibus pattern's top notes).

**Example 2** (p. 14, Figure 1.2.5): Mozart's K.283 -- surface voice moves "up by third, down by step" along melodic slots that are themselves shifting.

**Example 3** (p. 11, Figure 1.2.2): Beatles' "Eight Days a Week" -- melody returns to same pitch but different scale degree; surface stays fixed while abstract voices descend.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming every pitch change marks a change of abstract voice
  **Correction**: Leaps on the surface necessarily mark motion between abstract voices when background voices are triadic and connected by step (Westergaard's observation, p. 13)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking abstract voices must occupy specific registers
  **Clarification**: Abstract voices (scale degrees) exist in every octave; there is no "obligatory register" as in Schenkerian theory
