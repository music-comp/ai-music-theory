# Quick Definition
The distinction between concrete surface voices (perceptible melodies played by instruments) and abstract voices (scale degrees or melodic slots existing in every octave and none).

# Formal Definition
Surface or concrete voices articulate perceptible melodies localized in pitch and time. Abstract voices are scale degrees or "melodic slots" that are localized neither in time nor in register -- potentialities existing a step below every tonic and two steps above every dominant. Scale degrees *are* voices at some level, articulated not by concrete instruments but by abstract structures of melodic possibility. This produces a deep analogy between scale degrees and musical voices.

# Musical Context
The distinction is crucial for the quadruple hierarchy. In a piece like Bach's C major prelude (WTC I), figuration outlines concrete voices in particular registers. In Lusitano's "Heu me Domine," the reduction produces something more abstract -- a scale-like structure whose voices can appear in any octave. Schenkerian theory does not fully recognize this distinction, its reductions typically delivering concrete pitches.

# Examples
- Beethoven's Op. 54, II, mm. 38-41: a single surface voice moves along three abstract voices (the top notes of the omnibus pattern, Figure 1.2.1)

# Common Confusions
- When background voices are triadic and connected by step, leaps on the surface necessarily mark motion from one abstract voice to another (Westergaard's observation)
- There is no "obligatory register" for abstract voices, unlike Schenker's notion of "register transfer"
- The paradigmatic chord (concrete, localized) and paradigmatic scale (abstract, not localized) differ in function, not inherent structure
