# Quick Definition
A "thick," theory-laden approach to harmonic analysis in which chord labels encode the analyst's impression of a chord's function, behavior, historical origins, or perceived essence, rather than merely identifying scale-degree content.

# Formal Definition
Functional analysis uses harmonic symbols that go beyond identifying scale degrees to encode phenomenological impressions of a chord's role. A function theorist might label a mediant chord as V to record the claim that it behaves or sounds like a dominant. This approach simplifies the grammar of harmonic progressions at the expense of complicating chord identification. For example, Rameau analyzed the deceptive cadence as a descending-fifth progression where the tonic chord is represented by root, third, and sixth above the fundamental bass; vii-diminished is treated as V7 missing its root.

# Musical Context
The functional approach has deep historical roots, from Rameau's fundamental bass through Riemann's function theory. It can sometimes come dangerously close to circularity: at the extreme, labels like "dominant" are utterly divorced from scale-degree content, applied to any chord perceived as engendering a certain kind of expectation. The claim "dominants progress to tonics" then becomes definitional rather than empirical.

# Common Confusions
- Functional analysis can approach circularity when "dominant" means any chord that creates expectation of a tonic arrival, making "dominants progress to tonics" a definitional truth rather than an empirical claim.
- Eminent scholars have claimed that Roman numerals necessarily presuppose inversional equivalence, or that "dominant" assumes primacy of the bass voice. Tymoczko disagrees, arguing these are philosophical mistakes.
- Functional analysis is not inherently wrong -- describing subjective experience is a reasonable pursuit. Problems arise when one approach is tacitly assumed to be the only option.
