# Quick Definition

A "thick" and theory-laden approach to harmonic analysis in which labels encode the analyst's phenomenological impression of a chord, beliefs about its behavior, historical origins, or claims about its essence -- as opposed to merely recording scale-degree content.

# Core Definition

Functional analysis uses harmonic symbols that are "thick" and theory-laden -- encoding the analyst's phenomenological impression of a chord, beliefs about its behavior, theory of its historical origins, or claims about its "essence" (p. 253). A function theorist might label a mediant chord as V to record the claim that it behaves or sounds like a dominant (p. 253). This tradeoff simplifies the grammar of harmonic progressions at the expense of complicating chord identification. At the extreme, labels like "dominant" are "utterly divorced from their scale-degree content, applied to any chord that is perceived (by the analyst) as engendering a certain kind of expectation" (p. 255), making "dominants progress to tonics" definitional rather than empirical.

# Key Properties

1. Labels encode claims about function, behavior, or essence
2. Simplifies harmonic grammar at the cost of complicating chord identification
3. Can approach circularity when labels are divorced from scale-degree content
4. "Dominants progress to tonics" can become definitional, not empirical (p. 255)
5. Reflects "what a perceptive listener hears" (Rothgeb, p. 255)
6. Represents one end of a continuum from mechanistic to interpretive analysis

# Construction / Recognition

## To Perform Functional Analysis:
1. Listen to each chord's perceived function in context
2. Assign labels reflecting function rather than scale-degree content
3. Accept that similar scale-degree collections may receive different labels
4. Be aware that this approach encodes interpretation in the label itself

# Context & Application

Functional analysis is the traditional approach, associated with Riemann and the Schenkerian tradition. Tymoczko argues it sometimes comes "dangerously close to circularity" and deprives theorists of "virtually the only tool that can trace the subtle ways in which configurations of scale degrees change their behavior over the course of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries" (p. 256).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 253): A function theorist labels III+6 as V in Bach's "Jesu, meine Freude," recording the claim that the mediant acts like a dominant.

**Example 2** (p. 254): Treating the cadential six-four as V rather than I64, simplifying the grammar but creating notation where "V64" can refer to two different chords.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming functional labels are the only legitimate form of harmonic analysis
  **Correction**: Scale-degree analysis provides useful, falsifiable claims and shared vocabulary

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking Roman numerals necessarily presuppose inversional equivalence or the primacy of the bass
  **Clarification**: These are properties of one particular use of Roman numerals, not inherent in the notation (pp. 255-256)
