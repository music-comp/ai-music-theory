# Quick Definition
The historical evolution from Renaissance disguised repetition (subtle, concealed sequential patterns) to Baroque overt sequences (explicit, audible repetition), and the ongoing compositional technique of revealing or concealing sequential structure.

# Formal Definition
Renaissance music features repeating contrapuntal patterns that are often subtle and disguised -- sequential structure not immediately obvious to the listener (e.g., ascending fifths in Palestrina's "Hosanna" from *Spem in alium*). This evolves into the overtly repetitive sequences of the Baroque, which embed repeating patterns into larger blocks. Nineteenth-century composers add contrary-motion wedge sequences. The technique of the "disguised model" presents near-sequential structure moving from obscurity toward clarity: Beethoven's Op. 1 no. 3 variations theme reveals its sequential structure only gradually over three iterations.

# Musical Context
Disguised repetition is proposed as a defining characteristic of functional tonality. In simpler forms: the *Pathetique* uses octave displacements and converging cadences to disguise falling fifths. In more sophisticated forms: Chopin's E minor prelude embellishes a descending-fifth middleground with chromatic descent. Entire movements can be constructed from a handful of sequences. This perspective contrasts with Schenkerian analysis, which consigns sequential structure to the surface. The challenge is balancing the "mechanistic" (repetitive) and "organic" (stepwise melodic connection) perspectives.

# Common Confusions
- "Disguised" does not mean the sequence is inaudible or unreal; it means the sequential structure is not foregrounded but still shapes the music's logic
- Sequence-based reduction does not remove notes (like Schenkerian reduction) but removes "compositional decision points," transforming irregular surfaces back to regular templates
- Schenker was right that sequences contain important linear motions, but wrong to deny that sequences have harmonic significance
