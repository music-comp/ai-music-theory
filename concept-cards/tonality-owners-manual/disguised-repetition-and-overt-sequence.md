# Quick Definition

The historical evolution from Renaissance disguised repetition (subtle, concealed sequential patterns) to Baroque overt sequences (explicit, audible repetition), and the ongoing technique of revealing or concealing sequential structure. Disguised repetition is proposed as "a defining characteristic of functional tonality" (p. 201).

# Core Definition

Renaissance music features repeating contrapuntal patterns that are "often subtle and disguised... whose sequential structure is not immediately obvious" (p. 155). This evolves into overtly repetitive baroque sequences. The technique of the "disguised model" moves from obscurity toward clarity as music progresses: Beethoven's Op. 1 no. 3 theme reveals its sequential structure only gradually over three iterations (Figures 4.10.2-3, pp. 199-200). "Disguised repetition -- as opposed to the more overt repetition in rock or minimalism -- is a defining characteristic of functional tonality" (p. 201). The third variation reveals "a profound reimagining of the variation form, proceeding not by progressive embellishment... but by progressive analysis, stripping away decoration to reveal a latent musical logic" (p. 200).

1. Renaissance: subtle, concealed sequential patterns
2. Baroque: overt, audible repetition
3. "Disguised model" technique: obscurity moving toward clarity

5. Progressive analysis (Beethoven): stripping decoration to reveal logic
6. Creates "magical experience of inexplicable inevitability" (p. 201)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify:
1. Look for passages that sound freely composed but exhibit near-sequential regularity
2. Check successive iterations for increasing clarity of sequential pattern
3. Compare the surface with a hypothetical regular sequential template
4. Note which "compositional decision points" were added to disguise the pattern

# Context & Application

The Pathetique uses octave displacements and converging cadences to disguise falling fifths. Chopin's E minor prelude embellishes a descending-fifth middleground with chromatic descent. Beethoven's Op. 1 no. 3 reveals sequential structure progressively. This perspective proposes that "rather than imagining sequences as deviant, we can reconceive nonsequential functionality with reference to a fundamentally repetitive norm" (p. 202). This "tension between the mechanistic and organic devolves... into the conflict between traditional harmonic theory and Schenkerian analysis" (p. 202).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating disguised repetition as failed or sloppy composition
  **Correction**: It is a sophisticated technique creating "inexplicable inevitability"

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking disguised repetition is inaudible
  **Clarification**: Listeners perceive the regularity even without identifying it consciously; it shapes expectations
