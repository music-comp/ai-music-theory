# Quick Definition
The book's culminating synthesis: the collectional (quadruple) hierarchy as a point of contact between schema theory, scale theory, voice-leading geometry, neo-Riemannian theory, and Schenkerian analysis -- a unified framework revealing how different styles exploit the same fundamental geometrical substrate.

# Formal Definition
The collectional hierarchy represents the organizational strategy of surface voices moving inside chords that move inside scales that move inside the aggregate. The spiral diagrams provide unified geometrical models revealing the interdependence of harmony and counterpoint: "wheels within wheels, each turning independently of the others" (p. 527). Styles are characterized by different settings of this complex machine: Renaissance polyphony, AC/DC, Bach preludes, Romantic subjectivity, impressionism, jazz, and minimalism all arise from different configurations. The hierarchy bridges multiple theoretical traditions by showing that Schenkerian insights can be expressed in scale-theoretical language, and that schemas from different periods reflect the same geometrical core.

# Musical Context
The Conclusion traces nine of eleven Gjerdingen schemas to geometrical origins and shows how Bebop jazz extends the diatonic fifth-sequence by chromaticizing the voice leading. Charlie Parker's "Blues for Alice" is analyzed as three turns through the 2-in-12 chromatic diagram, connecting Bebop to an "ancient musical logic" beginning before Ockeghem (pp. 528-529). This demonstrates that Bebop is "more Romantic than modernist," complexifying rather than rejecting functional convention.

# Examples
- Nine Gjerdingen schemas traced to specific geometrical origins: dyadic space (Quiescenza, Fonte, Monte), triadic space (Prinner, Romanesca), canonic fourth-progression (Fenaroli), sentential logic (Meyer, Do-Re-Mi, Sol-Fa-Me) (p. 528)
- Charlie Parker's "Blues for Alice": three turns through the 2-in-12 diagram with upper-voice fifths sliding downward (Figures C1-C2, pp. 529-530)
- Process-music composition using contrary-motion technique inspired by the Waldstein (Figures C3-C4, pp. 531-532)

# Common Confusions
- The collectional hierarchy is not a claim that all music works the same way -- different styles use different "settings" of the same machine
- Musical knowledge is often implicit: "culture will preserve these discoveries, transmitting them as important idioms" without requiring conscious awareness of geometry (p. 528)
- The synthesis does not privilege any single theoretical tradition but shows they illuminate different aspects of the same underlying structure
