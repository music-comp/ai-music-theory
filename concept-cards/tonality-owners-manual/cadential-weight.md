# Quick Definition
The relative strength or finality of a cadential progression, determined by the number of descending-third steps traversed from the pre-cadential chord to the resolution.

# Formal Definition
Within the descending-thirds model, cadential weight corresponds to the number of rightward steps on the harmonic map from the last non-tonic chord to the tonic. A V--I cadence traverses two positions (the maximum direct leap), giving it the greatest cadential weight. A IV--I cadence traverses one position. Progressions involving more intermediate chords (e.g., ii--V--I or IV--V--I) accumulate weight through the combined motion. The cadential 6/4 (I6/4) embellishes the dominant, effectively delaying the arrival of the tonic to increase the sense of resolution.

# Musical Context
Cadential weight explains the hierarchy of cadence types in functional music: authentic cadences (V--I) feel more conclusive than plagal cadences (IV--I), and full cadences (ii--V--I) feel more conclusive than either. It also explains why the cadential 6/4 is so powerful -- it extends the dominant's duration, creating a greater sense of arrival when the tonic finally arrives.

# Examples
- The harmonic grammar (Figure 7.1.3) naturally produces cadences of varying weight depending on how far rightward the progression has traveled before resolving.
- In Bach's chorales, cadential schemas are reused across multiple phrases, with the inner-voice leading tone sometimes falling by third rather than resolving upward.
- The fauxbourdon ROTO variants (Figure 7.5.4) often delay the cadential dominant, creating greater accumulated cadential weight.

# Common Confusions
- Cadential weight is a property of the progression as a whole, not just the final two chords.
- The cadential 6/4 is functionally an embellishment of the dominant, not a second-inversion tonic chord, despite its appearance.
