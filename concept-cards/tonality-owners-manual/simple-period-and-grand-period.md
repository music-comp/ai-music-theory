# Quick Definition
The simple period is the length of the minimal block of music sufficient to generate a sequence (one application of the arrows). The grand period is the time for all voices to cycle back to their initial melodic positions, all transposed by the same amount.

# Formal Definition
In the presence of permutation, the grand period contains multiple simple periods. For a transpositional sequence (no permutation), the two are identical. For canonic sequences, the grand period is the number of simple periods needed for every voice to have passed through every melodic role. In "Three Blinde Mice," the simple period is one bar but the grand period is three bars (three voices cycling). In Bach's G# minor fugue, the simple period is one bar but the grand period is two bars. Different groups of voices may have different simple periods within the same sequence.

# Musical Context
Previous theorists focused on the grand period to the exclusion of the simple period, largely because they neglected permutation as a sequence-generating operation. This led to difficulties in identifying sequential units. Recognizing the simple period allows analysts to identify the minimal repeating structure even when voices have not yet cycled back to their original roles. Harmonically unstable sequences also typically have grand periods containing multiple simple periods, as voices cycle through different transpositions.

# Common Confusions
- The simple period is not the same as "one measure" or "one beat"; it is defined by the arrow structure, not metric duration
- Grand period and simple period coincide only in transpositional sequences (no permutation)
- Different voices can have different simple periods within the same sequence
