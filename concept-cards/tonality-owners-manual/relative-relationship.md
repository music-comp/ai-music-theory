# Quick Definition

The relationship between major and minor keys sharing the same (or nearly the same) pitch-class content (e.g., C major and A minor), which are close in pitch-class-changing key distance but distant in scale-degree-preserving distance.

# Core Definition

The relative relationship connects major and minor keys whose diatonic collections are identical or nearly so. In the pitch-class-changing model of key distance, relative keys are the nearest major/minor pairs because they share all (or almost all) of their pitch classes (p. 362, Fig. 8.1.2). In the scale-degree-preserving model, they are more distant because the tonic shifts by a third. On the circular key-space model (Fig. 8.3.3), the relative relationship provides one of the two main connections between major and minor keys (the other being the parallel relationship). The spatial extension of minor keys allows them to bridge otherwise-distant major keys: A minor is close to C major (relative), A major (parallel), G major (one-semitone voice leading from G major tonic to A melodic-minor leading tone), and D major (via the common modulatory pathway I-V-ii) (pp. 373-374).

# Prerequisites

- **Basic voice leading of scales** -- Understanding scalar voice leading provides the framework for measuring key distance

# Key Properties

1. Relative keys share the same (or nearly the same) pitch-class collection
2. Close in pitch-class-changing distance -- minimal displacement of pitch classes
3. Distant in scale-degree-preserving distance -- the tonic shifts by a third
4. The nearest major/minor pairs in the pitch-class model
5. Implicit in classical modulatory norms, most useful when different material is presented in both keys
6. Minor keys' spatial extension allows them to be close to multiple major keys simultaneously

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify the Relative Relationship:
1. Check that both keys share the same key signature (or nearly so)
2. Verify that the tonics are a minor third apart (relative minor is a minor third below the major tonic)
3. Note that the pitch-class collections overlap almost completely
4. Recognize that scale-degree roles differ substantially (C major's tonic is A minor's third degree)

# Context & Application

The relative relationship is one of the most important modulatory connections in functional tonality. It underlies the standard modulation from tonic to mediant in minor-key pieces and from tonic to relative minor (vi) in major-key pieces. This second notion of key distance is most useful when different material is presented in the two keys -- for example in the first and second themes of a sonata -- "or when composers have a wide variety of instruments at their disposal. For in this case no problems arise from the nearly half-octave scale-degree shift" (p. 362). The model dates back to the eighteenth-century work of Heinichen and Kellner (Fig. 8.1.6).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming relative keys are close in all respects
  **Correction**: They are close in pitch-class terms but not in scale-degree terms -- C major's tonic (C) becomes the third degree of A minor, a substantial shift of role

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking minor keys' spatial extension in key-space models arises from deep geometric features
  **Clarification**: The nonlocality arises from the convention of treating distinct minor scale forms as syntactically equivalent, not from inherent properties of musical geometry (p. 373)
