# Quick Definition
The relationship between major and minor keys sharing the same pitch-class content (e.g., C major and A minor), which are close in terms of pitch-class-changing key distance but distant in scale-degree-preserving distance.

# Formal Definition
The relative relationship connects major and minor keys whose diatonic collections are identical (or nearly so). In the pitch-class-changing model of key distance, relative keys are the nearest major/minor pairs because they share all (or almost all) of their pitch classes (Figure 8.1.2). In the scale-degree-preserving model, they are more distant because the tonic shifts by a third. On the circular key-space model (Figure 8.3.3), the relative relationship provides one of the two main connections between major and minor keys (the other being the parallel relationship). The spatial extension of minor keys allows them to bridge otherwise-distant major keys: A minor is close to C major (relative), A major (parallel), G major (one-semitone voice leading from G major tonic to A melodic-minor leading tone), and D major (via the common modulatory pathway I--V--ii).

# Musical Context
The relative relationship is one of the most important modulatory connections in functional tonality. It underlies the standard modulation from tonic to mediant in minor-key pieces and from tonic to relative minor (vi) in major-key pieces.

# Common Confusions
- The relative relationship is close in pitch-class terms but not in scale-degree terms: C major's tonic (C) becomes the third degree of A minor, a substantial shift of role.
- Minor keys' spatial extension in key-space models arises from the convention of treating distinct scale forms as syntactically equivalent, not from deep geometric features.
