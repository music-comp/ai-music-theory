# Quick Definition
A sequence that preserves the registral distance between voices from one grand period to the next. All canonic voice groups are automatically registrally stable, since each line passes through the same transposition sequence.

# Formal Definition
A collection of canonic voices (interlinked by arrows) is always registrally stable because each line passes through the same sequence of transpositions with each grand period. In the bottom passage of Figure 4.1.3, the top voice moves down four steps then up three (total: -1, down by step), while the middle voice moves up three then down four (also total: -1). The bass, not part of the canonic group, moves down by eight steps per grand period, forming a contrary-motion relationship with the upper voices. Registral stability contrasts with contrary-motion sequences that alter inter-voice distances.

# Musical Context
Registral stability is the norm for most functional sequences: the voices maintain their relative spacing from one grand period to the next, even when they permute melodic material. Contrary-motion sequences are the exception, becoming more important in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The distinction between registrally stable and contrary-motion sequences is an additional dimension beyond the harmonically stable/unstable distinction. A sequence can be harmonically stable but registrally unstable, or vice versa.

# Examples
- Figure 4.1.3: Bach WTC G# minor fugue -- upper two voices are registrally stable (canonic), bass is in contrary motion
- Most standard descending-fifth sequences maintain registral stability

# Common Confusions
- Registral stability is about the distance between voices from one grand period to the next, not about whether individual voices stay in the same register
- A sequence can have some voice groups registrally stable and others not, within the same passage
