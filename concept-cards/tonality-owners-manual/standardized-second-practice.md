# Quick Definition
The systematic approach to nonharmonic tones that emerged by the late baroque, incorporating new devices like seventh chords, cadential 64 chords, incomplete neighbors, and pedals within rules nearly as strict as Palestrina's.

# Formal Definition
By the late baroque, the Monteverdian freedom of the seconda prattica had been domesticated into what Tymoczko calls the "standardized second practice." Eighteenth-century composers embraced seventh chords, the cadential 64, incomplete neighbors, and pedals, but subjected them to rules almost as inviolable as those of the first practice. J. S. Bach is astoundingly systematic: of more than ten thousand nonharmonic tones in his chorales, all but about ten are well-behaved. This system remains in force throughout much of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

# Musical Context
The standardized second practice imposes a powerful constraint on musical analysis: given a passage of baroque or classical music, the theorist must find an analysis that rationalizes both melodic and harmonic behavior. Dissonances must belong to recognized species of nonharmonic tone, and harmonic successions must obey functional norms. These requirements imply that notes both leapt-to and leapt-away-from must necessarily be harmonic.

# Common Confusions
- The standardized second practice is not the same as Monteverdi's seconda prattica. Monteverdi advocated aesthetic freedom from rules; the standardized second practice subjects new devices to equally strict rules.
- Few composers after Monteverdi came anywhere close to his radical freedom until the early twentieth century. The notated tradition retained a "scholastic streak" right up to the modern era.
