# Quick Definition

The empirically supported observation that listeners' abilities to perceive large-scale musical structures -- such as long-range key relationships and tonal return in sonata form -- are far more limited than theorists have traditionally assumed.

# Core Definition

Tymoczko argues that "we should take seriously those experiments suggesting that our powers of long-range musical perception are less impressive than theorists have traditionally believed" and "should be sensitive to the social and institutional forces that encourage us to hide our limitations, exaggerating the power of our musical ears" (p. 404). Specifically, there is concern that "experts confidently claim to hear vast stretches of music 'representing' or 'prolonging' a single note; that they claim to hear a note in one measure directly connecting to another fifty measures away; or that they claim to hear a sense of return to the tonic after ten or more minutes" (p. 404). The danger is that "some substantial proportion of these claims might not be grounded in anything real" (p. 404).

Yet theory can also "sometimes change our experience" (p. 404), genuinely enriching perception. The challenge is "to stretch our ears while remaining realistic about human limitation" (p. 406).

# Key Properties

1. Most listeners cannot directly perceive long-range key structure
2. Absolute-pitch listeners may have direct access; relative-pitch listeners do not
3. Experiments suggest vulnerability to perceptual tricks (e.g., pieces rewritten to end in foreign keys)
4. Social and institutional forces encourage exaggeration of perceptual powers
5. Repeated listening provides cognitive access without guaranteeing genuine perceptual experience
6. Theory can sometimes genuinely change and enrich experience

# Construction / Recognition

## To Evaluate Perceptual Claims:
1. Ask whether the claimed perception requires absolute pitch
2. Consider whether the perception survives without score study or analytical training
3. Be skeptical of first-person testimony about long-range hearing
4. Distinguish "knowing that" from "feeling it directly"

# Context & Application

This limitation challenges Schenkerian claims about large-scale prolongation, sonata-form theories emphasizing tonal return, and any analytical approach positing perceptible connections across large temporal spans. Done badly, theory "is like playing tennis without a net, elevating blatantly implausible claims to the status of institutional common sense" (p. 406). Done well, it is "a delicate and intimate form of science that increases the world's supply of aesthetic value" (p. 406).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 402--403): Experiments showing listeners are vulnerable to tricks such as rewriting a piece to end in a foreign key.

**Example 2** (p. 403, citing Rosen 1988): The claim that sonata recapitulation creates "the most radical transformation of a theme from dissonance to resolution" -- challenged on perceptual grounds.

**Example 3** (p. 404, citing Cook 1987): It is questionable whether even absolute-pitch listeners experience recapitulation as a release of tension comparable to a dominant resolving.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Dismissing all large-scale analysis as meaningless because of perceptual limits
  **Correction**: Theory can genuinely enrich experience; the point is realistic skepticism, not nihilism

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking that listeners who track modulations through repeated listening are thereby demonstrating direct perceptual sensitivity
  **Clarification**: Cognitive access through repeated exposure is different from direct perceptual experience
