# Quick Definition

The historical process by which functional tonality expanded its chord vocabulary from triads to seventh chords and the cadential 6/4, driven by the need to harmonize descending melodies within a functional framework that tends to produce ascending melodic motion.

# Core Definition

Functional-harmonic restrictions likely drove the expansion from triads to seventh chords, partly as a way of recovering lost melodic possibilities (p. 330). "Strong" progressions (descending third, descending fifth, ascending step) tend to generate ascending melodic steps through efficient triadic voice leading, creating a tension with the preference for descending melody. Scale-degree progressions such as 2-1-7, which had indigenous modal harmonizations (e.g., ii-IV-V or ii-I6-V), gradually acquired functional alternatives involving sevenths and I6/4 (e.g., ii-ii7-V or ii-I6/4-V). The seventh chord fills in the descending third that arises from larger motions on the triadic circle. Similarly, I-iii-IV (a modal solution for 8-7-6 supporting I-?-IV) was supplemented by I-I-maj7-IV (pp. 328-330, Figs. 7.4.4-6).

# Key Properties

1. Seventh chords are not merely "richer" sonorities but solutions to a specific compositional problem
2. The problem: "strong" functional progressions tend to generate ascending melodic steps
3. Seventh chords fill in descending thirds within larger triadic voice-leading motions
4. The cadential 6/4 embellishes the dominant, providing another way to accommodate descending melody
5. The expansion is driven by the dialogue between harmonic and melodic forces
6. Five strategies composers use to negotiate the tension: (a) using "weak" progressions, (b) larger voice leadings, (c) descending-third melodies, (d) expanding harmonic vocabulary, (e) surface voices descending against ascending background

# Construction / Recognition

## To Understand Vocabulary Expansion:
1. Identify the melodic pattern (e.g., 5-4-3 descending)
2. Note the functional harmonic constraint (e.g., V-?-I)
3. Observe modal solutions that use non-functional chords (e.g., V-flat-VII-I)
4. See how functional solutions use seventh chords to fuse adjacent triads (e.g., V-V7-I)
5. Recognize I6/4 as a dominant embellishment rather than a tonic chord

# Context & Application

This expansion illustrates how the development of functional tonality was not purely harmonic but involved a dialogue between harmonic and melodic forces. In the V-?-I context with melody 5-4-3, modal options include IV6, flat-VII, vii-dim-6, and ii (Fig. 7.4.4). Functional music fuses V and vii-dim into a dominant-seventh composite (Figs. 7.4.5-6). This suggests that functional rules cannot be derived from voice-leading considerations alone; instead, "something like the opposite is true: at the most general level, melodic goals generate nonfunctional triadic progressions; to counteract this tendency, composers need to replace those triadic progressions with more complex alternatives" (p. 330).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating seventh chords as mere "enrichment" added to a complete triadic system
  **Correction**: Seventh chords are intrinsic to Rameau's theory and partly driven by the need to reconcile harmonic and melodic forces -- they solve a compositional problem, not just add color

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the cadential 6/4 is a tonic chord in second inversion
  **Clarification**: The cadential 6/4 is functionally a dominant embellishment, part of the expanded vocabulary for negotiating descending melody within functional constraints
