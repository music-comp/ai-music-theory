# Quick Definition
The historical process by which functional tonality expanded its chord vocabulary from triads to seventh chords and the cadential 6/4, driven by the need to harmonize descending melodies within a functional framework.

# Formal Definition
Functional-harmonic restrictions drove the expansion from triads to seventh chords as a way of recovering melodic possibilities lost during the transition from modal to functional harmony. Scale-degree progressions such as ^2--^1--^7 had indigenous modal harmonizations (e.g., ii--IV--V or ii--I^6^--V) that gradually acquired functional alternatives involving sevenths and I6/4 (e.g., ii--ii^7^--V or ii--I6/4--V). The seventh chord fills in the descending third that arises from larger motions on the triadic circle. Similarly, I--iii--IV (a modal solution for ^8--^7--^6 supporting I--?--IV) was supplemented by I--I^maj7^--IV.

# Musical Context
This expansion illustrates how the development of functional tonality was not purely harmonic but involved a dialogue between harmonic and melodic forces. The seventh chord is not merely a "richer" sonority but a solution to a specific compositional problem.

# Common Confusions
- Seventh chords are not an "enrichment" added to an already-complete triadic system: they are intrinsic to Rameau's theory and partly driven by the need to reconcile harmonic and melodic forces.
- The cadential 6/4 is functionally a dominant embellishment, not a tonic chord in second inversion.
