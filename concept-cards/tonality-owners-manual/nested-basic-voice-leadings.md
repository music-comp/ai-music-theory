# Quick Definition
Two or more basic voice leadings operating simultaneously at different structural levels -- for example, a triadic basic voice leading operating within a diatonic scale whose own basic voice leading is simultaneously active.

# Formal Definition
In Beethoven's Ninth Symphony scherzo passage (mm. 143--176), the chordal basic voice leading moves each triad's fifth up by scale step to become the root of the next triad (clockwise on the triadic spiral), while the scalar basic voice leading lowers the leading tone semitonally to connect fifth-related scales (counterclockwise on the scalar spiral). These are "hierarchically self-similar transformations occurring on two different structural levels" (Figure P8.3). The notation combines diatonic transposition T with chromatic transposition **T**: the scalar voice leading **T**_{-7}t_4 lowers the leading tone by semitone while keeping every other note fixed.

# Musical Context
This concept contrasts with the neo-Riemannian analysis of the same Beethoven passage, which models it with two nonhierarchical chromatic transformations (R and L) in alternation, without the mediation of diatonic collections. Tymoczko's hierarchical analysis describes the sequence using standard harmonic procedures and offers a unified explanation of the voice leadings, generalizable to any chord in any scale.

# Common Confusions
- The neo-Riemannian and hierarchical analyses are not right or wrong as a matter of fact -- they highlight different aspects of the music. The neo-Riemannian analysis connects to nineteenth-century chromaticism; the hierarchical analysis connects to standard diatonic procedures.
- Nested basic voice leadings are not restricted to the Beethoven passage: they are the general mechanism underlying modulatory sequences that generate repeating patterns of Roman numerals.
