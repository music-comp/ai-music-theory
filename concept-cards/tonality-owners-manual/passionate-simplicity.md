# Quick Definition
Tymoczko's characterization of Beethoven's distinctive aesthetic: pouring torrential expressive energy through a sieve of utterly standard devices, demonstrating that intellectual play with schematic content can coexist with unprecedented rhetorical power.

# Formal Definition
Passionate simplicity describes the paradox that Beethoven was simultaneously "highly cerebral and highly expressive" (p. 458). His music is built from sequences, schemas, and familiar contrary-motion idioms -- "commonplace material" (p. 458) -- yet achieves unprecedented emotional force. The intellectual content consists in exploring how basic functional idioms are quasi-sequential, fashioning music that highlights "the beauty of basic musical mathematics" (p. 471). This challenges the stereotype of Beethoven as purely a composer of overwhelming force, revealing instead "Beethoven theorist" -- a pattern-maker fascinated by the fundamental logic of functional harmony.

# Musical Context
This concept frames the entire Chapter 10. Tymoczko's aim is "to demystify Beethoven by showing how you might have written this music yourself" (p. 491), providing reductions simple enough to imagine inventing but complex enough to serve as compositional blueprints. The concept of passionate simplicity resolves the tension between the "Beethoven Hero" of Scott Burnham and the intellectual craftsman Tymoczko reveals.

# Examples
- Op. 10, no. 2, I, mm. 73-76: I-V-I progression announced as a contrary-motion near-fifth sequence, "both elementary and extraordinary" (Figure 10.2.12, p. 472)
- Hammerklavier second theme: four-unit Ludwig clarifying "the fundamental logic of functional harmony" (Figure 10.2.13, p. 472)
- Fifth Symphony coda: "beyond analysis, devoid of the non-obvious relationships that make verbal commentary worth reading" -- raw compositional force (p. 493)
- Pastorale sonata: Beethoven "seems to declare that he is a composer of patterns rather than melodies" (p. 500)

# Common Confusions
- "Passionate simplicity" does not mean Beethoven's music is simple -- the individual devices are simple, but their combination and deployment are extraordinarily sophisticated
- The concept challenges the mutual exclusivity of intellectual and emotional engagement in music
- Beethoven's motivic obsessiveness is less interesting to Tymoczko than the underlying patterns that recur from piece to piece
