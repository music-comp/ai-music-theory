# Quick Definition
The quadruple grouping structure of classical phrases, inherited from Western European dance music, in which four durational units are arranged to satisfy two constraints: that one unit repeats or varies another, and that the final unit forms a cadence.

# Formal Definition
Four two-bar groups can be organized into four main phrase types: ABAC (period), with the third unit recalling the first and organized as two loosely parallel halves; AABC (sentence), with immediate recall and organized as presentation (AA) plus continuation (BC); ABBC ("tail-development form"), where the third unit echoes the second; and AAAC, with multiple repetitions before the cadence. These can nest inside one another on multiple temporal levels, producing complex hierarchical structures such as sentences embedded within periods.

# Musical Context
These patterns become popular in the late eighteenth century and remain common in contemporary popular music. Classical composers often nest these templates on multiple temporal levels: an eight-bar sentence inside a sixteen-bar period, or even triply nested structures. Tymoczko argues for defining "sentence" and "period" in terms of grouping structure rather than Caplin's more subjective notion of "formal function."

# Examples
- Mozart's K.311, III: triply nested structure with a four-bar period continued by a four-bar sentence, forming an eight-bar sentence embedded in a sixteen-bar period (Figure 9.6.5, p. 439)
- Sam Cooke's "Cupid" (1961): two eight-bar sentences inside a sixteen-bar period (Figure 9.6.4, p. 439)
- Grace VanderWaal's "So Much More Than This" (2017): ABBC phrase; Marshmello's "Happier" (2018): ABAC period (Figure 9.6.3, p. 438)
- Beethoven's Hammerklavier scherzo: elision where m. 4 simultaneously ends one schema and begins another (Figure 9.6.6, pp. 438-439)

# Common Confusions
- Sentence and period are general grouping strategies available on any temporal level, not eight-bar structures with specific "formal functions" as Caplin defines them
- The ABBC "tail-development" form has no standard name but does occasionally appear in classical and popular music
- Quadruple grouping is not universal -- earlier music and more complex passages often have more flexible phrasing
