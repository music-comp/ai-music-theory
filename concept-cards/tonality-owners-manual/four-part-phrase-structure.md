# Quick Definition

The quadruple grouping structure of classical phrases, inherited from Western European dance music, in which four durational units are arranged to satisfy two constraints: that one unit repeats or varies another, and that the final unit forms a cadence.

# Core Definition

Four two-bar groups can be organized into four main phrase types satisfying the constraints of repetition and cadential closure (p. 437): ABAC (period), with the third unit recalling the first, organized as two loosely parallel halves with weak close after AB and stronger close after AC; AABC (sentence), with immediate recall, organized as presentation (AA) and continuation (BC); ABBC ("tail-development form"), where the third unit echoes the second; and AAAC, with multiple repetitions before the cadence. These templates can nest inside one another on multiple temporal levels.

# Prerequisites

- **Early-phrase vs. cadential progressions** -- Understanding how harmonic cycles differ across the phrase is needed to recognize these structures
- **Cadential weight** -- The concept of increasing conclusiveness is integral to how phrase types close

# Key Properties

1. Four basic arrangements satisfy the two constraints (repetition + cadence)
2. ABAC (period) and AABC (sentence) are by far the most common
3. ABBC ("tail-development form") is rare but does appear in classical and popular music
4. AAAC involves multiple repetitions before cadence (e.g., Beethoven's Op. 109 theme)
5. Templates can nest on multiple temporal levels (e.g., eight-bar sentences inside sixteen-bar periods)
6. Elision can produce overlapping formal units

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Phrase Type:
1. Divide the theme into four roughly equal durational units
2. Determine which units share melodic or motivic material
3. Identify the cadential unit (typically the fourth)
4. Classify: ABAC = period, AABC = sentence, ABBC = tail-development, AAAC = repeated-presentation
5. Check for nesting on larger temporal levels

# Context & Application

These patterns become popular in the late eighteenth century and remain common in contemporary popular music. Tymoczko advocates defining "sentence" and "period" as general grouping strategies available on any temporal level, rather than Caplin's eight-bar structures with specific "formal functions" determined by bass motion. This "thinner, more objective" definition gives "relatively neutral categories" for expressing different analytical perceptions (p. 442).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 439): Mozart's K.311, III -- triply nested structure with a four-bar period continued by a four-bar sentence, forming an eight-bar sentence embedded in a sixteen-bar period.

**Example 2** (p. 439): Sam Cooke's "Cupid" (1961) nests two eight-bar sentences inside a sixteen-bar period.

**Example 3** (p. 438): Grace VanderWaal's "So Much More Than This" (2017) as ABBC phrase; Marshmello's "Happier" (2018) as ABAC period.

**Example 4** (pp. 438-439): Beethoven's Hammerklavier scherzo -- elision where m. 4 simultaneously ends one four-bar schema and begins another "Ludwig" sentence.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Requiring all themes to fit one of four types exactly
  **Correction**: Many themes use hybrid or flexible arrangements; these are idealized categories

- **Error**: Assuming sentence and period must be exactly eight bars
  **Correction**: These grouping strategies can operate at any temporal level

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Defining sentence and period by "formal function" rather than grouping
  **Clarification**: Tymoczko argues that Caplin's function-based definitions lead to subjective judgments about rhetorical role, whereas grouping-based definitions are more intersubjective

- **Confusion**: Thinking ABBC "tail-development" form has no classical precedents
  **Clarification**: Examples appear in Haydn (Hob. XVI:8, IV), Mozart (K.304), and Beethoven (Op. 31 no. 3; Op. 79)
