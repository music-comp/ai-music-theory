# Quick Definition
The principle that nearly even chords can be understood as slight perturbations of completely symmetrical chords, generating families of related progressions through small distortions.

# Formal Definition
The Principle of Musical Approximation encourages us to think of nearly even chords as perturbations of completely symmetrical ones. A sequence of semitonally descending augmented triads can be labeled in multiple ways (C+-B+-Bb+, C+-Eb+-Gb+, C+-G+-D+, etc.) since the augmented triad is completely symmetrical, making these conceptual rather than sonic differences. The major triad, being only one semitone away from the augmented triad, nearly divides the octave into three equal parts. By lowering any note of each augmented triad, we obtain descending major-chord progressions with the same root intervals but sonically distinctive characters.

# Musical Context
This principle explains why major-third substitution works: because the triad is nearly symmetrical, substituting one major-third-related triad for another has only a small effect on the voices. The resulting progressions can be thought of as slight distortions of continuous parallel descent -- an asymmetrical major-chord analogue to perfectly parallel augmented-chord motion. The principle applies to minor triads (raising an augmented-triad note), diminished triads, and other chord types.

# Common Confusions
- The principle does not say nearly even chords *are* symmetric -- it says they can be productively *modeled* as perturbations of symmetric ones
- The closer a chord is to perfect symmetry (e.g., augmented triad), the tighter the connection between clockwise motion and stepwise descent
- More uneven chords (like 025 trichords) still follow the same geometry but with more contrary motion (Figure 2.6.1)
