# Quick Definition

The principle that nearly even chords can be understood as slight perturbations of completely symmetrical chords, generating families of related progressions through small distortions of perfectly parallel motion.

# Core Definition

The Principle of Musical Approximation "encourages us to think of nearly even chords as perturbations of completely symmetrical chords" (p. 52). The major triad is "nearly symmetrical -- just a single semitone away from the augmented triad -- and divides the octave into three almost-even parts" (p. 52). By lowering any note of each augmented triad in a descending sequence, "we can obtain a variety of descending major-chord progressions" with "the same root progressions we obtain in the augmented case, only now they are sonically distinctive" (p. 53). These "can be thought of as slight distortions of a continuous parallel descent" (p. 53).

# Key Properties

1. Nearly even chords are perturbations of symmetrical ones
2. Major triad is one semitone from augmented triad
3. Symmetrical chord sequences can be labeled multiple ways (conceptual, not sonic, differences)
4. Lowering/raising notes of symmetrical chords produces families of related progressions
5. The closer to symmetry, the tighter the connection between clockwise motion and descending voice leading
6. More uneven chords (like 025) require more distortion and produce more contrary motion

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply:
1. Start with a sequence of symmetrical chords (augmented triads descending by semitone)
2. Note that the sequence can be relabeled in multiple equivalent ways
3. Lower one note of each symmetrical chord to produce a major triad sequence
4. The resulting progressions share the same root intervals but are now sonically distinctive
5. Any members can be related by major-third substitution (t_1 **T**_{-4})

# Context & Application

The principle works for any chord type near symmetry: minor triads (raising augmented notes), diminished triads, dominant sevenths (near diminished sevenths), etc. It explains why major-third-related triads are efficient voice-leading neighbors despite being diatonically distant.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 52, Figure 2.1.7 top): Descending augmented triads labeled as C+-B+-Bb+ = C+-Eb+-Gb+ = C+-G+-D+.

**Example 2** (p. 53, Figure 2.1.7 bottom): Lowering a note of each produces root progressions C-B-Bb, C-Eb-Gb, C-G-D, all moving one step clockwise on the spiral.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Thinking nearly even chords are identical to symmetrical ones
  **Correction**: They are modeled as perturbations; the small asymmetry creates sonically distinctive progressions

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing the principle only applies to major triads
  **Clarification**: It applies to any chord near a symmetrical archetype (minor triads, seventh chords, etc.)
