# Quick Definition
The observation that functional tonality uses virtually the same repertoire of progressions in both major and minor modes, with the minor mode's greater attraction to the mediant (III) being a pre-existing modal tendency rather than a deviation.

# Formal Definition
Functional tonality exhibits an unusual degree of modal symmetry compared to other tonal systems. In rock, for example, VI--VII--i is common in aeolian but rare in ionian, while I--V--IV--I is common in ionian but rare in aeolian. Functional tonality avoids such mode-dependent asymmetries, using essentially the same set of allowed progressions in both major and minor. The one distinctive feature of minor-mode harmony is its greater attraction to the mediant -- both as a modulatory destination and as a tonicized chord within phrases. This reflects the fact that III remained the primary minor-mode harmonization of bass ^3 until well into the seventeenth century, being displaced by i^6^ long after I^6^ had become a major-mode default.

# Musical Context
The modal symmetry of functional tonality is historically contingent rather than logically necessary. Understanding the minor mode's mediant attraction as a historical survival (rather than a deviation from major-mode norms) is essential for understanding functional harmony as a historically evolving practice.

# Common Confusions
- The minor mode's mediant attraction is not a "rule" to be derived from first principles -- it is a historical accident showing that functional harmony is a contingent development, not a self-consistent logical system.
- Do not assume that minor-mode functionality is simply major-mode functionality transposed: the modes share a grammar but have distinct historical origins.
