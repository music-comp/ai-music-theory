# Quick Definition

The observation that functional tonality uses virtually the same repertoire of progressions in both major and minor modes, with the minor mode's greater attraction to the mediant (III) being a pre-existing modal tendency rather than a deviation from an established major-mode default.

# Core Definition

Functional tonality exhibits an unusual degree of modal symmetry compared to other tonal systems. In rock, for example, VI-VII-i is common in aeolian but rare in ionian, while I-V-IV-I is common in ionian but rare in aeolian. Functional tonality avoids such mode-dependent asymmetries, "using virtually the same repertoire of progressions in both major and minor" (p. 315). The one distinctive feature of minor-mode harmony is its greater attraction to the mediant -- both as a modulatory destination and as a tonicized chord within phrases. This reflects the historical fact that III remained the primary minor-mode harmonization of bass scale-degree 3 until well into the seventeenth century, being displaced by i6 long after I6 had become a major-mode default (p. 316). Tymoczko emphasizes that this asymmetry is historically contingent rather than logically necessary.

# Key Properties

1. Functional tonality uses essentially the same set of allowed progressions in both major and minor
2. This modal symmetry is unusual compared to other tonal systems (e.g., rock)
3. The minor mode's distinctive mediant attraction is a historical survival, not a deviation
4. III remained the primary minor-mode harmonization of bass 3 until well into the 17th century
5. The functional tradition is like an "hourglass" -- Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven represent maximal harmonic regimentation (p. 315)
6. In Palestrina's masses, III is significantly more common in minor-mode passages than in Bach's chorales (discussed further in Ch. 8)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Observe Modal Symmetry:
1. Compare the set of common progressions in major and minor pieces from the same period
2. Note that most progressions (I-IV-V-I, I-ii-V-I, I-vi-IV-ii-V-I, etc.) occur in both modes
3. Identify the mediant (III) as the primary asymmetry -- more common as a destination in minor
4. Track how III usage changes historically -- from frequent in Palestrina to less common in classical style

# Context & Application

Understanding the minor mode's mediant attraction as a historical survival is essential for understanding functional harmony as a historically evolving practice rather than a self-consistent logical system. The asymmetry between modes diminished over time as minor-mode practice increasingly mirrored major-mode norms, but the minor mode never fully abandoned its preexistent modal attraction toward the mediant. This insight has implications for understanding why modulations to III are more common in minor-key pieces than modulations to iii in major-key pieces.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming minor-mode functionality is simply major-mode functionality transposed
  **Correction**: The modes share a grammar but have distinct historical origins, particularly regarding the mediant

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Treating the minor mode's mediant attraction as a rule derivable from first principles
  **Clarification**: It is "a historical accident showing that functional harmony is a contingent development, not a self-consistent logical system" (p. 316)
