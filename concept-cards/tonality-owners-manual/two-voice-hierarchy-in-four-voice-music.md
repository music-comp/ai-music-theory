# Quick Definition

The principle that four-voice triadic counterpoint often reduces to two fundamental lines: alto and tenor shadow the soprano in doubly parallel motion along both chord and scale, while the bass moves independently. This qualitative change occurs when voice count exceeds chord size.

# Core Definition

When upper voices move from close to close or open to open position, they articulate doubly parallel motion (transposition along both chord and scale), reducing three upper voices to one fundamental line. Combined with an independent bass, four voices reduce to two (Figure 3.6.1, p. 126). "While it is possible to understand two- and three-voice counterpoint using nonhierarchical models, four-voice triadic counterpoint is more consistently hierarchical, with voices often moving inside an abstract and scale-like harmony" (p. 47). This qualitative change reflects that "with four or more voices, they are largely restricted to a single sonority and hence more reliant on voice exchanges" (p. 125). Historically, "two and three voices often feature in counterpoint classes, with four voices relegated to the domain of harmony; at a more fundamental level, however, this is a difference within counterpoint itself, a boundary we inevitably cross when the number of voices increases beyond the size of our harmonies" (pp. 125-126).

# Key Properties

1. Upper three voices reduce to one line via doubly parallel motion
2. Bass moves independently, creating a two-voice framework
3. Qualitative change occurs when voice count exceeds chord size
4. More than 30% of Bach chorale transitions are close-to-close
5. The grouping is flexible: voices can also divide into pairs or soprano vs. lower three
6. Inner-voice passing tones transform the skeleton into living music

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify:
1. Look for upper voices maintaining consistent configuration (C->C or O->O)
2. Check that upper voices move in parallel along both chord and scale
3. Verify bass moves independently
4. Note where the pattern breaks for analytical interest

# Context & Application

Bach's chorales show more than 30% of transitions connecting close-to-close triads, with another 12% open-to-open (Figures 3.6.2-3.6.3, p. 126). The principle underlies why counterpoint pedagogy historically focuses on two and three voices. The split is not always bass-vs-upper-voices: voices can divide into pairs (Figure 3.7.1, p. 132), or soprano against lower three. Tallis's Spem in alium is the extreme case: forty voices moving within a shifting three-note scalar field (Figure 3.7.7, p. 137).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming inner voices are unimportant because they "shadow" the soprano
  **Correction**: The reduction is structural; passing tones and voice-leading details in inner voices transform the skeleton into living counterpoint

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the bass/upper-voice split is the only possibility
  **Clarification**: Voices can group in other ways (pairs, soprano vs. lower three); the bass/upper-voice split is one organizational strategy
