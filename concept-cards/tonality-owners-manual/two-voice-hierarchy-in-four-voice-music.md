# Quick Definition
The principle that four-voice triadic counterpoint often reduces to two fundamental lines, with the alto and tenor shadowing the soprano in doubly parallel motion along both chord and scale, while the bass moves independently.

# Formal Definition
When upper voices move from close to close or open to open position, they articulate doubly parallel motion (transposition along both chord and scale), reducing three upper voices to one fundamental line. Combined with an independent bass, four voices reduce to two. This is why four-voice counterpoint qualitatively differs from two- or three-voice: with four or more voices in triadic contexts, composers are largely restricted to a single sonority type and hence more reliant on voice exchanges, as the number of voices exceeds the size of the harmony.

# Musical Context
This reduction is not a theoretical simplification but reflects how composers seem to think. Bach's chorales show more than 30% of transitions connecting close-to-close triads, with another 12% open-to-open. The principle underlies why counterpoint classes historically focus on two and three voices, with four voices relegated to "harmony" -- at a deeper level, this is a boundary within counterpoint itself, crossed when voice count exceeds chord size. The split is not always bass-vs-upper-voices: voices can also divide into pairs, or soprano against lower three.

# Common Confusions
- The reduction to two voices is a structural principle, not a claim that inner voices are unimportant; passing tones and voice-leading details in inner voices are what transform the skeleton into living music
- The grouping of voices is flexible and context-dependent, not always bass vs. upper three
