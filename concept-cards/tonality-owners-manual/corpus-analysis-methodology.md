# Quick Definition
Tymoczko's empirical approach to music theory combining computational corpus analysis with traditional scholarship: releasing open data and code while writing informally, focusing on large observable effects rather than subtle statistical findings, and understanding musical grammars as interpretive summaries of statistical tendencies.

# Formal Definition
Musical grammars are understood as "interpretive summaries of statistical tendencies" (p. 573) rather than exceptionless rules. The word "interpretive" serves a double function: acknowledging that multiple theories can account for the same observations (underdetermination), and pointing to the holistic nature of the enterprise where harmonic labeling, nonharmonic-tone identification, and harmonic theorizing are interdependent. The approach follows open-data philosophy, releasing all code and data for reproduction and criticism, while choosing informal presentation over null-hypothesis significance testing. Large effects can often be seen without sophisticated statistics.

# Musical Context
The methodology underlies the entire book's empirical claims. Corpus analysis serves multiple practical functions: for analysts, directing attention toward genuinely exceptional features; for students, suggesting what might come next; for theorists, pointing toward hypotheses about why practice developed as it did; for cognitive scientists, hinting at mental representations constituting stylistic competence. The approach acknowledges that "the paradox of analysis is that we have to know what we're looking for in order to find it" (p. 570).

# Examples
- Bach chorale data: 45 instances of I-X-I6, with 34 being I-vii-degrees-6-I6 and only 1 being I-ii-I6 (p. 569)
- Mozart piano sonatas: ~115 I-X-I6 progressions without a single indisputable I-ii-I6 (p. 569)
- Huron's treatment of ii-vii-degrees-6 idiom: different analytical choices produce a factor-of-10 discrepancy in the frequency of ii-I progressions (p. 570)
- Data and code available at madmusicalscience.com/taom (p. 571)

# Common Confusions
- Musical grammaticality is not the same as frequency, but unlike language, music may lack the semantic dimension that supports the distinction
- Theory-neutral datasets may be impossible because analysis is theory-dependent -- this is circular but "not necessarily viciously so" (p. 573)
- The approach focuses on large effects visible to the "naked eye" rather than subtle statistical patterns that may be artifacts of methodology
- Classical music is no longer a living tradition, so contemporary composition cannot adjudicate questions about historical grammaticality
