# Quick Definition

An analytical framework treating accompanimental textures as melodies moving along small, shifting chord-based scales rather than as concrete chorale-like voices. This models the "richer ontology" of eighteenth-century musicians who recognized accompanimental texture as qualitatively different from polyphony.

# Core Definition

The chords-as-scales approach "allows us to model this richer ontology without abandoning analytical rigor" (p. 206). It postulates "a concrete bass and soprano, the latter articulated by the top of an arpeggio that moves along a shifting triadic alphabet" (p. 207). The arpeggio "activates a background not localized to any specific register and substantially immune to standard contrapuntal strictures" (p. 207). "There is no possibility of forbidden parallels within the abstract background, nor between background and surface; parallels occur between concrete voices only" (p. 207). This recalls figured-bass thinking, "which often combines concrete soprano and bass with abstract harmonies" (p. 207).

# Key Properties

1. Treats accompaniment as melodies along chord-based scales, not chorale voices
2. Postulates concrete outer voices with abstract middle voices
3. Background not localized to specific register
4. Immune to standard contrapuntal strictures within and between background and surface
5. Parallels only between concrete voices
6. Recalls figured-bass thinking (concrete soprano/bass + abstract harmonies)
7. More analytically consistent than forced de-arpeggiation

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply:
1. Identify the concrete outer voices (soprano from top of arpeggio, bass)
2. Treat the arpeggio as activating a register-free chordal background
3. Analyze the soprano's motion along the shifting chordal scale
4. Check only concrete-voice parallels (not background or surface-background)

# Context & Application

Beethoven's Op. 2 no. 3 trio is analyzed with this approach (Figure P5.7, p. 207): a regular melodic sequence in a shifting scalar background avoids the problems of Schachter's chorale-style reduction. Weber (1817-1821) explicitly argued that arpeggiated lines are "not actual parts but only 'imagined' parts, and hence are less strictly bound by the rule against parallel fifths" (p. 207, fn. 7). The extreme notes of arpeggiation patterns tend to be treated as concrete voices when they form the outer voices of a texture.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Abandoning analytical rigor when using the chords-as-scales approach
  **Correction**: The approach maintains rigor by precisely defining which voices are concrete and which are abstract

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking this approach means anything goes for accompaniment
  **Clarification**: Parallels between concrete outer voices are still relevant; the approach relaxes only inner-voice and background-surface constraints
