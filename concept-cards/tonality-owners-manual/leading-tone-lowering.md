# Quick Definition
The flatward application of the diatonic scale's basic voice leading, lowering the leading tone (^7) by semitone to produce a scale one step clockwise on the circle of keys.

# Formal Definition
Leading-tone lowering is the flatward (clockwise) direction of the diatonic scale's basic voice leading. It moves a diatonic scale to its flat-side neighbor by lowering a single pitch class -- the leading tone -- by one chromatic semitone. For example, lowering B to B-flat transforms C major into F major. This operation corresponds to adding one flat (or subtracting one sharp) from the key signature. On the scalar spiral diagram, each clockwise step adds a flat. A series of five such steps maps C major's tonic to D-flat major's leading tone (Figure 8.1.7a).

# Musical Context
Leading-tone lowering is the mechanism behind the most common classical modulation: from tonic to subdominant (or equivalently, the reverse -- from dominant back to tonic). Beethoven's Ninth Symphony scherzo uses iterated leading-tone lowering at the scalar level (keys descending by fifth) while triads ascend by diatonic step at the chordal level (Figure P8.3).

# Examples
- C major to F major: Lower B to B-flat, keeping C, D, E, F, G, A fixed.
- Beethoven's Ninth scherzo: Eight clockwise steps along the scale spiral (Figure P8.3).
- Playing C major with fixed fingering, then lowering the fourth finger's note from B to B-flat to arrive at F major.

# Common Confusions
- Leading-tone lowering does not change the tonic's pitch class -- it changes which abstract voice (finger) plays the tonic role. After lowering B to B-flat, the tonic shifts from the third left-hand finger to the right-hand thumb (in Tymoczko's fingering example).
- Repeated leading-tone lowering accumulates flats: C--F--B-flat--E-flat--A-flat--D-flat--(etc.), eventually producing enharmonic equivalence.
