# Quick Definition

The flatward application of the diatonic scale's basic voice leading, lowering the leading tone (scale-degree 7) by semitone to produce a scale one step clockwise on the circle of keys -- the mechanism behind modulation from tonic to subdominant.

# Core Definition

Leading-tone lowering is the flatward (clockwise) direction of the diatonic scale's basic voice leading. It moves a diatonic scale to its flat-side neighbor by lowering a single pitch class -- the leading tone -- by one chromatic semitone. For example, lowering B to B-flat transforms C major into F major. This operation corresponds to adding one flat (or subtracting one sharp) from the key signature. On the scalar spiral diagram, each clockwise step adds a flat. A series of five such steps maps C major's tonic to D-flat major's leading tone (p. 364, Fig. 8.1.7a). This is the same operation calculated as **T**_{-7}t_4 in the spiral diagram recipe (p. 358).

# Key Properties

1. Lowers the leading tone (scale-degree 7) by one chromatic semitone
2. All other pitch classes remain fixed
3. Corresponds to adding one flat or subtracting one sharp
4. Clockwise motion on the scalar spiral diagram
5. Five successive applications map C major tonic to D-flat major leading tone (not tonic)
6. The mechanism behind modulation from tonic to subdominant (or dominant back to tonic)
7. Calculated as **T**_{-7}t_4 in the spiral diagram framework

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply Leading-Tone Lowering:
1. Identify the leading tone (7th degree) of the current key
2. Lower it by one chromatic semitone
3. All other notes remain fixed
4. The new key is a fifth below (or fourth above) the old tonic
5. At the piano with fixed fingering, the tonic shifts from one finger to another

# Context & Application

Leading-tone lowering is the mechanism behind modulation from tonic to subdominant. Beethoven's Ninth Symphony scherzo uses iterated leading-tone lowering at the scalar level (keys descending by fifth) while triads ascend by diatonic step at the chordal level -- eight clockwise steps along the scale spiral (p. 359, Fig. P8.3). Repeated leading-tone lowering accumulates flats: C-F-B-flat-E-flat-A-flat-D-flat, eventually producing enharmonic equivalence when the process completes a full circle.

# Examples

**Example 1**: C major to F major -- lower B to B-flat, keeping C, D, E, F, G, A fixed.

**Example 2** (p. 364, Fig. 8.1.7a): Five clockwise steps from C major to D-flat major, mapping the tonic to the new key's leading tone (150 degrees of clockwise motion).

**Example 3** (p. 359, Fig. P8.3): Beethoven's Ninth scherzo uses eight clockwise scalar steps (iterated leading-tone lowering) while triads take eighteen counterclockwise chordal steps.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Thinking leading-tone lowering changes the tonic's pitch class
  **Correction**: It does not change the tonic pitch class -- it changes which abstract voice (finger) plays the tonic role. After lowering B to B-flat, the tonic shifts from one finger to another (p. 363)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Assuming five flatward steps return to a scale-degree-equivalent position
  **Clarification**: Five flatward steps map C major's tonic to D-flat major's leading tone (not tonic), demonstrating that the two directions have asymmetric effects on scale-degree roles
