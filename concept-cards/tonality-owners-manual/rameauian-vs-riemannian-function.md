# Quick Definition

Two contrasting conceptions of harmonic function: Riemannian function defines chords by their *location* in harmonic space (tonic, subdominant, dominant), while Rameauian function defines them by their *way of moving* -- specifically, through the descending-fifth paradigm with third substitution.

# Core Definition

Riemannian functionality privileges primary triads as *locations* in harmonic space, grouping secondary chords into functional categories based on proximity to these poles. Rameauian functionality emphasizes the *motion* from tonic to dominant via a descending-fifth paradigm, where "root and third stay fixed while fifth and seventh descend by step" (p. 321, Figure 7.3.1). Rameau's *double emploi* allows a single triad (e.g., F--A--C) to stand for both F major and D minor, blurring third-related harmonies.

Tymoczko reconstructs a neo-Rameauian model: "well-behaved Rameauian progressions start with the tonic and move *forward* on the sequence of fifths to vii^o^ or V, either by descending fifth or using the *double emploi*" (p. 322). This produces seven progressions, all but one common in functional music. These are nearly equivalent to the progressions permitted by the descending-thirds model, since every Rameauian progression articulates a consecutive segment of the descending-third chain of pitch classes (Figure 7.3.3).

# Key Properties

1. Simple protofunctionality is Riemannian (emphasizing locations); mature functionality is more Rameauian (emphasizing motions)
2. Rameau's *double emploi* allows third-substitution, shortening the descending-fifth journey from tonic to dominant
3. The neo-Rameauian model produces seven progressions: the full circle of fifths, three four-chord progressions, and three shorter progressions
4. Every Rameauian progression articulates a segment of the descending-third pitch-class chain
5. Rameau's weakness is handling simple interchanges like I--V--I and I--IV--I, which it must treat as elisions

# Construction / Recognition

## To Analyze Using the Neo-Rameauian Model:
1. Start from the tonic on the descending-fifth sequence (I--IV--vii^o^--iii--vi--ii--V)
2. At each step, either continue by descending fifth or use third substitution (moving rightward by ascending step or descending third instead)
3. The progression should make a single forward pass through the sequence -- no backward motion
4. Verify that the result matches Figure 7.3.2

# Context & Application

In Bach's D major prelude from *The Well-Tempered Clavier*, the Rameauian perspective is particularly salient: "it feels as if the dancer is *moving in a particular way* rather than *visiting a collection of familiar places*" (p. 326). The prelude's progressions are 65% descending fifths, 19% descending thirds, with a smaller number of ascending and descending steps (Figure 7.3.7). This near-equivalence between Rameau's theory and Tymoczko's descending-thirds theory exemplifies theoretical underdetermination.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating one approach as "correct" and the other as "wrong"
  **Correction**: They offer different perspectives on the same practice; the choice is as much aesthetic as scientific

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking Rameau's theory is obsolete
  **Clarification**: It closely approximates modern functional theory and offers genuine insight, particularly for Bach's music where root motions feel salient
