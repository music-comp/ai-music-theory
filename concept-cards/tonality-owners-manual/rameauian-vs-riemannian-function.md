# Quick Definition
Two contrasting ways of conceiving harmonic function: Riemannian function defines chords by their *location* in harmonic space (tonic, subdominant, dominant), while Rameauian function defines them by their *way of moving* -- specifically, through types of root progression.

# Formal Definition
Riemannian functionality privileges primary triads as locations in harmonic space, grouping chords into functional categories (T, S, D) based on proximity to these poles. Rameauian functionality emphasizes the *motion* from tonic to dominant via a descending-fifth paradigm, where root and third stay fixed while fifth and seventh descend by step (Figure 7.3.1). Rameau's "double emploi" allows a single triad (e.g., F--A--C) to stand for both F major and D minor, blurring third-related harmonies. Both approaches are approximations to the same underlying practice -- an example of *theoretical underdetermination*, where different theories account for the same observable facts.

# Musical Context
Simple protofunctionality is decidedly Riemannian, privileging primary triads as locations. The more sophisticated descending-thirds model is more Rameauian, highlighting a specific way of moving from tonic to dominant. In Bach's music, particularly the D major prelude from *The Well-Tempered Clavier*, the Rameauian perspective often feels more salient: the music seems to be *moving in a particular way* rather than *visiting familiar places*.

# Common Confusions
- Neither approach is "right" or "wrong": they offer different perspectives on a practice that was largely understood tacitly. The choice between them is as much aesthetic as scientific.
- Rameau's theory has a weakness in handling simple interchanges like I--V--I and I--IV--I, treating them as elisions of more complex progressions. Tymoczko's descending-thirds model avoids this problem.
