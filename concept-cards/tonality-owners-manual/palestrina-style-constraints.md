# Quick Definition

The rigorous set of compositional constraints governing nonharmonic tone usage in Palestrina's music, representing the strictest application of the SNAP system, where virtually all dissonances can be labeled algorithmically and nonharmonic tones rarely obscure the entire duration of a consonance.

# Core Definition

The Palestrina style represents the most systematic application of the SNAP system (Suspensions, Neighbors, Anticipations, Passing tones). Its constraints include: (1) nonharmonic tones rarely obscure the entire duration of a consonance -- each takes up only part of a consonance; (2) nonharmonic tones rarely conspire to obscure a consonance entirely, making consonances "real sonic objects" that usually sound at some point during their lifespan (p. 213); (3) virtually all dissonances conform to SNAP categories; (4) the cambiata is the sole surviving incomplete neighbor; (5) anticipations almost always occur in the context of suspensions; (6) the downward resolution of suspensions is nearly inviolable. The resulting music is "astonishing in its systematicity, almost completely free of the occasional glitches that mark most human productions" (p. 212).

# Prerequisites

- **SNAP system** -- Understanding the four basic nonharmonic tone categories is needed to appreciate how Palestrina constrains them
- **Nonharmonic tone** -- The general concept of notes outside the prevailing harmony

# Key Properties

1. Music is highly susceptible to computational analysis due to extreme regularity and few changes of tonal center
2. A simple algorithm can label and remove virtually all nonharmonic tones, leaving a triadic skeleton
3. This is "perhaps the closest Western music has ever come to the theorist's dream of purely consonant harmony decorated by clearly nonharmonic embellishment" (p. 213)
4. Suspensions cluster near cadences rather than being distributed throughout phrases (Figs. 5.1.13-14)
5. Suspensions are unique in regularly licensing parallel fifths at the level of the harmonic skeleton
6. Despite systematicity, the style retains idioms (Renaissance sevenths, certain 6/4 chords) that resist clean nonharmonic reduction

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Palestrina Style Constraints:
1. Check that all nonharmonic tones belong to SNAP categories (suspension, neighbor, anticipation, passing tone)
2. Verify that consonances are real sonic objects -- not entirely obscured by nonharmonic tones
3. Look for the cambiata as the only permitted incomplete neighbor
4. Note that suspensions tend to cluster near cadential points
5. Confirm that suspensions resolve downward (with very rare exceptions)

# Context & Application

Counterpoint textbooks take Palestrina's practice as representative of the broader Renaissance tradition, but this is an oversimplification. Earlier polyphony (Josquin, Clemens) features incomplete neighbors, upward-resolving suspensions, and occasional inexplicable moments that Palestrina largely eliminates. The Palestrina style was codified into pedagogical rules, but the true grammar inferred from compositional practice is "inherently schematic, more complex and interesting than the simplifications found in textbooks" (p. 221). Pedagogues sometimes forbid practices that were merely somewhat uncommon, teaching an idealized version stricter than the actual style.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 212, Fig. 5.1.1): Pre-Palestrina irregularities -- Josquin's motet "Liber generationis" features both an incomplete neighbor and an upward-resolving suspension; Clemens's "Concussum est Mare" has a nonharmonic tone both leapt-to and leapt-away-from.

**Example 2** (pp. 218-220, Figs. 5.1.13-14): Statistical analysis of Palestrina's masses shows suspensions clustering near cadences, suggesting they serve as cadential signals rather than mere decoration.

**Example 3** (p. 215, Figs. 5.1.6-7): Edge cases including 6/4 chords that act as harmonies and "121" passing/neighboring tones that cut across the binary division of the beat.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating Palestrina's practice as perfectly systematic with no exceptions
  **Correction**: Even Palestrina retains idioms (Renaissance sevenths, some 6/4 chords) that resist nonharmonic reduction -- "inherited from the past and pointing toward the future" (p. 220)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking textbook rules perfectly capture Palestrina's actual practice
  **Clarification**: Pedagogues often forbid practices that were merely somewhat uncommon. Haydn's complaint about Kirnberger -- "too cautious, too confining" -- captures the gap between theory and practice (p. 211)

- **Confusion**: Believing the idealized consonant style of counterpoint textbooks actually existed
  **Clarification**: Palestrina wrote "just before and just after an imaginary consonant era" (p. 220), retaining pre-consonant idioms while anticipating future seventh chords
