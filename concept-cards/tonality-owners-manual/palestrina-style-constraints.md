# Quick Definition
The rigorous set of compositional rules governing nonharmonic tone usage, voice leading, and dissonance treatment in the music of Palestrina and his contemporaries.

# Formal Definition
The Palestrina style represents the strictest application of the SNAP system. Its constraints include: (1) nonharmonic tones rarely obscure the entire duration of a consonance; (2) each nonharmonic tone takes up only a part of a consonance; (3) virtually all dissonances can be categorized as suspensions, neighbors, anticipations, or passing tones; (4) the cambiata is the sole surviving incomplete neighbor; (5) anticipations almost always occur in the context of suspensions; (6) the downward resolution of suspensions is nearly inviolable. The resulting music is "astonishing in its systematicity, almost completely free of the occasional glitches that mark most human productions."

# Musical Context
Counterpoint textbooks take Palestrina's practice as representative of the broader Renaissance tradition, but this is an oversimplification -- Josquin, Palestrina, and others spoke different musical dialects. Palestrina's music is more susceptible to computational analysis than his predecessors' due to its extreme regularity. Despite this strictness, the style contains idioms (Renaissance sevenths, certain 64 chords) that resist clean nonharmonic reduction.

# Common Confusions
- Tymoczko cautions against distinguishing too sharply between Palestrina's style and later functional music. The same statistical tendencies (preference for primary triads, descending-fifth progressions) appear in Palestrina, albeit in weaker form.
- Pedagogues sometimes forbid practices that were merely somewhat uncommon in Palestrina, teaching an idealized version stricter than the actual style.
- The true grammar inferred from compositional practice is "inherently schematic, more complex and interesting than the simplifications found in textbooks."
