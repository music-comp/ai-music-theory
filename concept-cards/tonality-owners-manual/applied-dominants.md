# Quick Definition

Dominant or dominant-seventh chords that temporarily tonicize a non-tonic diatonic chord (written V/X or V7/X), enabling ascending-fifth modulations and chromatic enrichment within functional tonality.

# Core Definition

Applied (or secondary) dominants are chords that function as V or V7 of a diatonic chord other than the tonic. They are particularly characteristic of Bach's ascending-fifth sequences, where each sequential unit is tonicized by its own applied dominant. In the D major prelude from Book I of the Well-Tempered Clavier, keys ascend by fifth from D to A to E minor, B minor, and F-sharp minor, with each modulation enabled by applied dominants (p. 323, Fig. 7.3.5). The traditional rule of the octave calls for an applied dominant on scale-degree 6 (p. 332). In later classical style, ascending-fifth sequences are rarer and usually lack Bach's omnipresent tonicizations (p. 337).

# Prerequisites

- **Harmonic cycle theory** -- Understanding the descending-thirds model of functional progressions provides the framework for applied dominants
- **Subdominant and dominant cycles** -- The basic I-IV-V-I framework that applied dominants elaborate

# Key Properties

1. Written as V/X or V7/X, where X is the target chord
2. Characteristic of ascending-fifth sequences in Bach, where each unit is tonicized
3. The rule of the octave calls for an applied dominant on scale-degree 6
4. Create a chain of temporary key areas through successive tonicization
5. Less common in classical style than in baroque, with later composers using ascending fifths without systematic tonicization

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Applied Dominants:
1. Look for chromatic alterations that create a leading tone to a non-tonic chord
2. Check if the altered chord functions as V or V7 resolving to the target chord
3. In sequences, look for systematic tonicization of each new sequential unit
4. Note the presence of a resolving tritone directed toward the target chord

# Context & Application

Applied dominants play a crucial role in modulatory sequences and in the "up-and-down-the-ladder" schema (discussed in Ch. 8, section 5). They appear in the traditional rule of the octave as an applied dominant on scale-degree 6. Bach's use of omnipresent tonicization in ascending-fifth sequences represents a distinctive compositional approach that later composers largely abandoned, preferring less systematic chromatic enrichment.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming every applied dominant indicates a genuine modulation
  **Correction**: Applied dominants can function within a single key as chromatic embellishments; listeners cannot immediately distinguish a foreign dominant seventh as an applied chord versus the start of a new key

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking ascending-fifth sequences always require applied dominants
  **Clarification**: Bach's omnipresent tonicizations are characteristic of his style specifically; later classical composers used ascending-fifth sequences without systematic tonicization (p. 337)
