# Quick Definition
Statistical properties describing the likelihood that one chord will progress to another -- for example, the probability that V will go to I.

# Formal Definition
First-order properties describe the conditional probabilities of chord progressions: given chord X, what is the likelihood of chord Y following? Theories of functional harmony often highlight first-order properties, associating the experience of "dominantness" with a chord's tendency to move to the tonic. First-order properties can be further divided into forward-looking tendencies (e.g., for V to go to I) and backward-looking tendencies (e.g., for I to be approached by V). Dominants tend to be "constrained toward the future" while tonics are "constrained toward the past."

# Musical Context
First-order tendencies can arise from the conjunction of zeroth-order harmonic preferences and simple melodic constraints like the desire for stepwise motion. The distinction between forward-looking and backward-looking tendencies is key to understanding dominant tension and tonic release: when we hear a dominant, we have a good idea what is coming, while a tonic gives comparatively little sense of what might happen next.

# Common Confusions
- Some common progressions are common largely because of the popularity of their individual chords (zeroth-order effects), not because of genuine first-order tendencies. For example, IV-I in Palestrina may be common mainly because both IV and I are frequent.
- First-order properties alone do not define functionality. Tymoczko argues that functionality is "first and foremost a matter of chordal vocabulary" (zeroth-order).
- There are also second-order probabilities (the likelihood of a three-chord sequence continuing in a particular way), third-order, and so on.
