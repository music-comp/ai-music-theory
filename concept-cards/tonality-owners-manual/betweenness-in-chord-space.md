# Quick Definition
The geometrical property of the spiral diagram that encodes which chords can be inserted between two others without disrupting the overall voice-leading trajectory.

# Formal Definition
The spiral diagram gives a notion of betweenness, showing how larger voice leadings can be factored into smaller moves. A chord lies "between" two others on the spiral if inserting it preserves the overall direction and character of voice leading (e.g., stepwise descent). This geometrical structure encodes the musical intuition that certain chords lie "on the way" between others. These intuitions are packed into a single percept rather than many disconnected verbal descriptions -- "a picture is worth a thousand words."

# Musical Context
Betweenness is crucial for understanding how rock musicians construct chord progressions. The model shows that multiple chords can be inserted between a starting and ending chord while preserving the descending voice-leading trajectory. This explains why loop families contain multiple related progressions: they are different selections of "between" chords from the same overall path through chord space.

# Examples
- Two-step clockwise from C to Bb lowers each voice by major second; B major, Eb major, and G major all lie between C and Bb on the spiral, each preserving stepwise descending voice leading (Figure 2.1.6)
- The "Rose Parade" family: I-V-II-IV is a maximal selection of between-chords; eliminating any one produces "Helpless," "Eight Days a Week," or "Knockin' on Heaven's Door"
- The four angular positions on the spiral function as "slots" that can be filled or left open

# Common Confusions
- Betweenness is a geometrical (voice-leading) property, not a harmonic-function property -- chords that are functionally unrelated may be geometrically "between" others
- The concept applies to any spiral diagram, not just the 3-in-12 major triad diagram
- Betweenness on the spiral corresponds to voice-leading "factorization," not to temporal ordering in a piece
