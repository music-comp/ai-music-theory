# Quick Definition
The five strategies by which functional composers reconcile the tension between triadic harmonic progressions (which tend to produce ascending melodies) and the aesthetic preference for descending melodic motion.

# Formal Definition
"Strong" root progressions (descending fifth, descending third, ascending step) generate ascending triadic voice leading, conflicting with the general preference for descending melody. Tymoczko identifies five strategies composers use to resolve this tension: (1) using "weak" progressions that produce descending voice leading (IV--I, I--V); (2) larger voice-leading motions on the triadic circle, permitting passing tones that create brief seventh chords; (3) writing melodies that embed or disguise the circle of descending thirds; (4) expanding harmonic vocabulary to include seventh chords and the cadential 6/4; (5) having surface voices descend relative to an ascending background.

# Musical Context
This tension between harmonic and melodic forces is fundamental to functional tonality. Functional-harmonic restrictions likely drove the expansion from triads to seventh chords, partly as a way of recovering lost melodic possibilities that had indigenous modal harmonizations.

# Common Confusions
- Functional tonality does not avoid ascending fifths per se -- it avoids ascending fifths *not involving the tonic triad*. Progressions like IV--I and I--V are ubiquitous.
- It is unlikely that functional rules can be *derived* from voice-leading considerations alone: at the most general level, melodic goals generate nonfunctional triadic progressions, requiring more complex alternatives to maintain functionality.
