# Quick Definition

The five strategies by which functional composers reconcile the tension between triadic harmonic progressions (which tend to produce ascending melodies) and the aesthetic preference for descending melodic motion.

# Core Definition

"Strong" root progressions (descending fifth, descending third, ascending step) generate ascending triadic voice leading, conflicting with the general preference for descending melody. Tymoczko identifies five strategies composers use to resolve this: (1) using "weak" progressions that produce descending voice leading (especially IV--I and I--V, which are ubiquitous); (2) larger voice-leading motions on the triadic circle, permitting passing tones that create brief seventh chords (Figure 7.4.1); (3) writing melodies that embed or disguise the circle of descending thirds (Figure 7.4.2); (4) expanding harmonic vocabulary to include seventh chords and the cadential 6/4 -- fusing chords like V and vii^o^ into dominant-seventh composites (Figures 7.4.5--6); (5) having surface voices descend relative to an ascending background (p. 330).

# Prerequisites

- **Descending-thirds arrangement** -- The grammar that generates the tension between harmonic and melodic directions
- **Harmonic cycle theory** -- The framework within which the tension arises

# Key Properties

1. Functional tonality does not avoid ascending fifths *per se* -- it avoids ascending fifths *not involving the tonic triad*
2. Simple harmonic cycles like I--V--I often make a complete circle through the triadic space, enabling larger voice leadings
3. Functional-harmonic restrictions likely drove the expansion from triads to seventh chords as a way of recovering lost melodic possibilities
4. It is unlikely that functional rules can be *derived* from voice-leading considerations -- the opposite is closer to the truth
5. The five strategies are not mutually exclusive; composers routinely combine them

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify the Five Strategies:
1. **Weak progressions**: Look for IV--I, I--V, and other ascending-fifth motions involving the tonic
2. **Large voice leadings**: Look for complete circles on the triadic circle with passing tones
3. **Descending-third melodies**: Look for melodies embedding the descending-third sequence, harmonized in parallel or contrary motion
4. **Expanded vocabulary**: Look for seventh chords and I6/4 used to fill descending-third gaps between triadic harmonies
5. **Ascending background**: Look for surface melodic descent against background voice-leading ascent

# Context & Application

Scale-degree progressions such as 2--1--7, which had indigenous modal harmonizations (ii--IV--V, ii--I^6^--V), gradually acquired functional alternatives involving sevenths and I6/4 (ii--ii^7^--V, ii--I6/4--V). This suggests that "functional-harmonic restrictions likely drove the expansion from triads to seventh chords, partly as a way of recovering lost melodic possibilities" (p. 329).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 327, Figure 7.4.1): Functional tonality travels the "long way" around the triadic circle, creating larger voice leadings.

**Example 2** (pp. 328--329, Figures 7.4.4--6): Modal vs. functional approaches to harmonizing 5--4--3 over V--?--I. The functional solution fuses V and vii^o^ into a dominant seventh.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming functional composers always avoid ascending melodic motion
  **Correction**: Ascending motion is common; the issue is coordinating it with harmonic function, not eliminating it

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking functional rules can be derived from voice-leading principles alone
  **Clarification**: At the most general level, melodic goals generate *nonfunctional* triadic progressions; composers need more complex alternatives (seventh chords, I6/4) to maintain functionality
