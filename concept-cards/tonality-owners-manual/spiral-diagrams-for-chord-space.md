# Quick Definition
A two-dimensional geometrical representation of chord space in which each point represents an entire chord, with three types of motion (slide, loop, radial) corresponding to different musical operations.

# Formal Definition
A spiral diagram for an n-note chord in an o-note scale is constructed by: (A) Drawing a spiral with n loops, attaching its end to its beginning. The spiral starts clockwise at 12 o'clock and moves inward until reaching 9 o'clock for the nth time, then connects back to the start. (B) Marking o equally spaced points along this spiral, labeled with consecutive scale tones, by dividing the circle into o equal pie slices and placing a point at every nth slice border. Each point represents a *complete chord*. A chord's angular position corresponds to the sum of its pitch classes (mod scale size).

# Musical Context
The spiral diagrams are the central visual tool of the entire book, originally designed as pedagogical simplifications of higher-dimensional voice-leading geometry. They function as the "music-theoretical equivalent of a consumer product," allowing readers to enjoy sophisticated musical geometry without mastering its details. The book uses them to analyze rock music (3-in-12 graph), early music (2-in-7 and 3-in-7 graphs), and modulation (7-in-12 graph).

# Common Confusions
- Points represent *entire chords*, not single notes -- Tymoczko emphasizes this repeatedly as a common point of confusion
- The line's self-intersection is an artifact of 2D projection; the actual geometry does not self-intersect (appendix 2)
- The number of loops gives the chord size; the number of points gives the scale size
- Clockwise motion descends, counterclockwise ascends (because both descending musical motion and clockwise circular motion are defaults)
