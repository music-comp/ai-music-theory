# Quick Definition
In rock music, the sense of tonal center is often determined by phrasing, musical emphasis, and orchestration rather than by abstract pitch relationships or functional-harmonic resolution.

# Formal Definition
Rock centricity is frequently a matter of hypermetric accent, orchestral emphasis, and melodic contour rather than the dominant-tonic resolution that anchors classical tonality. Since all four loop families are rotations of the same underlying chord sequence, different songs can use identical pitch content with different tonal centers. This creates situations of genuine ambiguity -- "polytonality without dissonance" -- where scale degree is a function of attention, analogous to the duck/rabbit illusion.

# Musical Context
The flexibility of rock centricity allows for subtle modulations accomplished entirely through phrasing and orchestration, without changing the underlying chord loop. This suggests that centricity can be "a matter of delicate hints rather than unmistakable signposts." The concept challenges classical assumptions about how tonal centers are established and maintained.

# Examples
- O-Zone's "Dragostea Din Tei": modulates from C major (chorus) to A minor (verse) without departing from its C-G-a-F loop, accomplished hypermetrically by prolonging the G chord and then through orchestration (bass synths dropping out, Figure 2.5.6 context)
- The same D-A-E-G sequence heard as I-V-II-IV (D tonic = "Rose Parade"), I-V-bVII-IV (A tonic = "Natural Woman"), or I-bIII-bVII-IV (E tonic = "Air Near My Fingers"), Figure 2.5.6
- Three songs in three keys played simultaneously without dissonance (Figure 2.5.7)

# Common Confusions
- Rock centricity is not "tonality without function" -- many rock songs do use functional progressions, especially at cadences
- The ambiguity is not a defect but an expressive resource; performers can shift tonal center through subtle means
- Not all rock songs exhibit tonal ambiguity -- many have clear, unambiguous tonic
