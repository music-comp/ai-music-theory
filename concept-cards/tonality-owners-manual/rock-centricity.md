# Quick Definition

In rock, tonal centricity is "often a matter of phrasing and musical emphasis rather than abstract pitch relationships" (p. 68), established through hypermetric accent, orchestration, and melodic phrasing rather than harmonic function.

# Core Definition

Since the same four-chord sequence can be heard in four different keys depending on which chord receives the hypermetric accent, rock centricity is more fluid than classical tonality. O-Zone's "Dragostea Din Tei" modulates between C major and A minor "without ever departing from its repeating C-G-a-F loop" (p. 68), using hypermetric prolongation and orchestration (bass synthesizer drop-outs) to shift the sense of center. "These modulations suggest that centricity can be a matter of delicate hints rather than unmistakable signposts" (p. 68). Scale degree becomes "a function of attention" -- analogous to the duck/rabbit illusion (p. 67).

# Key Properties

1. Centricity established through phrasing, hypermetric accent, and orchestration
2. The same chord loop can support multiple tonal centers
3. Modulation can occur without changing the chord progression
4. Bass instrument entries/exits can shift the sense of center
5. Scale degree becomes "a function of attention" (duck/rabbit analogy)
6. Not all rock songs exhibit tonal ambiguity -- many have clear tonic

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Rock Centricity:
1. Identify the repeating chord loop
2. Note which chord receives the hypermetric accent (downbeat of the phrase)
3. Check whether melodic phrasing reinforces one chord as tonic
4. Listen for orchestrational cues (bass entries, texture changes)

# Context & Application

This concept challenges the assumption that centricity requires harmonic-functional confirmation (V-I cadences). In rock, centricity is more like a perspective on a shared progression than a fixed property of the music itself. The flexibility allows for expressively subtle modulations.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 68): O-Zone's "Dragostea Din Tei" -- modulates from C major to A minor using only hypermetric and orchestrational cues.

**Example 2** (pp. 67-68, Figure 2.5.6): D-A-E-G heard as four different songs with different tonics.

**Example 3** (p. 67, Figure 2.5.7): Three songs in three keys played simultaneously without dissonance.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming rock songs must establish centricity through V-I cadences
  **Correction**: Rock centricity operates through phrasing, hypermetric accent, and orchestration

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking modulation in rock requires a change of chord progression
  **Clarification**: Modulation can occur by shifting the hypermetric accent within the same loop

- **Confusion**: Thinking rock centricity means "tonality without function"
  **Clarification**: Many rock songs do use functional progressions, especially at cadences
