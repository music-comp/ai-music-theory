# Quick Definition

The understanding that the cadential six-four chord (I64) is hierarchically dependent on the following dominant, so that progressions like ii6-I64-V are understood as ii6-(I64=>V), with the six-four embellishing rather than replacing the dominant -- one of the few cases of genuine harmonic hierarchy Tymoczko endorses.

# Core Definition

Though Tymoczko argues that functional harmony is largely nonhierarchical and chord-to-chord, the cadential six-four is a clear exception: progressions like "ii6-I64-V move from a supertonic to a dominant that is itself embellished by a second-inversion triad; in symbols ii6-(I64=>V)" (p. 433). This brings "otherwise anomalous progressions under the umbrella of standard functional principles; sequences such as V65/V-I64-V or vi-I64-V can be understood to move from the initial chord to the final dominant by way of a subsidiary or decorative 64" (p. 433). The parallel tonic-decorating idiom V-IV64-I works analogously: V-(IV64=>I) (p. 433).

# Key Properties

1. The cadential I64 embellishes the following dominant, not the preceding chord
2. This hierarchical view resolves otherwise anomalous progressions (V65/V-I64-V, vi-I64-V)
3. A parallel idiom decorates the tonic: V-IV64-I as V-(IV64=>I)
4. Secondary dominants provide another limited example of harmonic hierarchy
5. "Centricity is sticky" -- changes more slowly than harmonies (p. 434)
6. The scope of harmonic embedding is much more limited than Schenkerian theory claims

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Hierarchical Six-Four:
1. Locate second-inversion triads in cadential or pre-cadential positions
2. Check whether the six-four resolves to the chord it decorates (dominant or tonic)
3. Analyze the preceding chord's relationship to the decorated chord, not the six-four
4. Use the notation (X64=>Y) to indicate hierarchical dependence

# Context & Application

This represents one of the few cases where Tymoczko endorses genuine harmonic hierarchy. It provides a bounded alternative to Schenkerian claims about wholesale prolongation. The key insight is that secondary dominants are "simultaneously a clear example of harmonic recursion and also evidence for its limited scope" (p. 436) -- any secondary dominant could signal either a momentary digression or a genuine key change, and "virtually any nonsyntactical progression X-Y can be normalized by the insertion of the appropriate secondary dominant" (p. 436).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 433): Standard cadential pattern ii6-I64-V understood as ii6-(I64=>V).

**Example 2** (p. 433): Tonic-decorating pattern V-IV64-I in Palestrina's Quando lieta sperai, Chopin's Mazurka Op. 17, no. 3, and Mozart's K.310.

**Example 3** (pp. 434-435): Bach's "Herr Jesu Christ" -- secondary dominants creating limited hierarchical embedding, with the E minor vi both as tonic of its own cycle and as vi in G major.

**Example 4** (pp. 435-436): Haydn's final piano sonata, Hob. XVI:52, last movement -- sequential structure reinforces harmonic hierarchy through phrase parallelism.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Generalizing from the cadential six-four to claim that all harmony is hierarchical
  **Correction**: This limited embedding does not generalize to all of harmony; most harmonic progressions are chord-to-chord

- **Error**: Assuming secondary dominants always embellish their successors
  **Correction**: "Virtually any nonsyntactical progression X-Y can be normalized by the insertion of the appropriate secondary dominant" (p. 436), showing the limits of the embedding model

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the six-four is a "second-inversion tonic"
  **Clarification**: It is hierarchically dependent on the dominant -- a decorative elaboration, not an independent harmony

- **Confusion**: Assuming the language analogy for harmonic embedding generalizes fully
  **Clarification**: "Where we perceive language with almost crystalline accuracy... we perceive music through a low-fidelity haze" (p. 437), limiting the scope of embedding
