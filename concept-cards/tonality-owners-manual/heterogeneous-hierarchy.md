# Quick Definition
A model of musical organization in which diverse, relatively independent systems -- harmonic, contrapuntal, melodic, modulatory, sequential, and formal -- are superimposed rather than unified under a single recursive logic as in Schenkerian theory.

# Formal Definition
Rather than Schenker's vision of nested, fundamentally similar musical levels guided by unified logic (like layers of an onion), heterogeneous hierarchy posits that music involves diverse forms of organization superimposed: a harmonic system directing chord flow, a contrapuntal system overseeing surface voice motion (inherited from the Renaissance), a melodic system creating longer-range linear coherence, a modulatory system governing key progression, a sequential system generating varied repetition, and formal systems concerned with phrasing and large-scale development. These systems are relatively simple in isolation but combine to form wonderfully complex musical resources.

# Musical Context
This concept is the book's major contribution to form theory. It reframes the relationship between harmony and melody: melody is "locally free but hierarchically structured" while harmony is "largely nonhierarchical but locally constrained." This explains why melody is hard to describe (strategic, goal-directed) while harmony is more amenable to syntactic theorizing (rule-governed). The analogy is to walking: legs obey local alternation of left and right, yet journeys are nevertheless directed.

# Examples
- The opening of Mozart's Symphony No. 40, K.550, where melodic strategy (linear descent from ^5 to ^7) operates within local harmonic grammar constraints (Figure 9.1.1-9.1.2, p. 408-409)
- Chopin's Revolutionary Etude, where a contrapuntal logic of stepwise descending voice leading generates diverse harmonic effects across diatonic and chromatic domains (Figures 9.3.8-9.3.9, pp. 423-424)
- Classical sentence form, where grouping conventions, harmonic cycles, and melodic strategy interact but are not reducible to one another (Section 9.7)

# Common Confusions
- Heterogeneous hierarchy does not deny all hierarchy in music -- it denies that all hierarchy follows the same recursive logic
- It does not reject Schenkerian insights about melodic structure, only the claim that harmony, melody, and form are all governed by the same principles of prolongation
- "Schenker was right about melody but wrong about harmony" (p. 412) -- melody has long-range hierarchical structure, but harmony largely follows local chord-to-chord rules
