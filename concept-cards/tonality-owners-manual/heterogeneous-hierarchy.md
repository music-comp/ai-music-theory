# Quick Definition

A model of musical organization in which diverse, relatively independent systems -- harmonic, contrapuntal, melodic, modulatory, sequential, and formal -- are superimposed rather than unified under a single recursive logic as in Schenkerian theory.

# Core Definition

Tymoczko proposes that music involves "diverse forms of organization superimposed: a harmonic system directing the flow of chords, a contrapuntal system overseeing the motion of surface voices (and largely inherited from the Renaissance), a melodic system creating longer-range linear coherence, a modulatory system governing the progression of keys, a sequential system generating varied repetition, formal systems concerned with phrasing and long-term development, and so on" (p. 407). These systems are "relatively simple when considered in isolation, for they come together to form a wonderfully complex set of musical resources." This contrasts with "Schenker's vision of many fundamentally similar musical levels, guided by a unified logic and nested like the layers of an onion" (p. 407).

# Prerequisites

- **Quadruple hierarchy** -- Understanding how chords nest inside scales inside the chromatic aggregate is needed to see how different organizational systems can be superimposed
- **Melodic strategy** -- The concept of longer-range melodic goals requires understanding the strategic dimension of melody
- **Functional harmony** -- The harmonic grammar that constitutes one of the independent systems must be understood first

# Key Properties

1. Multiple independent organizational systems operate simultaneously in tonal music
2. Melody is "locally free but hierarchically structured" while harmony is "largely nonhierarchical but locally constrained" (p. 412)
3. The harmonic grammar provides "the rules of the game" while phrase-level melodic templates are "the higher-level goals" (p. 409)
4. Systems are relatively simple in isolation but create complexity through superimposition
5. Schenker was "right about melody but wrong about harmony" (p. 412)
6. Large-scale form arises from "collage-like arrangements of smaller sections" rather than prolongational unity (p. 446)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Heterogeneous Organization:
1. Analyze the harmonic grammar locally (chord-to-chord progressions)
2. Identify melodic strategy separately (longer-range stepwise connections, arpeggiations)
3. Note sequential and schematic patterns as independent organizational layers
4. Observe phrase structure (sentence/period) as a distinct grouping system
5. Track modulatory pathways at the key level
6. Recognize that these systems interact but are not reducible to one another

# Context & Application

This concept is the central theoretical contribution of Chapter 9 and arguably of the entire book. It reframes the relationship between the multiple dimensions of musical organization, providing an alternative to Schenkerian monism. The analogy is to walking: "our legs obey a local alternation of left and right, and yet our journeys are nevertheless directed" (p. 407). Similarly, in basketball, players pursue strategic goals within the constraints of rules. The framework applies across all functional music, though different styles emphasize different systems.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 408): The opening of Mozart's Symphony No. 40, K.550, where melodic strategy (linear descent from 5-hat to 7-hat) operates within local harmonic grammar constraints, showing melody and harmony as distinct but compatible systems.

**Example 2** (pp. 419-425): Chopin uses a single melodic strategy (stepwise descent) to produce effects ranging from diatonic modality to functional harmony to chromaticism, demonstrating the independence of melodic and harmonic systems.

**Example 3** (pp. 437-446): Classical phrase structure shows how grouping (sentence/period), harmonic cycles, and melodic strategy interact as three distinct but superimposed organizational systems.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating the heterogeneous hierarchy as denying all musical hierarchy
  **Correction**: It denies that all hierarchy follows the same recursive logic, not that hierarchy exists

- **Error**: Assuming harmonic and melodic analysis must produce the same structural picture
  **Correction**: These are independent systems that may suggest different structural readings of the same passage

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing heterogeneous hierarchy rejects Schenkerian insights about melody
  **Clarification**: It endorses Schenker's insight about long-range melodic connection while rejecting his claims about harmonic prolongation

- **Confusion**: Thinking the independence of systems means they never interact
  **Clarification**: The systems interact constantly -- e.g., sentence form conventions produce specific harmonic-melodic correlations -- but remain conceptually distinct
