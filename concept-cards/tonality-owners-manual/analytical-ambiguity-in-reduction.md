# Quick Definition

The condition in which a musical passage admits multiple equally plausible reductive analyses, revealing that reduction is fundamentally interpretive rather than purely mechanical. Tymoczko prefers to imagine music as "suspended between two poles," with reduction producing "partially true models" (p. 204).

# Core Definition

Analytical ambiguity arises when "nonharmonic analysis forces us to choose between two equally plausible readings" (p. 204) -- for example, an intervallic view (parallel tenths) and a chordal view (parallel arpeggiations) of the same passage. "If we think a piece's harmonies are straightforwardly real and nonharmonic analysis a matter of uncovering an objectively existing 'harmonic skeleton,' then one analysis must be right and the other wrong. But I prefer to imagine the music as suspended between these two poles; if so, then even this simple form of reduction is fundamentally interpretive, producing partially true models of complex and ambiguous musical surfaces" (p. 204). The three traditions evoked (modal counterpoint and functional harmony) represent different partially valid perspectives on the same music.

# Key Properties

1. Multiple equally plausible readings coexist
2. Reduction is fundamentally interpretive, not mechanical
3. Music is "suspended between two poles"
4. Even simple reduction produces partially true models
5. Ambiguity increases in later music (Mahler vs. Palestrina)
6. Ambiguity does not invalidate analysis; it enriches it

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Ambiguity:
1. Attempt two or more different reductions of the same passage
2. Check if both produce plausible results
3. If so, the passage is analytically ambiguous
4. Consider both readings as partially true models

# Context & Application

Figure P5.1 (p. 204) shows a Bach passage admitting both intervallic and chordal readings, "evoking the traditions of modal counterpoint and functional harmony." The scalar approach to textural reduction (Figure P5.3) offers multiple valid analyses of the same arpeggio pattern. Analytical ambiguity pervades all three reduction types and increases in later music. The pedagogical danger is that students may avoid idiomatic passages that seem to violate rules, "wanting to 'play the game right'" (p. 206), when in fact the rules are more flexible than the theory suggests.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming one reduction must be right and the other wrong
  **Correction**: Music can be genuinely suspended between interpretations; both models may be partially true

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking ambiguity means analysis is pointless
  **Clarification**: Multiple partially true models each illuminate different aspects of the music
