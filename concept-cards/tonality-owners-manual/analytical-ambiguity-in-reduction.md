# Quick Definition
The condition in which a musical passage admits multiple equally plausible reductive analyses, revealing that reduction is fundamentally interpretive rather than purely mechanical.

# Formal Definition
Analytical ambiguity arises when nonharmonic reduction, textural reduction, or summarizing reduction fails to yield a single correct analysis of a musical passage. This can happen when there is no way to eliminate purportedly nonharmonic notes, when those notes have genuinely harmonic functions, or when multiple equally valid readings coexist. Tymoczko prefers to imagine music as "suspended between two poles," with even simple forms of reduction producing partially true models.

# Musical Context
Analytical ambiguity is pervasive across all three types of reduction. In nonharmonic reduction, a passage may admit both intervallic and chordal readings. In textural reduction, arpeggiated passages sometimes resist clean de-arpeggiation. In summarizing reduction, there are inherently multiple valid paraphrases. This ambiguity increases in later music (e.g., Mahler's Ninth Symphony), where nonharmonic reduction becomes autobiographical and interpretive rather than intersubjective and syntactical.

# Common Confusions
- Ambiguity does not mean analysis is pointless. Multiple partially true models can each illuminate different aspects of the music.
- The existence of ambiguity does not invalidate the nonharmonic system; it reveals that the system is an approximately true model we apply to music composed by people who thought differently.
