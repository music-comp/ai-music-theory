# Quick Definition
A mnemonic system for teaching four-voice triadic counterpoint, based on upper-voice configurations: Open, Unusual (DI/OO), Close, and Half-open, providing students with positive goals rather than just prohibitions.

# Formal Definition
OUCH theory models four-voice triadic counterpoint by treating the upper three voices as moving along a graph of configurations while the bass moves independently. The grammar: U_DI goes to C; C goes anywhere except U_OO; H goes to C or O; O goes anywhere except U_DI; U_OO goes to O. The C-to-O and O-to-C transitions are also permitted (more common than expected). These rules account for about 85% of four-voice triadic voice leadings in Bach chorales and about 75% in Palestrina's masses.

# Musical Context
The approach extends figured-bass upper-voice configurations to all possible arrangements with good spacing (not just those comfortable under the hand). It clarifies that changes in one line have implications for others due to norms of vertical spacing. Students first learn close-position only, then open only, then close and open together, then half-open, and finally the rare DI and OO positions. Following these rules makes parallel octaves impossible and parallel fifths unlikely in upper voices. The system provides positive counterpoint -- "if I am at this point in musical space, my most obvious destinations are..."

# Common Confusions
- OUCH theory treats the bass-vs-upper-voices division as universal, but voices can actually be grouped in other ways (pairs, soprano vs. lower three, etc.) -- this is addressed in Section 7
- The approach works because upper voices often form complete triads while bass moves independently, but this is one particular organizational strategy, not the only possibility
