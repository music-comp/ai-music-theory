# Quick Definition

A mnemonic system for teaching four-voice triadic counterpoint based on upper-voice configurations: Open, Unusual (DI/OO), Close, and Half-open. It provides students with positive goals ("if I am here, my destinations are...") rather than just prohibitions, accounting for about 85% of Bach's chorale voice leadings.

# Core Definition

OUCH theory models four-voice triadic counterpoint by treating the upper three voices as moving along a graph of configurations while the bass moves independently (Figure 3.6.4, p. 127). The grammar: U_DI goes to C; C goes anywhere except U_OO; H goes to C or O; O goes anywhere except U_DI; U_OO goes to O. The DI and OO configurations are relabeled "U" for Unusual, "producing a snappy mnemonic while reinforcing the configurations' rarity" (p. 127). C-to-O and O-to-C transitions are also permitted. These rules account for about 85% of four-voice triadic voice leadings in Bach chorales and about 75% in Palestrina's masses (p. 128).

# Key Properties

1. Mnemonic: O-U-C-H (Open, Unusual, Close, Half-open)
2. Rules: U_DI->C; C->anywhere except U_OO; H->C or O; O->anywhere except U_DI; U_OO->O
3. Accounts for ~85% of Bach chorale voice leadings, ~75% of Palestrina
4. Following rules makes parallel octaves impossible and parallel fifths unlikely in upper voices
5. Extends figured-bass pedagogy to all possible arrangements with good spacing
6. The approach provides positive counterpoint (possibilities, not just prohibitions)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply OUCH:
1. Determine current upper-voice configuration (C, O, H, or U)
2. Consult the grammar for permitted destinations
3. Move upper voices to the new configuration while bass moves independently
4. Add passing tones and other embellishments after establishing the skeleton

## Teaching Sequence:
1. Start with close-position only
2. Add open position
3. Allow close and open together
4. Introduce half-open
5. Finally add the rare U positions

# Context & Application

The approach generalizes traditional figured-bass pedagogy, extending upper-voice configurations to all possible arrangements consistent with good spacing, "not just those that lie comfortably under the hand" (p. 128). It clarifies that "changes in one line have implications for the others, arising from norms of vertical spacing" (p. 128). Bach's "Ich dank' dich, lieber Herr" demonstrates OUCH thinking in practice: close position is the default until parallels threaten, forcing a shift to half-open, then open-octave, before returning to close (Figure 3.6.5, p. 128). OUCH theory works because upper voices often form complete triads while the bass moves independently -- but this is one particular organizational strategy, as Section 7 (Thinking Within the Chord) shows more flexible groupings.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Applying OUCH rules rigidly without considering context
  **Correction**: The rules cover ~85% of cases; the remaining ~15% includes valid progressions that violate the grammar

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Assuming OUCH theory models all four-voice counterpoint
  **Clarification**: It treats the bass-vs-upper-voices division as primary, but voices can group differently (pairs, soprano vs. lower three, etc.) -- addressed in Section 7
