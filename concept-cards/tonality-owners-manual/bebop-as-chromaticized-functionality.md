# Quick Definition
The analysis of Bebop and later jazz as extensions of functional tonality that chromaticize the descending voice leading of the diatonic fifth-sequence, allowing roots to descend by semitone, tritone, or fifth -- making Bebop "more Romantic than modernist."

# Formal Definition
In Bebop, the 2-in-7 diatonic spiral diagram is replaced by the 2-in-12 chromatic spiral, which models both root progressions and upper-voice pairs connected by efficient voice leading. Tritone substitution extends the circle of dyadic fifths, allowing chord roots to descend by tritone as well as by fifth. Upper-voice pairs (third-seventh, fifth-ninth, etc.) slide downward semitonally, maintaining efficient voice leading across tritone-substituted progressions. This represents a continuation of the "ancient musical logic" of descending voice leading that begins before Ockeghem and runs through Neil Young's "Helpless."

# Musical Context
This analysis demonstrates that the book's theoretical framework extends beyond common-practice tonality to twentieth-century styles. Bebop musicians "complexified" functional convention, "adding new stories to the building rather than razing it to the ground" (p. 529). The chromaticized space provides a unified model where both traditional and substituted progressions are represented.

# Common Confusions
- Bebop is not a rejection of functional harmony but an extension of it into chromatic space
- Tritone substitution is not arbitrary but reflects the voice-leading geometry of the 2-in-12 space
- The same analytical framework (spiral diagrams, efficient voice leading) applies to both Renaissance polyphony and Bebop
