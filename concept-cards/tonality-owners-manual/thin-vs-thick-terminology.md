# Quick Definition

Tymoczko's preference for "thin" uses of music-theoretical terms involving only minimal presuppositions, as opposed to "thick" definitions loaded with methodological commitments that balkanize the field into distinct linguistic communities.

# Core Definition

Thin terminology uses words with minimal theoretical baggage: "D phrygian" means "music that generally uses the two-flat diatonic collection while articulating D as its tonal center," making "no assumptions about the presence of reciting tones or the utility of the plagal/authentic distinction" (p. 21). Roman-numeral labels like "I6" mean "scale degrees 1, 3, and 5 with 3 in the bass," carrying "no presumptions about roots or inversional equivalence" (p. 21). Thick terminology bakes beliefs into words, risking "the already-small field of music theory will be even further divided" (p. 21).

# Key Properties

1. Thin terms carry minimal presuppositions
2. Thick terms bake theoretical commitments into definitions
3. Thick terminology risks transforming important generalizations into trivial assertions of linguistic convention
4. Thin terms are better suited to slowly evolving practices with fuzzy boundaries
5. The preference connects to Wittgenstein's observations about the difficulty of defining "game"
6. The risk of thick terminology is the "no true Scotsman fallacy" applied to music

# Construction / Recognition

## To Assess Terminological Thickness:
1. Identify the term's definition as used
2. Check which theoretical commitments are presupposed
3. Ask whether those commitments are empirically supported or contested
4. Consider whether a thinner definition would preserve the term's utility while reducing dogmatism

# Context & Application

Connected to the broader philosophical issues of the chapter, including the vagueness of concepts like "seventh chord," "cadence," and "second theme." Tymoczko argues that conceptual boundaries are inherently fuzzy, with "a central core of paradigmatic cases surrounded by a penumbra of increasingly problematic examples" (p. 20). Insisting on strict definitions leads to dogmatism.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 21): "Modality" -- thin: largely diatonic music with possible tonal centers; thick: presupposes reciting tones, plagal/authentic distinction.

**Example 2** (p. 21): "I6" -- thin: scale degrees 1, 3, 5 with 3 in bass; thick: presupposes root, inversional equivalence, harmonic function.

**Example 3** (p. 20): Caplin's "authentic cadence" requiring root-position dominant lasting until the tonic -- thick definition that withholds the term from moments that sound cadential.

**Example 4** (p. 20): Hepokoski/Darcy's "second theme" requiring a preceding "medial caesura" -- thick definition risking transformation of generalization into tautology.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using thick definitions to settle empirical disputes by definitional fiat
  **Correction**: This transforms important generalizations ("second themes are usually preceded by a pause") into trivial assertions of convention

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking thin terminology is imprecise or anti-theoretical
  **Clarification**: It is deliberately minimal to avoid presupposing contested claims, not to avoid precision
