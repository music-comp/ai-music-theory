# Quick Definition
A graph comparing a chord's zeroth-order frequency to its frequency either before or after a particular chord, revealing whether specific progressions are genuinely favored, neutral, or suppressed.

# Formal Definition
A tendency histogram is a visualization tool that compares a chord's context-independent frequency (zeroth-order probability) to its conditional frequency in relation to a specific other chord (first-order probability). When the first-order line lies above the zeroth-order line, the progression happens more than expected; when they are nearly the same, the chord's frequency is unchanged by context; when the first-order line lies below, the progression is suppressed. This allows analysts to distinguish genuine harmonic tendencies from progressions that are merely common because their constituent chords are common.

# Musical Context
Tendency histograms are particularly useful for revealing the divergence between raw frequency and tendency: some common progressions (like IV-I in Palestrina's ionian music) are common largely because both chords are individually popular, not because of a genuine directional tendency. The graphs encapsulate a wealth of tacit musical knowledge and reveal the congealing of functional behavior over centuries.

# Common Confusions
- A common progression is not necessarily a "tending" progression. High-frequency chords will form many progressions simply by being common, without any genuine directional pull.
- The absence of a tendency does not mean a progression never occurs, only that it occurs at roughly the rate expected from the chords' individual frequencies.
- Tendency histograms reveal both favored and suppressed progressions, providing a richer picture than simple frequency counts.
