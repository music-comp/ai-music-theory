# Quick Definition
In a fixed chromatic universe, enharmonic equivalence means that differently spelled scales (e.g., C major and D-double-flat major) are the same acoustic entity, with the different spellings indicating different *paths* through voice-leading space rather than different musical locations.

# Formal Definition
Enharmonic equivalence is produced by loops in scale space, just as chordal inversion is produced by loops in chord space. A series of modulations that makes a complete loop surrounding the center of scale space changes the abstract voices' scale-degree functions (e.g., the C slot goes from tonic to leading-tone function). By contrast, a series that does not complete a loop returns voices to their original functions. In a Pythagorean framework, enharmonic spellings indicate genuinely different scales (B-sharp major differs from C major by about a quarter of a semitone). In a fixed twelve-tone chromatic universe, enharmonic spelling instead signifies the presence of a *nontrivial route* from a chord to itself -- a topological rather than acoustic distinction.

# Musical Context
Enharmonic equivalence requires chromaticism: because diatonic modulations tend to cancel out (adding and subtracting the same small collection of sharps and flats), composers who limit themselves to diatonic key areas will not travel in complete circles through key space. Only once key areas are conceived chromatically does enharmonicism become commonplace.

# Common Confusions
- Not all enharmonic respellings represent genuine voice leadings: merely notational respellings (like writing G-sharp major as A-flat major for convenience) have no observable consequence -- if you were watching a pianist's hands, you could not tell the respelling had occurred.
- Genuine enharmonicism involves an observable change in abstract voices' scale-degree roles, detectable through the finger-modeling exercise described in the text.
- Enharmonicism just *is* chordal inversion at the scalar level -- the same geometrical phenomenon operating on a different structural level.
