# Quick Definition
A hierarchical approach to voice leading where an abstract chord provides scale-like collections within which surface voices move, the chord itself shifting by efficient voice leading in the background -- like a melodic sequence inside a modulating scale.

# Formal Definition
Surface voices move along the pitches of an abstract background chord that sounds its notes in every octave, providing a field of contrapuntal play. These background slots shift as the chord changes, typically by efficient voice leading. The surface voices' motion-within-the-chord is unperturbed by background shifts. This can be modeled with nested spiral diagrams: one for voices moving within the chord (using little-t transposition along the chord and tau transposition along the dyad), another for the chord moving within the scale (using big-T transposition). This requires two diagrams rather than one, characteristic of four-voice music's hierarchical depth.

# Musical Context
The opening of Beethoven's *Waldstein* exemplifies this: a triadic background descends chromatically (C-B-Bb-A) while right-hand voices ascend along it, producing a chain of ascending thirds E-G-B-D-F-A-C. The *Tristan* prelude opens similarly, and the technique reappears at its climax. The approach implements Schenkerian insight that voice leading occurs in a musical "background" that is simultaneously real and not always obvious in the score. Tallis's forty-voice *Spem in alium* is the extreme case: forty voices move within a shifting three-note scalar field.

# Common Confusions
- "Thinking within the chord" is not metaphorical; it literally means voices move along the intrinsic scale defined by the harmony's notes in all octaves
- The background voice leading is not the same as Schenkerian prolongation, though it resonates with Schenker's insight; here, doublings and voice exchanges are artifacts of surface voices moving within a simpler background
