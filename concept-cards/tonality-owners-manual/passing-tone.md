# Quick Definition

A nonharmonic tone that connects two chord tones by stepwise motion in the same direction, typically occurring on a metrically weak beat. In the SNAP system, passing tones are the most common and "purely connective" species.

# Core Definition

A passing tone is a nonharmonic tone approached and left by step in the same direction, connecting two chord tones. They can be diatonic or chromatic and occur in ascending or descending forms. Some passing tones cut across the binary division of the beat ("121" passing tones with durations 1-2-1, where the nonharmonic tone has duration 2). Schenker took passing tones as the model for all dissonances, asserting that no "composite sound" exists between the dissonant passing note and the sustained note (p. 249). Tymoczko reverses this argument, suggesting that suspensions are fundamentally less reducible than passing tones.

# Prerequisites

- **Nonharmonic tone** — Understanding what constitutes a note outside the prevailing harmony

# Key Properties

1. Approached and left by step in the same direction
2. Connects two chord tones
3. Typically unaccented (metrically weak beat) in the first practice
4. Can be diatonic or chromatic
5. "121" passing tones cut across the binary division of the beat (p. 215)
6. Can have harmonic effects, especially in ascending subdominant contexts

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify a Passing Tone:
1. Locate a note that is not part of the prevailing harmony
2. Verify it is approached by step from a chord tone
3. Verify it continues by step in the same direction to another chord tone
4. Check metrical placement (typically weak beat in Renaissance style)

# Context & Application

Passing tones are among the most common nonharmonic tones across all tonal styles. In the ascending subdominant (IV-to-I with ascending 6-7-8), a "merely passing" seventh scale degree can create dominant functionality, particularly when reinforced by 4-3 to create a resolving tritone (p. 223). Chromatic passing tones in later music can slide entire triads from dominant to tonic, creating beautiful harmonically significant flashes of color.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 215, Fig. 5.1.7): "121" passing tones that cut across the binary division of the beat in Palestrina's Pope Marcellus Mass (Gloria, m. 9) and Willaert's O salutaris hostia (m. 36).

**Example 2** (p. 223, Fig. 5.2.3): Ascending subdominants across a range of composers, where a passing seventh scale degree creates dominant functionality.

**Example 3** (p. 250, Fig. 5.6.4): Chopin's Nocturne Op. 27, no. 2, mm. 32-33, where chromatic passing tones slide complete triads from dominant to tonic.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming all passing tones are purely decorative
  **Correction**: The ascending passing seventh in IV-to-I progressions can create genuine dominant functionality (p. 223)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Schenker's claim that passing tones form no "composite sound"
  **Clarification**: Tymoczko argues it is vital to hear the composite sounds formed by passing tones and harmonic tones -- e.g., the composite sound formed by a passing D-sharp and the harmonic A (p. 250)

- **Confusion**: Thinking passing tones always occur on weak beats
  **Clarification**: "121" passing tones cut across the binary division of the beat, sometimes called "fake suspensions" (p. 215)
