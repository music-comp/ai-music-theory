# Quick Definition
The Schenkerian approach in which early-phrase harmonic progressions are understood as hierarchically nested inside a single "structural" progression spanning the entire phrase, with initial tonics "attaching" syntactically to final dominants -- a view Tymoczko challenges with his sequential alternative.

# Formal Definition
In the prolongational (recursive) model, the phrase is organized by a single structural T-S-D-T cycle, with earlier harmonic cycles hierarchically embedded within it. What makes the final dominant "cadential" on this view is not its rhythmic position or use of root-position chords, but its syntactic "connection" to the initial tonic. This contrasts with the sequential model where harmonic cycles are concatenated one after another, with the final cadence distinguished by greater cadential weight rather than recursive embedding. The debate hinges on whether chords can meaningfully be said to "last" longer than they actually sound.

# Musical Context
This is "perhaps the single most pressing problem of contemporary music theory" (p. 417). Textbooks by Laitz (The Complete Musician) and Clendinning/Marvin (The Musician's Guide) teach the recursive conception as "the phrase model." The debate directly affects how harmony is taught and how students learn to hear musical structure.

# Examples
- Haydn's G minor sonata, Hob. XVI:44, II: Laitz postulates three cycles embedded within a fourth; Tymoczko sees four successive cycles (Figure 9.2.5, p. 416)
- Lerdahl and Jackendoff's five-stage reduction of Mozart's K.331, I: the first three levels are compatible with traditional theory; the fourth and fifth extend nonharmonic reduction to larger timescales (Figure 9.2.6, p. 417)
- Bach's A minor fugue, mm. 39-48: flexible texture without clear phrase breaks makes it difficult to determine which tonic "attaches" to a cadence (Figure 9.2.7, p. 418)

# Common Confusions
- There is "little or no solid evidence" supporting the prolongational view and "no historical evidence that earlier composers in fact thought in this way" (p. 418)
- It is unclear how to empirically test whether the recursive picture is true
- Sequential and recursive hearings may both be available, with different listeners perceiving the same piece differently -- raising alarming questions about the foundations of music theory
