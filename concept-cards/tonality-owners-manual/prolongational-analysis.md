# Quick Definition

The Schenkerian approach in which early-phrase harmonic progressions are understood as hierarchically nested inside a single "structural" progression spanning the entire phrase, with initial tonics "attaching" syntactically to final dominants -- a view Tymoczko challenges with his sequential alternative.

# Core Definition

In the prolongational (recursive) model, the phrase is organized by a single structural T-S-D-T cycle, with earlier harmonic cycles "hierarchically embedded within a higher-level 'structural' progression" (p. 416). What makes the final dominant "cadential" on this view is not its rhythmic position or use of root-position chords but "the fact that it 'connects' syntactically to the initial tonic" (p. 416). Tymoczko's sequential alternative holds that harmonic cycles are "concatenated sequentially, placed one after another like beads on a string" (p. 415), with the final cadence distinguished by greater cadential weight rather than recursive embedding. He considers deciding between these two models "perhaps the single most pressing problem of contemporary music theory, as it directly affects elementary pedagogy" (p. 417).

- **Cadential weight** -- Understanding degrees of cadential conclusiveness

# Key Properties

1. The recursive model claims initial tonics "attach" syntactically to final dominants
2. The sequential model treats harmonic cycles as concatenated, not nested
3. Both models agree on four basic phrase properties: root-position tonic at beginning/end, stepwise melodic descent, chordal arpeggiation, and bass reserving root-position dominant for final cadence
4. The debate hinges on whether chords can meaningfully "last" longer than they sound
5. Different listeners may genuinely perceive the same piece in fundamentally different ways

# Construction / Recognition

## To Distinguish the Two Models:
1. Identify whether early-phrase progressions are heard as nested within a larger progression (recursive) or as independent cycles preceding the cadence (sequential)
2. Test whether the initial tonic seems to "persist" through intervening material
3. Consider whether irregular phrasing can be accommodated by the recursive model
4. Ask whether the distinction is empirically testable

# Context & Application

Textbooks by Laitz (The Complete Musician) and Clendinning/Marvin (The Musician's Guide) teach the recursive conception as "the phrase model." Tymoczko finds "little or no solid evidence supporting" the prolongational view "and no historical evidence that earlier composers in fact thought in this way" (p. 418). The disagreement is "nothing short of alarming -- as if linguists regularly disagreed about which nouns attached to which verbs" (p. 419).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 416): Haydn's G minor sonata, Hob. XVI:44, II -- Laitz postulates three cycles embedded within a fourth; Tymoczko sees four successive cycles (Figure 9.2.5).

**Example 2** (p. 417): Lerdahl and Jackendoff's five-stage reduction of Mozart's K.331, I -- first three levels compatible with traditional theory; fourth and fifth extend nonharmonic reduction to larger timescales (Figure 9.2.6).

**Example 3** (p. 418): Bach's A minor fugue, mm. 39-48 -- flexible texture without clear phrase breaks makes it "difficult to determine what initial C major chord should attach to the strong final cadence" (Figure 9.2.7).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming the prolongational view is empirically established
  **Correction**: There is "little or no solid evidence" supporting it and no historical evidence composers thought this way

- **Error**: Treating sequential hearing as inferior musicianship
  **Correction**: It may be that both modes are available and uncorrelated with musical quality

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the two models produce different surface analyses
  **Clarification**: Both models often agree on which notes are structurally important -- they disagree about why

- **Confusion**: Assuming the debate is merely terminological
  **Clarification**: It has direct consequences for pedagogy and for understanding what listeners actually hear
