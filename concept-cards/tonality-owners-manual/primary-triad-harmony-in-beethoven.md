# Quick Definition

Beethoven's distinctive emphasis on I, IV, and V triads enriched by contrary-motion counterpoint (the Ludwig), which replaces the standard fifths-and-steps outer-voice patterns with novel contrapuntal possibilities and gives primary-triad harmony new expressive depth.

# Core Definition

Primary-triad Ludwigs create "V-I-IV or IV-I-V progressions" through contrary motion, "endowing primary-triad harmony with new contrapuntal depth, replacing its fifths-and-steps defaults with novel outer-voice possibilities" (p. 460). The Ludwig provides "an alternative to the fifths-and-steps pattern" (p. 461). More complex Ludwigs act as "V-I-vii/V or as V/IV-IV-vii," replacing Gjerdingen's oscillatory Quiescenza "with a more energetic expansion" (p. 460). Beethoven's harmony "often consists of simple tonic and dominant alternations, with many fewer secondary diatonic triads than in J. S. Bach or Brahms" (p. 453).

# Prerequisites

- **The Ludwig** -- The contrary-motion schema that enriches primary-triad harmony
- **Functional analysis** -- Understanding of harmonic function and primary triads

# Key Properties

1. I, IV, and V triads dominate Beethoven's harmonic vocabulary
2. Contrary motion (Ludwig) provides contrapuntal depth to simple harmony
3. The Ludwig replaces standard fifths-and-steps outer-voice patterns
4. Harmonic simplicity is offset by rhythmic variety and registral expansion
5. The quasi-sequential character of I-V-I is exploited: contrary motion divides the octave almost in half
6. Beethoven's "incessant Ludwiging may represent a kind of musical understanding" (p. 472)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Recognize Primary-Triad Harmony in Beethoven:
1. Note the dominance of I, IV, and V with relatively few secondary diatonic triads
2. Look for contrary-motion outer voices enriching the simple progressions
3. Check for Ludwig patterns (alternating thirds and sixths in contrary motion)
4. Observe whether rhythmic and registral variety compensates for harmonic simplicity
5. Listen for the near-sequential quality of I-V-I progressions

# Context & Application

The Fifth Symphony opens with thirty-eight measures using essentially only tonic and dominant (p. 483). This limitation is not a deficiency but a deliberate aesthetic choice enabling the Ludwig and other contrary-motion patterns. Passages like Op. 10, no. 2, mm. 73-76 "seem to announce that the I-V-I progression can be a contrary-motion near sequence, dividing the octave almost in half" (p. 472). The Hammerklavier second theme uses a four-unit contrary-motion Ludwig to dramatize "the underlying mechanisms of functional harmony, raising its descending thirds to melodic salience" (p. 472).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Dismissing primary-triad harmony as simplistic
  **Correction**: It is harmonically simple but contrapuntally sophisticated, enriched by the Ludwig

- **Error**: Assuming all great music requires harmonic complexity
  **Correction**: Beethoven demonstrates that contrapuntal and gestural complexity can substitute for harmonic complexity

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking Beethoven's harmonic simplicity reflects early-period immaturity
  **Clarification**: It appears throughout his career as a deliberate aesthetic choice

- **Confusion**: Equating primary-triad emphasis with rock harmony
  **Clarification**: Beethoven's I-IV-V is enriched by contrary-motion counterpoint that gives it a fundamentally different character
