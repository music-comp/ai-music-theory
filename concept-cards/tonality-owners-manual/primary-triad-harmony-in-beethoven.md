# Quick Definition
Beethoven's distinctive emphasis on I, IV, and V triads enriched by contrary-motion counterpoint (the Ludwig), which replaces the standard fifths-and-steps outer-voice patterns with novel contrapuntal possibilities and gives primary-triad harmony new expressive depth.

# Formal Definition
Primary-triad Ludwigs create V-I-IV or IV-I-V progressions through contrary motion, endowing the simplest harmonic vocabulary with contrapuntal richness that goes beyond the standard fifths-and-steps defaults. The Ludwig provides "an alternative to the fifths-and-steps pattern" (Figure 10.1.4, p. 461), where "D" refers to a generic dominant (V or vii-degrees). More complex Ludwigs act as V-I-vii-degrees/V or V/IV-IV-vii-degrees, replacing Gjerdingen's oscillatory Quiescenza with energetic expansion. The simplicity of the harmonic vocabulary combined with contrapuntal sophistication is the hallmark of Beethoven's language.

# Musical Context
Beethoven's harmony often consists of "simple tonic and dominant alternations, with many fewer secondary diatonic triads than in J. S. Bach or Brahms" (Prelude to Ch10, p. 453). The Fifth Symphony opens with the only other harmony in the first thirty-eight measures being the Italian sixth at the end of the first phrase. This limitation is offset by rhythmic variety and contrary-motion counterpoint, producing music that seems like "unrefined tonal ore" fashioned into compelling art.

# Examples
- Op. 18, no. 3 opening: V-I-IV Ludwig using primary triads (Figure 10.1.3, p. 461)
- Fifth Symphony, mm. 7-44: obsessive tonic-dominant focus with rhythmic variety compensating for harmonic limitation (Figure 10.4.3, p. 483)
- Op. 10, no. 2, I, mm. 73-76: I-V-I progression presented as contrary-motion near-fifth sequence dividing the octave almost in half (Figure 10.2.12, p. 472)

# Common Confusions
- Primary-triad harmony in Beethoven is not simplistic -- it is harmonically simple but contrapuntally sophisticated
- The emphasis on I-IV-V is a deliberate aesthetic choice enabling the Ludwig and other contrary-motion patterns, not a limitation
- Beethoven's "incessant Ludwiging may represent a kind of musical understanding, an implicit knowledge that teeters on the edge of the explicit" (p. 472)
