# Quick Definition

Chopin's use of a single melodic strategy -- stepwise descending voice leading -- to generate a range of harmonic effects spanning diatonic modality, functional harmony, and chromaticism, demonstrating the independence of melody and harmony as heterogeneous forces.

# Core Definition

Chopin deploys descending stepwise voice leading in three harmonic environments: diatonic triads (producing modal-sounding progressions), functional harmony (conforming to standard chord grammar), and chromatic seventh chords (producing nonfunctional chromaticism). The A minor prelude features a background of third-related triads connected by stepwise root descent (e-G-b-D-(F)-a), progressing to seventh chords linked by semitonal voice leading (p. 420). "The entire prelude is built on descending melodic steps: in the opening, diatonic steps connect mysterious triads that just barely conform to functional norms; in the middle, chromatic steps connect functionally anomalous sevenths" (p. 421). This demonstrates the Prime Directive: similar voice-leading operations produce different results in different collectional contexts.

# Prerequisites

- **Melodic strategy** -- The concept of strategic melodic goals within harmonic constraints
- **Prime Directive** -- The principle that similar voice-leading operations apply across collections
- **Heterogeneous hierarchy** -- The independence of melodic and harmonic systems

# Key Properties

1. A single melodic strategy (stepwise descent) produces modal, functional, and chromatic effects
2. Short clockwise motions in spiral diagrams operate across different collectional environments
3. Diatonic triads, chromatic triads, and chromatic seventh chords all support stepwise descent
4. Chopin shows "explicit awareness of the Prime Directive" (p. 424) -- scrambling stepwise descents
5. The contrapuntal logic is deeper than any specific harmonic effect it generates

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Prime Directive in Chopin:
1. Look for stepwise descending voice leading in all voices
2. Identify the collectional environment (diatonic triads, chromatic triads, chromatic sevenths)
3. Trace the motion on spiral diagrams (short clockwise moves)
4. Note transitions between collectional environments using the same voice-leading strategy
5. Check for scrambling of voice order (as in Op. 17, no. 4)

# Context & Application

These analyses demonstrate that "melody and harmony are heterogeneous, independent forces that can be combined in a wide variety of ways" (p. 419). Where Schenker says traditional concepts "fail" for the Revolutionary Etude, Tymoczko identifies the same chromatic 6-5 sequence but continues the stepwise descent through the following dominant sevenths. The deeper logic is "a fundamentally contrapuntal logic that exploits stepwise descending voice leading, taking short clockwise motions in a pair of spiral diagrams" (p. 424).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 420): Chopin's first etude, Op. 10, no. 1 -- stepwise descending voice leading fully compatible with functional harmony.

**Example 2** (pp. 420-422): A minor prelude -- diatonic triads connected by stepwise descent progressing to chromatic sevenths, analyzed as "short clockwise moves in two different geometrical spaces."

**Example 3** (p. 423): A minor Mazurka, Op. 7, no. 2 -- modal opening (iv-VI-i) from short descending moves on the triadic spiral, progressing to chromatic seventh-chord descent.

**Example 4** (pp. 423-424): Revolutionary Etude -- both Schenker and Tymoczko identify chromatic 6-5 sequence, but Tymoczko continues the descent through dominant sevenths where Schenker postulates a persistent C(b).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating Chopin's chromaticism as "beyond" analytical explanation
  **Correction**: It manifests a contrapuntal logic that can generate functional effects alongside nonfunctional ones

- **Error**: Following Schenker in postulating static notes where continuous descent is occurring
  **Correction**: The background is a sequence (continuous process) rather than a static harmony

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking Schenker's and Tymoczko's analyses of the Revolutionary Etude are contradictory
  **Clarification**: Both identify the same initial voice leading; the disagreement is about what happens next -- continuous descent vs. prolongation of a persistent note

- **Confusion**: Assuming diatonic and chromatic passages reflect different compositional techniques
  **Clarification**: Chopin uses the same technique (stepwise descent) in both environments, suggesting continuity between modal, functional, and chromatic practice
