# Quick Definition
The aspiration to separate the project of labeling harmonies from that of theorizing about their meaning and role, using harmonic labels as relatively neutral ground for evaluating claims about syntactic behavior.

# Formal Definition
Analytical objectivity, as Tymoczko advocates it, means using scale-degree analysis to provide relatively theory-neutral descriptions against which we can test assertions about harmonic behavior. The behavior, origin, or essence of a chord should have little influence on its label. A term like "dominant" becomes roughly synonymous with "V or vii-diminished," and "dominants proceed to tonics" becomes a testable, falsifiable claim rather than a definitional truth. Even a computer can check whether scale degrees 5, 7, and 2 typically proceed to scale degrees 1, 3, and 5.

# Musical Context
The tradeoff between functional and scale-degree analysis is between simplifying chord identification and simplifying the grammar of harmonic progressions. Tymoczko advocates for relatively objective descriptions as a foundation, while acknowledging that the difference between approaches is one of degree rather than kind. This objectivity is essential for tracing the subtle ways in which configurations of scale degrees change their behavior over the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

# Common Confusions
- Analytical objectivity does not mean analysis is purely mechanical. Even scale-degree analysis requires decisions about whether sonorities are harmonic or nonharmonic.
- Theorists who reject "trivial transliteration of note-content" often underestimate the regularity in local harmonic progressions -- regularity that becomes unmistakable at the level of objective description.
- Rejecting scale-degree analysis deprives theorists of virtually the only tool that can trace subtle changes in how scale-degree configurations change their behavior over centuries.
