# Quick Definition
An eighteenth-century figured-bass convention prescribing standard harmonizations for each bass scale degree in ascending and descending scalar passages, functioning as a practical theory of functional tonality.

# Formal Definition
The rule of the octave (ROTO) provides a default harmonization for stepwise bass motion through the scale. The traditional version calls for an applied dominant on ^6 and dominants on ^5 and ^4 (Figure 7.5.3, top). However, many functional composers preferred an alternative "fauxbourdon ROTO" that uses parallel first-inversion chords to move through a weaker chord on ^5 to a metrically strong arrival on ^4, continuing downward via Gjerdingen's "Prinner" (Figure 7.5.3, bottom). The fauxbourdon ROTO admits several variants: substituting IV for ii^6^, V^2^/IV for V^6^, and most importantly I6/4 for iii^6^.

# Musical Context
The rule of the octave is not merely a pedagogical schema but embodies a theory of functional tonality, as Holtmeier (2007) has argued. Its guiding principle -- that bass lines move along an abstract chain of descending thirds from ^3 to ^5 -- connects it directly to the descending-thirds model of harmonic function.

# Common Confusions
- The fauxbourdon ROTO's I6/4 substitution for iii^6^ recreates the ubiquitous modal progression I--V--IV--I within a functional context -- an idiom that is statistically common even while theoretically puzzling.
- Many "passing 6/4" chords in textbooks are actually instances of this specific fauxbourdon ROTO schema rather than examples of a general technique (Figure 7.5.7: more than 90% involve tonic or dominant).
