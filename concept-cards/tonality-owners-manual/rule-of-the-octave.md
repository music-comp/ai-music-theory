# Quick Definition

An eighteenth-century figured-bass convention prescribing standard harmonizations for each bass scale degree in ascending and descending scalar passages, functioning as a practical theory of functional tonality with an important alternative "fauxbourdon" variant.

# Core Definition

The rule of the octave (ROTO) provides a default harmonization for stepwise bass motion through the scale. The traditional version calls for an applied dominant on scale-degree 6 and dominants on scale-degrees 5 and 4 (p. 332, Fig. 7.5.3, top). However, many functional composers preferred an alternative "fauxbourdon ROTO" that uses parallel first-inversion chords to move through a weaker chord on scale-degree 5 to a metrically strong arrival on scale-degree 4, continuing downward to I via Gjerdingen's "Prinner" (p. 332, Fig. 7.5.3, bottom). The fauxbourdon ROTO admits several variants: substituting IV for ii6, V2/IV for V6, and most importantly I6/4 for iii6, which yields the I-V-IV-I progression of Neil Young's "Helpless" (p. 333). As Holtmeier (2007) argued, the rule of the octave is not merely a pedagogical schema but embodies a theory of functional tonality.

# Prerequisites

- **Harmonic cycle theory** -- The ROTO is organized by the descending-thirds principle
- **Descending-thirds arrangement** -- The ROTO's guiding principle is that bass lines move along a chain of descending thirds from scale-degree 3 to scale-degree 5

# Key Properties

1. Traditional ROTO: applied dominant on 6, dominants on 5 and 4
2. Fauxbourdon ROTO: parallel first-inversion chords with root-position chord on 4
3. Key substitution: I6/4 for iii6, yielding the I-V-IV-I progression
4. More than 90% of textbook "passing 6/4 chords" are instances of the fauxbourdon ROTO schema (p. 334, Fig. 7.5.7)
5. The ROTO embodies a theory of functional tonality, not merely a pedagogical tool
6. The traditional ROTO creates harmonic accents on metrically weak beats; the fauxbourdon alternative does not

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Rule of the Octave Usage:
1. Look for a stepwise descending bass line spanning an octave (or partial octave)
2. Check whether harmonization follows the traditional ROTO (applied dominant on 6) or the fauxbourdon variant (parallel first-inversion chords)
3. In the fauxbourdon variant, look for the characteristic I6/4 substitution for iii6
4. Note whether "passing 6/4" chords are actually instances of the fauxbourdon ROTO schema

# Context & Application

The ROTO appears prominently in Bach's Fifth Brandenburg Concerto, where the opening uses the fauxbourdon idiom to harmonize a bass descending from D4 to D3, with parallel first-inversion chords except for a root-position chord on scale-degree 4 (p. 331, Fig. 7.5.2). The fauxbourdon ROTO's I6/4 substitution recreates the ubiquitous modal progression I-V-IV-I within a functional context -- statistically common even while theoretically puzzling. Brahms uses the fauxbourdon ROTO in the second theme of his G major violin sonata Op. 78 to create a strong sense of retrofunctionality (p. 333, Fig. 7.5.5).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating all "passing 6/4 chords" as instances of a general technique
  **Correction**: More than 90% of "passing 6/4s" are instances of the specific fauxbourdon ROTO schema (Fig. 7.5.7), involving tonic or dominant

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the I-V-IV-I progression is always retrofunctional
  **Clarification**: The fauxbourdon ROTO's I6/4 substitution for iii6 recreates this progression within a functional context -- classical composers often group chords to avoid the explicit V-IV, placing it across a phrase boundary (p. 333)
