# Quick Definition
The use of repeating melodic-harmonic patterns transposed to different pitch levels, which became a powerful compositional technique and contributed significantly to the development of functional harmony.

# Formal Definition
Sequential patterning involves the repetition of a musical unit at different pitch levels, creating systematic progressions through harmonic space. Tymoczko identifies the mid-seventeenth-century enthusiasm for sequences as an important inflection point in the development of functional tonality. Sequences exploit the geometry of diatonic dyads and triads, and their systematic voice-leading patterns require an implicit understanding of chord structure (root, third, fifth). Renaissance sequences are connected to canon and imitation, while baroque sequences become codified as specific functional vocabulary.

# Musical Context
Sequential patterns are found throughout the Renaissance, where they arise naturally from imitative counterpoint. Ascending-fifth sequences, descending-fifth sequences, ascending-third patterns, and descending-step/ascending-fifth patterns all appear. The development of sequences represents one of several independent processes in the emergence of functional tonality. The fifths-and-steps arrangement itself has an inherently sequential structure.

# Common Confusions
- Sequential patterning in the Renaissance is more flexible than in the baroque, where sequences become codified as specific harmonic vocabulary (e.g., the Pachelbel progression).
- Sequences require some implicit understanding of inversional equivalence, since constructing them requires knowing which motions go in which voices.
- The mid-seventeenth-century enthusiasm for sequences is one reason for highlighting Corelli in the development of functional tonality.
