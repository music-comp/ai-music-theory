# Quick Definition

Themes built on contrary motion and diatonic thirds rather than stepwise descent, requiring an expanded catalogue of reductive targets beyond traditional Schenkerian templates.

# Core Definition

Tymoczko argues that Schenkerian theory's "single-minded focus on stepwise melody -- and its concomitant disdain for sequential structure -- is not well-equipped" (p. 428) to analyze themes whose structural foundation is contrary motion and diatonic voice exchanges rather than stepwise linear descent. The Op. 109 variations theme exemplifies this: each four-measure unit is "a near sequence by contrary motion, presenting thirds on E, B, E, and A(sharp), with each third accompanied by a voice exchange" (p. 426). The theme is composed of "two overlapping chains of thirds: ascending thirds in the left hand, overlapping with descending thirds in the right" (p. 427). This represents a broader incompatibility between Beethoven's lexicon and Schenkerian expectations, requiring "an expanded vocabulary of reductional targets" (section title, p. 426).

# Prerequisites

- **The Ludwig** -- The contrary-motion patterns that constitute Beethoven's characteristic vocabulary
- **Basic voice leading** -- Understanding diatonic third voice leadings and voice exchanges

# Key Properties

1. Structural foundation is contrary motion rather than stepwise descent
2. Voice exchanges are essential to the schema, not decorative
3. Near-sequential patterns serve as legitimate reductive targets
4. The basic voice leading and voice exchanges combine to produce near contrary-motion fifth sequences
5. Composer-specific vocabulary must be considered when producing reductions

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify:
1. Look for themes organized around diatonic thirds rather than stepwise lines
2. Check for voice exchanges that are structural rather than ornamental
3. Test whether the theme forms a near sequence by contrary motion
4. Verify that standard Schenkerian descent produces a forced or insensitive reading

# Context & Application

These themes demonstrate that "we need to consider a composer's individual vocabulary when producing musical reductions" (p. 429). The concept challenges the Schenkerian motto "semper idem sed non eodem modo" by showing that different composers deploy different strategies and schemas that vary with time and place.

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 426-427): Beethoven's Op. 109 variations theme -- each four-bar unit is a near sequence by contrary motion with two overlapping chains of thirds (Figures 9.4.1-9.4.3).

**Example 2** (p. 428): The third variation of Op. 109 explicitly articulates the contrary-motion reduction, "the composer helpfully analyzing his own music" (Figure 9.4.4).

**Example 3** (pp. 430-431): Op. 22 minuet opens with ascending-fifth contrary motion Eb-Bb-F decorated with voice exchanges and lower neighbors (Figures 9.4.7-9.4.8).

**Example 4** (p. 430): Op. 21 (First Symphony) minuet uses a similar ascending-fifth pattern (Figure 9.4.8).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Promoting weak notes to primary melodic status to force a Schenkerian reading
  **Correction**: Forte and Gilbert promote the first measure's weak F-sharp to primary status and ignore the theme's close on scale-degree 3 (p. 428)

- **Error**: Treating voice exchanges as always decorative
  **Correction**: In these themes, voice exchanges are essential to the schematic structure

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking these themes are "unanalyzable"
  **Clarification**: They simply require different reductive targets than Schenkerian theory provides

- **Confusion**: Assuming all functional themes must articulate stepwise descent
  **Clarification**: Contrary-motion and sequential patterns are equally valid reductive endpoints
