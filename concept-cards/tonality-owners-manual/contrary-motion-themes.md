# Quick Definition
Themes built on contrary motion and diatonic thirds rather than stepwise descent, characteristic of Beethoven's compositional vocabulary, which require an expanded catalogue of reductive targets beyond traditional Schenkerian templates.

# Formal Definition
Contrary-motion themes use the diatonic third's basic voice leading and voice exchanges as their structural foundation, organizing material as near sequences by contrary motion rather than as stepwise linear descents. The Op. 109 variations theme exemplifies this: each four-measure unit is a near sequence by contrary motion, presenting thirds on E, B, E, and A-sharp, with each third accompanied by a voice exchange. This represents "an expanded vocabulary of reductional targets" (Section 9.4 title) that supplements Schenkerian stepwise descent with sequential and contrary-motion patterns as legitimate analytical end-states.

# Musical Context
This concept challenges the single-minded Schenkerian focus on stepwise melody and its "concomitant disdain for sequential structure" (p. 428). Beethoven's third variation of Op. 109 makes the contrary-motion design explicit, "the composer helpfully analyzing his own music" (p. 427). Standard Schenkerian analysis is "not well-equipped" to analyze such themes because their vocabulary lies outside the Schenkerian catalogue of acceptable reductive targets.

# Examples
- Beethoven's Op. 109 variations theme: near sequence by contrary motion with two overlapping chains of thirds (Figures 9.4.1-9.4.3, pp. 426-427)

# Common Confusions
- These themes are not "unanalyzable" -- they simply require different reductive targets than Schenkerian theory provides
- Voice exchanges in these contexts are not decorative but essential to the schematic structure
- The incompatibility between Beethoven's lexicon and Schenkerian expectations is a broader issue: "we need to consider a composer's individual vocabulary when producing musical reductions" (p. 429)
