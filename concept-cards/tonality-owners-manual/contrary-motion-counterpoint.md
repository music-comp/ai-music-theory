# Quick Definition
Beethoven's characteristic fondness for outer-voice contrary motion as a comprehensive approach to functional counterpoint, encompassing the Ludwig, wedge sequences, registral expansion, and other patterns that exploit the fifth-based geometry of diatonic dyads.

# Formal Definition
Contrary-motion patterns in Beethoven range from simple Ludwig units to elaborate passages combining free 5-6 ascent with descending thirds and sixths. When augmented by parallel-motion options (Figure 10.2.6), contrary and parallel motion together provide "a simple but comprehensive set of outer-voice defaults for functionally tonal music-making" (p. 468). This adopts the Tinctorian perspective, analyzing functional counterpoint in terms of parallel and contrary patterns that isolate contrapuntal possibilities consistent with the harmonic grammar. The resulting idioms are "more general than the independent atoms that usually go by that name, being closer a complete list than an arbitrary set of building blocks" (p. 468).

# Musical Context
Contrary motion is Beethoven's default, distinguishing him from Mozart who favored outer-voice parallelism. It serves as a pitch-domain analogue of the crescendo, enabling registral expansion that is fundamental to Beethoven's sublime aesthetic. The Ludwig is the most common manifestation, but Beethoven also uses contrary-motion wedges, contrary motion along triads, and free combinations.

# Examples
- Op. 90, I, mm. 105-107: contrary-motion wedge (Figure 10.2.7, p. 469)
- Op. 2, no. 3, IV, mm. 127-133: contrary motion along F major triad progressing to ascending-fifth Ludwig (Figure 10.2.8, p. 469)
- Op. 130, I, mm. 18-19: contrary-motion pattern doubled at the third, extended through a complete octave (Figure 10.2.9, p. 469)
- Op. 2, no. 1, IV, mm. 34-42: free 5-6 ascent in left hand with contrary-motion melody descending through thirds and sixths (Figure 10.2.10, p. 469)

# Common Confusions
- Contrary motion is not just an occasional device but a comprehensive approach to tonal counterpoint in Beethoven
- The distinction between parallel and contrary patterns is more fundamental than the distinction between individual schemas
- Contrary-motion patterns consistent with functional harmony are a "complete list" rather than an arbitrary collection of idioms
