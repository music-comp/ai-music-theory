# Quick Definition

The specific pathways by which each Renaissance mode evolves toward ionian characteristics: mixolydian primarily through scale-degree preservation (raising the seventh), phrygian through scale-degree change (its tonic evolves into a minor-key dominant), and aeolian/dorian through a more complex combination.

# Core Definition

Each mode evolves toward ionian through a characteristic mechanism. *Mixolydian* is one accidental away from ionian and readily incorporates ionian routines by raising its seventh degree (scale-degree-preserving). It also adopts ionian qualities by exchanging primary and secondary centers, evolving from "G music that also emphasizes C" to "C music that emphasizes G" (scale-degree-changing, p. 386). *Phrygian* is maximally distant from ionian and the only mode not permitting an authentic cadence. Its tonic evolves into a minor-key dominant: "in its statistical behavior, the phrygian tonic is evolving into a minor-key dominant" (p. 389, Figure 8.6.9). *Aeolian and dorian* lie between these poles, requiring more extensive adjustments, with secondary major triads (VI, III, VII) playing a much larger role than in later functional music.

# Key Properties

1. Mixolydian: primarily scale-degree-preserving; one accidental from ionian
2. Phrygian: primarily scale-degree-changing; maximally distant from ionian; tonic becomes dominant
3. Aeolian/dorian: mixed mechanisms; slower process; secondary major triads remain prominent
4. Phrygian E chords are "constrained in how they move" (like a dominant) while A minor chords are "constrained in how they are approached" (like a tonic) -- role reversal (p. 389)
5. In Palestrina, III is far more common than in Bach; i^6^ gradually displaces III as primary harmonization of bass 3-hat in minor (Figure 8.6.13)
6. The process is "crooked and halting," not a linear progression (p. 392)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Analyze a Mode's Homogenization:
1. Identify the ostensible mode
2. For mixolydian: check frequency of raised vs. lowered seventh degree
3. For phrygian: check whether E chords behave as dominants (constrained in what follows) or tonics (constrained in what precedes)
4. For aeolian/dorian: compare the prominence of secondary major triads (VI, III, VII) to later functional norms
5. Note whether ambiguity about the tonal center is a feature of the music

# Context & Application

During the paratonal century (1550--1650), "functional and modal techniques exist side-by-side," producing music whose principles are "liquid and negotiable" (p. 392). Our sense of Renaissance music "digressing into major" reflects having acclimated to the scarcity of secondary major triads in later music -- "it would be more accurate to use a double negative: it *deemphasizes them less than J. S. Bach*" (p. 391).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 387, Figures 8.6.5--6): Scheidt's mixolydian "Gelobet seist du" and Marenzio's "Vorria parlare e dire" -- scale-degree-preserving homogenization with subtonic emphasis.

**Example 2** (pp. 388--389, Figures 8.6.8--9): Schein's "Aus tiefer Noth" -- phrygian tonic evolving into a minor-key dominant.

**Example 3** (pp. 390--391, Figure 8.6.10): Schutz's *St. Matthew Passion* Introit -- late modal music with weak minor-mode centricity.

**Example 4** (p. 391, Figure 8.6.14): Morley's "Leave, alas, this tormenting" (1600) -- remarkably early minor-mode functionality.

- **Error**: Applying a single homogenization model to all modes
  **Correction**: Each mode evolves through a different characteristic mechanism

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the paratonal period was confused or primitive
  **Clarification**: It was "a flexible, nondogmatic, and open period in which multiple syntaxes overlap" (p. 393)
