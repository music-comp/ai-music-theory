# Quick Definition
The two specific mechanisms by which Renaissance modes evolve toward ionian: scale-degree-preserving transformation (altering specific degrees while keeping the tonic fixed) and scale-degree-changing transformation (shifting which note serves as tonic while keeping pitches fixed).

# Formal Definition
Tymoczko's theory of modulation illuminates modal homogenization by identifying two mechanisms corresponding to the two notions of key distance. In scale-degree-preserving transformation, the modal tonic stays fixed while other degrees change position (e.g., G mixolydian raises ^7 to become G ionian). In scale-degree-changing transformation, a fixed pitch vocabulary changes meanings, with centricity shifting from one note to another (e.g., G mixolydian becomes C ionian by shifting the tonal center from G to C). Different modes tend toward different mechanisms: mixolydian primarily uses scale-degree-preserving mapping; phrygian uses scale-degree-changing mapping (the phrygian tonic evolves into a dominant); aeolian and dorian lie between these poles (Figure 8.6.1).

# Musical Context
During the paratonal century (1550--1650), functional and modal techniques coexist, producing music whose basic principles are "liquid and negotiable." Highly functional pieces (Morley, 1600) antedate highly modal pieces (Schutz, ca. 1665). The forces of minor-mode centricity are weak, liable to being dislodged by the scale's major triads. In Palestrina's masses, the secondary major triads (VI, III, VII) play a much more significant role than in later functional music.

# Examples
- Mixolydian to ionian (Figure 8.6.1): G mixolydian becomes G ionian (scale-degree-preserving) or becomes C ionian (scale-degree-changing). Marenzio's "Vorria parlare e dire" (1586) shows a substantially ionian mixolydian with strong subtonic emphasis (Figure 8.6.6).
- Phrygian to minor (Figure 8.6.9): Schein's "Aus tiefer Noth" (1627) shows phrygian E chords behaviorally becoming dominants -- constrained in how they move but not how they are approached (Figure 8.6.8).
- Aeolian/Dorian: Schutz's *St. Matthew Passion* Introit shows weak G minor centricity pulled toward secondary major triads on C, F, and B-flat (Figure 8.6.10).
- Figure 8.6.12--8.6.13: Statistical evidence showing III far more common in Palestrina than in Bach, with i^6^ gradually displacing III as the primary harmonization of bass ^3 in minor.

# Common Confusions
- The process is not a simple "correction" of modal errors: the paratonal period featured flexible, nondogmatic music where multiple syntaxes coexisted productively.
- Phrygian evolves in the opposite direction from mixolydian: the phrygian tonic becomes a dominant (scale-degree change) while the mixolydian tonic stays a tonic (scale-degree preservation).
- Our sense of Renaissance music "digressing into major" reflects our having acclimated to the scarcity of secondary major triads in later music -- modal music simply deemphasizes them less than Bach.
