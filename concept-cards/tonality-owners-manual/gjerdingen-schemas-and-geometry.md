# Quick Definition
Tymoczko's reinterpretation of Gjerdingen's galant schemas (Do-Re-Mi, Prinner, Romanesca, Quiescenza, etc.) as reflections of abstract voice-leading geometry rather than isolated historical idioms, tracing nine of eleven schemas to specific geometrical origins.

# Formal Definition
The book identifies theoretical origins for nine of eleven schemas listed in Gjerdingen's Music in the Galant Style appendix: dyadic space for the Quiescenza, Fonte, and Monte; triadic space for the Prinner and Romanesca; a canonic fourth-progression for the Fenaroli; and basic sentential logic (double transposition) for the Meyer, Do-Re-Mi, and Sol-Fa-Me. These are "not isolated idioms specific to the galant period but reflections of very general voice-leading possibilities known to composers in many genres" (Conclusion, p. 528).

# Musical Context
This reconception highlights the deeper structure behind style-specific idioms, showing that the same geometrical substrate appears across different musical dialects. Schemas are understood as the most basic forms most closely connected to abstract musical geometry, which is then embellished, varied, and refined to produce style-specific idioms. This is why similar schemas reappear across many different styles.

# Examples
- Do-Re-Mi schema employs double transposition between I and V (Figure 9.1.4, p. 410)
- The general Pachelbel progression versus the particular "Romanesca" (I-V6-vi-I6) of the galant (Conclusion, p. 529)
- The "converging cadence" as one manifestation of the diatonic third's basic voice leading, particularly important in the classical period but belonging to a larger genus (Conclusion, p. 530)

# Common Confusions
- Schemas are not merely historical curiosities but reflect fundamental geometrical possibilities
- The same schema can appear in different styles because the underlying geometry is universal
- Musical knowledge is often implicit -- composers discover these possibilities without realizing they are internalizing geometrical knowledge
