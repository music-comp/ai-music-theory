# Quick Definition

Tymoczko's reinterpretation of Gjerdingen's galant schemas as reflections of abstract voice-leading geometry rather than isolated historical idioms, tracing nine of eleven schemas to specific geometrical origins.

# Core Definition

The book identifies "theoretical origins for nine of the eleven schemas listed in the appendix of Gjerdingen's Music in the Galant Style: dyadic space for the Quiescenza, Fonte, and Monte; triadic space for the Prinner and Romanesca; a canonic fourth-progression for the Fenaroli; and basic sentential logic for the Meyer, Do-Re-Mi, and Sol-Fa-Me" (Conclusion, p. 528). These patterns are "not isolated idioms specific to the galant period but reflections of very general voice-leading possibilities known to composers in many genres" (p. 528). Tymoczko reconceives schemas "so as to highlight their deeper structure, taking their most basic forms to be those most closely connected to abstract musical geometry: a mathematical substrate that is then embellished, varied, and refined to produce the more specific idioms characterizing individual styles" (p. 529).

# Prerequisites

- **Voice-leading geometry** -- The mathematical framework underlying the geometrical connections
- **Double transposition** -- Three schemas (Meyer, Do-Re-Mi, Sol-Fa-Me) are instances of double transposition

# Key Properties

1. Nine of eleven Gjerdingen schemas traced to geometrical origins
2. Schemas reflect "very general voice-leading possibilities" not style-specific idioms
3. Musical knowledge is often "implicit, known in ways that may not be directly accessible to conscious self-reflection" (p. 528)
4. Given enough time, musicians will "eventually discover these possibilities, thereby internalizing a kind of geometrical knowledge without realizing it" (p. 528)
5. Culture preserves these discoveries as "important idioms or musical figures of speech" (p. 528)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Trace a Schema to Geometry:
1. Identify the voice-leading space relevant to the schema (dyadic, triadic, etc.)
2. Determine the basic voice-leading operation (transposition, inversion, basic voice leading)
3. Show that the schema's core pattern corresponds to efficient motion in that space
4. Distinguish the general geometrical pattern from the style-specific instantiation

# Context & Application

This reconception allows distinguishing "the general Pachelbel progression, the ubiquitous progression that harmonizes parallel thirds as root-third and third-fifth in alternation, from the particular 'Romanesca' that operates in the galant, a I-V6-vi-I6 opening gambit that is comparatively infrequent elsewhere" (p. 529). Similarly, the "converging cadence" is "just one manifestation of the diatonic third's basic voice leading, particularly important in the classical period but belonging to a larger genus" (p. 530).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating schemas as arbitrary historical conventions
  **Correction**: Most schemas reflect fundamental geometrical possibilities that are rediscovered across styles

- **Error**: Assuming Gjerdingen's schemas apply only to the galant period
  **Correction**: The underlying geometry is universal; galant schemas are style-specific instantiations

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking musicians must consciously understand geometry to use schemas
  **Clarification**: Musical knowledge is often implicit -- composers internalize geometrical knowledge without realizing it

- **Confusion**: Equating the general geometrical pattern with its style-specific form
  **Clarification**: The Pachelbel progression is general; the Romanesca is its galant-specific instantiation
