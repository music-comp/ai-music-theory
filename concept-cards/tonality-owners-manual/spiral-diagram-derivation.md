# Quick Definition
The two mathematical methods for constructing spiral diagrams: as the union of interleaved helices (one per chord mode) viewed from above, or as subspaces (line segments) within the higher-dimensional geometrical space containing all possible n-note chords.

# Formal Definition
Method 1 (union of helices): Starting from an n-note chord in an o-note scale, chromatic transposition traces a helix in a three-dimensional space where the vertical axis represents pitch sum. Transposition along the chord (t1) creates a new interleaved helix at pitch-sum distance o. For an n-note chord, n interleaved helices represent the n modes. Viewed from above, these produce the spiral diagram. Method 2 (subspace of chord space): The spiral diagrams are line segments in the higher-dimensional space determined by the equations for chord regions. Points on the left boundary relate by t1 to points on the right; moving off the right boundary requires applying t1 to all coordinates. Three flavors exist: when n divides o (diagonal voice leading connects same-angle chords), when n is coprime to o (basic voice leading connects angular neighbors), and mixed cases.

# Musical Context
The spiral diagrams are the book's central geometrical tool, representing all possible combinations of big-T transposition along the scale and little-t transposition along the chord. They reveal how hierarchically nested transpositions combine, which combinations nearly counteract each other (producing efficient voice leading), and why enharmonic respelling is necessary when modulatory schemas are repeated to return to their starting key.

# Common Confusions
- The spiral diagrams are abstract and topological (like subway maps) rather than concrete and geometrical (like topographical maps) -- distances on the diagram do not directly correspond to voice-leading sizes
- The apparent self-intersection of spiral curves is an artifact of projecting higher-dimensional structures onto 2D paper -- the helices do not actually intersect
- The diagrams come in three flavors depending on the relationship between chord size and scale size, each with different structural properties
