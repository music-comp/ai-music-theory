# Quick Definition
A general model of musical repetition combining three elements: a "cell" or "unit" of melodic lines, a collection of transpositions applied at each repeat, and a permutation moving lines among voices. Sequences are not just harmonic patterns but fundamental transformational processes.

# Formal Definition
The model uses Taneyev arrow notation: arrows point from source to destination voices and are labeled with transpositions. In most sequences, arrows form a stable structure that stays fixed as the music repeats. The model resolves difficulties in identifying sequential units by distinguishing the simple period (minimal block sufficient to generate the sequence) from the grand period (time for all voices to cycle back to initial melodic positions, all transposed by the same amount). The theory of voice leading and the theory of sequence are one and the same: every sequence determines a repeating contrapuntal pattern between analogous chords, and every voice leading can be expanded into a sequence.

# Musical Context
This framework encompasses Renaissance disguised repetition, baroque overt sequences, nineteenth-century contrary-motion wedges, and modern flexible transformations. The model reveals connections between seemingly distinct domains of repetition, canon, and voice leading. A central discovery of the Renaissance was that voice leadings could be repeated and chained algorithmically to convert isolated events into ongoing processes.

# Common Confusions
- Sequences are not just harmonic progressions; they are transformational processes combining transposition and permutation
- Previous theorists often neglected permutation as a sequence-generating operation, leading them to focus on the grand period and miss the simple period
