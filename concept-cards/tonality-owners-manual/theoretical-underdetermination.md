# Quick Definition

The principle that multiple different theoretical frameworks can accurately describe the same set of musical practices, with no single theory being uniquely "correct."

# Core Definition

Theoretical underdetermination is "our ability to construct different theories to account for one and the same set of observable facts" (p. 325). In physics, there are Lagrangian, Hamiltonian, and vector formulations of Newtonian mechanics. In music, there are "Rameau's descending-fifth theory, my own descending-thirds theory, and many others besides: theories based on idioms, functional categories, linear relationships, and so on" (p. 326). None is right or wrong; they offer different perspectives on a practice that was largely understood tacitly. "In choosing among them we must ask which gives the simplest and most compelling explanation of the broadest range of musical behavior. Different theorists will make different choices, reflecting preferences that are as much aesthetic as scientific" (p. 326).

# Key Properties

1. Multiple theories can describe the same musical practice equally well
2. Near-equivalence between theories does not make them identical -- they differ in scope and elegance
3. The choice among theories involves aesthetic as well as scientific criteria
4. Underdetermination does not mean "anything goes" -- theories make different predictions and have different strengths
5. The existence of multiple valid theories does not undermine the reality of the patterns they describe

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Theoretical Underdetermination:
1. Formulate two or more theoretical accounts of the same body of musical practice
2. Compare their predictions: do they produce approximately the same set of permitted/forbidden progressions?
3. Check whether each theory's strengths complement the other's weaknesses
4. Note that the practice itself antedates and is independent of any particular theoretical description

# Context & Application

The near-equivalence between Rameau's descending-fifth theory and Tymoczko's descending-thirds theory is the primary example: every rightward progression on the descending-thirds model articulates a consecutive segment of Rameau's descending-fifth paradigm (Figure 7.3.3). Both account for the same body of practice but emphasize different aspects. Rameau struggles with simple interchanges (I--V--I); Tymoczko's model handles them naturally.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 325, Figure 7.3.3): Each of Rameau's three basic progressions articulates a segment of the descending-third chain of pitch classes, demonstrating near-equivalence.

**Example 2** (p. 326): Bach's D major prelude analyzed through both lenses -- the Rameauian root-motion perspective and the descending-thirds perspective produce compatible but differently emphasized analyses.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Concluding that because theories are underdetermined, theoretical distinctions do not matter
  **Correction**: Different theories have different strengths, make different predictions, and illuminate different aspects of practice

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Equating underdetermination with relativism
  **Clarification**: Some theories are genuinely better than others for specific purposes; the point is that no single theory captures everything
