# Quick Definition
A harmonically stable sequence has transposition arrows differing only by octave, producing transpositionally related blocks regardless of cell content. A harmonically unstable (wedge) sequence has arrows with different transpositions, creating contrary-motion patterns that move through unrelated vertical states.

# Formal Definition
Harmonically stable sequences are structure-preserving in a very general way: all transpositions produce the same interval classes, so the sequence sounds "the same" harmonically at each iteration. Harmonically unstable sequences apply different transpositions to different voices, producing contrary-motion wedge patterns. Their grand periods typically contain multiple simple periods as voices cycle through different transpositions until returning to initial alignment. Unstable sequences in earlier music often exploit invertible counterpoint to create consonant results from the wedge structure.

# Musical Context
Schoenberg's "Angst und Hoffen" (Op. 15 no. 7) contains a wedge sequence transposing voices by different amounts. The second E minor prelude from *The Well-Tempered Clavier* features an invertible-counterpoint sequence at the twelfth. As music evolves from the Renaissance to the twentieth century, contrary-motion wedge sequences become increasingly important for harmonic expansion, providing access to new and unusual harmonic states through regular melodic motion.

# Common Confusions
- "Harmonically stable" does not mean the harmony stays the same; it means successive blocks are transpositionally related, preserving harmonic quality
- Harmonically unstable sequences are not random; they follow strict rules producing contrary-motion patterns, often exploiting invertible counterpoint for consonance
