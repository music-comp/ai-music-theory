# Quick Definition
The use of invertible counterpoint within sequential structures, where harmonically unstable sequences exploit the exchangeability of voices at specific intervals (octave, tenth, twelfth) to produce consonant results from contrary-motion patterns.

# Formal Definition
Any sequence with differing transpositional subscripts technically involves invertible counterpoint. In harmonically unstable sequences, the different transpositions applied to different voices create contrary-motion wedge patterns. These pass through unrelated vertical states unless the sequential unit features invertible counterpoint -- counterpoint that remains consonant when voices are exchanged at a specific interval. The 5-6 voice-leading pattern is invertible counterpoint at the tenth: one voice moves down by fifth while the other moves up by sixth, equivalent to transposing by tenth and then shifting the whole pattern. This can be embedded in triadic sequences as root-fifth, fifth-third, or both simultaneously.

# Musical Context
The second E minor prelude from *The Well-Tempered Clavier* features an invertible-counterpoint sequence at the twelfth (Figure 4.1.5). The 5-6 pattern (invertible at the tenth) generates one of the most common ascending-step sequences, with three canonic voices each sounding third-fifth, fifth-root, and root-third in succession. Two-voice canonic sequences can be generated from parallel or antiparallel motion by expanding or contracting every other chord, a form of contrary-motion distortion.

# Common Confusions
- Invertible counterpoint is not a separate concept from sequences; it is a structural property that makes harmonically unstable sequences consonant
- The 5-6 pattern is simultaneously invertible counterpoint at the tenth and a canonic sequence -- these are two descriptions of the same thing
