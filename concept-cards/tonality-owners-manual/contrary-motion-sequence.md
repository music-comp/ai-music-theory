# Quick Definition

A sequence that alters the distance between voice groups from one grand period to the next. All contrary-motion sequences can be derived from the enumeration of diatonic consonances, sharing the same Tinctorian pattern of relative motion. They are "more esoteric, a secret compositional art" (p. 188).

# Core Definition

Contrary-motion sequences require that transpositional arrows separate voices into at least two independent groups with different transpositions (p. 175). Every such sequence "can be derived from the list of diatonic consonances" (p. 184): the enumeration of consonances under a fixed note is itself a contrary-motion sequence with a four-note grand period, almost a repeating contrapuntal pattern (Figure 4.7.1, p. 184). Applying the Tinctoris Transform produces sequences moving by fifth, third, and step (Figure 4.7.2, p. 185). The center-of-gravity-preserving versions (the "Ludwig schema," -4/+3 and -3/+4) are "by far the most common of all these sequences" (p. 186). The Morte schema uses the -3/+4 combination to move from tonic to dominant (Figure 4.7.4, p. 186).

# Prerequisites

- **Voice Exchange** — Required for contrary-motion sequences
- **Harmonically Stable and Unstable Sequences** — Contrary-motion sequences are harmonically unstable

# Key Properties

1. Voices separate into at least two groups with different transpositions
2. All derivable from the enumeration of diatonic consonances
3. Share the same Tinctorian pattern of relative motion
4. Ludwig schema (-4/+3, -3/+4): most common type
5. Morte schema: -3/+4 from tonic to dominant
6. Step-sequences (-1/+6, -6/+1) are genuine, not factorable into fifths without an added voice
7. Ubiquitous in Beethoven, common in Bach/Scarlatti, rare in Mozart

# Construction / Recognition

## To Derive:
1. Start with the enumeration of consonances under a fixed note
2. Apply the Tinctoris Transform (various transpositions)
3. The result is a contrary-motion sequence

## To Recognize:
1. Look for voices moving in opposite directions
2. Check for the characteristic pattern of consonance types: third-octave-sixth-fifth-third
3. Identify which Tinctorian variant is used

# Context & Application

Contrary-motion sequences "help guide the transition from nineteenth-century functionality to twentieth-century modernism" (p. 184). They are "a secret compositional art not often subjected to theoretical or pedagogical scrutiny" (p. 188). The step-sequences require special treatment: adding a voice converts them into fifth-sequences (Figure 4.7.5, p. 186). Bach's WTC first F major fugue and Beethoven's *Hammerklavier* both use this factoring (Figures 4.7.6-7, p. 187).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming contrary-motion sequences are a small or unusual category
  **Correction**: They are ubiquitous in Beethoven and common in Bach/Scarlatti

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking "contrary motion" means only strict contrary motion
  **Clarification**: The term encompasses oblique and similar motion between grand periods (p. 159)
