# Quick Definition
A sequence that alters the distance between voice groups from one grand period to the next, typically with some voices ascending and others descending by different amounts. Requires voice exchanges and encompasses both oblique and similar motion between grand periods.

# Formal Definition
Registrally stable sequences preserve distance between voices from one grand period to the next; contrary-motion sequences alter those distances by one or more octaves. Canonic voice groups are always registrally stable (each line passes through the same transposition sequence). Contrary motion between groups requires that the transpositional arrows separate voices into at least two independent groups. Every contrary-motion sequence can be derived from the list of diatonic consonances: the enumeration of consonances under a fixed note is itself a contrary-motion sequence with a grand period of four notes, almost a repeating contrapuntal pattern.

# Musical Context
Contrary-motion sequences help guide the transition from nineteenth-century functionality to twentieth-century modernism. They are "more esoteric, a secret compositional art not often subjected to theoretical or pedagogical scrutiny." They are ubiquitous in Beethoven, common in Bach and Scarlatti, and rare in Mozart. The *Pathetique* episode's final section is a one-bar contrary-motion sequence moving hands an octave apart with each iteration. All such sequences can be derived by transposing the list of consonances, sharing the same Tinctorian pattern of relative motion.

# Common Confusions
- "Contrary motion" here encompasses oblique and similar motion between grand periods, not just strict contrary motion
- A two-voice sequence cannot be simultaneously contrary and canonic, but three or more voices can combine canon within groups with contrary motion between them
- All contrary-motion sequences share the same Tinctorian structure derived from the enumeration of consonances
