# Quick Definition

The Ludwig schema is a center-of-gravity-preserving enumeration of diatonic consonances (-4/+3 and -3/+4 contrary-motion patterns), "by far the most common of all these sequences" (p. 186). The Morte schema (John Rink's term) uses the -3/+4 ascending-fifth wedge to move from tonic to dominant.

# Core Definition

The Ludwig schema corresponds to the -4/+3 and -3/+4 entries in the table of contrary-motion fifth sequences (Figure 4.7.2, p. 185). One voice descends by fifth (-4 diatonic steps) while the other ascends by fourth (+3), or vice versa. These are center-of-gravity-preserving: ascending and descending motions nearly cancel. On the spiral diagram, the -4/+3 pattern "moves largely radially through the space of diatonic dyads" (p. 186), systematically enumerating consonances at each radial position before moving angularly. The Morte schema uses -3/+4 to move from tonic to dominant, "often with an augmented sixth tonicizing the dominant" (Figure 4.7.4, p. 186). Its first three notes commonly move from tonic to subdominant, "ascending-fifth counterpoint articulating a descending-fifth progression" (p. 186).

# Key Properties

1. Ludwig: -4/+3 and -3/+4 (center-of-gravity-preserving)
2. Most common of all contrary-motion sequences
3. Enumerates consonances most efficiently (largely radial motion)
4. Morte: -3/+4 from tonic to dominant with augmented sixth
5. Parenthesized accidentals turn fifths to tritones, making fully invertible at octave
6. Ubiquitous in Beethoven, common in Bach/Scarlatti, rare in Mozart

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Ludwig:
1. Look for voices moving in opposite directions by fourth and fifth
2. Check that center of gravity is approximately preserved
3. Verify the consonance pattern: third-octave-sixth-fifth-third

## To Identify Morte:
1. Look for ascending-fifth wedge moving toward the dominant
2. Check for augmented sixth tonicizing the dominant
3. First three notes typically move I to IV

# Context & Application

The Ludwig schema embeds the contrary-motion pattern familiar from many functional passages. The Morte schema appears as a standard galant voice-leading pattern. All contrary-motion sequences share the same Tinctorian structure; the Ludwig/Morte are the most common instances. These patterns are "esoteric, a secret compositional art not often subjected to theoretical or pedagogical scrutiny" (p. 188).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming "Ludwig" refers to a specific Beethoven piece
  **Correction**: It is named for the Ludwig schema discussed in Section 10.1, describing a general contrary-motion type

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the Ludwig and Morte are different in kind from other contrary-motion sequences
  **Clarification**: They all share the same Tinctorian structure; Ludwig/Morte are the most common instances
