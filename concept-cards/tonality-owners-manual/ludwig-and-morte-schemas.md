# Quick Definition
The Ludwig schema is a center-of-gravity-preserving enumeration of diatonic consonances (the -4/+3 and -3/+4 contrary-motion sequences), by far the most common of all contrary-motion sequences. The Morte schema (John Rink's term) uses the -3/+4 combination to move from tonic to dominant.

# Formal Definition
The Ludwig schema corresponds to the -4/+3 and -3/+4 entries in the table of contrary-motion fifth sequences (Figure 4.7.2), where one voice descends by fifth (-4 diatonic steps) while the other ascends by fourth (+3), or vice versa. These sequences are center-of-gravity-preserving: the ascending and descending motions nearly cancel. They systematically enumerate consonances at each radial position before moving between fifth and sixth on the spiral diagram (Figure 4.7.3). The Morte schema uses the ascending-fifth version (-3/+4) to move from tonic to dominant, often with an augmented sixth tonicizing the dominant. Its first three notes commonly move from tonic to subdominant.

# Musical Context
The Ludwig schema embeds the contrary-motion pattern from Figure 1.5.6 and is ubiquitous in Beethoven, common in Bach and Scarlatti, rare in Mozart. The Morte schema appears in passages moving from I to V. Parenthesized accidentals turn perfect fifths into tritones, making the sequences fully invertible at the octave. Contrary-motion sequences are described as "esoteric, a secret compositional art not often subjected to theoretical or pedagogical scrutiny" -- "magic tricks jealously guarded from the public."

# Common Confusions
- The Ludwig schema is not a specific piece by Beethoven; it is named for the "Ludwig" schema discussed in Section 10.1
- All contrary-motion sequences share the same Tinctorian structure; the Ludwig/Morte schemas are the most common instances
- The -4/+3 pattern enumerates consonances most efficiently, moving largely radially with minimal angular motion
