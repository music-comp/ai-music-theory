# Quick Definition
The gradual acceptance and standardization of seventh chords as independent harmonic entities, evolving from the irreducible Renaissance seventh through the early 1600s to become a standard part of functional harmonic vocabulary.

# Formal Definition
Seventh chord standardization is one of several largely independent processes in the development of functional tonality. Seventh chords evolved from the Renaissance seventh (an irreducible suspension-based sonority) into accepted harmonic entities. The process was driven by the logical consequence of treating all suspensions as decorative: if a suspension always stands for its resolution, then irreducible suspension-based seventh chords must be considered harmonic rather than nonharmonic. The broad acceptance of seventh chords and second-inversion triads is identified by Tymoczko as a distinct historical process occurring alongside (but separate from) modal homogenization, inversional equivalence, and other developments.

# Musical Context
Renaissance sevenths appear as early as Dufay and are used throughout the Renaissance. The most common form resembles and evolves into the baroque predominant seventh, while other forms occasionally produce sounding dominant sevenths. Fetis wrongly claimed dominant seventh chords originated at the end of the sixteenth century, mistaking standard Renaissance practice for innovation. The early 1600s represent a time when additional functional vocabulary, including seventh chords, was being standardized.

# Common Confusions
- Seventh chords did not suddenly appear at a specific historical moment. They evolved from Renaissance suspension-based idioms through a gradual process of reinterpretation.
- The idealized consonant style of counterpoint textbooks never actually existed: even Palestrina's music contains irreducible seventh chords.
- The standardization of seventh chords is a separate process from other developments like modal homogenization or inversional equivalence.
