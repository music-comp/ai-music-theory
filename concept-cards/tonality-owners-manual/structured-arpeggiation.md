# Quick Definition

A type of melodic sequence where a surface voice systematically articulates a series of background lines that need not themselves be sequential, creating foreground repetition from regular arpeggiative motion along a nonsequential background.

# Core Definition

"The most basic form of melodic sequence is structured arpeggiation, in which a surface voice systematically articulates a series of background lines that need not themselves be sequential" (p. 188). The background voices may initially appear in fixed octaves (suggesting a chorale-like background with specific pitches) and later switch octaves (suggesting a more abstract scalar background). "The sequence is a foreground phenomenon, the product of surface voices moving regularly along a nonsequential background" (p. 189). The technique is the inverse of "de-arpeggiation" discussed in the Prelude to Ch. 5, where arpeggiated textures are converted into chorales.

# Prerequisites

- **Thinking Within the Chord** — The concept of voices moving within a chordal background

# Key Properties

1. Surface voice articulates background lines systematically
2. Background need not be sequential
3. Foreground repetition arises from regular arpeggiative motion
4. Background may shift between fixed-octave and abstract-scalar
5. Creates hierarchy: sequential surface over nonsequential background
6. Inverse of de-arpeggiation (textural reduction)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify:
1. Look for a single voice moving through what sounds like multiple lines
2. Check if the arpeggiated pattern is regular and repeating
3. Verify that the implied background voices are not themselves sequential
4. Track whether background voices appear in fixed or shifting octaves

# Context & Application

Bach's first G major cello suite (BWV 1007) opening arpeggiates three voices with initially systematic registral assignment that becomes more flexible (Figure 4.8.1, p. 188). Chopin's A minor prelude has a very similar structure. The use of nonharmonic tones is interesting: the top voice is always decorated by a diatonic lower neighbor, so notes can be simultaneously an octave displacement and a neighbor.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating arpeggiation as simple arpeggio patterns
  **Correction**: Structured arpeggiation involves a systematic relationship between surface melody and background voices

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Assuming the background must be sequential because the surface is
  **Clarification**: The sequence is a foreground phenomenon; background voices can be freely composed
