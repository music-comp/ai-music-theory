# Quick Definition
A type of melodic sequence in which a surface voice systematically articulates a series of background lines that need not themselves be sequential, creating the impression of repetition through regular arpeggiative motion.

# Formal Definition
In structured arpeggiation, the sequence is a foreground phenomenon produced by surface voices moving regularly along a nonsequential background. The background voices may initially appear in fixed octaves (suggesting a chorale-like background with specific pitches) and later switch octaves (suggesting a more abstract scalar background). This creates a hierarchy: the arpeggiative pattern transposes systematically, but the underlying harmony may not be sequential at all.

# Musical Context
The opening of Bach's first G major cello suite (BWV 1007) is a classic example, arpeggiating three voices with initially systematic registral assignment that becomes more flexible. The technique appears in Chopin's A minor prelude (similar structure), Amy Beach's "Fire-Flies," and Louis Armstrong's "West End Blues." Distinguishing transposition along the chord from transposition along the scale can be difficult: over a fixed harmony, chordal transposition preserves the harmonic/nonharmonic distinction while scalar transposition does not.

# Common Confusions
- Structured arpeggiation is not the same as simple arpeggio patterns; it involves a systematic relationship between a surface melody and background voices
- The distinction between harmonic tones (transposing along the chord) and nonharmonic tones (transposing along the scale) has transformational significance
