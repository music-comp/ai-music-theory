# Quick Definition

The second characterization of musical knowledge: the internalization of idioms and tendencies that conform to no general laws -- "like knowledge of fashion or slang, knowing a musical style is often a matter of knowing to do things this way and not another, for no particularly good reason" (p. 3).

# Core Definition

Particular musical knowledge refers to style-specific practices that cannot be derived from more general principles. It is "the purview of schema theory, deriving from Leonard Meyer by way of Robert Gjerdingen" (p. 3). This knowledge was historically preserved through hands-on pedagogical traditions that "can preserve idioms resisting systematic generalization" (p. 19). Earlier composers "were trained from an early age and in a hands-on manner" (p. 19), which enabled transmission of knowledge that textbooks cannot capture.

# Key Properties

1. Style-specific practices conforming to no general laws
2. Comparable to knowledge of fashion or slang
3. Transmitted through hands-on apprenticeship, not textbooks
4. Resists systematic generalization
5. Studied through schema theory
6. Many particular idioms nonetheless originate in constraints inherent to musical materials

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Particular Knowledge:
1. Find a recurring musical pattern that is style-specific
2. Check whether it can be derived from more general principles
3. If not, it represents particular knowledge -- a schema or idiom
4. Note that even particular knowledge may reflect optimal paths through geometrical space

# Context & Application

Tymoczko argues that particular knowledge and general principles coexist: "composers learn both specific idioms and also more general and systematic principles" (p. 29). The distinction between "deep" and "shallow" schemas captures this: the deep Prinner is a general voice-leading pattern used from Renaissance to the present, while the shallow Prinner is a specific galant mannerism (p. 31).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 27, Figure 1.5.1): Bach's cadential formula where V2 bass steps down to tonic without resolving -- a particular idiom not captured by general rules about seventh resolution.

**Example 2** (p. 29): Galant preference for I6 over iii in the nonsequential Romanesca -- a particular practice predating the eighteenth century.

**Example 3** (p. 31): Gjerdingen's "Prinner" functioning as precadential "riposte" in galant music but as a cadence in the Renaissance -- the same pattern taking different rhetorical roles.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating particular knowledge as entirely arbitrary
  **Correction**: Many schemas originate in constraints inherent to musical materials -- "responses to the contours of musical space" (p. 31)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking particular and general knowledge are opposed
  **Clarification**: Both coexist; the "deep" version of a schema is general while its "shallow" version is style-specific
