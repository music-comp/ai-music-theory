# Quick Definition
A spacing-preserving voice leading between unrelated chords (neither transpositionally nor inversionally related), which preserves chordal-step distance and cannot produce voice crossings -- the most general form of the crossing-free voice leadings that underlie the spiral diagrams.

# Formal Definition
A voice leading is spacing-preserving (strongly crossing-free) if it preserves chordal-step distance, meaning its voices can never be arranged in register so that they cross. If plotted on the pitch-class circle, each voice glides smoothly to its destination without sounding the same pitch class as any other voice. Spacing-preserving voice leadings between transpositionally related chords combine T (scale transposition) and t (chord transposition); between inversionally related chords, I and i. For unrelated chords, a spacing-preserving voice leading is called an interscalar transposition. All interscalar transpositions between any two chords can be derived by combining any one with transpositions along the chord. Voice crossings never make a voice leading smaller, so hierarchical self-similarity can arise as a byproduct of the search for efficient voice leading.

# Musical Context
Interscalar transposition provides the formal basis for the spiral diagrams and for understanding how voices move between different chords in a way that avoids crossings. The concept connects to Schenkerian analysis, which deemphasizes voice exchanges by assigning them to the surface, preserving crossing-free backgrounds that are recognizably scalar.

# Common Confusions
- Interscalar transposition is not the same as ordinary transposition -- it applies between chords that are not related by transposition
- Spacing-preserving does not mean "small" -- it means crossing-free, which is a structural rather than quantitative property
- The distinction between spacing-preserving voice leadings and voice exchanges is fundamental: the spiral diagrams represent only the former
