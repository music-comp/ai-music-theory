# Quick Definition
Melodic patterns that transpose along both the chord (preserving harmonic/nonharmonic distinctions) and the scale (not preserving them), creating hierarchical networks where the distinction between chord tones and passing tones plays a structural transformational role.

# Formal Definition
Melodic sequences can be modeled with two kinds of transformational arrows: little-t arrows representing intervals within the triad (preserving harmonic/nonharmonic distinctions) and big-T arrows representing intervals within the scale (not preserving them). Over a fixed harmony, chordal transposition preserves the distinction between harmonic and nonharmonic tones while scalar transposition does not. A good rule of thumb: open noteheads (chord tones) change positions under scalar transposition but stay fixed under chordal transposition. These structures extend to tonal answers in fugues, where alternating tonic-dominant entries are exact transpositions of complex hierarchical structures.

# Musical Context
Armstrong's "West End Blues" moves a motive down along the Eb major chord, with the first note a diatonic upper neighbor. Amy Beach's "Fire-Flies" moves motives along both D and A melodic-minor scales, then along the A minor triad. Mozart's K.310 second theme contains five melodic sequences in twelve measures. Classical music is deeply repetitive even when not strictly sequential, with motives moving along chords, scales, chordal subsets, and bespoke collections. Tonal answers in fugues are exact melodic sequences moving along the tonic-dominant fifth.

# Common Confusions
- Tonal answers are not distortions of the subject; they are exact transpositions of complex hierarchical structures
- The same motive can be subjected to multiple interpretations, treated first as scalar intervals then as triadic intervals
- Distinguishing chordal from scalar transposition can be difficult but is essential for understanding melodic structure
