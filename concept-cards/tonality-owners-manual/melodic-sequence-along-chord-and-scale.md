# Quick Definition

Melodic patterns transposing along both the chord (preserving harmonic/nonharmonic distinctions, little-t) and the scale (not preserving them, big-T), creating hierarchical networks where the chord-tone/passing-tone distinction plays a structural role. Tonal answers in fugues are exact transpositions of these hierarchical structures.

# Core Definition

Two kinds of transformational arrows model melodic sequences: "little-t arrows representing intervals within the triad and big-T arrows representing intervals within the scale" (p. 189). Over a fixed harmony, "transposition along the chord preserves the distinction between harmonic and nonharmonic, while transposition along the scale does not" (p. 190). "Intervallic content is not something fixed, but rather a variable whose value is revealed by musical development: one and the same motive can be subjected to multiple interpretations" (p. 190). Fugue subjects' tonal answers are "exact transpositions of complex hierarchical structures" (p. 190), with notes associated with tonic answered down by fourth and dominant notes by fifth.

# Key Properties

1. Two arrow types: little-t (chordal, preserving H/NH) and big-T (scalar, not preserving)
2. Over fixed harmony: chordal transposition preserves chord-tone status
3. Open noteheads change position under scalar transposition, stay fixed under chordal
4. The same motive can be interpreted with either arrow type
5. Tonal answers are exact hierarchical transpositions, not distortions
6. "Interscalar transposition" can shift melodies between different-sized collections

# Construction / Recognition

## To Analyze:
1. Identify whether a motive transposes along the chord or the scale
2. Check if open noteheads (chord tones) change position: yes = scalar, no = chordal
3. Track how the distinction between harmonic and nonharmonic tones transforms

# Context & Application

Armstrong's "West End Blues" moves a motive along the Eb chord (Figure 4.8.2). Beach's "Fire-Flies" uses both scalar and chordal transposition (Figure 4.8.3). Mozart K.310 second theme has five melodic sequences in twelve measures (Figure 4.8.5). Mozart K.533 moves the same intervals between a three-note triad and a four-note seventh chord -- "interscalar transposition" that Debussy later used between diatonic and whole-tone collections (Figure 4.8.7, p. 192).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating tonal answers as distortions of the subject
  **Correction**: They are exact transpositions of complex hierarchical structures

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking scalar and chordal transposition produce the same results
  **Clarification**: They differ in whether the harmonic/nonharmonic distinction is preserved
