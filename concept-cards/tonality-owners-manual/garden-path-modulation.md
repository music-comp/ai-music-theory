# Quick Definition
A modulatory technique where key areas are juxtaposed without a strong signal of the new key, creating the impression of a nonsyntactical harmonic "hiccup" across the key boundary.

# Formal Definition
Named by analogy with garden-path sentences in linguistics (e.g., "the old man the boat"), garden-path modulations juxtapose key areas such that the progression spanning the boundary is not syntactical in either key. Unlike pivot-chord modulation (which creates an overlap) or direct modulation (which signals the new key with an unambiguous dominant), garden-path modulations produce seemingly modal or anomalous progressions that are retrospectively demystified once the new key is established. The term reflects the listener's experience of being led down an interpretive path that turns out to be incorrect.

# Musical Context
Garden-path modulations can create the *effect* of modality even while conforming to functional tonal laws within each key area. They are relatively uncommon compared to pivot-chord or direct modulation but appear as a deliberate expressive device, particularly in the nineteenth century.

# Examples
- Bach's "In allen meinen Taten" (BWV 13.6, mm. 3--4): A V--iii progression sounds modal, but everything before D minor is syntactical in B-flat major and everything after is syntactical in F major (Figure P7.5).
- Grieg's "Gade" (Op. 57, no. 2, mm. 99--103): A dramatically mixolydian-sounding cadence is immediately and retrospectively demystified as spanning a key boundary. The passage rewrites the opening theme, which originally used a standard V--I cadence (Figure P7.5).

# Common Confusions
- Garden-path modulations are not actual modal progressions -- each key area individually conforms to functional tonal syntax. The apparent modality is an artifact of the juxtaposition.
- This technique illustrates that syntax must be considered in the context of aesthetics: nineteenth-century composers sometimes maintained deniability while creating the *impression* of nonfunctionality.
