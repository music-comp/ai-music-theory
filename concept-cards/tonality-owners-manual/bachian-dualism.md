# Quick Definition

The systematic contrast between ascending-fifth and descending-fifth progressions in J. S. Bach's music, reflecting a dyadic compositional logic based on the two forms of the diatonic third's basic voice leading.

# Core Definition

Tymoczko identifies a dualistic structure in Bach where ascending fifths (using the diatonic third's ascending basic voice leading) and descending fifths (using the descending form) are thematized as contrasting musical directions. The ascending form generates tension-increasing progressions while the descending form produces tension-releasing cadences (p. 343). C. P. E. Bach's claim that "the third of a major triad generally has a tendency to ascend" may reference this ascending-subdominant practice (p. 344). The two forms of the basic voice leading can be related by *diatonic* inversion around the supertonic, with the ascending subdominant being the inversional counterpart of V--I (Figure 7.7.2).

In Bach's chorales, approximately a third of root-position IV--I progressions incorporate an ascending leading tone, while in Beethoven's string quartets this occurs only a handful of times (p. 345).

# Key Properties

1. Bach thematizes the contrast between ascending-fifth and descending-fifth basic voice leadings
2. Ascending fifths increase tension; descending fifths release it
3. The two forms are related by diatonic inversion around the supertonic
4. Paradoxically, IV--I is both a musical antipode to V--I and an alternative expression of the same functional logic (both permit resolving tritones)
5. The music is consistent with triadic grammar but arises from a different, dyadic thought process
6. The practice largely disappears by the classical era

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Bachian Dualism:
1. Look for systematic contrasts between ascending-fifth and descending-fifth sequential passages
2. Check whether ascending-subdominant progressions (IV--I with ascending 6-hat) appear regularly
3. Note whether the two forms of the basic voice leading are highlighted in outer voices
4. Look for formal sections organized by the ascending/descending contrast

# Context & Application

In the pieces Tymoczko analyzes, Roman numerals are accurate but uninformative -- "the real compositional thinking operates at the dyadic level of the basic voice leading" (p. 343). This perspective is crucial for understanding Bach's preludes, inventions, and fugues. The pattern of ascending-fifth tension followed by descending-fifth release anticipates the "up-and-down-the-ladder" schema of classical developments.

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 345--346, Figure 7.7.4): The first two-part invention (BWV 772) -- initial Pachelbel sequence inverted around B, producing ascending-subdominant progressions with dominant-tonic energy.

**Example 2** (pp. 347--348, Figures 7.7.6--8): The C-sharp major prelude from *WTC* Book I -- ascending-fifth sequences ratchet up tension, followed by descending-fifth release.

**Example 3** (pp. 349--350, Figure 7.7.9): "Ach, lieben Christen, seid getrost" (BWV 256) -- rapidly alternates ascending-fifth schemas and descending-fifth cadences.

**Example 4** (pp. 350--356, Figures 7.7.10--14): The second B minor fugue from *WTC* -- episodes use ascending vs. descending basic voice leading to articulate three large formal sections.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Analyzing Bach's ascending-fifth passages purely in triadic Roman-numeral terms
  **Correction**: While the analysis will be technically correct, it misses the dyadic logic that generates the passages

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Equating Bachian dualism with Riemannian dualism
  **Clarification**: Bachian dualism operates diatonically (inversion around the supertonic) rather than chromatically (as in Riemann/Oettingen)

- **Confusion**: Thinking the ascending subdominant is a universal feature of functional harmony
  **Clarification**: It appears in about a third of Bach's IV--I progressions but virtually disappears in classical-era music -- it is dialect-specific
