# Quick Definition
The systematic contrast between ascending-fifth and descending-fifth progressions in J. S. Bach's music, reflecting a dyadic compositional logic based on the two forms of the diatonic third's basic voice leading.

# Formal Definition
Tymoczko identifies a dualistic structure in Bach where ascending fifths (using the diatonic third's ascending basic voice leading) and descending fifths (using the descending form) are thematized as contrasting musical directions. The ascending basic voice leading generates tension-increasing progressions, while the descending form produces tension-releasing cadences. This dualism connects to the music-theoretical tradition of "dualism" (Riemann, Oettingen), which conceives ascending fifths as inversions of descending fifths, though here the inversion operates *diatonically* rather than chromatically. C. P. E. Bach's claim that "the third of a major triad generally has a tendency to ascend" may reference this ascending-subdominant practice.

# Musical Context
This dyadic logic often underlies music that is consistent with triadic Roman-numeral grammar but seems to arise from a different thought process. Roman numerals become accurate but uninformative -- the real compositional thinking operates at the dyadic level of the basic voice leading. This perspective is crucial for understanding Bach's preludes, inventions, and fugues.

# Examples
- The first two-part invention (BWV 772): The initial Pachelbel-type sequence is inverted around B near the end, producing ascending-subdominant progressions with dominant-tonic energy (Figure 7.7.4).
- The C-sharp major prelude from *WTC* Book I: Ascending-fifth sequences ratchet up tension, followed by descending-fifth sequences that release it (Figure 7.7.6--7.7.8).
- "Ach, lieben Christen, seid getrost" (BWV 256): Rapidly alternates ascending-fifth and descending-fifth cadences (Figure 7.7.9).
- The second B minor fugue from *WTC*: Episodes use ascending vs. descending basic voice leading to articulate large formal sections (Figures 7.7.10--7.7.14).

# Common Confusions
- Bachian dualism is not the same as Riemannian dualism: it operates diatonically (inversion around the supertonic) rather than chromatically.
- The ascending subdominant (IV--I with ascending leading tone) appears in about a third of Bach's root-position IV--I progressions but virtually disappears in Beethoven -- this is not a universal feature but a dialect-specific practice.
