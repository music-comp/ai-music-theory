# Quick Definition

Resolutions where familiar harmonic objects move contrary to standard expectations on the spiral diagrams (counterclockwise/ascending voice leading): the ascending-fifth resolution of the diatonic third, the "ascending subdominant" IV-I, and retrofunctional seventh-chord resolutions where the seventh ascends.

# Core Definition

On the spiral diagrams, "expected" resolutions move clockwise (descending voice leading) while wrong-way resolutions move counterclockwise (ascending voice leading). For triads, V-I and IV-I have a dualistic symmetry: each has a direct voice leading (two voices moving stepwise) and an indirect voice leading (two voices by third, one by step) (Figure 3.4.10, p. 117). Both indirect voice leadings can be embellished with passing tones to create a diminished triad: the V-I form produces a standard descending-fifth progression, while the IV-I form produces an "ascending subdominant." For seventh chords, counterclockwise motion produces common-tone resolutions where the seventh ascends, "gradually liberated from their contrapuntal obligations" as the nineteenth century progresses (p. 146).

# Key Properties

1. Move counterclockwise on spiral diagrams (ascending voice leading)
2. Dualistic symmetry between V-I and IV-I voice leadings
3. Both have direct (stepwise) and indirect (third-based) forms
4. Passing tones create diminished triads in both cases
5. Seventh-chord wrong-way = ascending seventh (retrofunctional)
6. Become increasingly important in the 19th century

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify:
1. Check voice-leading direction: ascending = wrong-way
2. For triads, compare V-I (descending steps) with IV-I (ascending steps)
3. For seventh chords, look for the seventh ascending rather than descending

# Context & Application

The "ascending subdominant" creates a structural parallel with the descending dominant that is not merely a "weaker" version. Nineteenth-century composers increasingly allow the "dissonant" seventh to ascend, enabling fuller exploration of tetrachordal geometry. This expansion culminates in twentieth-century concepts of tritone and minor-third substitution for dominant chords.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating wrong-way resolutions as errors or weaknesses
  **Correction**: They are legitimate voice-leading patterns with a long compositional history

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the ascending subdominant is a weaker V-I
  **Clarification**: It is structurally parallel to the descending dominant, a dualistic partner, not a weaker version
