# Quick Definition
Resolutions in which familiar harmonic objects move contrary to standard expectations: the ascending-fifth resolution of the diatonic third, the "ascending subdominant" (IV-I with passing tones creating a diminished triad), and counterclockwise (retrofunctional) seventh-chord resolutions.

# Formal Definition
On the spiral diagrams, "expected" resolutions move clockwise (descending voice leading) while "wrong-way" resolutions move counterclockwise (ascending voice leading). For diatonic thirds, the ascending basic voice leading can combine plagal root motion with an authentic tritone resolution (^6-^7-^1 against ^4-^3). For triads, the IV-I progression has both a direct voice leading (two voices descending by step) and an indirect one (two voices moving by third, one by step) embellished with passing tones to create a diminished triad -- the "ascending subdominant." For seventh chords, counterclockwise motion produces "common-tone" resolutions where the seventh ascends.

# Musical Context
These resolutions represent a dualistic symmetry between dominant (V-I) and subdominant (IV-I) fifth motions, each having direct and indirect voice leadings. The tension between functional tonality and descending voice leading is a recurring theme: counterclockwise resolutions become increasingly important as the nineteenth century progresses, with the "dissonant" seventh gradually liberated to ascend. This expansion enables a more thorough exploration of tetrachordal geometry, culminating in twentieth-century concepts of tritone and minor-third substitution.

# Common Confusions
- "Wrong-way" does not mean incorrect; these are legitimate and historically important voice-leading patterns that move against the expected direction on the spiral
- The ascending subdominant is structurally parallel to the descending dominant, not a weaker version of it
