# Quick Definition
Modulations by small chromatic intervals (typically semitone) that preserve scale-degree roles, familiar in jazz and popular music as a dramatic "key change" for a final chorus or solo section.

# Formal Definition
Sidestepping (or pumping-up) modulations exploit scale-degree-preserving key distance, where the nearest keys are semitonally related (e.g., C major to D-flat major). The same musical material is performed in both keys, with each scale degree shifting by the same small chromatic interval. This produces a direct, dramatic effect because the entire musical texture shifts uniformly. The technique is most relevant when instruments do the same thing before and after the modulation -- for instance, a singer performing the same melody in both keys. These modulations are slides along the scalar spiral diagram.

# Musical Context
Sidestepping is common in jazz, popular music, and musical theater, often used to heighten energy for a final chorus. It sometimes appears in classical music as well: Lewin (1984) and McCreless (1996) discuss examples including the end of the scherzo of Beethoven's *Hammerklavier* (Op. 106) and Miles Davis's "So What."

# Examples
- Beethoven's *Hammerklavier*, Op. 106, scherzo ending: A dramatic semitonal shift.
- Miles Davis, "So What": Modulates up by semitone for the bridge.
- Standard pop "pump-up": A song modulating from E-flat to E major for its final chorus.

# Common Confusions
- Sidestepping is not the same as fifth-based modulation: semitonally related keys share very few pitch classes despite being close in scale-degree-preserving distance.
- The dramatic effect comes precisely from the uniform shift of all scale degrees, creating a sense of elevated energy without changing the musical material.
