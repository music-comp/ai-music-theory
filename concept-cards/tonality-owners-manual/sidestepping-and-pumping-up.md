# Quick Definition

Modulations by small chromatic intervals (typically semitone) that preserve scale-degree roles, familiar in jazz and popular music as a dramatic "key change" for a final chorus or solo section.

# Core Definition

Sidestepping (or pumping-up) modulations exploit scale-degree-preserving key distance, where the nearest keys are semitonally related (e.g., C major to D-flat major). The same musical material is performed in both keys, with each scale degree shifting by the same small chromatic interval. This produces a direct, dramatic effect because the entire musical texture shifts uniformly. The technique is most relevant "when instruments do the same thing before and after the modulation" -- for instance, a singer performing the same melody in both keys (p. 361). These modulations are slides along the scalar spiral diagram, corresponding to the scale-degree-preserving model of key distance where parallel major and minor (and semitonally related keys) are the closest neighbors (p. 362, Fig. 8.1.1).

# Key Properties

1. Entire texture shifts uniformly by a small chromatic interval (usually semitone)
2. Scale-degree roles are preserved -- every scale degree moves by the same interval
3. Nearby keys in scale-degree-preserving distance share very few pitch classes
4. Most relevant when the same material is performed in both keys
5. Represented as slides along the scalar spiral diagram
6. Common in jazz, popular music, and musical theater
7. Sometimes found in classical music as well

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Sidestepping/Pumping-Up:
1. Check whether the entire musical texture shifts uniformly by a small interval (usually semitone or whole tone)
2. Verify that the same melodic/harmonic material continues in the new key
3. Confirm that scale-degree roles are preserved (tonic maps to tonic, dominant to dominant, etc.)
4. Note the dramatic, energy-heightening effect

# Context & Application

Sidestepping is common in jazz, popular music, and musical theater, often used to heighten energy for a final chorus. It sometimes appears in classical music: Lewin (1984) and McCreless (1996) discuss examples including the end of the scherzo of Beethoven's Hammerklavier sonata (Op. 106). Miles Davis's "So What" modulates up by semitone for its bridge section. The technique highlights the distinction between the two models of key distance: semitonally related keys are maximally close in scale-degree-preserving terms but quite distant in pitch-class-changing terms (sharing very few common tones).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 361): Beethoven's Hammerklavier, Op. 106, scherzo ending -- a dramatic semitonal shift discussed by Lewin (1984) and McCreless (1996).

**Example 2** (p. 361): Miles Davis, "So What" -- modulates up by semitone for the bridge section.

**Example 3**: Standard pop "pump-up" -- a song modulating from E-flat to E major for its final chorus, creating a sense of elevated energy without changing the musical material.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Thinking semitonally related keys are close in all respects
  **Correction**: They are close in scale-degree-preserving distance but quite distant in pitch-class-changing distance -- C major and D-flat major share very few pitch classes

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Assuming the dramatic effect comes from the distance of the modulation
  **Clarification**: The dramatic effect comes precisely from the uniform shift of all scale degrees, creating a sense of elevated energy without changing the musical material -- the modulation is actually very short in scale-degree-preserving terms
