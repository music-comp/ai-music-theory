# Quick Definition

Three-voice sequences dividing into a canonic pair and an independent voice moving in contrary motion, exploiting the tritone's symmetry or the near-symmetry of diatonic intervals. The 2 + 1 arrangement produces some of the most fundamental sequences in functional tonality.

# Core Definition

When three-voice sequences have only two crossed arrows, they often divide 2 + 1: two canonic upper voices against an independent bass (p. 175). The tritone's symmetry means semitone and fifth transposition reproduce the same pitch classes, keeping bare tritones vertically aligned. With the bass transposed, this produces sequences of incomplete dominant sevenths (upper voices descending by semitone, bass falling by fifth) -- "an important sequence in virtually all functionally tonal music from the baroque onward" (p. 176). Replacing tritones with chromatic/diatonic fifths or thirds yields the common patterns of upper voices alternating steps and unisons (fifths) or thirds and steps (thirds) against a leaping bass (Figures 4.5.2-3, pp. 176-177).

# Key Properties

1. Two canonic voices plus one independent (contrary-motion) voice
2. Tritone symmetry: semitone and fifth transposition produce same pitch classes
3. Produces descending-fifth incomplete-seventh-chord sequences
4. Nearly even intervals (fifths, thirds) approximate tritone behavior
5. Three parameters vary: interval type, bass transposition, overall transposition
6. Rounds dividing 2 + 1 enliven static harmony (standing-on-the-dominant)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify:
1. Look for two voices moving in canon
2. Check for a third voice moving independently (often contrary motion)
3. Determine the interval in the canonic pair (tritone, fifth, third)

# Context & Application

Three-voice rounds dividing 2 + 1 date to the early sixteenth century (Figure 4.5.6). They produce Prinner, Fenaroli, and standing-on-the-dominant schemas. Beethoven's Op. 10 no. 3, I, mm. 333ff features a round with upper voices in canon contrary to the bass (Figure 4.5.7). Bach WTC second F major fugue and Beethoven Op. 10 no. 3, IV both use the pattern (Figures 4.5.4-5).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming the canon in the upper pair is always audible
  **Correction**: It can be "almost completely inaudible" as in Haydn's E minor sonata (p. 181)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking sonically diverse passages must have different underlying structures
  **Clarification**: The differences are parameters (interval type, bass transposition, overall transposition) that disguise shared organization
