# Quick Definition
Three-voice sequences where voices divide into a canonic pair and an independent voice moving in contrary motion, exploiting the tritone's symmetry or near-symmetry of diatonic intervals.

# Formal Definition
When three-voice sequences have only two crossed arrows, the structure often divides 2 + 1: two canonic upper voices against an independent bass. The tritone's symmetry means semitone and fifth transposition reproduce the same pitch classes, keeping bare tritones vertically aligned. With the bass transposed, this produces sequences of incomplete dominant sevenths (upper voices descending by semitone, bass falling by fifth). Replacing tritones with fifths (nearly even intervals) yields the common pattern of upper voices alternating steps and unisons while the bass leaps, or upper voices alternating thirds and steps for diatonic thirds.

# Musical Context
The 2 + 1 arrangement is fundamental to functional tonality. Upper voices descending by semitone against a descending-fifth bass produces one of the most important sequences in all tonal music from the baroque onward. Triadic composers also use rounds dividing 2 + 1 to enliven static harmony, producing standing-on-the-dominant, Prinner, and Fenaroli schemas. The Beethoven Op. 10 no. 3 passage embeds a two-voice canon in contrary motion with the bass.

# Common Confusions
- The 2 + 1 division is about the arrow structure, not necessarily the audible texture; the canon in the upper pair can be "almost completely inaudible" (as in Haydn's E minor sonata)
- These sequences differ sonically but share a fundamental organization; the differences are in the interval type, bass transposition, and overall transpositional motion
