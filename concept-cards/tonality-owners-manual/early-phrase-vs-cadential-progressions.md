# Quick Definition

The distinction between harmonic cycles that appear early in a phrase (avoiding melodic 2-hat-1-hat and bass 5-hat-1-hat) and those that close it (featuring root-position V-I and stepwise melodic descent to tonic) -- treated by Tymoczko as "alternative paths through a single harmonic map" differing in strategy rather than syntax.

# Core Definition

Different kinds of harmonic cycles tend to appear in different parts of the phrase: "early-phrase cycles will typically avoid melodic 2-hat-1-hat in favor of other scale degrees, just as the bass 5-hat-1-hat will likely be reserved for the final cadence" (p. 413). Some theorists describe early-phrase progressions as "prolongational," but to the traditional harmonic theorist "they are essentially similar, alternative paths through a single harmonic map. The difference between them is the result of large-scale strategy rather than syntax" (p. 413). Cadential weight, which can be quantified by features such as the presence of a predominant, root-position tonic-dominant, and melodic 2-hat-1-hat, generally increases throughout a phrase.

# Prerequisites

- **Harmonic cycles** -- Both early-phrase and cadential progressions are instances of harmonic cycles
- **Cadential weight** -- The measure of how conclusive a progression is

# Key Properties

1. Early-phrase cycles avoid root-position V-I and melodic 2-hat-1-hat
2. Cadential cycles feature root-position dominant and stepwise melodic arrival on tonic
3. Both types traverse the same harmonic map but to different degrees
4. Cadential weight generally increases throughout a phrase
5. Inversional equivalence becomes important because it allows diverse bass lines to express the same chord progressions
6. Specific inversions are highly likely at specific phrasal positions in Bach's chorales, but when grouped by function, distribution is much more even

# Construction / Recognition

## To Distinguish Early-Phrase from Cadential:
1. Check for root-position V-I progression (cadential marker)
2. Check for melodic 2-hat-1-hat (cadential marker)
3. Check for predominant chord (increases cadential weight)
4. Note position within the phrase (beginning, middle, end)
5. Assess overall cadential weight using features like: presence of predominant, root-position dominant, melodic descent to tonic

# Context & Application

This distinction is "perhaps the single most pressing problem of contemporary music theory, as it directly affects elementary pedagogy" (p. 417). The choice between sequential hearing (cycles placed one after another like beads on a string) and recursive hearing (early-phrase cycles nested inside a structural progression) is fundamental. Tymoczko sides with the sequential model but acknowledges both may be available to listeners. A simple algorithm multiplying first-order harmonic probabilities by zeroth-order phrasal probabilities produces "reasonably idiomatic output, comparable to the efforts of a good second-semester undergraduate" (p. 413).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 413): Bach's chorales -- cycles increase in strength throughout the phrase, measured by a simple model assigning "strength points" to features of the cycle.

**Example 2** (pp. 415-416): Haydn's G minor sonata, Hob. XVI:44, II -- Laitz interprets four successive harmonic cycles as three embedded within a fourth; Tymoczko sees four sequential cycles.

**Example 3** (p. 414): When inversions are ignored and chords grouped by functional category, early-phrase and cadential positions show a "much more even distribution."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating every V chord as cadential
  **Correction**: Root-position V-I is a cadential marker, but inverted dominants in early-phrase positions serve different strategic functions

- **Error**: Ignoring phrasal position when analyzing harmonic progressions
  **Correction**: The same progression can serve different strategic roles at different positions

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking early-phrase progressions are fundamentally different from cadential ones
  **Clarification**: They are "essentially similar, alternative paths through a single harmonic map" differing in degree rather than kind

- **Confusion**: Assuming the phrase model (recursive embedding) is established fact
  **Clarification**: There is "little or no solid evidence supporting it" despite Schenkerian dominance (p. 418); "no historical evidence that earlier composers in fact thought in this way" (p. 418)
