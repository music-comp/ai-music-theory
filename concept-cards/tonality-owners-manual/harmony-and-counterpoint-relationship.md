# Quick Definition
The context-dependent relationship between linear (contrapuntal) and vertical (harmonic) forces, which varies across different chord-and-scale environments: descending voice leading is retrofunctional with triads but functional with seventh chords, and different geometrical environments provide different harmonic affordances.

# Formal Definition
With diatonic thirds, descending melodic motion produces descending-fifth progressions and a distinct sense of functionality. With diatonic triads, descending melodies produce retrofunctional progressions (ascending thirds and fifths). With diatonic seventh chords, functionality is restored -- descending voice leading again produces descending fifths. This context-dependence refutes both the Schenkerian "compatibilist" view (counterpoint inevitably generates functional harmony) and the Cohnian "incompatibilist" view (chromaticism and function are opposed forces).

# Musical Context
Randy Newman's "When She Loved Me" embeds nonfunctional triadic patterns (like Gesualdo's "Moro Lasso") within a functional seventh-chord progression. The Beatles' I-II-IV-I in "Eight Days a Week" draws nonfunctional triads out of functional seventh-chord origins (V7/V-ii^ø7-V7-I from Bobby Rydell's "Forget Him"). These demonstrate the bidirectional relationship: nonfunctional idioms embedded in functional contexts, and functional idioms stripped down to nonfunctional surfaces. The spiral diagrams visualize this spectrum from smooth cooperation to total effacement of functionality.

# Common Confusions
- There is no fixed relationship between counterpoint and harmony; the relationship depends on chord size, scale, and context
- The functional/nonfunctional distinction is a continuum, not a binary opposition
