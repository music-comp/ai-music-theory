# Quick Definition

A nonharmonic tone that sounds a note of the upcoming chord before that chord arrives, typically on a metrically weak beat. The rarest of the four SNAP categories in Palestrina's music.

# Core Definition

An anticipation is a nonharmonic tone that arrives at a chord tone before the rest of the harmony changes to that chord. In Palestrina's music, anticipations almost always occur in the context of suspensions, to the point where it might make more sense to speak of a "suspension/anticipation system" rather than of anticipations as a freestanding class (p. 214). Freestanding anticipations (not decorating suspensions and not themselves decorated) comprise less than 1% of Palestrina's dissonances, making them less frequent than ascending neighbors. Freestanding anticipations are much more common in Josquin.

# Prerequisites

- **Nonharmonic tone** — Understanding what constitutes a note outside the prevailing harmony

# Key Properties

1. Arrives at a chord tone before the harmony changes
2. Typically metrically weak
3. Rarest SNAP category in Palestrina (less than 1% of dissonances as freestanding events)
4. Almost always occurs in the context of suspensions in Palestrina
5. More common as freestanding events in Josquin
6. Can be recursively decorated by neighbor tones (decorated anticipation)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify an Anticipation:
1. Locate a note that belongs to the upcoming chord but sounds during the current one
2. Verify it typically occurs on a weak beat
3. Check whether it is connected to a suspension (common in Palestrina)
4. Distinguish from chord tones that arrive early due to voice-leading

# Context & Application

In the baroque period, "arpeggiated anticipations" appear in Bach's chorales, where an arpeggiating voice (usually moving down by third, from tonic to subdominant) reaches the next harmony an eighth note early (p. 238). Of more than ten thousand nonharmonic tones in Bach's chorales, most anomalies involve these arpeggiated anticipations -- reminiscent of the start of Monteverdi's "Ohime" (p. 238).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 214, Fig. 5.1.4): Palestrina's mass In duplicibus minoribus (II), Credo, mm. 163-165 -- a lower neighbor A decorating a quarter-note anticipation B.

**Example 2** (p. 214, Fig. 5.1.4): Palestrina's mass De Beata Marie Virginis (I), Sanctus, mm. 19-20 -- the same figure must be read as a passing tone to avoid parallel fifths.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating anticipations as a freestanding nonharmonic category equally common as the others
  **Correction**: In Palestrina, freestanding anticipations are less than 1% of dissonances -- they almost always decorate suspensions

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Assuming the same melodic formula always functions as an anticipation
  **Clarification**: The same figure can be an anticipation in one context and a passing tone in another -- e.g., when reading it as an anticipation would produce parallel fifths (p. 214)
