# Quick Definition

Repeating harmonic patterns transposed diatonically at regular intervals, forming one of three independent subsystems of functional harmony, with almost all common sequences featuring descending-fifth progressions repeating at some regular interval.

# Core Definition

Functional sequences are classified by their interval of transposition from one grand period to the next (Figure 7.6.1). For a given diatonic interval, there are four possible two-chord sequences. Sequences typically move by small intervals with descent preferred to ascent -- the familiar preference for stepwise descending melody, "now governing larger spans of musical time" (p. 336). Almost all common functional sequences feature descending-fifth progressions repeating at regular intervals. The main exceptions are pure descending thirds (whose connection to harmonic cycles is clear) and the Pachelbel sequence (D4A2), where the engine of repetition is deceptive rather than authentic (p. 340).

Sequences and harmonic cycles form a continuum: "nonsequential harmony is *itself* quasi-sequential, an oblique and disguised reworking of the circle of descending thirds" (pp. 340--341).

# Key Properties

1. Sequences typically move by small intervals, with descent preferred over ascent
2. From Corelli to Mozart, descending-third transposition rises to almost 25% of sequences
3. Descending-step sequences: fauxbourdon is most popular in Corelli, supplanted by descending fifths in later styles
4. Ascending-step sequences: fauxbourdon and ascending 5--6 are by far the most common
5. The ascending 5--6 (D3A4) is common while its retrograde (descending 6--5, A3D4) is virtually unknown, demonstrating sequences are not merely linear
6. Fourth and ascending-third motion are rare for all composers

# Construction / Recognition

## To Classify a Sequence:
1. Identify the interval of transposition between successive units
2. Determine the root progressions within each unit (e.g., D5, A2, D3, A4)
3. Match to one of the four possibilities for that interval class
4. Note whether the sequence uses descending-fifth progressions (most common) or another pattern

# Context & Application

In Corelli's Op. 1, fauxbourdon is the most popular descending-step sequence type. By Mozart, descending fifths dominate. Ascending-fifth sequences are particularly characteristic of Bach and rare in other styles. The continuum between sequence and harmonic cycle is illustrated by Schumann's "Ich grolle nicht," which seamlessly transitions between the two (Figure 7.6.11).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating all sequences as contrapuntally motivated (purely linear)
  **Correction**: The asymmetry between ascending 5--6 (common) and descending 6--5 (rare) shows sequences have harmonic content, not just linear logic

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing sequences and harmonic cycles are fundamentally different systems
  **Clarification**: They form a continuum; "the magic of functional music lies in the way it balances identity and difference, disguising its omnipresent repetition just enough to keep our ears on their toes" (p. 342)
