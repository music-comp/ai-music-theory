# Quick Definition
The question of whether rock's modal harmony continues an unbroken folk tradition from the Renaissance or represents an independent rediscovery of triadic possibilities driven by underlying geometry.

# Formal Definition
Tymoczko investigates whether rock modality is linked by continuous folk tradition to Renaissance modality, concluding that the evidence favors reinvention over continuity. Pre-1950 American and English folk recordings tend to be harmonically spare (unaccompanied, droning, or oscillating between two chords). It is "surprisingly difficult to find recordings that are (a) clearly modal, (b) made before 1950, and (c) feature three or more distinct triads." Folksongs show extraordinary flexibility, appearing in different modes, time signatures, and harmonizations across different recordings.

# Musical Context
The history of "The House of the Rising Sun" illustrates the transformation: Ashley and Foster's 1933 recording is a major-mode blues with functional harmony; Dylan's 1962 version borrows chords from Dave van Ronk with a functional descending-bass lament; the Animals' 1964 recording places the harmonies in root position, creating the aggressive retrofunctional progression we now know. "Scarborough Fair" undergoes similar transformations from major-mode I-V-I (Heath/Payant, 1955) to the canonical dorian chords (Carthy, 1965). The modality of the 1960s is "the delayed echo of modernism."

# Common Confusions
- The resemblance between Renaissance and rock modality is real but due to shared geometry, not folk transmission
- Early 20th-century folk music is often *more* functional than its descendants, not less
- The modality of 1960s rock is a creative reinvention, not a passive inheritance
