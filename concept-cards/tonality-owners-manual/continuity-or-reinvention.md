# Quick Definition

The question of whether rock's modal harmony continues an unbroken folk tradition or represents an independent rediscovery -- Tymoczko finds the evidence favors reinvention, calling 1960s modality "the delayed echo of modernism" (p. 86).

# Core Definition

Tymoczko investigates whether rock modality is linked by continuous folk tradition to Renaissance modality. Pre-1950 folk recordings "tend to be harmonically spare, featuring either unaccompanied melodies, drones, or perhaps oscillating between two chords" (p. 83). "It is surprisingly difficult to find recordings that are (a) clearly modal, (b) made before 1950, and (c) feature three or more distinct triads" (p. 83). Folk songs show "extraordinary flexibility" in mode, time signature, and harmonization across recordings. "The modality of the 1960s is best understood as the delayed echo of modernism, popular musicians rejecting functionality a half-century after classical composers did" (p. 86).

# Key Properties

1. Pre-1950 folk recordings are mostly monophonic or harmonically simple
2. Folk songs change modes and harmonizations across different recordings
3. Triadic modality with 3+ chords is largely a 1960s phenomenon
4. Early 20th-century folk is often *more* functional than its descendants
5. Rock modality is a creative reinvention, not passive inheritance
6. The resemblance to Renaissance modality reflects shared geometry

# Construction / Recognition

## Evidence for Reinvention:
1. Track the recorded history of a folk song across decades
2. Note changes in harmonization, mode, and number of chords
3. Observe that explicit modal-triadic harmonization is a mid-20th-century development
4. Compare early recordings (harmonically spare) with later versions (modal-triadic)

# Context & Application

The case studies of "House of the Rising Sun" and "Scarborough Fair" illustrate how songs transformed from functional or monophonic folk into modal-triadic rock through a series of creative reharmonizations by specific musicians (Van Ronk, Dylan, the Animals; MacColl, Carthy, Simon and Garfunkel).

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 83-85): "House of the Rising Sun" -- Ashley/Foster 1933 (functional blues) -> Dylan 1962 (functional lament) -> Animals 1964 (retrofunctional root-position triads).

**Example 2** (pp. 85-86): "Scarborough Fair" -- Heath/Payant 1955 (major, I-V-I) -> MacColl/Seeger 1957 (dorian) -> Carthy 1965 (canonical dorian chords).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming folk songs are fixed vessels for transmitting modal harmony
  **Correction**: Folk songs show extraordinary flexibility; the same tune appears in different modes and harmonizations

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking resemblance implies historical connection
  **Clarification**: The resemblance reflects shared triadic geometry, not folk transmission
