# Quick Definition

A balanced pair of modulatory sequences where the second largely undoes the work of the first, with a rhetorically marked pivot at the "point of farthest remove."

# Core Definition

The paired-sequence design is "a balanced pair of sequences with the second largely undoing the work of the first" (p. 377). The pivot between the two sequences is often rhetorically marked as a "point of farthest remove," where the music delays -- "standing on the dominant, introducing a new theme, or reaching a strong cadence" (p. 377). Compared to the helicopter drop, "the departure and return are more equal and balanced: where the former takes a large leap on circular scale space, followed by a series of short steps back, the latter involves a pair of broadly comparable motions" (p. 378).

# Prerequisites

- **Modulatory schemas** -- The broader category of modulatory patterns

# Key Properties

1. Two sequences: departure and return are broadly comparable in scale
2. The pivot (point of farthest remove) is rhetorically marked
3. Rhetorical marking may include: standing on dominant, new theme, strong cadence, augmented sixth
4. More balanced than the helicopter drop
5. Common in sonata-form development sections

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify a Paired-Sequence Design:
1. Locate two sequential passages in the development section
2. Verify that the second sequence reverses the modulatory direction of the first
3. Identify the pivot point between them (point of farthest remove)
4. Check for rhetorical marking at the pivot (cadence, standing on dominant, new theme)
5. Compare the scale of the two sequences -- they should be broadly comparable

# Context & Application

The paired-sequence design is one of the most important development-section schemas, balancing departure and return in a satisfying arch form. It is more sophisticated than the helicopter drop, which relies on asymmetry for its dramatic effect.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 377, Figure 8.4.4): Haydn's Symphony no. 62, I, development -- ascending arpeggio (descending-third near sequence: A^7^--F-sharp^7^--b--G--E^7^--C-sharp), then descending arpeggio (descending fifths: C-sharp^7^--F-sharp^7^--B^7^--E^7^--A^7^) with augmented sixth and standing on dominant at the pivot.

**Example 2** (p. 378, Figure 8.4.5): Beethoven's Op. 2, no. 1, I, development -- ascending-step sequence from A-flat to C minor, then immediate retracing by descending fifths back to A-flat, with thematic material liquidated into generic tonal material.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Expecting the two sequences to use the same interval of transposition
  **Correction**: The two sequences need only be broadly comparable in scale; they may use different intervals

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the paired-sequence design is the same as the up-and-down-the-ladder
  **Clarification**: The up-and-down-the-ladder is a specific *type* of paired-sequence design that uses ascending and descending fifths; the paired-sequence design is the more general category
