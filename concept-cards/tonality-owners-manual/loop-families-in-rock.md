# Quick Definition
Groups of related chord progressions generated by selecting chords from each angular position on the spiral diagram, forming repeating clockwise loops through chord space.

# Formal Definition
A loop family consists of progressions that (a) begin and end at the tonic; (b) move clockwise through a single turn in chord space, selecting one of the three chords at every angular position; and (c) contain no harmonically distant chords (roots related by semitone or tritone). A maximal four-chord loop selects one chord from each angular position; three-chord subloops are generated by eliminating a nontonic chord. There are exactly four such progressions satisfying these constraints, and remarkably, all four are identical under rotation of the same underlying sequence D-A-E-G.

# Musical Context
Loop families reveal hidden relationships between seemingly unrelated rock songs. They are united by "family resemblance" -- features more obvious when considering the group as a whole. Rock music is "nearly sequential," repeatedly taking short clockwise motions through abstract harmonic space, with variety at each position rather than exact repetition. The four angular positions on the chromatic chord circle function as "slots" that can be filled or left open -- a contrapuntal analogue to tonal functions.

# Examples
- **Family 1** ("Rose Parade"): I-V-II-IV (maximal). Three-chord subsets: I-V-IV ("Helpless"), I-II-IV ("Eight Days a Week"), I-V-II ("Knockin' on Heaven's Door"), Figure 2.3.4
- **Family 2** ("Air Near My Fingers"): I-bIII-bVII-IV (maximal). Subsets: I-bVII-IV ("Tangled Up in Blue"), I-bIII-bVII ("Stairway to Heaven"), I-bIII-IV ("I Can See for Miles"), Figure 2.4.3
- **Family 3** ("Natural Woman"): I-V-bVII-IV (maximal). Subsets include I-bVII-IV and I-V-IV (shared with Family 1), and I-V-bVII ("I'm a Loser"), Figure 2.4.4
- **Family 4** ("Hey Joe"): C-G-D-A-E, a five-chord ascending-fifth pattern. Subsets include i-VI-VII-i and I-V-vi-IV (the "axis progression"), Figure 2.7.2

# Common Confusions
- The four families are all rotations of the same underlying progression (D-A-E-G with different tonics) -- they are in some deep sense "the same"
- I-V-II-VI (the fourth rotation) is almost unknown in rock because VI-I makes an unsatisfying return to tonic
- Superimposing three families in different keys produces "polytonality without dissonance" (Figure 2.5.7)
