# Quick Definition

Groups of related chord progressions generated by selecting chords from each angular position on the spiral diagram, forming repeating clockwise loops through chord space -- united by "family resemblance" rather than surface similarity.

# Core Definition

A loop family consists of progressions satisfying three constraints: "(a) begin and end at the tonic; (b) move clockwise through a single turn in chord space, selecting one of the three chords at every angular position; and (c) contain no harmonically 'distant' chords whose roots are related by semitone or tritone" (p. 66). There are exactly four such maximal progressions, and "remarkably, these four progressions are all identical under rotation" of the sequence D-A-E-G (p. 67). Three-chord subsets are generated by eliminating one nontonic chord. The four positions function as "slots" analogous to tonal functions (p. 60).

# Key Properties

1. Four maximal loop families, all rotations of the same sequence D-A-E-G
2. Each selects one chord from each angular position on the spiral
3. Three-chord subsets generated by eliminating a nontonic chord
4. Families reveal hidden relationships between seemingly unrelated songs
5. Rock music is "nearly sequential," taking short clockwise motions through chord space
6. I-V-II-VI (fourth rotation) is almost unknown because VI-I makes an unsatisfying return

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify a Loop Family:
1. Map the progression's chords onto the spiral diagram
2. Check if the path traces a clockwise loop through roughly one turn
3. Verify that all chords are diatonically close (no semitone or tritone root relations)
4. Identify which angular positions are filled and which are skipped

# Context & Application

The loop families reveal that rock progressions like "Helpless," "Eight Days a Week," and "Rose Parade" are deeply related despite sounding different. Three of the four families can be superimposed in different keys without dissonance, creating "polytonality without dissonance" where centricity depends on where one feels the hypermetric accent (pp. 67-68).

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 57-60): Family 1 ("Rose Parade"): I-V-II-IV. Subsets: I-V-IV ("Helpless"), I-II-IV ("Eight Days a Week"), I-V-II ("Knockin' on Heaven's Door").

**Example 2** (pp. 61-63): Family 2 ("Air Near My Fingers"): I-bIII-bVII-IV. Subsets: I-bVII-IV ("Tangled Up in Blue"), I-bIII-bVII ("Stairway to Heaven").

**Example 3** (pp. 63-64): Family 3 ("Natural Woman"): I-V-bVII-IV, the retrograde of the classical ascending 5-6 sequence.

**Example 4** (pp. 71-74): Family 4 ("Hey Joe"): five-chord ascending-fifth pattern C-G-D-A-E.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Analyzing rock loop progressions using classical functional categories
  **Correction**: These progressions embody a nonclassical norm; the II chord is "a thing-in-itself, largely unknown in classical harmony and not reducible to its categories" (p. 59)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the four families are fundamentally different
  **Clarification**: They are all rotations of D-A-E-G with different tonics -- "in some deep sense the same" (p. 67)
