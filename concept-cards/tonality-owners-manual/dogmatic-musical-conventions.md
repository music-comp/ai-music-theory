# Quick Definition

The fourth characterization of musical knowledge: conventions shaped by avoidances now considered unmotivated or superstitious, born of a Platonic conception of music as objective and extra-human (p. 2).

# Core Definition

Dogmatic musical conventions encompass practices that were once treated as laws of nature but are now recognized as culturally specific norms. These include "near-universal avoidance of parallel perfect intervals, drawing a sharp boundary between consonance and dissonance, using only some of the available triadic progressions, limiting scalar vocabulary to a small number of modes, satisfying the formal demands of fugue and sonata, returning to the opening key at the end of the piece" (p. 2). These were "born of a Platonic conception of music as objective and extra-human -- once basic science, part of the quadrivium alongside mathematics and astronomy" (p. 2).

# Key Properties

1. Born of a Platonic conception of music as objective and extra-human
2. Music was once part of the quadrivium alongside mathematics and astronomy
3. "Rules" have been reconceived as conventions rather than laws of nature
4. Grammatical norms are multilayered -- the same passage can violate a true generalization while conforming to more nuanced rules
5. A "rule not followed in practice" should make us suspicious (p. 18)
6. The goal is to separate genuine regularities from unmotivated strictures

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Dogmatic Conventions:
1. Examine a theoretical "rule" against actual compositional practice
2. Check whether the rule is consistently followed or has systematic exceptions
3. Assess whether the rule is motivated by audible musical effect or by extramusical philosophy
4. Consider whether the rule is a true generalization or an overly strict formalization
5. Use corpus analysis to test theoretical claims against actual behavior

# Context & Application

Dogmatic conventions create a pedagogical paradox: introductory harmony textbooks devoted to conventions that have "largely lost their force," counterpoint manuals "shockingly similar to those of the sixteenth century," and advanced analytical methodologies "explicitly designed to establish the aesthetic superiority of the White Germanic Genius" (p. 2). Tymoczko argues for replacing dogma with empirically grounded understanding while preserving genuine regularities.

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 18-19, Figure 1.3.1): Prout's discussion of suspension-masked parallel fifths -- a "rule not followed in practice" where he endorses a prohibition then immediately concedes the Old Masters violated it.

**Example 2** (pp. 19-20): The analogy to English's prohibition on split infinitives -- "a more-or-less illusory rule with no grounding in actual linguistic behavior."

**Example 3** (pp. 20-21): William Caplin insisting that an authentic cadence "must" involve a root-position dominant that lasts until the tonic arrives -- withholding "cadence" from moments that may sound cadential.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Declaring unusual passages "ungrammatical" because they violate oversimplistic generalizations
  **Correction**: "Exceptions to the rules" may simply be features of a practice, explained by no more general principle

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking dogmatic conventions are entirely wrong
  **Clarification**: Corpus analysis often provides "limited and defeasible evidence in favor of music-theoretical common sense" -- the conventions are generally correct while also breaking down in important ways

- **Confusion**: Rejecting all rules because of occasional exceptions
  **Clarification**: Grammatical norms are multilayered; the same passage can violate one generalization while conforming to more nuanced rules
