# Quick Definition
The fourth characterization of musical knowledge: conventions shaped by avoidances now considered unmotivated or superstitious, born of a Platonic conception of music as objective and extra-human.

# Formal Definition
Dogmatic musical knowledge encompasses conventions that were once treated as laws of nature but are now recognized as culturally specific norms. These include the near-universal avoidance of parallel perfect intervals, sharp boundaries between consonance and dissonance, restriction to certain triadic progressions, limited scalar vocabulary, and formal demands like returning to the opening key. These conventions were "born of a Platonic conception of music as objective and extra-human."

# Musical Context
Dogmatic conventions create a pedagogical paradox: introductory harmony textbooks are devoted to functional-harmonic conventions that have "largely lost their force," counterpoint manuals are "shockingly similar to those of the sixteenth century," and advanced analytical methodologies were "explicitly designed to establish the aesthetic superiority of the White Germanic Genius." The challenge is to separate genuine musical insight from unmotivated dogma.

# Examples
- The prohibition on parallel perfect intervals, which composers avoided even in cases where they are very difficult to hear (e.g., tucked in inner voices)
- Prout's discussion of suspension-masked parallel fifths -- a "rule not followed in practice" that should make us suspicious (Figure 1.3.1)
- The insistence that nonharmonic tones are "merely decorative"
- Strict definitions that transform generalizations into tautologies (the "no true Scotsman fallacy" in music theory)

# Common Confusions
- Dogmatic conventions are not entirely wrong -- corpus analysis often provides "limited and defeasible evidence in favor of music-theoretical common sense"
- The goal is not to reject all rules but to distinguish genuine regularities from unmotivated strictures
- Grammatical norms are multilayered: the same passage can simultaneously violate a true generalization while conforming to more nuanced rules
