# Quick Definition
Replacing a chord with one whose root is a major third away, exploiting the near-symmetry of the triad so that the substitution has only a small effect on the individual voices.

# Formal Definition
Major-third substitution replaces one major triad with another a major third away, using the voice leadings t_1 **T**_{-4} (PL) or t_{-1} **T**_4 (LP). Because the major triad nearly divides the octave into three equal parts (being only one semitone from the augmented triad), this substitution moves each voice by at most one semitone. On the spiral diagram, major-third-related triads occupy the same angular position at different radial distances, so substitution corresponds to radial motion.

# Musical Context
Major-third substitution explains a wide variety of chromatic triadic progressions throughout Western music. Any descending sequence of major triads can be rewritten by substituting major-third-related chords, producing different root progressions that share the same stepwise descending voice leading. This generates the families of progressions found in both classical chromaticism and rock harmony. It is the triadic analogue of the tritone substitution familiar from jazz.

# Examples
- C-B-Bb can become C-Eb-Gb or C-G-D through major-third substitution (Figure 2.1.7)
- Gesualdo's chromaticism involves major-third-related progressions (C# major to A minor involves LP voice leading)
- The three radial positions on the major-triad spiral at any angular position are related by major-third substitution
- Rock progressions using bIII (e.g., I-bIII-bVII-IV) exploit major-third substitution of the V chord

# Common Confusions
- Major-third substitution is not the same as tritone substitution -- the former replaces by M3 (4 semitones), the latter by tritone (6 semitones)
- The substitution changes the root but preserves most of the voice leading, which is why it sounds natural
- It works for any nearly even chord, not just major triads -- the closer to perfect symmetry, the smaller the voice-leading disruption
