# Quick Definition

A sequence where some or all transposition arrows cross, so melodic material moves from voice to voice, creating canonic (imitative) relationships. On the dyadic spiral, canonic sequences arise from an odd number of steps (without voice exchanges). Canon and contrary motion are "byproducts of the basic mechanics of sequential voice leading" (p. 169).

# Core Definition

Crossed arrows partition voices into groups sharing melodic content (p. 156). A three-voice sequence can have no crossings (transpositional), two crossed (two-voice canon), or all three crossing (three-voice canon). Without voice exchanges, odd steps on the dyadic spiral produce canonic sequences; with an odd number of voice exchanges, even steps produce canons (Figure 4.3.1, p. 165). The most common canonic pattern is single-step motion on the spiral (the basic voice leading), appearing in "a substantial proportion of baroque and classical sequences" (p. 166). Little-t transposition (along the chord) is "inherently canonic, cycling voices through different chordal elements" (p. 161). The presence or absence of audible canonic patterning "is a matter of whether composers decide to draw out the canonic potential latent in their material, with noncanonic sequences often obscuring preexisting canonic relationships" (p. 169).

# Key Properties

1. Defined by crossed arrows in the Taneyev notation
2. On dyadic spiral: odd steps (no exchanges) or even steps (with exchanges)
3. Most common = single-step motion (basic voice leading)
4. Canon arises as a byproduct of sequential voice leading, not deliberate artifice
5. Noncanonic sequences often obscure latent canonic relationships
6. Historically: canonic aspect paramount first, harmonic patterning valued later

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct:
1. Start with any voice leading between transpositionally related chords
2. Draw crossed arrows showing which voice's material goes where
3. Apply arrows repeatedly to generate the sequence

## To Recognize:
1. Check if melodic material moves between voices at each repetition
2. On the dyadic circle, count steps: odd (without exchanges) = canonic
3. Look for imitative relationships that may be hidden by figuration

# Context & Application

The Pathetique episode opens with a canonic sequence. "Three Blinde Mice" (1609) uses permutation without transposition. The connection is "theoretically surprising, as one might not expect that repetition had anything to do with canon" (p. 162). Palestrina's imitative passages gradually came to be valued for harmonic patterning as well (Bach, Mozart). Maria Szymanowska's Polonaise disguises the inherent canon (Figure 4.3.9, p. 170).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Thinking canonic sequences require deliberate compositional artifice
  **Correction**: Canon arises as a byproduct of sequential voice-leading mechanics; it is an "outcropping that the rock-climber may choose to grasp or not" (p. 169)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Treating canonic and regular sequences as separate categories
  **Clarification**: Canonic sequences are a natural consequence of the arrow model when arrows cross; they are not a special type imposed on the sequence
