# Quick Definition
A sequence where some or all transposition arrows cross, so that melodic material moves from one voice to another, creating canonic (imitative) relationships between voices.

# Formal Definition
Crossed arrows partition voices into groups sharing the same melodic content. A three-voice sequence can have: no crossings (transpositional), two crossed arrows (two-voice canon with one independent voice), or all three arrows crossing (three-voice canon). Canonic sequences arise from an odd number of steps on the dyadic spiral (without voice exchanges). The simplest and most common canonic sequence is generated by single-step motion on the spiral, producing the diatonic third's basic voice leading. Canon and contrary motion arise as byproducts of basic sequential voice-leading mechanics, not requiring deliberate compositional artifice.

# Musical Context
The *Pathetique* Ab major episode opens with a canonic sequence. "Three Blinde Mice" (1609) uses permutation without transposition. The connection between canon and sequence is "in the first instance theoretically surprising, as one might not expect that repetition had anything to do with canon." Historically, the canonic aspect was initially paramount (Palestrina's imitative passages), but these moments gradually came to be valued for their harmonic patterning as well (Bach, Mozart). The presence or absence of audible canonic patterning depends on whether composers draw out or obscure the latent canonic potential.

# Common Confusions
- Canonic sequences are not separate from "regular" sequences; they are a natural consequence of the arrow model when arrows cross
- Many noncanonic sequences obscure preexisting canonic relationships; the canon is latent even when not drawn out on the surface
- Little-t transposition (along the chord) is inherently canonic, cycling voices through different chordal elements
