# Quick Definition

The fundamental gap between structure apparent in musical scores and what can actually be heard by even well-trained listeners -- identified as "theory's fundamental challenge" (p. 15).

# Core Definition

The eye-ear divergence undermines "the common music-theoretical inference from patterns-in-scores to listeners' awareness of those patterns" (p. 16). Tymoczko calls this inference the "what-you-see-is-what-you-hear" principle, which is central to projects as different as Lerdahl's a prioristic psychology (assuming "experienced listeners" recreate an accurate copy of the score in their minds) and Gjerdingen's historicist schema theory (proposing score analysis as a tool for reconstructing earlier listeners' psychology). The divergence means there is "no fixed relationship between compositional technique and communicative meaning, or how something is made and what it says" (p. 17).

# Key Properties

1. Structure visible in scores is not necessarily audible to listeners
2. Long-term tonal closure (return to tonic) is not particularly salient for most listeners
3. Composers avoided parallel perfect intervals even where very difficult to hear
4. There is no fixed relationship between compositional technique and communicative meaning
5. Music is written for audiences, not judges
6. Theory can enrich perceptual experience by sensitizing ears to structure

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Eye-Ear Divergence:
1. Find a pattern clearly visible in the score
2. Ask whether a well-trained listener could consciously identify it without the score
3. Consider whether experimental evidence supports audibility
4. Assess whether the pattern serves as a compositional *tool* rather than communicative *content*

# Context & Application

The divergence is relevant to evaluating all analytical claims about musical structure. Tymoczko argues for a nuanced position: he "accept[s] that musical styles are characterized, in part, by recurring regularities and patterns" and that "theory can enrich our perceptual experience by sensitizing our ears to structure" (p. 16). But he rejects the claim that "structure-in-scores is ipso facto available to listeners' conscious reflection" (p. 16). This applies to sound engineers' decisions about reverb and panning, formal strategies like sonata form, and galant schemata.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 15): Long-term tonal closure in sonata form -- experiments by Cook (1987) and Marvin and Brinkman (1999) suggest listeners may not perceive the resolution.

**Example 2** (p. 16): Composers avoiding parallel perfect intervals even when tucked in inner voices where they are nearly inaudible.

**Example 3** (p. 75): Tymoczko's own surprise at discovering retrofunctional structure in rock music he had spent his life playing -- "clear regularities in a genre, explained in part by the structure of musical space, but not explicitly present in the mind of at least one historically situated listener."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using the divergence to dismiss all score-based analysis as meaningless
  **Correction**: Score analysis remains valuable; the issue is the inference from visual patterns to conscious perception

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the divergence means scores are irrelevant to musical understanding
  **Clarification**: Structure in scores can enrich perceptual experience once we are sensitized to it; the issue is the automatic assumption of audibility

- **Confusion**: Equating "not consciously heard" with "musically irrelevant"
  **Clarification**: Compositional techniques can serve as tools for creating compelling energetic trajectories without requiring conscious tracking
