# Quick Definition
The fundamental gap between structure apparent in musical scores and what can actually be heard by even well-trained listeners.

# Formal Definition
The divergence between eye and ear is identified as "theory's fundamental challenge." It undermines the common music-theoretical inference from patterns-in-scores to listeners' awareness of those patterns -- what Tymoczko calls the "what-you-see-is-what-you-hear" principle. This principle is central to both Fred Lerdahl's a prioristic psychology and Gjerdingen's historicist schema theory, yet it is known to fail in the case of long-term tonal closure and likely fails in other cases as well.

# Musical Context
The problem is familiar in twentieth-century contexts (audibility of twelve-tone organization) but applies equally to earlier music. Both introspection and experimental research suggest that long-term tonal closure (return to tonic at the end of a long piece) is not particularly salient for most listeners, yet sonata form seems to presume its importance. Composers avoided parallel perfect intervals even where they are very difficult to hear. There is no fixed relationship between compositional technique and communicative meaning.

# Examples
- Recapitulation in sonata form: Does it truly "resolve" the "large-scale dissonance" of the second theme in a foreign key? Experiments by Cook (1987) and Marvin and Brinkman (1999) suggest listeners may not perceive this
- Rock harmony: Tymoczko himself was "genuinely surprised" by the degree of structure in music he had spent his life playing and listening to (Section 2.7)
- Chopin's arpeggiation patterns and Ligeti's "touches bloquees": simple physical gestures creating outsized impressions of complexity

# Common Confusions
- The eye-ear divergence does not mean scores are irrelevant -- structure in scores can enrich perceptual experience once we are sensitized to it
- It does not imply listeners hear "nothing" -- rather, conscious tracking of compositional technique is not required for musical understanding
- Music is written for *audiences*, not judges (contra Gjerdingen's figure-skating analogy)
