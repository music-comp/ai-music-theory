# Quick Definition
The gradual reduction of the available modes to just major and minor, as ionian-mode harmonic routines were transmitted to other modes through the spread of accidentals.

# Formal Definition
Modal homogenization is one of several largely independent processes involved in the emergence of functional tonality. It involves the reduction of the multiple available modes (ionian, dorian, phrygian, mixolydian, aeolian, etc.) to just major and minor. The ionian mode provides the paradigm because its major triads occupy the positions of tonic, subdominant, and dominant, so that a preference for major chords reinforces the main functional poles. By contrast, this same preference can have a centrifugal effect in dorian or aeolian. Modal homogenization could scarcely occur before about 1550, as accidentals (required to transmit ionian routines to other modes) were primarily a matter of musica ficta linked to specific cadential formulae.

# Musical Context
In the early sixteenth century, different modes feature different harmonic patterns: dorian often emphasizes i, flat-VII, III, and V. Early protofunctionality is a mode-bound option rather than an inviolable law. The ionian mode's leading tone lies within the key signature, not requiring explicit accidentals; this makes it the natural starting point for functional routines that later spread to other modes as accidentals become more freely notated.

# Common Confusions
- Modal homogenization is not synonymous with the origin of functional tonality. It is one of several independent processes (along with inversional equivalence, seventh chord standardization, sequential patterning, etc.) that occurred at different times.
- The favoring of fifth-progressions among C, F, and G major triads regardless of which white-note mode is in use is one of the most challenging features of sixteenth-century music -- earlier thinking operated in an "absolute space" where particular chords had tonic-independent tendencies.
