# Quick Definition

The reduction of the available modes to just major and minor -- one of the several independent processes constituting the development of functional tonality. Different modes feature different harmonic patterns (e.g., dorian emphasizes i, bVII, III, V), which are gradually assimilated to the ionian paradigm.

# Core Definition

Modal homogenization is the reduction of available modes to just major and minor (p. 257). It is one of the multiple separate processes in the evolution of functional tonality. Different modes feature different harmonic patterns: in dorian, the subdominant is deemphasized relative to the subtonic, and pieces revolve around i, bVII, III, and V (p. 268). Early protofunctionality is mode-bound -- analogous to the blues in 1960s rock, a package of musical moves adopted or avoided according to taste (p. 268). Ionian provides the paradigm because its major triads occupy tonic, subdominant, and dominant positions, so a preference for major chords reinforces the main functional poles (p. 290). Modal homogenization could scarcely occur before ~1550 because accidentals were linked to specific cadential formulae via musica ficta (p. 291).

# Prerequisites

- **Protofunctionality** — Protofunctional idioms begin in ionian mode before homogenization spreads them

# Key Properties

1. Reduction of modes to major and minor
2. One of several independent processes in tonality's development
3. Different modes have different harmonic patterns (not all equally proto-functional)
4. Ionian is the paradigm because its primary triads are all major
5. Requires explicit accidentals (not just musica ficta) to spread ionian routines
6. Could scarcely occur before ~1550

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Modal Homogenization:
1. Check whether modes other than major/minor are being used
2. Note whether the harmonic patterns resemble ionian-mode protofunctionality
3. Look for accidentals that transform other modes toward ionian patterns
4. Track whether mode-specific patterns are being assimilated to a common functional template

# Context & Application

The process is distinct from protofunctionality per se. Protofunctional idioms may appear in one mode (ionian) without spreading to others. Homogenization is the spread of these idioms across all modes, eventually producing the major/minor system.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 268): In dorian, the subdominant is deemphasized relative to the subtonic, and pieces revolve around i, bVII, III, and V -- a different harmonic profile from ionian protofunctionality.

**Example 2** (p. 291): The leading tone within the ionian key signature does not require explicit accidentals, making ionian the natural starting point for protofunctionality.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating modal homogenization as the sole process creating functional tonality
  **Correction**: It is one of multiple independent processes (p. 258)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking all modes are equally "proto-functional"
  **Clarification**: Different modes have different harmonic tendencies; ionian is the most naturally proto-functional
