# Quick Definition

A modulatory schema pairing ascending-fifth sequences (building tension) with descending-fifth sequences (releasing tension), originating in the baroque and becoming a central classical-era developmental template.

# Core Definition

The up-and-down-the-ladder schema is "a version of the paired-sequence design that ... pairs ascending- and descending-fifth modulatory sequences: the ascending fifths increase the tension and often employ the diatonic third's ascending basic voice leading; the descending fifths release the tension and return us to equilibrium" (p. 379). In its classical sonata-development form, it typically involves: (1) an early turn to the dominant's parallel minor; (2) a two-step ascending-fifth sequence v--ii--vi; (3) continuation to V/vi, often with augmented sixth and sometimes preceded by 8--7--natural-6--flat-6--5 bass descent; (4) arrival on vi as the point of farthest remove; (5) return by descending-fifth sequence, quick turn to global dominant, or direct motion from V/vi (p. 380).

The "up-then-down" organization reflects the linear ordering of diatonic keys (IV--I--V--ii--vi--iii), which are "bounded at both ends by non-proximate keys," requiring a there-and-back structure (p. 380).

# Prerequisites

- **Paired-sequence design** -- The up-and-down-the-ladder is a specific type of paired-sequence design
- **Functional sequences** -- The ascending- and descending-fifth sequences that comprise the schema

# Key Properties

1. Ascending fifths ratchet up tension via *Sturm-und-Drang* i--V^7^/V progressions
2. The energetic peak is discharged by descending-fifth movement
3. V/vi is typically the point of farthest remove
4. Up-then-down is energetically preferable to down-then-up (tension then relaxation)
5. Originates in the baroque; survives into the nineteenth century
6. The "ladder" refers to the circle of fifths arranged linearly from flat to sharp side
7. Occasionally appears in transitions and recapitulations, not just developments

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify the Schema:
1. Look for ascending-fifth modulations in a development section (i--V^7^/V sequences)
2. Locate the point of farthest remove (often vi, with standing on dominant or augmented sixth)
3. Trace the return path: descending fifths, quick turn to global dominant, or direct motion
4. Note the rhetorical marking at the pivot (cadence, new theme, caesura)
5. Check for the specific classical features (dominant's parallel minor, ascending to V/vi)

# Context & Application

The schema appears throughout classical music, "particularly in the relatively simple developments of Mozart's piano sonatas" (p. 380). In larger pieces it sometimes occupies only one part of the development. It can also appear in transitions (Beethoven's Op. 2, no. 3) and recapitulations (Op. 18, no. 2). The ascending fifths have a characteristic sonic signature: *Sturm-und-Drang* i--V^7^/V progressions.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Expecting the schema only in development sections
  **Correction**: It occasionally appears in transitions and recapitulations

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking V/vi as the point of farthest remove is only an early-eighteenth-century feature
  **Clarification**: It persists throughout classical music, even in Mozart's later piano sonatas

- **Confusion**: Equating "ladder" with literal pitch ascent/descent
  **Clarification**: The ladder refers to the circle of fifths from flat side to sharp side; the physical pitch direction varies
