# Quick Definition
The first of four characterizations of musical knowledge: understanding music as navigation through abstract spaces of mathematical possibility.

# Formal Definition
The mathematical characterization treats musical knowledge as a matter of knowing how to move through abstract geometrical spaces. This aspect is "highly susceptible to generalization and modeling, revealing surprising structure across a very wide range of practices." It encompasses the theory of voice leading (pioneered by Richard Cohn), which uses geometry and mathematics to elucidate the connection between harmony and melody.

# Musical Context
The mathematical approach reveals deep structural connections across genres -- from Renaissance polyphony to jazz, rock, and contemporary composition. It allows generalization of core techniques including voice leading, counterpoint, sequence, motivic development, nonharmonic tones, and modulation so they apply to any chords and scales whatsoever. The spiral diagrams central to this book are one practical manifestation of this mathematical perspective.

# Examples
- Voice-leading geometry models chords as points in higher-dimensional spaces, with voice leadings as paths between them (Prelude to Ch. 2)
- The spiral diagram for major triads in chromatic space reveals why rock progressions favor certain root motions (Ch. 2, Figure 2.1.3)
- The near-symmetry of the major triad (one semitone from the augmented triad) explains the Principle of Musical Approximation (Figure 2.1.7)

# Common Confusions
- The mathematical characterization is only one of four aspects of musical knowledge -- it does not capture particular, implicit, or dogmatic dimensions
- Mathematical structure does not mean composers were doing mathematics; they intuitively navigated these spaces without formal understanding
- The mathematics is descriptive, not prescriptive -- it reveals possibilities rather than dictating rules
