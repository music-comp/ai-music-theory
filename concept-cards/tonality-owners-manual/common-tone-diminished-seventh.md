# Quick Definition

A standard idiom where a diminished seventh resolves by counterclockwise motion on the chromatic spiral, with three voices ascending by semitone. Tymoczko generalizes this to "common-tone dominant sevenths" where a dominant seventh replaces the diminished seventh, sharing three of its notes -- even though some progressions feature no common tones at all.

# Core Definition

On the 4-in-12 chromatic seventh-chord space (Figure 3.8.4, p. 140), dominant sevenths sharing three notes with the same diminished seventh are enclosed in trapezoidal boxes. Counterclockwise steps from diminished to dominant seventh produce standard "common-tone resolutions" with predominantly ascending voice leading (p. 141). One-step counterclockwise motion between dominant sevenths produces four "common-tone dominant seventh" progressions that "simply replace the standard common-tone diminished chord with a dominant seventh sharing three of its four notes" (p. 142). Tymoczko calls these "common-tone dominant sevenths, even though some of the progressions feature no common tones!" (p. 142). The spiral diagram thus generalizes beyond contexts where literal common tones are present, "moving beyond a specific idiom to a more general form of tetrachordal retrofunctionality in which seventh chords resolve contrary to our expectations" (p. 146).

# Key Properties

1. Standard common-tone: diminished seventh resolves counterclockwise, three voices ascending by semitone
2. Generalized: dominant seventh replaces diminished, sharing three notes
3. Four common-tone dominant seventh progressions:
   - Reverse of dominant resolution (seventh rises to leading tone)
   - Upper neighbor to dominant a major second below
   - Semitonal lower neighbor
   - Augmented sixth to cadential six-four
4. Some progressions lack literal common tones despite the name
5. Counterclockwise direction on the spiral = retrofunctional/ascending voice leading

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify:
1. Look for predominantly ascending voice leading between seventh chords
2. Check if three voices ascend by semitone (standard form)
3. For the generalized form, check if a dominant seventh shares three notes with the diminished seventh it replaces
4. Verify counterclockwise motion on the chromatic seventh-chord space

# Context & Application

The reverse dominant resolution (seventh rising to leading tone) becomes "increasingly important over the course of the nineteenth century" (Figure 3.8.8, p. 142). Supertonic dominant seventh to tonic appears in Haydn, Mozart, Grieg, and John Williams (Figures 3.8.10-11, p. 143). In late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century American music, ascending resolutions harmonize blue notes, "explicitly linking them to the common-tone diminished sevenths of the classical tradition" (p. 144) -- seen in Scott Joplin, Charles Trevathan, and Antonio Maggio (Figure 3.8.13, p. 144). Semitonally ascending diminished sevenths (Beethoven Op. 2 no. 1) are yet another variant (Figure 3.8.14, p. 145).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming "common-tone" resolutions require literal common tones
  **Correction**: The generalized category includes progressions with no common tones; the term refers to voice-leading direction (counterclockwise = ascending), not pitch sharing

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Limiting common-tone diminished seventh to its textbook form
  **Clarification**: The spiral diagram reveals it as one instance of a broader category of tetrachordal retrofunctionality with predominantly ascending voice leading
