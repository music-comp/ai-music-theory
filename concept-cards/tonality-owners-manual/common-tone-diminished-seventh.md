# Quick Definition
A standard idiom in which a diminished seventh chord resolves by counterclockwise motion on the chromatic seventh-chord spiral, with three voices ascending by semitone; Tymoczko generalizes this to "common-tone dominant sevenths" where a dominant seventh replaces the diminished seventh, sharing three of its four notes.

# Formal Definition
On the 4-in-12 space of chromatic seventh chords (Figure 3.8.4), dominant sevenths sharing three notes with the same diminished seventh are enclosed in trapezoidal boxes. Counterclockwise steps from diminished to dominant seventh produce the standard "common-tone resolutions" of second-semester theory, with predominantly ascending voice leading. The generalization replaces the diminished seventh with one of its in-box dominant-seventh neighbors, producing four "common-tone dominant seventh" progressions -- even though some feature no common tones at all.

# Musical Context
The four common-tone dominant seventh resolutions include: (1) reverse of the standard dominant resolution (seventh rises to leading tone), increasingly important in the nineteenth century; (2) dominant seventh as upper neighbor to the dominant a major second below (found in Haydn and Mozart); (3) semitonal lower neighbor resolution; (4) augmented-sixth-to-cadential-six-four. These ascending resolutions were used in late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century American music to harmonize blue notes, linking them to classical common-tone diminished sevenths. The spiral diagram generalizes beyond contexts where literal common tones are present.

# Common Confusions
- "Common-tone" is the traditional name, but the generalized category includes progressions with no common tones -- the term refers to voice-leading direction (counterclockwise), not literal pitch sharing
- Semitonally ascending diminished sevenths (e.g., Beethoven Op. 2 no. 1) are another variant of this category
