# Quick Definition

Tymoczko's name for a family of contrary-motion contrapuntal patterns, central to Beethoven's style, in which outer voices alternate between diatonic thirds and sixths while moving in opposite directions -- exploiting the near-quarter-octave symmetry of the diatonic third.

# Core Definition

The Ludwig embellishes a basic contrary-motion dyadic pattern by adding stepwise contrary motion and lower neighbors (p. 459). The minor third, being exactly one fourth of an octave, has the property that its most efficient voice leading to its tritone transposition equals its smaller voice exchange. The diatonic third inherits this symmetry approximately, allowing sequences where one voice sounds thirds while the other alternates thirds and steps (p. 459). The resulting patterns "fuse contrapuntal fifth-progressions, deriving from dyadic voice-leading geometry, with harmonic fifth-progressions, deriving from functional convention" (p. 460). The Ludwig comes in descending-fifth and ascending-fifth varieties, with or without lower-neighbor embellishment.

# Key Properties

1. Outer voices alternate between thirds and sixths in contrary motion
2. Can be embellished with stepwise motion and lower neighbors
3. Produces V-I-IV or IV-I-V progressions in simplest form (primary-triad harmony)
4. More complex versions act as V-I-vii/V or V/IV-IV-vii
5. Comes in octave-focused (emphasizing octaves/unisons) and third-focused (emphasizing imperfect consonances) variants
6. Is "not so much 'the Ludwig schema' as 'Ludwig-ing'" -- a flexible network of compositional options (p. 466)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct a Ludwig:
1. Begin with a consonant dyad (third or sixth)
2. Move outer voices in contrary motion by step or third
3. Arrive at the next consonant dyad (sixth or third)
4. Continue the pattern, choosing between descending-fourth or ascending-fifth at each juncture
5. Embellish with lower neighbors or stepwise motion as desired

## To Recognize:
1. Look for outer voices moving in contrary motion through thirds and sixths
2. Check for primary-triad harmony (I, IV, V) as the harmonic result
3. Note whether the pattern repeats with registral expansion
4. Watch for the characteristic Beethovenian unidirectionality

# Context & Application

The Ludwig appears in almost every Beethoven movement, producing a characteristically Beethovenian sound. It functions simultaneously as an improvisational riff, a tool for dramatic registral expansion (pitch-domain analogue of the crescendo), and evidence of theoretical fascination with the quasi-sequential qualities of basic functional idioms. Clementi is the likely historical precedent. "In some sense Ludwiging is just contrary motion itself, exploiting the fifth-based geometry of diatonic dyads" (p. 466).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 459): Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel's "April" from Das Jahr -- exact contrary-motion sequence with minor-third outer voices, showing the chromatic prototype.

**Example 2** (p. 461): Op. 18, no. 3 opening and Appassionata second theme -- primary-triad Ludwigs creating V-I-IV progressions.

**Example 3** (p. 461): Pathetique Sonata -- Ludwig schema opening both first and second movements, the second as major-mode variation.

**Example 4** (p. 471): Eroica first movement, mm. 57-60 -- three consecutive Ludwig units in massive sonic accretion combining register, dynamics, rhythm, and orchestration.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating the Ludwig as a single fixed idiom
  **Correction**: It is a flexible network of compositional options -- "Ludwiging" rather than a rigid pattern

- **Error**: Analyzing Ludwig passages purely in terms of Roman numerals
  **Correction**: The schematic dyads (e.g., "merely passing" B-G) may be essential to the schema but not the Roman-numeral harmony (p. 462)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Assuming the Ludwig is unique to Beethoven
  **Clarification**: It is fundamental to functional counterpoint generally, but is characteristically Beethovenian because of his unidirectional use for registral expansion

- **Confusion**: Thinking the Ludwig is simply parallel thirds
  **Clarification**: It involves contrary motion producing alternating thirds and sixths, not parallel motion
