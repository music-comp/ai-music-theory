# Quick Definition
Tymoczko's name for a family of contrary-motion contrapuntal patterns, central to Beethoven's style, in which outer voices alternate between diatonic thirds and sixths while moving in opposite directions -- exploiting the near-quarter-octave symmetry of the diatonic third.

# Formal Definition
The Ludwig embellishes a basic contrary-motion dyadic pattern by adding stepwise contrary motion and lower neighbors. The minor third, being exactly one fourth of an octave, has the property that its most efficient voice leading to its tritone transposition equals its smaller voice exchange. The diatonic third inherits this symmetry approximately, allowing sequences where one voice sounds thirds while the other alternates thirds and steps. The Ludwig fuses contrapuntal fifth-progressions (from dyadic voice-leading geometry) with harmonic fifth-progressions (from functional convention). It comes in descending-fifth and ascending-fifth varieties, with or without lower-neighbor embellishment.

# Musical Context
The Ludwig is not merely an idiom but a "conceptual scheme, a very general way of approaching tonal-functional counterpoint" (p. 460). It appears in almost every Beethoven movement, functioning simultaneously as: (1) a favorite improvisational riff, (2) a tool for producing dramatic gestural waves and registral expansion, and (3) evidence of theoretical fascination with quasi-sequential qualities of basic functional idioms. The Ludwig can be understood as contrary motion itself, exploiting the fifth-based geometry of diatonic dyads.

# Examples

- Pathetique Sonata: Ludwig schema opening both first and second movements, the second movement as major-mode variation (Figure 10.1.7, p. 461)
- Eroica first movement, mm. 57-60: three consecutive Ludwig units in massive sonic accretion (Figure 10.2.11, p. 471)

# Common Confusions
- The Ludwig is not a single fixed idiom but a flexible network of compositional options ("not so much 'the Ludwig schema' as 'Ludwig-ing'") (p. 466)
- Despite being fundamental to functional counterpoint, it is characteristically Beethovenian because of his unidirectional use for registral expansion, unlike other composers who change direction
- Clementi is the likely historical precedent -- Beethoven reportedly said those who study Clementi learn techniques not available from Mozart
