# Quick Definition

The two elementary functional cycles -- I-IV-I (subdominant) and I-V-I (dominant) -- which are the building blocks of all functional harmony, with the dominant cycle growing more complex over time while the subdominant cycle remains static or decreases in prominence.

# Core Definition

The simplest forms of functionality are built around elementary subdominant and dominant cycles: I-IV-I, I-V-I, and I-IV-V-I, all articulated by root-position chords (p. 312). As functional harmony develops historically, the dominant cycle increases in complexity (incorporating ii, vii-dim, and eventually seventh chords), coming to play a greater and greater role, while the subdominant cycle remains static or even decreases in prominence. The combined cycle I-IV-V-I divides the circle of diatonic triads into three nearly even segments, creating a functional sequence of tonic, subdominant, and dominant whose pitch classes articulate a chain of descending thirds (p. 318). Simple root-position exchanges like I-IV-I and I-V-I divide the registral octave nearly evenly, with bass moving by approximately half octave while melody moves by approximately one sixth of an octave.

# Key Properties

1. Three elementary cycles: I-IV-I, I-V-I, I-IV-V-I
2. All articulated by root-position chords in their simplest forms
3. Historical asymmetry: dominant cycle elaborates while subdominant remains static
4. I-IV-V-I divides the circle of diatonic triads into three nearly even segments
5. The unsmoothed progressions V-I and IV-I remain primary signifiers of functionality across all periods
6. The expansion from I-IV-V-I to I-vi-IV-ii-vii-dim-V-I smooths transitions using third-related chords
7. Ascending fifths not involving the tonic triad are rare -- functional tonality avoids nontonic ascending fifths rather than ascending fifths per se (p. 326)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Elementary Functional Cycles:
1. Look for root-position I-IV-I (subdominant cycle) or I-V-I (dominant cycle)
2. Check for the combined I-IV-V-I cycle
3. In more elaborate progressions, trace the expansion: I-vi-IV-ii-vii-dim-V-I
4. Note whether the progression conforms to the descending-thirds harmonic map

# Context & Application

These cycles form the core of all functional tonality, from simple protofunctionality through mature classical practice. The asymmetry between subdominant and dominant development is a key feature distinguishing early from late functionality. The two-way fit between the harmonic grammar and actual practice "poses a challenge for those who would like to replace Roman numerals with particular schemas" (p. 313), since one would need a fine-tuned collection of idioms to reproduce the model's progressions.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 312, Fig. 7.1.3): The harmonic grammar arranges diatonic triads in a chain of thirds descending from tonic to dominant; chords are free to move rightward but leftward motion must follow specific arrows.

**Example 2** (p. 318): The expansion from I-IV-V-I to I-vi-IV-ii-vii-dim-V-I smooths transitions between functional poles using third-related chords that share all but one note.

**Example 3** (p. 326, Fig. 7.4.1): Functional tonality often "travels the long way around" the triadic circle, with simple cycles like I-V-I and I-IV-I making a complete circle through the space.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Thinking the subdominant cycle is simply "less important" than the dominant
  **Correction**: The subdominant cycle is foundational to functionality, but its structure remains simpler while the dominant cycle elaborates over time

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Assuming ascending fifths (I-V, IV-I) are prohibited by the harmonic grammar
  **Clarification**: Ascending fifths not involving the tonic triad are rare, but ascending fifths involving the tonic (I-V, IV-I) are ubiquitous and fundamental (p. 326)
