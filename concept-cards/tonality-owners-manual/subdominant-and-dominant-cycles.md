# Quick Definition
The two elementary functional cycles -- I--IV--I (subdominant) and I--V--I (dominant) -- which are the building blocks of all functional harmony, with the dominant cycle growing more complex over time while the subdominant remains static.

# Formal Definition
The simplest forms of functionality are built around elementary subdominant and dominant cycles: I--IV--I, I--V--I, and I--IV--V--I, all articulated by root-position chords. As functional harmony develops historically, the dominant cycle increases in complexity (incorporating ii, vii^o^, and eventually seventh chords), coming to play a greater role, while the subdominant cycle remains static or decreases in prominence. The combined cycle I--IV--V--I divides the circle of diatonic triads into three nearly even segments, creating a functional sequence of tonic, subdominant, and dominant whose pitch classes articulate a chain of descending thirds.

# Musical Context
These cycles form the core of all functional tonality, from simple protofunctionality through mature classical practice. The asymmetry between subdominant and dominant development is one of the key features distinguishing early from late functionality. In simple protofunctionality, root-position exchanges like I--IV--I and I--V--I divide the registral octave nearly evenly.

# Examples
- The expansion from I--IV--V--I to I--vi--IV--ii--vii^o^--V--I smooths transitions between functional poles using third-related chords.
- Bach chorales: The dominant cycle encompasses V, vii^o^, and various seventh-chord configurations.
- The unsmoothed progressions V--I and IV--I remain primary signifiers of functionality across all periods.

# Common Confusions
- The subdominant cycle is not simply "less important" -- it is foundational to functionality, but its structure remains simpler while the dominant cycle elaborates over time.
- Ascending fifths (I--V, IV--I) are not prohibited by the harmonic grammar; rather, ascending fifths *not involving the tonic triad* are rare.
