# Quick Definition

The complete system of two-voice counterpoint built from thirds and sixths, governed by the diatonic dyad circle. Its atomic moves are the basic voice leading, two voice exchanges, and their combinations, which together generate all possible voice leadings between diatonic imperfect consonances.

# Core Definition

The imperfect consonance system comprises all voice leadings between diatonic thirds and sixths, represented on the 2-in-7 spiral diagram (Figure 3.1.1, p. 97). Since the chord has two notes, the line of transposition winds twice around the circle; since the diatonic scale has seven notes, there are seven equally spaced points. Sliding along the spiral transposes along the scale; looping around transposes along the chord, turning thirds into sixths and vice versa. The system reveals that fifth-related dyads are geometrically adjacent, explaining why "linearly oriented music will contain a plethora of fifth-related imperfect consonances, even when roots are not the explicit objects of compositional attention" (p. 99). Its atomic moves are: (1) the basic voice leading, (2) the two-step voice exchange, (3) the five-step voice exchange, and (4) their combinations producing antiparallel motion. Any three-voice diatonic voice leading between consonant sonorities will have two voices behaving according to this system unless one sonority contains no imperfect consonances (p. 123).

# Key Properties

1. Thirds and sixths are inversionally related, occupying different radial positions on the same spiral
2. Single clockwise step produces the descending basic voice leading (T_{-4}t_1)
3. Fifth-related dyads are adjacent on the diagram, privileging fifth motion
4. Odd numbers of basic voice leadings transpose along the chord (third to sixth); even numbers do not
5. All derived voice leadings (parallel, antiparallel, basic) are permissible for imperfect consonances
6. The same graph represents any two-note interval in any seven-note scale
7. Parallel motion and shifts between third and sixth generate all moves on the expanded diagram

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct the 2-in-7 Spiral:
1. Draw a spiral that winds twice around a circle (two loops for a two-note chord)
2. Place seven equally spaced points along the spiral (seven for the diatonic scale)
3. Label with consecutive scale tones
4. Each point represents a complete two-note chord (third or sixth)
5. Add curved arrows for two-step and five-step voice exchanges

## To Recognize Imperfect System Motion:
1. Identify the two-voice dyad at each point
2. Measure angular distance traveled (number of basic voice leadings)
3. Odd steps = transposition along chord; even steps = no chordal transposition

# Context & Application

Thirds and sixths predominate in Western music partly because composers favor imperfect consonances and partly because all derived voice leadings are permissible. Perfect consonances require more cautious treatment due to forbidden parallels and the ambiguous status of fourths and tritones. The system persists in three- and four-voice contexts: in three-voice counterpoint, two voices almost always connect imperfect consonances; in four-voice music, pairs of voices often move along their respective dyadic circles. Common idioms can be generated by moving one hand along a scale or arpeggio while the other alternates between third and sixth (Figure 3.2.5, p. 107).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming perfect consonances are modeled on the same diagram
  **Correction**: Each interval type has its own spiral diagram; the imperfect system specifically models thirds and sixths

- **Error**: Confusing angular distance on the diagram with root motion
  **Correction**: Angular distance counts basic voice leadings applied; root motion is a consequence

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing the dyadic circle of fifths is the same as the acoustic circle of fifths
  **Clarification**: The dyadic circle is independent of both the acoustic circle and the circle connecting diatonic scales (p. 99)

- **Confusion**: Thinking the imperfect system is limited to imperfect consonances alone
  **Clarification**: It is the complete geometry of two-voice counterpoint that privileges thirds and sixths; perfect consonances have parallel but more restricted geometry
