# Quick Definition
The complete system of two-voice counterpoint built from thirds and sixths, governed by the diatonic dyad circle, encompassing the basic voice leading, two voice exchanges, and their combinations as the "atomic moves" of the imperfect system.

# Formal Definition
The imperfect consonance system comprises all voice leadings between diatonic thirds and sixths. Its atomic moves are: (1) the basic voice leading (single clockwise/counterclockwise step on the spiral), (2) the two-step voice exchange, (3) the five-step voice exchange, and (4) combinations of basic voice leadings with voice exchanges producing antiparallel motion. Parallel motion and shifts between third and sixth generate all moves on the expanded diagram. Any three-voice diatonic voice leading between consonant sonorities will have two voices behaving according to this system unless one sonority contains no imperfect consonances at all.

# Musical Context
Thirds and sixths predominate in Western music partly because composers favor imperfect consonances and partly because all derived voice leadings are permissible -- parallel motion, antiparallel motion, and the basic voice leading itself. Perfect consonances require more cautious treatment. The system persists in three- and four-voice contexts: in three-voice counterpoint, two of three voices almost always connect imperfect consonances; in four-voice music, pairs of voices often move along their respective dyadic circles. Common idioms can be generated by moving one hand along a scale or arpeggio while the other alternates between third and sixth.

# Common Confusions
- The "imperfect system" is not just about imperfect consonances; it is the complete geometry of two-voice counterpoint that privileges thirds and sixths
- Perfect consonances have their own parallel geometry but with more restrictions on usage
