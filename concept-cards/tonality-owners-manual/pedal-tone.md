# Quick Definition
A sustained note, initially harmonic, that is held through one or more chord changes, becoming nonharmonic against the changing harmony above.

# Formal Definition
A pedal tone is any note that is initially harmonic and sustained through one or more chord changes. Pedals are both a rhetorical effect and a species of nonharmonic tone -- one can create the sense of a pedal by changing chords over a static bass note, even if that bass is always part of the prevailing harmony. In practice, pedal tones almost always occur on the tonic or dominant scale degree and typically return to harmonic status before they move. Uniquely among nonharmonic tones, pedals are irreducible: removing them requires recomposition rather than simple reduction.

# Musical Context
Pedal tones are one of the new contrapuntal devices embraced in the eighteenth-century standardized second practice. Mozart's piano sonatas contain about a hundred pedals, all conforming to standard principles (tonic or dominant scale degree, returning to harmonic status before moving). Bach's chorales contain a few exceptions to these norms.

# Common Confusions
- Many textbooks dodge the irreducibility of pedals by stipulating that the rest of the music embellishes the pedal tone itself. Tymoczko argues this conflates syntactic nonharmonic reduction with the very different enterprise of summarizing reduction.
- The formal definition (any initially harmonic note sustained through chord changes) is overbroad, encompassing phenomena like Monteverdi's "suspensions that resolve by leap," which may not feel like pedals.
