# Quick Definition

Schoenberg's argument in Harmonielehre that the concept of "nonharmonic tones" is incoherent: "Either there is no such thing as non-harmonic tones, or they are not non-harmonic." Tymoczko agrees partly, proposing that nonharmonic tones are best understood as "melodically constrained" rather than harmonically irrelevant.

# Core Definition

In chapter 17 of his Harmonielehre (1911), Schoenberg protests: "non-harmonic matters have just as little place in a textbook of harmony as do non-medical matters in a textbook of medicine" (p. 221). Behind this verbal complaint, Tymoczko identifies the deeper observation that nonharmonic tones range from "merely connective" (serving melodic aims) to those whose purpose is to create a specific sound. In these latter cases, allegiance to nonharmonicity keeps us from understanding a genuinely harmonic logic (p. 222). Tymoczko proposes that nonharmonic tones are "melodically constrained in ways that harmonic tones are not" (p. 226), a formulation that preserves the concept's utility while acknowledging Schoenberg's insight.

1. Schoenberg's complaint seems verbal but contains a deeper insight
2. Some nonharmonic tones create specific sounds that are genuinely harmonic in effect
3. The safest understanding: nonharmonic tones are "melodically constrained" (p. 226)
4. Schoenberg went too far in denying nonharmonicity altogether -- the regularities are real
5. The Monteverdi-Artusi controversy was about something real (p. 226)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply This Critique:
1. Identify nonharmonic tones in a passage
2. Ask whether any create a specific harmonic sound or sonority
3. If multiple nonharmonic tones conspire to produce the same chord, consider whether harmony is the goal
4. Recognize that "nonharmonic" refers to melodic constraints, not harmonic irrelevance

# Context & Application

The critique connects to the broader tension between rules and aesthetic judgment. Tymoczko shows multiple examples where nonharmonic tones produce harmonically significant sonorities: ascending subdominants where passing tones create dominant function (p. 223), Chopin's dominant thirteenths formed by multiple nonharmonic tones (p. 225, Fig. 5.2.5), and the Tristan chord (p. 225, Fig. 5.2.6). As Schoenberg put it, "these are chords: not of the system, but of music" (p. 226).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 222, Fig. 5.2.1): Two interpretations of the 6/4 chord in Palestrina's mass Descendit angelus Domini -- the 6/4-5/3 pattern is ubiquitous and cannot be treated as accidental.

**Example 2** (p. 225, Fig. 5.2.5): Multiple different nonharmonic tones conspire to produce the same "dominant thirteenth" sonority in Chopin's mazurkas, suggesting harmony is goal rather than byproduct.

**Example 3** (p. 225, Fig. 5.2.6): The Tristan chord, so ubiquitous that it is reasonably considered harmonic despite being explainable as byproduct of nonharmonic tones.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Following Schoenberg in completely denying the existence of nonharmonicity
  **Correction**: The regularities of nonharmonic usage are real; the Monteverdi-Artusi controversy was about something genuine

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking Schoenberg's complaint is merely about terminology
  **Clarification**: The verbal complaint hides the deeper observation that some nonharmonic tones serve genuinely harmonic purposes
