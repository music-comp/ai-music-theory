# Quick Definition
Schoenberg's argument in Harmonielehre (1911) that the concept of "nonharmonic tones" is fundamentally incoherent -- that such tones are either harmonic or they are not truly part of music.

# Formal Definition
In chapter 17 of his Harmonielehre, Schoenberg argues that the concept of nonharmonic tones is the weakest point of the harmonic system: "Either there is no such thing as non-harmonic tones, or they are not non-harmonic." Tymoczko argues that behind Schoenberg's verbal complaint lies a deeper observation: nonharmonic tones range from "merely connective" (serving primarily melodic aims) to those whose purpose is to create a specific sound. In these latter cases, uncritical allegiance to nonharmonicity keeps us from understanding a genuinely harmonic logic. Tymoczko proposes that nonharmonic tones are best understood as "melodically constrained" rather than "non-harmonic."

# Musical Context
Schoenberg's critique connects to the broader tension between rules and aesthetic judgment in music. Tymoczko agrees that Schoenberg was "fundamentally right" that musically sensitive theory cannot treat nonharmonic tones as having nothing to do with harmony. However, Schoenberg went too far in denying the existence of nonharmonicity altogether, which ignores unmistakable regularities in nonharmonic usage.

# Common Confusions
- Schoenberg's complaint seems verbal ("if it were non-harmonic, it would not be there"), but contains the deeper insight that some nonharmonic tones have genuinely harmonic purposes.
- The safest understanding of "nonharmonic" is that such tones are melodically constrained in ways that harmonic tones are not -- e.g., the fourth in a 64 is never doubled and almost always resolves downward.
- Schoenberg's absolute denial of nonharmonicity is too extreme: the Monteverdi-Artusi controversy was about something real.
