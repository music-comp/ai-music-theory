# Quick Definition

A IV--I progression in which scale degree 6 ascends by step through 7 to the tonic, using the diatonic third's ascending basic voice leading, functioning as the inversional counterpart of the V--I progression and creating a resolving tritone.

# Core Definition

The ascending subdominant is a IV--I progression that incorporates an ascending leading tone -- scale degree 6 moving up through 7 to the tonic. It uses the diatonic third's ascending basic voice leading and is the diatonic inversional counterpart of V--I, related by inversion around the supertonic (Figure 7.7.2). "Paradoxically IV--I is *both* a musical antipode to V--I, and *also* an alternative expression of the same functional logic" (p. 344) -- both permit a passing note that creates a resolving tritone. C. P. E. Bach's observation that "the third of a major triad generally has a tendency to ascend" may reference this practice (p. 344).

The ascending 7 spans a continuum from clearly harmonic (supporting a V chord) to purely nonharmonic (a passing tone connecting IV to I). In J. S. Bach's chorales, approximately a third of root-position IV--I progressions incorporate this ascending leading tone, while in Beethoven's string quartets it occurs only a handful of times (p. 345).

# Key Properties

1. IV--I with scale degree 6 ascending stepwise (6--7--8) to the tonic
2. Uses the diatonic third's ascending basic voice leading
3. Related to V--I by diatonic inversion around the supertonic
4. Both IV--I and V--I permit a passing note creating a resolving tritone
5. The ascending 7 is virtually mandatory when 6--1 is in the bass (Ch. 5, fn. 26)
6. Common in Bach (~1/3 of root-position IV--I progressions) but rare in classical-era music
7. Dialect-specific: reflects a fundamentally different conception of functional progression
8. Spans a continuum from clearly harmonic to clearly nonharmonic

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify an Ascending Subdominant:
1. Locate a IV--I progression
2. Check whether scale degree 6 (the third of IV) ascends by step through 7 to 1
3. Look for simultaneous 4--3 motion creating a resolving tritone
4. Assess whether the passing 7 sounds harmonic or purely connective
5. Note the diatonic third's ascending basic voice leading in the upper voices

# Context & Application

The ascending subdominant suggests that "the picture of two opposite functions derives from later musical dialects, rather than being intrinsic to functional harmony itself" (p. 345). In Bach's music, "dominant" may be a superordinate genus containing both descending-fifth and ascending-fifth species. This practice is central to Bachian dualism and helps explain passages that are consistent with but not well-illuminated by standard Roman-numeral analysis.

The concept also exemplifies the analytical challenge of nonharmonic tones with harmonic effects (Ch. 5, p. 223): some ascending leading tones clearly require a Roman numeral, others support one only shakily, and still others do not call for one at all.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Always assigning a V Roman numeral to the passing 7 in ascending subdominants
  **Correction**: The figure spans a continuum; some instances require a Roman numeral and others do not

- **Error**: Assuming the ascending subdominant is always present in IV--I progressions
  **Correction**: It is dialect-specific -- common in Bach, rare in classical-era music

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the ascending subdominant is "wrong" or nonfunctional
  **Clarification**: It follows the same voice-leading logic as V--I, just inverted; C. P. E. Bach evidently considered it standard practice

- **Confusion**: Equating all IV--I progressions with a passing 7 as equally "dominant"
  **Clarification**: The degree of dominant functionality varies case by case
