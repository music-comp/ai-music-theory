# Quick Definition

The distinction between directly experiencing a musical property (feeling tension "in the chord itself") and having cognitive access to information that allows one to identify or calculate the property without genuine perceptual experience.

# Core Definition

Tymoczko distinguishes between two types of musical knowledge. Experiential knowledge involves "direct sensitivity to interval and chord quality as such" -- hearing a dominant seventh as "a perceptual whole" whose character seems "immediate, forceful, and present," with "the sense of dominantness" preceding awareness of component pitches (p. 403). Calculational knowledge involves using one type of information to derive another: the imaginary absolute-pitch student who counts semitones to identify intervals demonstrates this (pp. 402--403).

The distinction bears on two separable issues: (1) "we can sometimes *think* we are sensitive to musical features even when we demonstrably are not" -- a problem tractable through experiment; (2) "we are sometimes capable of passing empirical tests *even without being able to hear the relationship as such*" -- a deeper problem experiments cannot resolve (p. 402).

# Key Properties

1. Experiential knowledge feels immediate, pre-reflective, "embedded in the perceptual experience" (p. 403)
2. Calculational knowledge requires conscious processing steps
3. Experiments can test sensitivity but cannot determine whether it is experiential or calculational
4. Long-range key perception is particularly vulnerable to the confusion between the two
5. Most relative-pitch listeners have only calculational access to key identity
6. Theory can sometimes convert calculational into experiential knowledge

# Construction / Recognition

## To Distinguish Experiential from Calculational:
1. Does the perception arise before conscious analysis? (experiential)
2. Does it require tracking, counting, or score-following? (calculational)
3. Would the perception survive if attention were distracted? (experiential)
4. Does it depend on prior knowledge of the piece? (likely calculational)
5. Note that self-reports are unreliable -- people routinely confuse the two

# Context & Application

This distinction presents "a profound challenge to music theory" (p. 404). It demands skepticism about claims regarding long-range perception, sensitivity to the social forces encouraging exaggeration, and worry that "some substantial proportion of [music-theoretical] claims might not be grounded in anything real" (p. 404). At the same time, "theory can sometimes change our experience" (p. 404), and "a lifetime of experience" shows both that "we can be drastically mistaken about what we hear" and that "theory can sometimes enrich our experience" (p. 406).

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 402--403): The imaginary absolute-pitch student who identifies intervals by counting semitones -- correct answers, no direct intervallic experience.

**Example 2** (p. 403): Hearing a dominant seventh as a perceptual whole whose dominantness precedes awareness of component pitches.

**Example 3** (p. 404): A non-absolute-pitch listener who knows a piece has returned to the tonic through rhetoric, instrumentation, or score study -- cognitive access without experiential resolution.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming that anyone who can identify a musical feature also directly perceives it
  **Correction**: Passing tests does not prove experiential sensitivity -- calculation can produce the same results

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking that if one "knows" a piece is in the home key, one necessarily "feels" tonal resolution
  **Clarification**: Knowing "that" is different from experiencing it -- "the experience of dissonance is felt and immediate in a way that the calculation of keys is not" (p. 404)
