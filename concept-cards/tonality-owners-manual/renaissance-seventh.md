# Quick Definition
A sounding seventh chord, formed by suspension, that cannot be reduced to a consonance -- an idiom appearing as early as Dufay and used throughout the Renaissance.

# Formal Definition
A Renaissance seventh occurs when the voices not sounding a dissonant suspension form a consonance that is itself dissonant with the suspension's tone of resolution. The result is a seventh chord that is irreducible: replacing the nonharmonic tone with its resolution produces a second seventh chord rather than a consonance. The starred notes in these configurations cannot be classified as dissonances according to standard contrapuntal theory. The idiom arises because the remaining voices form the maximal consonance not containing the suspension resolution.

# Musical Context
Renaissance sevenths tend to have cadential function. The most common form resembles and evolves into the baroque predominant seventh, while other forms occasionally produce sounding dominant sevenths. This idiom antedates the notion of a "governing triad" while also evolving into the seventh chords of the classical era. Seventh chords are the inevitable result of attempting to reconcile earlier practice with the philosophical view that nonharmonic tones must be decorative.

# Common Confusions
- Fetis wrongly claimed dominant seventh chords originated at the end of the sixteenth century, mistaking standard Renaissance practice for a harbinger of the future.
- The Renaissance seventh is simultaneously new and old -- it antedates the notion of a "governing triad" while also evolving into later seventh chords.
- Everything makes sense as long as we abandon the assumption that a definite consonance governs the entire texture at the moment of suspension.
- The idealized consonant style of counterpoint textbooks never actually existed: Palestrina's music retains irreducible idioms inherited from the past and pointing toward the future.
