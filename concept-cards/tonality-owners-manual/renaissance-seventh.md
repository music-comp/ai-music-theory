# Quick Definition

A sounding seventh chord formed by suspension that cannot be reduced to a consonance -- an idiom appearing as early as Dufay and used throughout the Renaissance, demonstrating that the idealized consonant style of counterpoint textbooks never actually existed.

# Core Definition

A Renaissance seventh occurs when the voices not sounding a dissonant suspension form a consonance that is itself dissonant with the suspension's tone of resolution. The result is a seventh chord that is irreducible: replacing the nonharmonic tone with its resolution produces a second seventh chord rather than a consonance. The idiom arises because the remaining voices form the maximal consonance not containing either the suspended note or its resolution (except for the 9-8 suspension over a root-position triad). The starred notes in these configurations cannot be classified as dissonances according to standard contrapuntal theory (p. 216). The idiom appears at least once per movement on average in Palestrina's music (fn. 16).

# Key Properties

1. Formed by suspension where the non-suspended voices create a consonance dissonant with the resolution
2. Cannot be reduced to a consonant background
3. Appears as early as Dufay and is used throughout the Renaissance
4. Tends to have cadential function
5. The most common form evolves into the baroque predominant seventh
6. Some forms occasionally produce sounding dominant sevenths
7. Standard resolutions involve root progressions of a descending third or descending fifth (Fig. 5.1.9)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify a Renaissance Seventh:
1. Locate a suspension in a multi-voice texture
2. Check whether the non-suspended voices form a consonance
3. Determine the suspension's tone of resolution
4. If the consonance formed by the other voices is dissonant with the resolution, you have a Renaissance seventh
5. Verify that replacing the suspension with its resolution does NOT produce a consonance

# Context & Application

Renaissance sevenths demonstrate that the idealized consonant style of counterpoint textbooks never actually existed. As Tymoczko puts it, "Palestrina wrote just before and just after an imaginary consonant era" (p. 220). The idiom is "simultaneously new and old -- an idiom that antedates the notion of a 'governing triad' while also evolving into the seventh chords of the classical era" (p. 219). Seventh chords are "the inevitable result of attempting to reconcile the earlier practice with the philosophical view that nonharmonic tones must necessarily be decorative" (p. 220).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Attempting to reduce all Renaissance suspensions to consonant backgrounds
  **Correction**: Some suspension configurations produce irreducible seventh chords where no consonant background exists

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing Fetis that dominant seventh chords originated at the end of the sixteenth century
  **Clarification**: Fetis mistook standard Renaissance practice for a harbinger of the future; sounding dominant sevenths appear in Palestrina as a matter of routine (p. 219)

- **Confusion**: Thinking the idealized consonant style of counterpoint textbooks actually existed
  **Clarification**: Palestrina's music retains irreducible idioms "inherited from the past and pointing toward the future" (p. 220)
