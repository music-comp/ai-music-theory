# Quick Definition

A notation system adapted from the Russian theorist Sergei Taneyev, using labeled arrows to show how melodic lines are transposed and permuted from one sequential unit to the next. Uncrossed arrows produce transpositional sequences; crossed arrows produce canonic sequences.

# Core Definition

Each arrow points from source to destination voice and is labeled with a transposition T_x (Figure 4.1.2, p. 156). "Whatever music is in the top voice is transposed by x and placed in the second voice, and whatever music is in the second voice is transposed by y and is sent to the top" (p. 156). The arrows form a stable structure that stays fixed as the music repeats. Uncrossed arrows: transpositional sequences. Crossed arrows: canonic sequences, partitioning voices into groups sharing melodic content. A three-voice sequence can have no crossings (no canon), two crossed (two-voice canon), or all three crossing (three-voice canon) (Figure 4.1.3, p. 157). The notation has two variants: cross-voice T_x arrows linking notes across voices, and within-voice T_x t_y arrows linking notes within voices (Figure 4.2.4, p. 161).

# Key Properties

1. Arrows point from source to destination voice, labeled with transposition
2. Uncrossed arrows = transpositional; crossed = canonic
3. Stable structure fixed across repetitions
4. Three-voice sequences have 0, 2, or 3 crossings
5. Two notational variants: cross-voice and within-voice
6. Cross-voice suits larger units; within-voice generalizes more readily

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply:
1. Identify the cell (minimal repeating block)
2. For each voice, determine where its melodic content goes in the next unit
3. Draw an arrow from source to destination, label with the transposition interval
4. Crossed arrows indicate canonic relationships

# Context & Application

Adapted from Taneyev's 1909 work. The notation clarifies the distinction between simple and grand period. Transpositional arrows with different subscripts involve invertible counterpoint (p. 158). "In most sequences, the arrows form a stable structure that stays fixed as the music repeats" (p. 156). The notation reveals that permutation is a fundamental sequence-generating operation, long neglected by theorists focused only on the grand period.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Confusing arrows' transpositional labels with melodic direction
  **Correction**: Arrows describe how material moves between units, not melodic direction within a unit

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking melodic-interval and transpositional-label notations are different systems
  **Clarification**: They carry the same information for a given registral position but diverge when octave displacements occur (Figure 4.2.3, p. 161)
