# Quick Definition
A notation system adapted from the Russian theorist Sergei Taneyev, using labeled arrows to show how melodic lines are transposed and permuted from one sequential unit to the next.

# Formal Definition
Each arrow points from a source voice to a destination voice and is labeled with a transposition (T_x). Whatever music is in the source voice is transposed by x and placed in the destination voice. Uncrossed arrows produce transpositional sequences (each voice retains its material). Crossed arrows produce canonic sequences (voices share melodic content). A three-voice sequence can have: no crossings (no canon), two crossed arrows (two-voice canon), or all three crossing (three-voice canon). The notation can use either scalar transpositions linking notes across voices, or hierarchically nested transpositions (T_x t_y) linking notes within voices.

# Musical Context
Taneyev's original notation (from his 1909 work) has been adapted here to generate new sequential units from predecessors. The notation clarifies the simple period (one set of arrows applied once) versus grand period (enough applications for voices to return to initial melodic positions). It also reveals that transpositional arrows with different subscripts technically involve invertible counterpoint. The two notational variants (cross-voice T_x arrows vs. within-voice T_x t_y arrows) are complementary: cross-voice arrows suit larger sequential units, while dual transpositions generalize more readily.

# Common Confusions
- The arrows describe how material moves from one unit to the next, not the direction of melodic motion within a unit
- Melodic-interval notation (measuring intervals between notes) and transpositional-label notation carry the same information for a given registral position but differ when octave displacements occur
