# Quick Definition

An alternative to the traditional rule of the octave that uses fauxbourdon (parallel first-inversion chords) to harmonize descending bass lines, with a metrically strong arrival on scale degree 4 rather than creating harmonic accents on weak beats.

# Core Definition

The "fauxbourdon rule of the octave" or "fauxbourdon ROTO" harmonizes descending bass lines using parallel first-inversion chords, moving *through* a weaker chord on 5-hat to a metrically strong arrival on 4-hat, which can continue downward to I via the Prinner schema (p. 332). The traditional rule of the octave calls for an applied dominant on 6-hat and dominants on 5-hat and 4-hat, but this creates harmonic accents on metrically weak beats; the fauxbourdon alternative avoids this problem (Figure 7.5.3). The idiom admits several variants: "substituting IV for ii^6^, V^2^/IV for V^6^, and, most importantly, I64 for iii^6^" (p. 332). The I64 substitution yields the I--V--IV--I chord progression of Neil Young's "Helpless," recreating a modal progression within a functional context (p. 333).

# Key Properties

1. Uses parallel first-inversion chords descending by step, with root-position chord on 4-hat
2. Avoids the metrical problems of the traditional rule of the octave
3. Permits several substitutions: IV for ii^6^, V^2^/IV for V^6^, I64 for iii^6^
4. The I64 substitution recreates the ubiquitous I--V--IV--I modal progression
5. The idiom is "perhaps the most important exception to the general principles governing harmonic cycles" (p. 331)
6. Can be reversed as "standing on the predominant," with I64 connecting predominant chords on 6-hat and 4-hat

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct the Fauxbourdon ROTO:
1. Start from the tonic (scale degree 1/8, usually high register)
2. Descend by step using first-inversion triads
3. Place a root-position chord on scale degree 4
4. Continue down to the tonic via the Prinner or similar schema
5. Optionally substitute I64 for iii^6^ on scale degree 5

# Context & Application

Classical composers often skirt past the V--IV progression by grouping chords in pairs so that V^6^--IV^6^ occurs across a phrase boundary. But they occasionally exploit it for its retrofunctional qualities, as in Brahms's G major violin sonata Op. 78 (Figure 7.5.5), "whose melodic-harmonic core would not sound out of place on a classic-rock playlist" (p. 333).

More than 90% of so-called "passing 6/4 chords" are instances of the fauxbourdon ROTO schema (Figure 7.5.7), suggesting a specific idiom rather than a general technique.
