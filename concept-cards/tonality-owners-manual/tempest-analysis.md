# Quick Definition

Tymoczko's analysis of the Tempest sonata (Op. 31, no. 2) first movement as almost entirely schematic music built from six sequential processes with ascending bass lines, demonstrating how Beethoven constructs compelling formal arguments from sequences, schemas, and contrary-motion patterns.

# Core Definition

The exposition is analyzed as six schematic processes, each featuring an ascending bass line (p. 479): (1) Ludwig-based main theme articulating three motives (arpeggio, cascade, turn); (2) ascending-step sequence in the consequent with disguised model; (3) fauxbourdon-based ascending-fifth transition; (4) nonsequential second theme (the only nonsequential harmony in the exposition); (5) registral expansion by transposition along the dyad; (6) descending-third contrary-motion return and closing Ludwig fragment. The movement is "almost entirely sequential in its harmony" (p. 472). These six processes provide "a sense of ownership that I do not get from Schenkerian analysis, allowing me to understand how actual human beings might find their way to music as complex and impressive as the Tempest" (p. 480).

# Prerequisites

- **The Ludwig** -- The primary contrary-motion schema used throughout
- **Reduction as paraphrase** -- The analytical philosophy guiding the analysis
- **Disguised model technique** -- Used in both the consequent and transition

# Key Properties

1. Six sequential/schematic processes constitute the exposition
2. Each process features an ascending bass line -- a "remorseless ratchet"
3. The ascending-step sequence runs through the entire movement across all sections
4. Three motives (arpeggio, cascade, turn) rotate throughout the exposition
5. The recapitulation simultaneously repeats and continues the sequential process
6. The upper-voice pedal at m. 189 reaches the piano's edge, embodying Kantian sublimity

# Construction / Recognition

## To Analyze Using Schematic Reduction:
1. Identify recurring sequential patterns and schemas
2. Reduce each section to its schematic process
3. Note how processes connect to form larger continuities
4. Look for the overall trajectory across sections (e.g., ascending bass)
5. Compare with Schenkerian reduction to assess which captures more musical detail

# Context & Application

The Tempest tests recent theories of form: some analysts put the main theme at m. 1, others at m. 21; the piece lacks familiar cadential markers. Tymoczko argues for a four-part exposition containing five large paragraphs (p. 474). The formal identity of sections "clarifies only gradually" -- another instance of "becoming clear only in retrospect" (p. 476). This epitomizes Beethoven's style: "ubiquitous motives, stock musical patterns, avoidance of straightforward melody, and extremes of dynamics and register" (p. 480).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 474): Opening period -- Ludwig articulating three motives (arpeggio, cascade, turn), with the Ludwig continuing across the phrase boundary.

**Example 2** (p. 476): Disguised ascending-fifth transitional sequence using fauxbourdon triads, with phrasing accelerating from four bars to two to one.

**Example 3** (p. 473): Recapitulation's upper-voice pedal at D6 -- Beethoven reaches the piano's edge, creating "unusual dissonances as the notes crunch against the piano's edge," connected to Kant's internal sublime.

**Example 4** (pp. 479-480): Six-process reductive summary -- contrasted with Schmalfeldt's Schenkerian reduction, which it is "inconceivable" one could use as a compositional starting point.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming the piece needs further reduction beyond six schematic processes
  **Correction**: "It would be quixotic to suggest that the piece would be aesthetically defective if it possessed no further unity" (p. 480)

- **Error**: Treating the ascending-step sequence as limited to one section
  **Correction**: The sequence runs through the entire movement: E-a, F-sharp-b, G-C, A-d, [B-e], C-f, C-sharp-f-sharp, D-g, E-A (p. 481)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the formal ambiguity is a problem to be resolved
  **Clarification**: "Becoming clear only in retrospect" is a compositional strategy, part of Beethoven's "playful humor" (p. 476)

- **Confusion**: Assuming Schenkerian reduction captures the piece better
  **Clarification**: Schmalfeldt's Schenkerian reduction "singling out" notes from within sequences seems implausible as a blueprint for composition
