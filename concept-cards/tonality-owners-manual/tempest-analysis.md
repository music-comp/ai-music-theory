# Quick Definition
Tymoczko's analysis of the Tempest sonata (Op. 31, no. 2) first movement as almost entirely schematic music built from six sequential processes, demonstrating how Beethoven constructs compelling formal arguments from sequences, schemas, and contrary-motion patterns rather than from harmonic complexity.

# Formal Definition
The exposition is analyzed as six schematic processes, each featuring an ascending bass line: (1) Ludwig-based main theme, (2) ascending-step sequence in the consequent, (3) fauxbourdon-based ascending-fifth transition with disguised model, (4) nonsequential second theme, (5) registral expansion by transposition along the dyad, (6) descending-third contrary-motion return and closing Ludwig fragment. The development largely repeats the transitional sequence. The recapitulation introduces expressive laments and a notable upper-voice pedal at the piano's edge. The entire movement is "almost entirely schematic, composed of a handful of sequences stitched together with a few moments of i-V-i functionality" (p. 482).

# Musical Context
The Tempest exemplifies Beethoven's style: "ubiquitous motives, stock musical patterns, avoidance of straightforward melody, and extremes of dynamics and register" (p. 480). It tests recent theories of form since it lacks familiar cadential markers. The analysis demonstrates that sequence-based reduction provides a "sense of ownership" that Schenkerian analysis does not, allowing us to understand how human beings might create such complex music.

# Common Confusions
- The analysis does not claim the piece lacks further unity -- it questions whether six familiar processes need further reduction to be aesthetically satisfying
- Schmalfeldt's Schenkerian reduction (Figure 10.3.10) singling out notes from within sequences seems implausible as a blueprint for composition
- The ascending-step sequence runs through the entire movement: exposition, development, and recapitulation (Figure 10.3.13, p. 481)
