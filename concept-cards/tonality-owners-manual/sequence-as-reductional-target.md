# Quick Definition
An analytical approach that traces irregular musical surfaces back to simpler sequential origins, treating sequences as compositional templates or middlegrounds that are distorted to produce freely-composed-sounding results.

# Formal Definition
Sequence-based reduction comes in two forms: (1) removing surface notes to reveal a shorter sequential background (as in Chopin's E minor prelude, where semitonal descents embellish a descending-fifth sequence), and (2) postulating a sequential template of similar length and removing "compositional decision points" to transform irregular surfaces into regular patterns (as in Beethoven's Op. 1 no. 3). The technique of the "disguised model" moves from obscurity toward clarity as music progresses. This approach reflects the belief that much music is covertly repetitive even where it does not initially appear so.

# Musical Context
Chopin's E minor prelude embellishes a descending-fifth seventh-chord middleground with chromatic descents traversing the edges of a four-dimensional hypercube. Beethoven's Op. 1 no. 3 variations movement derives its theme from the circle-of-fifths sequence (diatonic third's basic voice leading), with a new bass creating primary-triad harmonies. The third variation reveals the completely sequential structure -- progressive analysis stripping away decoration. This contrasts with Schenkerian reduction, which consigns sequential structure to the surface in favor of nonsequential middlegrounds.

# Common Confusions
- This is not the same as Schenkerian reduction: sequence-based reduction postulates regular templates of similar length, not shorter backgrounds
- The sequential middleground and the Schenkerian middleground are complementary, not competing truths: functional tonality features both covert sequential organization and longer-range stepwise connections
- Disguised repetition as a compositional technique is distinct from sequence as a theoretical reduction
