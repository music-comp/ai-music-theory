# Quick Definition

An analytical approach tracing irregular musical surfaces back to simpler sequential origins, treating sequences as compositional templates or middlegrounds that are distorted to produce freely-composed-sounding results. Two forms: removing notes to reveal shorter backgrounds, or removing "compositional decision points" from same-length templates.

# Core Definition

Sequence-based reduction comes in two forms (pp. 198-201): (1) removing surface notes to reveal a shorter sequential background (Chopin's E minor prelude, where semitonal descents embellish descending-fifth seventh chords); (2) postulating a sequential template of similar length and removing "compositional decision points" to transform irregular surfaces into regular patterns (Beethoven's Op. 1 no. 3, where a circle-of-fifths sequence is distorted). "Rather than imagining sequences as deviant, we can reconceive nonsequential functionality with reference to a fundamentally repetitive norm" (p. 202). This contrasts with Schenkerian analysis, which "consigns sequential structure to the surface, as decorating a fundamentally nonsequential middleground" (p. 202).

# Key Properties

1. Two forms: note-removal (Chopin) and decision-point-removal (Beethoven)
2. Postulates sequences as middlegrounds, not surfaces
3. "Sequence-based reduction reflects the belief that a great deal of music is covertly repetitive" (p. 201)
4. Contrasts with Schenkerian reduction which treats sequences as surface decoration
5. Both perspectives may contain complementary truths
6. The sequential and Schenkerian middlegrounds are not mutually exclusive

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply:
1. Identify passages with near-sequential regularity
2. Hypothesize a regular sequential template
3. Determine how the surface deviates from the template
4. Ask: could the composer have started with the regular pattern and distorted it?

# Context & Application

Chopin's E minor prelude: descending-fifth seventh-chord middleground embellished with chromatic descent traversing a four-dimensional hypercube (Figure 4.10.1). Beethoven's Op. 1 no. 3: circle-of-fifths sequence with new bass creating primary-triad harmonies; third variation reveals completely sequential structure (Figures 4.10.2-6). "The challenge is to balance these vital but seemingly incompatible perspectives" -- sequential regularity and Schenkerian stepwise connections (p. 202).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming sequence-based reduction and Schenkerian reduction are incompatible
  **Correction**: They may represent "complementary truths" about the same music

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking sequence-based reduction removes notes like Schenkerian reduction
  **Clarification**: The Beethoven form removes "compositional decision points" from a template of similar length, not notes from a shorter background
