# Quick Definition

Repeating chord progressions where voices connect nontrivially across the repeat boundary, creating an illusion of endless descent while remaining stationary -- the harmonic analogue of Shepard tones. Term coined by David Feurzeig.

# Core Definition

A Shepard-tone passacaglia is a progression "in which voices connect nontrivially across the repeat, as when soprano becomes alto, alto tenor, and so on: here, what initially seems like repetition can also function as continuation" (p. 66). The effect arises whenever a complete loop on the spiral transposes each voice down by one chordal step. The canonic lines are "constructed by listeners -- supported by rich, strummed guitar chords that suggest but do not completely determine specific voices" (p. 64). The stepwise motions "are really there in the music, and can be identified even by the casual listener" (p. 65).

# Key Properties

1. Voices connect nontrivially across the repeat: soprano becomes alto, alto becomes tenor, etc.
2. Repetition simultaneously functions as continuation
3. Embeds a hidden canon or round endlessly descending by step
4. The canon is constructed by listeners, not articulated by specific instruments
5. Pervasive in rock music across all four loop families
6. In classical music, voice leading is notated; in rock, it is partially audience-constructed

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify:
1. Find a repeating chord progression
2. Trace each voice through the progression and across the repeat
3. Check if soprano at the repeat connects to alto (or the next-lowest voice) of the new cycle
4. If voices shift down one chordal step across the repeat, it is a Shepard-tone passacaglia

# Context & Application

The endless tumbling descent -- "moving always forward yet also staying the same" -- functions as "an apt representation of helplessness, capturing the persistence of memory alongside inexorable progression of time" (p. 66). It explains why listeners are "inclined to listen to these three harmonies over and over, always progressing yet never reaching a conclusion" (p. 66).

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 64-65, Figures 2.5.1-2.5.3): "Helpless" -- D4 in vocal connects to D4 in guitar, which descends to C#, B, A, embedding a three-voice continuously descending canon.

**Example 2** (p. 66, Figure 2.5.5): "You Won't See Me" -- main melody and background vocals explicitly articulate a continuous descending scale.

**Example 3** (p. 66, Figure 2.5.4): "Eight Days a Week" -- analogous canon with voices entering every two measures.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Expecting to hear the canon explicitly in the recording
  **Correction**: The canon is constructed by listeners connecting common tones and stepwise motions; it is implicit, not explicit

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking all repeating progressions are Shepard-tone passacaglias
  **Clarification**: Only those where voices connect nontrivially across the repeat (i.e., a complete loop on the spiral)
