# Quick Definition
Repeating chord progressions in which voices connect nontrivially across the repeat, creating an illusion of endless descent (or ascent) while remaining stationary -- the harmonic analogue of Shepard tones.

# Formal Definition
A Shepard-tone passacaglia is a repeating chord progression where what initially seems like repetition also functions as continuation, because the voices continue their trajectories across the repeat boundary. Soprano becomes alto, alto becomes tenor, and so on, embedding a hidden canon or round that endlessly descends (or ascends) by step. The term was coined by David Feurzeig. These progressions arise whenever a complete loop on the spiral diagram returns to the starting chord, transposing each voice down by one chordal step.

# Musical Context
Shepard-tone passacaglias are pervasive in rock music and can be found in all four loop families analyzed in Chapter 2. They represent a key expressive device: the endless, tumbling descent -- always progressing yet staying the same -- can function as a powerful representation of emotional states like helplessness or longing. The voice leading in rock is partially *constructed by listeners* from strummed guitar chords, unlike classical music where voice leading is embodied in notation.

# Examples
- Neil Young's "Helpless": D4 in vocal connects to D4 in guitar, which descends to C#, B, A, then shifts up by octave to G4 -- embedding a three-voice continuously descending canon (Figures 2.5.1-2.5.3)
- Beatles' "Eight Days a Week": analogous canon (Figure 2.5.4)
- Beatles' "You Won't See Me": main melody and background vocals explicitly articulate a continuous descending scale (Figure 2.5.5)
- The "fundamental canon of rock": all three main loop families are identical under rotation of the four-chord sequence D-A-E-G, each producing an equivalent descending canon (Figure 2.5.8)

# Common Confusions
- Unlike a traditional round, the canon is not articulated by actual instruments but *constructed by listeners* who connect common tones and stepwise motions
- The stepwise motions are "really there in the music" and identifiable by casual listeners, even though the canonic structure is implicit
- Not all repeating progressions are Shepard-tone passacaglias -- only those where voices connect nontrivially across the repeat
