# Quick Definition
The transformation of a complex instrumental texture into a chorale-like arrangement, rationalizing anomalous notes by postulating multiple voices.

# Formal Definition
Textural reduction converts arpeggiated or otherwise complex textures into chorale-like backgrounds. In its simplest form ("de-arpeggiation"), it verticalizes an arpeggiated texture to form a chorale containing the same pitches -- the inverse of "structured arpeggiation." A more sophisticated form postulates surface voices moving along backgrounds that are abstract and scale-like, using the chords-as-scales approach.

# Musical Context
Textural reduction is commonly applied to Alberti-bass patterns, arpeggiated accompaniments, and other figurated textures. It serves a syntactic purpose by rationalizing notes that might otherwise appear anomalous. However, many idiomatic passages resist clean de-arpeggiation without producing forbidden parallel fifths or octaves.

# Common Confusions
- Not all arpeggiated textures can be cleanly de-arpeggiated into well-behaved chorale textures. Some produce forbidden parallels when verticalized, which suggests composers treated accompaniment as "nonspecific accompanimental stuff" rather than as concrete polyphonic voices.
- Earlier composers likely learned these idioms as routines before noticing they could be said to contain forbidden parallels, much as English speakers accept the dummy subject in "it's raining."
