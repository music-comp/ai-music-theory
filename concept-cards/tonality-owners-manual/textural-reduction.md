# Quick Definition

The transformation of a complex instrumental texture into a chorale-like arrangement, rationalizing anomalous notes by postulating multiple voices. In its simplest form ("de-arpeggiation"), it verticalizes an arpeggiated texture to form a chorale containing the same pitches -- the inverse of structured arpeggiation.

# Core Definition

Textural reduction "can also serve a syntactic purpose, rationalizing anomalous notes by postulating multiple voices" (p. 204). Simple de-arpeggiation "converts an arpeggiated texture into a chorale containing the very same pitches; it is the inverse of what Section 4.8 called 'structured arpeggiation'" (p. 204). A more sophisticated form "postulates surface voices moving along backgrounds that are more abstract and scale-like" (p. 205) -- the chords-as-scales approach. Figure P5.3 contrasts two approaches to Bach's G major prelude: one postulating a three-voice chorale, the other a melodic pattern moving along a small shifting scale. Many idiomatic passages resist clean de-arpeggiation, producing forbidden parallels (Figures P5.4-6, pp. 205-206).

# Prerequisites

- **Nonharmonic Reduction** — The simpler reduction that textural reduction extends

# Key Properties

1. Converts arpeggiated textures into chorale-like backgrounds
2. Simple form: de-arpeggiation (inverse of structured arpeggiation)
3. Sophisticated form: chords-as-scales (abstract scalar backgrounds)
4. Many idiomatic passages resist clean de-arpeggiation (produce parallels)
5. Composers did not always think of arpeggiated textures as representing chorales
6. Eighteenth-century musicians recognized a class of "accompanimental stuff" distinct from polyphony

# Construction / Recognition

## To Perform:
1. Identify arpeggiated or figurated patterns
2. Simple: verticalize into simultaneous chords
3. Check for forbidden parallels in the result
4. If parallels arise, consider the chords-as-scales alternative
5. Postulate concrete soprano and bass with abstract middle voices

# Context & Application

Mozart's Alberti-bass patterns sometimes produce parallel fifths when verticalized (Figure P5.4). Classical IV-I progressions frequently suggest parallel octaves with the melody, "closer to rule than exception" (p. 205). A composer who genuinely imagined Alberti patterns as concrete voices "simply would not write the music in Figure P5.4, as it is clearly and obviously defective from that point of view" (p. 205). Schema theorists argue this gap is explained by how composers learned their trade -- as practice, not theory.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming all arpeggiated textures can be cleanly verticalized
  **Correction**: Many produce forbidden parallels, suggesting composers did not always think of accompaniment as concrete polyphony

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking parallels in de-arpeggiation indicate compositional errors
  **Clarification**: Composers "did write such passages, frequently and, one presumes, without guilt" (p. 206); the issue is our analytical model, not their practice
