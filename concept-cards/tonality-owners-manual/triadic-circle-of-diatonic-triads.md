# Quick Definition

The 3-in-7 spiral diagram representing diatonic triads, where the line of transposition winds three times with seven equally spaced points. Unlike the chromatic triadic circle, adjacent chords here are close both contrapuntally (single-step voice leading) and harmonically (sharing two of three notes).

# Core Definition

The spiral diagram for diatonic triads has its line of transposition winding three times around the circle with seven equally spaced points (Figure 3.4.1, p. 113). The basic voice leading is T_{-5}t_2 (equivalently T_2t_{-1}): the root descends by one diatonic step, lowering the center of gravity by 1/3. Iterating produces a chain of thirds: (C, E, G) -> (B, E, G) -> (B, D, G) -> (B, D, F) and so on, reaching descending-step transposition at every third iteration (p. 113). "The circle of diatonic triads is special insofar as it encodes a kind of harmonic similarity as well: adjacent chords on the circle are close both contrapuntally (linked by single-step voice leading) and harmonically (sharing two of three notes)" (p. 114). The primary triads I, IV, V divide the circle into arcs of 2, 3, and 2, forming maximally distinct harmonic poles (p. 117).

# Key Properties

1. Three loops for a three-note chord; seven points for the diatonic scale
2. Adjacent chords share two of three notes (harmonic similarity)
3. Adjacent chords are linked by single-step voice leading (contrapuntal proximity)
4. The basic voice leading (T_{-5}t_2) lowers center of gravity by 1/3 of a step
5. Primary triads I, IV, V nearly evenly divide the circle (arcs of 2, 3, 2)
6. Clockwise motion produces descending melodic steps correlated with retrofunctional progressions
7. The same graph represents any three-note chord in any seven-note scale

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct:
1. Draw a spiral winding three times around a circle
2. Place seven equally spaced points along the spiral
3. Label with scale tones; each point represents a complete triad
4. Clockwise step applies T_{-5}t_2

## To Recognize in Music:
1. Look for three-voice passages with consistent clockwise or counterclockwise motion
2. Count steps on the circle to determine root progression type
3. Two steps = fifth motion; three steps = step transposition

# Context & Application

The triadic circle reveals how melody and harmony constrain each other. Descending melodic motion produces clockwise steps tending toward retrofunctional progressions (ascending thirds, ascending fifths), creating tension with functional norms. Renaissance "generalized fauxbourdon" uses short clockwise motions freely; functional composers restrict to harmonically acceptable options (Figure 3.4.9, p. 117). The diatonic analogue of chromatic third substitution preserves harmonic quality, unlike chromatic substitution (Figure 3.4.2, p. 114). There is an interesting dualistic symmetry between V-I and IV-I fifth-progressions (Figure 3.4.10, p. 117).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming descending melodic steps always produce functional progressions
  **Correction**: On the triadic circle, descending steps produce retrofunctional progressions; functionality requires restricting to certain options

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Conflating contrapuntal proximity with harmonic similarity in chromatic vs. diatonic contexts
  **Clarification**: In chromatic triadic space, contrapuntally close chords (C and E major) can be harmonically distant; in diatonic space, contrapuntal and harmonic proximity coincide (p. 114)
