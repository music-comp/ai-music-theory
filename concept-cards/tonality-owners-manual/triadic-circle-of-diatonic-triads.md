# Quick Definition
The 3-in-7 spiral diagram representing diatonic triads, where the line of transposition winds three times around the circle with seven equally spaced points. Adjacent chords are connected by single-step voice leading and share two of three notes.

# Formal Definition
The basic voice leading for the triadic circle is T_-5 t_2 (equivalently T_2 t_-1): the root descends by one diatonic step, lowering the center of gravity by 1/3. Iterating produces a chain of thirds connected by single-step voice leading: (C, E, G) -> (B, E, G) -> (B, D, G) -> (B, D, F) and so on. Unlike the chromatic triadic circle, the diatonic circle encodes harmonic similarity: adjacent chords share two of three notes, making diatonic "third substitution" harmonically preserving. The primary triads I, IV, V divide the circle into arcs of 2, 3, and 2, maximally distinct harmonic poles.

# Musical Context
The triadic circle reveals how melody and harmony constrain each other in three-voice counterpoint. Descending melodic motion produces clockwise steps that tend toward retrofunctional progressions (ascending thirds, ascending fifths), creating a tension with functional norms. Renaissance "generalized fauxbourdon" uses short clockwise motions freely; functional composers must restrict themselves to harmonically acceptable options. The circle can model music from Ockeghem's descending 6-5 sequences to Israel Kamakawiwo'ole's "Over the Rainbow."

# Common Confusions
- The diatonic triadic circle links harmonic and contrapuntal similarity (adjacent chords share notes), unlike the chromatic triadic circle where contrapuntally close chords (e.g., C major and E major) can be harmonically distant
- Descending melodic steps produce retrofunctional progressions on this circle, opposite to what happens with dyads
