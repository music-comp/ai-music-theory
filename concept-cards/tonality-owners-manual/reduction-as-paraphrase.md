# Quick Definition
A reconception of musical reduction not as revealing hidden prolongational structure, but as constructing speculative blueprints -- simplified versions of a passage that are simple enough to imagine inventing, yet rich enough to imagine expanding into the finished piece.

# Formal Definition
Reduction as paraphrase treats analytical reduction as a hypothesis about the longer strategic goals animating a passage, rather than a claim that less-important material "represents" or "prolongs" more-important material. A good reduction is "an analytical way station: simple enough that we can imagine ourselves inventing it, and yet rich enough that we can imagine expanding it into the finished piece" (p. 411). This approach reconceives the Schenkerian "background" in scale-theoretic terms, with surface voices moving along abstract alphabets (subchord, chordal, scalar, chromatic) nested within each other.

# Musical Context
This approach allows the benefits of Schenkerian analysis while jettisoning its problematic metaphysics. It permits sequences, schemas, and contrary-motion patterns as legitimate reductive targets, rather than insisting all reductions terminate in stepwise voice-leading chorales. The ontology of the reductive target also differs: it yields scalar backgrounds with abstract "voices" available in every octave, rather than specific pitches.

# Examples
- Beethoven's Op. 109 variations theme analyzed as near sequence by contrary motion, with sequences as atomic reductive units (Figures 9.4.1-9.4.5, pp. 426-428)
- The Tempest exposition reduced to six schematic processes, each a recognizable pattern that could guide improvisation (Figure 10.3.9, pp. 479-480)
- Contrast with Schenkerian reductions that flatten personal vocabulary into generic patterns (e.g., Schenker's analysis of Op. 22 minuet, Figure 9.4.10, p. 431)

# Common Confusions
- Reduction as paraphrase does not claim that reductions are arbitrary -- good reductions must be musically plausible blueprints
- Unlike Schenkerian reduction, sequences and voice exchanges can be analytical end-states requiring no further reduction
- The approach is "insufficiently hierarchical" from a Schenkerian perspective but "more hierarchical" in recognizing the ontological difference between chord and scale
