# Quick Definition

A hierarchical structure in which surface voices move inside chords that move inside scales that are themselves moving through chromatic space, with analogous transformations (transposition, inversion) occurring at each level.

# Core Definition

The quadruple hierarchy describes Western music as having four nested levels: (1) voice in chord, (2) chord in scale, (3) scale in chromatic aggregate, and (4) chromatic space itself. At each level, transposition and inversion are available. Motion at different levels produces different phenomena: "motion of scale within aggregate produces modulation, motion of chord within scale produces voice leading, motion of voice within chord produces motivic development, chordal inversion, and what I call 'structured arpeggiation'" (p. 15). The hierarchy forms a "recursive architecture that will be more familiar to computer scientists than to music theorists, yet it is one that every musician intuitively grasps" (p. 15). Particularly important is "the technique of counteracting, or nearly counteracting, an operation at one level with its analogue at another" (p. 15).

# Key Properties

1. Four nested levels: voice in chord, chord in scale, scale in aggregate, chromatic space
2. The same operations (transposition, inversion) are available at each level
3. Each collection maps its degrees to those of the enclosing collection
4. The number of levels can vary (hence also "collectional hierarchy")
5. Counteracting operations at different levels produces musically significant results
6. Chords can function as small scales and vice versa

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Hierarchical Motion:
1. Determine the levels active in a passage (chordal, scalar, chromatic)
2. Identify motion at each level (e.g., melody moving within a chord, chord moving within a scale)
3. Check whether operations at different levels counteract each other
4. Describe the passage as a combination of motions at different hierarchical levels

# Context & Application

The quadruple hierarchy is one of the central themes of the entire book (noted in the Preface, p. xii). It extends "chordal set theory" beyond chromatic and diatonic set theory to govern the motion of voices within chords. One obstacle is the seeming ontological gulf between chord and scale; Tymoczko argues this distinction is functional rather than inherent -- "in twentieth-century languages such as impressionism or jazz, seven-note collections can act as concrete chords" while "smaller collections can act like scalar fields of play" (p. 12).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 10, Figure 1.2.1): Beethoven's Op. 54, II, mm. 38-41 -- right-hand melody moves sequentially along the top three notes of the omnibus pattern, as if they formed a three-note scale.

**Example 2** (p. 11, Figure 1.2.2): Beatles' "Eight Days a Week" -- melody returns to same pitch but different scale degree, with melody and harmony "canceling out" in countervailing motion on two hierarchical levels.

**Example 3** (p. 12, Figure 1.2.4): Lusitano's "Heu me Domine" (c. 1553) -- triadic background ascends semitonally while concrete voices descend to stay within registral bounds, creating a Shepard-tone-like illusion.

**Example 4** (p. 14, Figure 1.2.6): The quadruple hierarchy visualized with chromatic, scalar, and chordal degrees numbered so that middle C is 60.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming chords and scales are ontologically distinct categories
  **Correction**: The distinction is functional, not structural; any collection can serve as either chord or scale

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the hierarchy always has exactly four levels
  **Clarification**: The number of levels can vary (hence "collectional hierarchy"); sometimes there are fewer or more

- **Confusion**: Believing it is impossible to transpose notes outside the relevant collection
  **Clarification**: Nonchord tones can be treated as neighbors or passing tones relative to chord tones, allowing transposition along any collection (Figure 1.2.3)
