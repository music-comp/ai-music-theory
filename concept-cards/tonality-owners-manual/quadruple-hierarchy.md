# Quick Definition
A hierarchical structure in which surface voices move inside chords that move inside scales that are themselves moving through chromatic space, with analogous transformations occurring at each level.

# Formal Definition
The quadruple hierarchy (also called the "collectional hierarchy") describes Western music as having four nested levels: (1) voice in chord, (2) chord in scale, (3) scale in chromatic aggregate, and (4) chromatic space itself. At each level, the same operations -- transposition and inversion -- are available. Motion at different levels gives rise to different phenomena: modulation (scale within aggregate), voice leading (chord within scale), and motivic development/structured arpeggiation (voice within chord). The hierarchy forms a recursive architecture where each collection maps its degrees to those of the enclosing collection.

# Musical Context
The quadruple hierarchy is one of the central themes of the entire book. It opens a new analytical project of determining the precise combination of background and foreground motion that reproduces a given passage. It extends "chordal set theory" beyond chromatic set theory and diatonic set theory to govern the motion of voices within chords. Particularly important is the technique of counteracting an operation at one level with its analogue at another.

# Common Confusions
- Chords and scales are not ontologically distinct -- in twentieth-century languages like impressionism or jazz, seven-note collections can act as concrete chords, while smaller collections can act like scalar fields
- The hierarchy does not always have exactly four levels; the number can vary (hence also "collectional hierarchy")
- Transposing along a chord can apply to notes *outside* the chord by treating nonchord tones as neighbors or passing tones relative to chord tones (Figure 1.2.3)
