# Quick Definition
The removal of nonharmonic notes from a musical passage to reveal an underlying "harmonic skeleton" of chord tones.

# Formal Definition
Nonharmonic reduction is the simplest form of analytical reduction, in which nonharmonic tones (passing tones, neighbors, suspensions, anticipations) are eliminated to leave behind a skeleton containing only chord tones. It is a kind of syntactic analysis analogous to parsing a sentence into its component clauses, dividing music into "structural" harmonies decorated by nonharmonic tones in stereotypical ways.

# Musical Context
Nonharmonic reduction is most appropriate for styles with strict policies regarding harmonic content and melodic embellishment, such as Palestrina and Bach. It is the foundation of traditional harmonic analysis and a pedagogical tool for helping students understand earlier music. It works much better in theory than in practice, since even strict styles present ambiguities.

# Examples
- Figure P5.1: Bach's Partita in B-flat, BWV 825, opening of the first Minuet -- two equally plausible readings (intervallic parallel tenths vs. chordal parallel triadic arpeggiations) demonstrate that nonharmonic reduction can be fundamentally interpretive
- In Palestrina's music, a computer can perform reasonably accurate nonharmonic reduction, leaving behind a triadic skeleton (Ch. 5, section 1)

# Common Confusions
- Nonharmonic reduction is often assumed to be an objective, mechanical process yielding a single correct answer. Tymoczko argues it is fundamentally interpretive, producing "partially true models of complex and ambiguous musical surfaces."
- The existence of a reducible surface does not mean reduction is analytically or aesthetically advisable -- syntax may allow for a reduction that misrepresents the music's semantics.
