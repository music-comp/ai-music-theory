# Quick Definition

The view that musical grammars are best understood not as exceptionless laws but as interpretive summaries of statistical tendencies -- sorting musical behaviors into "standard" or "unusual" to varying degrees, where standardness comes in degrees rather than binary categories.

# Core Definition

Musical grammars "aspire to provide a useful description of musical behavior as being 'standard' or 'unusual' to varying degrees" (p. 573). The word "interpretive" serves a double function: first, "it registers the reality that we can construct multiple theories accounting for the same set of observable facts" (underdetermination); second, "it points to the holistic nature of the grammatical enterprise" where harmonic labeling, nonharmonic-tone identification, and harmonic theorizing are interdependent (p. 573). Unlike Chomskian grammar, where frequency and grammaticality can diverge through semantics, music may lack the semantic dimension that supports this distinction.

# Prerequisites

- **Corpus analysis methodology** -- The empirical methods that generate the data grammars summarize

# Key Properties

1. Grammars sort behaviors into "standard" or "unusual" along a continuum
2. Multiple theories can account for the same observations (underdetermination)
3. Analysis is holistic: harmonic labeling depends on harmonic theory and vice versa
4. Music may lack the semantic dimension that separates frequency from grammaticality in language
5. The grammars are "largely true generalizations" rather than exceptionless laws
6. Classical music is no longer a living tradition, limiting our ability to adjudicate grammaticality

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct an Interpretive Summary:
1. Analyze a corpus of music using a consistent analytical framework
2. Identify statistical regularities and tendencies
3. Present these as a grammar of what is standard vs. unusual in the style
4. Acknowledge that alternative analytical frameworks might produce different summaries
5. Release data and code for others to critique and reproduce
6. Treat exceptions as expected rather than as threats to the grammar

# Context & Application

This metatheoretical position justifies the book's approach of treating traditional harmonic theory as "largely true generalizations." Grammars serve practical functions: for analysts, directing attention toward genuinely exceptional features; for students, suggesting what might come next; for theorists, pointing toward hypotheses about why practice developed as it did; for cognitive scientists, hinting at mental representations constituting stylistic competence (p. 573).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 572): Root-position I-V-IV-I is "common in rock and rare in classical music" but hearing it in classical music produces "mild surprise rather than genuine perplexity" -- unlike the incomprehensibility of unfinished linguistic utterances.

**Example 2** (p. 572): "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" is rare but grammatical because semantics supports comprehension; whether analogous musical distinctions exist is unclear.

**Example 3** (p. 572): Hypothetical mock-classical compositions featuring I-ii-I6 progressions would tell us "less about the classical style than about the contemporary theoretical community."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating musical grammar as analogous to Chomskian linguistic grammar
  **Correction**: Music may lack the semantic dimension separating frequency from grammaticality

- **Error**: Expecting a musical grammar to be exceptionless
  **Correction**: "Sometimes exceptions happen" -- grammars are generalizations, not laws

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking "interpretive" means "arbitrary"
  **Clarification**: Grammars must account for observable statistical patterns; "interpretive" refers to the existence of multiple valid theoretical frameworks

- **Confusion**: Assuming theory-dependent analysis makes corpus study useless
  **Clarification**: The circularity is not vicious; open data allows others to test alternative frameworks
