# Quick Definition
The view that musical grammars are best understood not as exceptionless laws (like Chomskian linguistic grammar) but as interpretive summaries of statistical tendencies -- sorting musical behaviors into "standard" or "unusual" to varying degrees, where standardness comes in degrees rather than binary categories.

# Formal Definition
Musical grammars aspire to provide "a useful description of musical behavior as being 'standard' or 'unusual' to varying degrees" (p. 573). Unlike linguistic grammar, where "grammaticality" can be separated from frequency through semantics (rare but grammatical sentences are still comprehensible), music may lack the semantic dimension supporting such a distinction. Without a living community of "native speakers," the question of what is grammatical in a dead musical style reduces largely to questions of frequency. The grammars are "interpretive" in two senses: multiple theories can account for the same data (underdetermination), and the enterprise is holistic (analysis depends on the very theory being tested).

# Musical Context
This metatheoretical position justifies the book's approach of treating traditional harmonic theory as "largely true generalizations" rather than exceptionless laws. It acknowledges that "sometimes exceptions happen" without invalidating the grammar. The approach simultaneously bolsters traditional theories (nonharmonicity, descending-third arrangement of triads, significance of sequences) and identifies their limits (schema-theoretical recognition that music is fundamentally idiomatic).

# Examples
- The linguistic analogy: "colorless green ideas sleep furiously" is rare but grammatical because semantics supports comprehension; root-position I-V-IV-I is common in rock and rare in classical, but hearing it in classical music produces "mild surprise rather than genuine perplexity" (p. 572)
- The I-ii-I6 progression: virtually absent from Bach and Mozart, yet periodically found in theory texts (pp. 569, 572)
- The hypothetical of contemporary composers writing mock-classical music with I-ii-I6: this would tell us "less about the classical style than about the contemporary theoretical community" (p. 572)

# Common Confusions
- Musical grammar is not the same as linguistic grammar -- music may lack the semantic dimension that separates frequency from grammaticality in language
- "Interpretive" does not mean "arbitrary" -- grammars must account for observable statistical patterns
- The holistic interdependence of analysis and theory does not make the enterprise viciously circular, but it does mean theory-neutral datasets may be impossible
