# Quick Definition

A metaphor describing how the harmonic vocabulary of functional tonality first tightens (from baroque through classical) and then loosens (through the nineteenth century), with Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven representing the point of maximal harmonic regimentation.

# Core Definition

The functional tradition "is like an hourglass, with Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven the point of maximal harmonic regimentation" (p. 315, Figure 7.1.7). Early functional music (baroque) has a relatively wide harmonic vocabulary including vi--V, nonsequential iii, and other penumbral progressions. During the classical period, harmonic practice congeals to its most restricted state. In the nineteenth century, the vocabulary expands again, "leading to an increasing use of ct^o7^ and ii^ø7^ and the recovery of iii" (p. 315).

# Prerequisites

- **Harmonic cycle theory** -- The grammar whose scope changes historically
- **Functional penumbra** -- The variable zone that widens and narrows

# Key Properties

1. Baroque: wider harmonic vocabulary including vi--V, nonsequential iii
2. Classical (Haydn/Mozart/Beethoven): maximal restriction -- narrowest penumbra
3. Nineteenth century: expansion with ct^o7^, ii^ø7^, recovery of iii
4. The core grammar remains stable while the penumbra changes
5. Rock and pop show modal asymmetries not found in functional tonality (e.g., VI--VII--i common in aeolian but rare in ionian)
6. Minor mode retains greater attraction to the mediant throughout all periods

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply the Hourglass Model:
1. Identify the historical period of the music
2. Check whether the harmonic vocabulary matches the expected width of the penumbra
3. Note whether progressions that would be anomalous in one period are normal in another
4. Be cautious about applying classical-era norms to baroque or Romantic music

# Context & Application

The hourglass model explains why applying a single rigid harmonic grammar to all periods produces inaccurate results. It also explains the minor mode's persistent fondness for the mediant -- not as a deviation from an established default but as a pre-existing modal attraction that "was never fully abandoned" (p. 200), with III remaining the primary harmonization of bass 3-hat until well into the seventeenth century.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Applying Mozart-era harmonic norms to all functional music
  **Correction**: The classical era is the point of *maximal* restriction; baroque and Romantic music have wider vocabularies

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the hourglass implies classical music is "purer" or "better"
  **Clarification**: It simply describes a historical pattern of restriction and expansion -- no period is normative
