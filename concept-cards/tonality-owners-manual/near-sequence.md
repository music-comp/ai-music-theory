# Quick Definition

A passage exhibiting repetition that is disguised or inexact, where the impression of repetition persists despite small alterations in the unit, transposition, or permutation. Near sequences occupy a continuum from strict sequence to free composition.

# Core Definition

Near sequences encompass several subtypes (Ch. 4, Sections 8-9): (1) variable sequences where the transpositional interval, unit, or permutation changes slightly (Figure 4.9.1, p. 193); (2) passages with eliminated sequential units creating surprise (Figure 4.9.2, p. 194); (3) passages repeating one progression within a two-chord alternation (Figure 4.9.3, p. 195). They also include purely melodic sequences (transposing patterns within a nonsequential harmonic background), structured arpeggiation, and "transformationally restricted passages" that draw from a small collection of related transformations (Figure 4.9.6, p. 196). "Like most musical laws, the law of sequence is flexible, with the impression of repetition persisting despite small alterations here and there" (p. 188).

# Prerequisites

- **Repeating Contrapuntal Pattern** — The strict pattern that near sequences deviate from

# Key Properties

1. Impression of repetition persists despite inexactness
2. Three types of alteration: unit, transposition, permutation
3. Variable sequences change transpositional interval or compress units
4. Eliminated units create surprise within an ongoing process
5. Chord repetition generates consecutive fifths or thirds from alternating patterns
6. A continuum from strict sequence to free composition

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify:
1. Look for passages with approximate but not exact repetition
2. Check for changes in transpositional interval between iterations
3. Look for "missing" iterations that would complete a regular pattern
4. Check for repeated chords within a two-chord alternation

# Context & Application

The Pathetique middle section (Figure P4.3, p. 153) embeds the ascending basic voice leading in nonsequential triads. Louise Farrenc's Op. 19 combines variable transposition and unit compression (Figure 4.9.1). Mozart K.279 development has an eliminated unit creating a "glitch" (Figure 4.9.2). Ellington's "Satin Doll" uses chord repetition within an ascending 5-6 pattern (Figure 4.9.4). The concept locates sequences within a "broader class of transformationally restricted passages" (p. 196), suggesting a continuum determined by "which features of contrapuntal structure are held fixed" (p. 196).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Dismissing near sequences as sloppy composition
  **Correction**: They are sophisticated techniques; "some of the most sophisticated examples of musical patternmaking are irregular to some degree" (p. 188)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking sequences must be exact
  **Clarification**: The law of sequence is flexible; near sequences are the norm, not the exception, in much repertoire
