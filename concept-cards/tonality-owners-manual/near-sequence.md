# Quick Definition
A passage exhibiting repetition that is disguised or inexact, where the impression of repetition persists despite small alterations in the unit, transposition, or permutation.

# Formal Definition
Near sequences encompass several subtypes: (1) variable sequences where the transpositional interval, unit, or permutation changes slightly; (2) passages with eliminated sequential units creating surprise; (3) passages repeating one progression within a two-chord alternation. They also include purely melodic sequences (transposing patterns within a non-sequential harmonic background), structured arpeggiation, and transformationally restricted passages that draw from a small collection of related transformations. Near sequences occupy a continuum from strict sequence to free composition.

# Musical Context
The *Pathetique* middle section (mm. 79-86) presents a near sequence stating a brief melody on Eb, Db, and Ab, embedding the diatonic third's ascending basic voice leading. More broadly, disguised repetition is a defining characteristic of functional tonality, with the impression of repetition persisting despite alterations. Beethoven's Op. 1 no. 3 theme is a quasi-regular structure derived from sequential origins. Analysts have not always been sensitive to the full range of near sequences, suggesting that regularity is a rigid norm when in fact the law of sequence is flexible.

# Common Confusions
- Near sequences are not failed or sloppy sequences; they are sophisticated compositional techniques that use inexactness expressively
- Variable sequences can change the transpositional level, the unit size, or the permutation -- any of the three parameters of the sequence model
