# Quick Definition
Movement along the spiral path itself, corresponding to transposition along the larger scale-like collection, shifting the chord's notes by the same number of scale steps.

# Formal Definition
Sliding along the spiral corresponds to transposition along the enclosing scale (T_y). Clockwise motion descends and counterclockwise motion ascends. One needs to move n slices clockwise to transpose an n-note chord down by one scale step. A slide is the first of three fundamental operations on the spiral diagram. Every path on the spiral can be decomposed into a combination of slides and loops, with the total angular motion equaling the sum of intervals moved by all voices.

# Musical Context
Sliding is the most straightforward operation on the spiral, representing parallel transposition of all chord tones by the same scale interval. For the 3-in-12 major triad diagram, a one-step clockwise slide (**T**_{-1}) transposes all notes down by one chromatic semitone. Slides are musically important because they represent the chromatic sequences that are fundamental to both classical and rock harmony.

# Common Confusions
- Sliding is not the only way to move on the spiral -- loops and radial motions are equally important
- A slide by itself does not produce efficient voice leading between nearby chords; that requires a combination of slide and loop
- The direction convention (clockwise = descending) is a notational choice, not a mathematical necessity
