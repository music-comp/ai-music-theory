# Quick Definition
A "thin," relatively theory-neutral approach to harmonic analysis that uses Roman numerals to refer to collections of scale degrees rather than encoding claims about a chord's function, behavior, or essence.

# Formal Definition
Scale-degree analysis interprets Roman numerals as referring to tertian structures over a bass. A symbol like "I6" means "the 63 sonority over scale degree 3^" or "scale degrees 1^, 3^, and 5^ with 3^ in the bass." This approach is relatively modern and contrasts with functional analysis, which uses "thick," theory-laden symbols encoding the analyst's impression of a chord's function, origin, or essence. Scale-degree analysis is largely equivalent to figured-bass symbols and provides a common vocabulary for discussing disagreements about perceived function.

# Musical Context
Tymoczko defaults to scale-degree analysis throughout his book for three reasons: (1) it allows exploration of how collections like iii6 gradually change their function over centuries; (2) it provides a shared vocabulary for expressing disagreement about perceived function; (3) it leads to greater methodological clarity, since claims like "dominants proceed to tonics" become testable by checking whether scale degrees 5, 7, and 2 typically proceed to scale degrees 1, 3, and 5.

# Examples
- Figure P6.1: Bach's chorale "Jesu, meine Freude" (BWV 64.8) -- a scale-degree analyst labels the starred chords as mediants (III), while a function theorist might label them as V
- The cadential six-four: scale-degree analyst labels it I64 ("scale degrees 1, 3, and 5 with 5^ in the bass"), while the function theorist calls it a V embellished by a 65-43 progression

# Common Confusions
- Roman numerals do not necessarily presuppose inversional equivalence or imply that I and I6 share "tonic-triadness." As Tymoczko says, "the sea cucumber is biologically an animal despite its name."
- The difference between scale-degree and functional analysis is one of degree rather than kind -- there is a continuum between mechanistic labeling and highly interpretive analysis.
- Scale-degree analysis is not "trivial transliteration" -- it is a powerful tool for charting the development of harmonic functionality and testing harmonic theories empirically.
