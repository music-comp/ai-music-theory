# Quick Definition

The perspective in which chords with different doublings (e.g., CGG and CCG) are treated as "the same" entity, implemented through the hierarchical picture where surface voices move within abstract scale-like chords whose notes may not all be present or may appear in multiple octaves.

# Core Definition

Cardinality equivalence is "the perspective on which CGG and CCG are 'the same,' and hence represented by a single location in geometrical space" (p. 540). The hierarchical picture implements this by treating chords like CGG and CCG "as different configurations of surface voices within a single underlying two-note 'scale' CG" (p. 540), analogous to how the Waldstein analysis (Section 3.7) represents different harmonic configurations as surface-level motion within a three-voice background. This allows: (1) chords to function as "abstract scale-like objects whose notes exist in every octave" (p. 540); (2) background voice leadings "containing notes not found on the surface" (p. 540); and (3) "splitting" where a single voice divides into multiple voices and "merging" where multiple voices collapse into one (pp. 541-542).

# Prerequisites

- **Voice-leading geometry** -- The mathematical framework where cardinality equivalence arises as a problem
- **Quadruple hierarchy** -- The hierarchical picture that resolves the problem

# Key Properties

1. CGG and CCG are "genuinely different contrapuntally" (the former is two diatonic steps from F major) even though harmonically similar
2. The hierarchical picture assigns doublings and voice exchanges to the surface level
3. Background voice leadings can contain notes not literally present on the surface
4. Voice "splitting" promotes doublings from surface to background; "merging" does the reverse
5. Quantization offers an alternative to postulating specific background voice leadings

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply Cardinality Equivalence:
1. Identify the underlying chord (e.g., CG as the abstract two-note scale)
2. Treat surface voices as configurations within this abstract chord
3. Assign doublings and voice exchanges to the surface level
4. Allow the number of background voices to change through splitting and merging
5. Alternatively, use quantization to avoid postulating specific background voice leadings

# Context & Application

This concept addresses a fundamental challenge in voice-leading geometry: constructing circular spaces for chords with fixed doublings "inevitably spreads sonically similar objects throughout the spiral" (p. 539). The hierarchical approach resolves this by keeping the background clean and assigning doublings to the surface. It also enables analysis of passages where the number of voices changes.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 540): The Waldstein analysis (Section 3.7) -- four surface voices pass through different configurations represented by a three-voice background.

**Example 2** (pp. 541-542): Figure 9.3.1 -- right-hand chords where the alto doubles the bass at the octave, then the two B voices "split" into different abstract voices as the harmony changes (G, B, B, D) to (F-sharp, A, C, D).

**Example 3** (pp. 540-541): Palestrina passage (Figure 4.9.6) -- bass alternating between two chordal moves, reinterpreted through quantization as the frog hopping between rising and falling lily pads.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming CGG and CCG are the same contrapuntally because they are harmonically similar
  **Correction**: CGG is two diatonic steps from F major; CCG is not -- they are genuinely different contrapuntally

- **Error**: Treating the hierarchical picture as merely a simplification
  **Correction**: It is a substantive analytical framework that bridges mathematics and musical intuition

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking cardinality equivalence eliminates voice-leading distinctions
  **Clarification**: It assigns doublings to the surface while preserving crossing-free backgrounds

- **Confusion**: Confusing splitting/merging with entering/exiting voices
  **Clarification**: Entering/exiting occurs at chord boundaries; splitting/merging occurs during a chord's lifetime when doublings become independent voices
