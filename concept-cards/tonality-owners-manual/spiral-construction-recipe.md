# Quick Definition
A step-by-step procedure for constructing a spiral diagram for any n-note chord in any o-note scale, applicable to any hierarchical level of musical structure.

# Formal Definition
To represent transposition along both an n-note collection ("chord") and an o-note collection ("scale"): (A) Draw a spiral with n loops, attaching its end to its beginning. Start clockwise at 12 o'clock, move inward until reaching 9 o'clock for the nth time, then connect the end clockwise to the start. (B) Mark off o equally spaced points along the spiral by dividing the circle into o equal pie slices and moving along the spiral, placing a point at the border of every nth slice. Label with consecutive scale tones (typically C at the innermost point at 12 o'clock, moving clockwise in a descending fashion).

# Musical Context
The recipe is completely general, equally applicable to voice in chord, chord in scale, and scale in chromatic aggregate. The book focuses on a small collection of spaces: 3-in-12 (chromatic triads for rock), 2-in-7 and 3-in-7 (diatonic dyads and triads for early music), and 7-in-12 (diatonic scales for modulation). Very small scales like 2-in-3 (two-note triadic subsets) are used for melody. Multiple spiral diagrams can describe nested musical motion at different hierarchical levels. The reader can learn to construct diagrams for any chord in any scale.

# Common Confusions
- The number of loops gives the chord size, the number of points gives the scale size -- not the other way around
- Clockwise descends, counterclockwise ascends (a convention, not a mathematical necessity)
- A chord's angular position = the sum of its pitch classes (mod scale size); this can be verified by the reader
