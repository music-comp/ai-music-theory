# Quick Definition
A geometric framework in which chords are represented as points in higher-dimensional spaces, with voice leadings as paths between them, revealing the deep mathematical structure underlying musical practice.

# Formal Definition
Voice-leading geometry represents each chord as a point in a space where each musical voice corresponds to its own dimension. The geometry is twisted, singular, and higher-dimensional. The spiral diagrams used throughout the book are simplified two-dimensional representations of this geometry, designed as "pedagogical simplifications" that free the mind from imagining higher-dimensional spaces while preserving musically relevant relationships. A chord's angular position on the spiral corresponds to the sum of its pitch classes.

# Musical Context
The geometrical approach to voice leading was developed by Tymoczko in collaboration with Clifton Callender, Noam Elkies, Rachel Hall, and Ian Quinn. In *A Geometry of Music* (2011), Tymoczko used the full higher-dimensional models; in *Tonality: An Owner's Manual*, the simpler spiral diagrams serve the same purpose. The geometry is intrinsically neutral with respect to harmonic progression, acquiring directionality from external factors such as the preference for descending melody.

- The 3-in-12 spiral diagram represents major triads in chromatic space, with each point representing an entire chord (Figure 2.1.3)

- Self-intersection of the spiral line is an artifact of depicting intrinsically higher-dimensional geometry in two dimensions

# Common Confusions
- Points on the spiral represent *entire chords*, not single notes -- this is a common source of reader confusion noted by Tymoczko
- The actual geometric line does not intersect itself; self-intersection is an artifact of 2D projection (see appendix 2)
- The geometry does not prescribe what music should sound like -- it describes the space of possibilities that composers navigate
