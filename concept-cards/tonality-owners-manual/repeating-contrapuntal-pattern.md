# Quick Definition
A one-note sequence where the repeating cell is just a single chord, characterized by the melodic intervals that occur between units rather than internal structure. Any harmonically stable repeating contrapuntal pattern corresponds to a type of motion on a spiral diagram.

# Formal Definition
Repeating contrapuntal patterns combine two operations: a voice leading determining the melodic intervals, and a permutation moving those intervals among voices. They can be described as "voice X moves up by 3 and then this melodic interval moves to voice Y." At least three chords are needed to identify the pattern (two for the intervals, one more to observe them moving). The interval-and-arrow notation specifies initial melodic intervals in parentheses, which move around the texture following arrows. This is slightly more general than transposition-and-arrow notation, as it is insensitive to initial registral positions.

# Musical Context
Repeating contrapuntal patterns connect sequences to voice leadings and spiral diagrams: every harmonically stable pattern is a repeated motion through a spiral diagram, and every voice leading between transpositionally related chords can be expanded into such a pattern. This connection means that the theory of voice leading and the theory of sequence are one and the same. In large-unit sequences, these patterns connect analogous chords in adjacent units. There is a general recipe: start with any voice leading between transpositionally related chords, draw transpositional arrows, and use them to form a sequence.

# Common Confusions
- A single-chord unit seems paradoxical, but the "sequence" is defined by the intervals between chords, not internal structure
- Two chords alone are ambiguous (they permit two equally good continuations); at least three are needed to identify the pattern
- Any harmonically stable repeating contrapuntal pattern necessarily corresponds to a motion on a spiral diagram
