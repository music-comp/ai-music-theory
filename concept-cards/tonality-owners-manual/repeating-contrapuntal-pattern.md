# Quick Definition

A one-note sequence where the repeating cell is a single chord, characterized by melodic intervals between units rather than internal structure. Any harmonically stable repeating contrapuntal pattern corresponds to a motion on a spiral diagram, establishing that "the theory of voice leading and the theory of sequence are one and the same" (p. 163).

# Core Definition

Repeating contrapuntal patterns combine two operations: a voice leading determining melodic intervals and a permutation moving those intervals among voices (p. 160). At least three chords are needed to identify the pattern. The interval-and-arrow notation specifies initial melodic intervals in parentheses, which move around the texture following arrows (p. 161). "Any such sequence will necessarily correspond to a type of motion in one of our spiral diagrams" (p. 161). There is a two-way mapping: "every sequence determines a repeating contrapuntal pattern between analogous chords of successive units; and every voice leading can be expanded into a larger-unit sequence" (p. 163). There is a general recipe for constructing sequences from voice leadings (Figure 4.2.9, p. 164).

# Key Properties

1. Cell is a single chord; pattern defined by between-unit intervals
2. At least three chords needed for identification
3. Every harmonically stable pattern = a motion on a spiral diagram
4. Every voice leading between transpositionally related chords can become a sequence
5. Interval-and-arrow notation is insensitive to registral position
6. Little-t transposition (along chord) is inherently canonic

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct:
1. Start with any voice leading between transpositionally related chords
2. Draw transpositional arrows between corresponding notes
3. Use those arrows to form a repeating pattern or larger-unit sequence

## To Recognize:
1. Look for consistent melodic intervals between adjacent chords
2. Track how those intervals move among voices
3. Need at least three chords to disambiguate

# Context & Application

Repeating contrapuntal patterns appear from Josquin to Stravinsky (Figure 4.2.2, p. 160). They are "an important part of the Renaissance practice of stretto fuga" (p. 163, fn. 9). The connection between canon and sequence is "theoretically surprising, as one might not expect that repetition had anything to do with canon" (p. 162). Historically, the canonic aspect was initially paramount; harmonic patterning became valued later. In large-unit sequences, these patterns connect analogous chords in adjacent units (Figure 4.2.6, p. 163).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Expecting at least two chords to identify a repeating pattern
  **Correction**: Two chords are ambiguous (permit two continuations); three chords are needed

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking single-chord units are paradoxical
  **Clarification**: The "sequence" is defined by intervals between chords, not internal structure
