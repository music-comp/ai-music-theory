# Quick Definition
A simple, early form of functional harmony that appeared in the early 1500s as an optional set of ionian-mode idioms emphasizing root-position I, IV, and V chords, gradually strengthening over centuries into mature functional tonality.

# Formal Definition
Protofunctionality describes the gradual emergence of functional harmony as a set of compositional tendencies rather than a sudden shift. Following Lowinsky, Tymoczko argues that protofunctionality appeared in the early 1500s as optional ionian-mode idioms emphasizing root-position primary triads (I, IV, V). Over two centuries, these routines colonized increasing amounts of music, ultimately coming to resemble universal laws rather than genre-specific tendencies. The concept rejects essentialist or saltationist accounts of tonality's origin, instead likening the process to the evolution of dinosaurs into birds -- gradual and involving numerous separate processes at different times.

# Musical Context
Protofunctionality is evident as early as 1500 in secular, homophonic, popular music (Dalza's pavanes, Petrucci's frottola collections) and also appears in statistical tendencies within polyphonic music (Palestrina's masses). It is characterized by: (a) emphasis on primary triads moving by fifth; (b) favoring I6 over iii; (c) simple predominant-dominant-tonic progressions; (d) frequent vii-diminished-6--I cadences; (e) precursors to schemas like "Fenaroli" and "Prinner"; (f) half, full, and deceptive cadences in familiar roles.

# Common Confusions
- Protofunctionality is NOT a sudden emergence of tonal harmony. It developed gradually over centuries with multiple separate processes occurring at different times.
- Early protofunctional music differs from later functional music in important ways: nonfunctional progressions are not forbidden, there is no strong commitment to a single diatonic collection, harmonic routines are simple, inversional equivalence is weak, and noncadential leading tones often descend.
- The same primary-triad emphasis found in protofunctional music can also be found in Sacred Harp polyphony, the Carter family, three-chord rock-and-roll, and contemporary popular music.
