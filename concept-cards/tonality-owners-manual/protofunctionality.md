# Quick Definition

A simple, early form of functional harmony appearing in the early 1500s as an optional set of ionian-mode idioms emphasizing root-position I, IV, and V, gradually strengthening over centuries into mature functional tonality. Tymoczko likens the process to the evolution of dinosaurs into birds.

# Core Definition

Protofunctionality describes the gradual emergence of functional harmony as compositional tendencies rather than a sudden shift. Following Lowinsky, Tymoczko argues it appeared in the early 1500s as optional ionian-mode idioms emphasizing root-position primary triads (I, IV, V). Over two centuries, these routines "colonized an increasing swath of music, ultimately coming to resemble universal laws rather than genre-specific tendencies" (p. 257). The transition was largely implicit -- composers learning patterns without necessarily recognizing them explicitly (p. 257). Protofunctionality is characterized by: (a) emphasis on primary triads moving by fifth; (b) favoring I6 over iii; (c) simple predominant-dominant-tonic progressions; (d) frequent vii-dim-6--I cadences; (e) precursors to schemas like Fenaroli and Prinner; (f) half, full, and deceptive cadences (p. 290).

# Key Properties

1. Ionian-mode idioms emphasizing root-position I, IV, and V
2. Appeared as early as 1500 in secular, homophonic, popular music
3. 70-80% of chords are primary triads in early protofunctional music (p. 265)
4. Nonfunctional progressions are not forbidden -- flexible tendencies rather than rigid rules (p. 267)
5. No strong commitment to a single diatonic collection; bVII common in ionian contexts (p. 267)
6. Simple oscillatory harmonic routines (I-IV-I, I-V-I) rather than extended progressions
7. Inversional equivalence is weak; no robust role (p. 268)
8. Noncadential leading tones often descend (p. 268)
9. No orderly modulation through secondary keys (p. 269)
10. Mode-bound option rather than inviolable law (p. 268)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Protofunctional Music:
1. Check for emphasis on root-position primary triads (I, IV, V)
2. Note whether I6 is preferred over iii for bass degree 3
3. Look for simple oscillatory harmonic cycles
4. Check for nonfunctional progressions occurring freely alongside functional ones
5. Note whether bVII appears even in ionian contexts

# Context & Application

Protofunctionality appears in Dalza's pavanes (1508), Petrucci's frottola collections (1504), and throughout sixteenth-century homophony. It also appears statistically in Palestrina's polyphony (about 85% functional progressions in the Pope Marcellus Mass vs. ~95% in fully functional music, p. 289). The same primary-triad emphasis appears in Sacred Harp polyphony, the Carter family, and three-chord rock-and-roll.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating protofunctionality as a sudden emergence of tonal harmony
  **Correction**: It developed gradually over centuries with multiple processes at different times

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking protofunctional music is indistinguishable from later functional music
  **Clarification**: Key differences include: nonfunctional progressions are freely permitted, bVII is common, harmonic routines are simple, inversional equivalence is weak, noncadential leading tones often descend
