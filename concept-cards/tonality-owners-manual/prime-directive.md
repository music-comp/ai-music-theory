# Quick Definition

An analytical maxim: "whenever you find an interesting musical technique, try to generalize it to every possible chordal and scalar environment" (p. 5).

# Core Definition

The Prime Directive is Tymoczko's foundational methodological principle, encouraging analysts to ask whether superficially different idioms arise from a unified musical logic. It functions in three ways: (1) *explanatory*, allowing us to understand why certain idioms might have survived (e.g., they combine harmonic consistency with parallel stepwise motion); (2) *generalizing*, allowing adaptation of earlier techniques to new harmonic domains; and (3) *connective*, revealing that composers across centuries may be "doing the same thing" at an abstract level (pp. 8-9). It is not necessary that composers themselves understood the general logic; "it is more likely that their practice developed intuitively and without any abstract theoretical framework" (p. 9).

# Key Properties

1. It is an analytical maxim, not a compositional rule
2. It encourages generalization across chordal and scalar environments
3. It does not require that composers themselves understood the general logic
4. It serves explanatory, generalizing, and connective functions
5. It leads directly to the concept of the quadruple hierarchy

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply the Prime Directive:
1. Identify an interesting musical technique in a specific context
2. Abstract the technique from its particular chordal/scalar environment
3. Ask what the technique would look like in other environments (different chord types, different scales)
4. Look for instances of the generalized technique across styles and periods
5. Assess whether the instances share a common contrapuntal or geometric logic

# Context & Application

The Prime Directive is applied throughout the book to connect practices across Renaissance, baroque, classical, Romantic, jazz, and rock traditions. It motivates the entire analytical framework, from Gesualdo's trick through the spiral diagrams to the analysis of rock harmony. The maxim leads to the quadruple hierarchy, where the same basic procedures (transposition, inversion) occur on multiple levels simultaneously.

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 5-8, Figures 1.1.1-1.1.6): Gesualdo's trick generalized from chromatic parallel tenths to diatonic parallel thirds (Pachelbel), parallel major sevenths (Stravinsky), parallel perfect fourths (Mahanthappa).

**Example 2** (p. 9, Figure 1.1.8): McCoy Tyner deploying close-position triads in both diatonic and pentatonic scales, exhibiting "a more abstract understanding of contrapuntal possibility."

**Example 3** (p. 7, Figure 1.1.7): Stravinsky's *Rite of Spring* at R57, using Gesualdo's trick with 014 trichords while melodies move along diatonic scales.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Applying the Prime Directive to claim all instances are aesthetically equivalent
  **Correction**: Generalization reveals shared structure but does not eliminate stylistic differences or relative aesthetic value

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the Prime Directive claims composers consciously understood the generalizations
  **Clarification**: It is an analytical tool for theorists; composers' practice likely developed intuitively

- **Confusion**: Believing generalization eliminates the value of particular schemas
  **Clarification**: Schemas have independent value; the Prime Directive reveals deeper commonalities beneath them
