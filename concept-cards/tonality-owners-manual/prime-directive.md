# Quick Definition
An analytical maxim: whenever you find an interesting musical technique, try to generalize it to every possible chordal and scalar environment.

# Formal Definition
The Prime Directive is Tymoczko's foundational methodological principle, encouraging analysts to ask whether superficially different idioms arise from a unified musical logic. It functions in three ways: (1) *explanatory*, allowing us to understand why certain idioms survived; (2) *generalizing*, allowing adaptation of earlier techniques to new harmonic domains; and (3) *connective*, revealing that composers across centuries may be "doing the same thing" at an abstract level.

# Musical Context
The Prime Directive is applied throughout the book to connect practices across Renaissance, baroque, classical, Romantic, jazz, and rock traditions. It leads directly to the concept of the quadruple hierarchy, where the same basic procedures (transposition, inversion) occur on multiple levels simultaneously. It is not necessary that composers themselves understood the general logic -- their practice may have developed intuitively.

# Common Confusions
- The Prime Directive does not claim composers consciously understood the generalizations -- it is an analytical tool for theorists
- It does not eliminate stylistic differences; rather, it reveals commonalities beneath surface variety
- Generalization does not mean all instances are equally good or musically equivalent -- particular schemas have their own value
