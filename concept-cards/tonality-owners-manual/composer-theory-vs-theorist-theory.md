# Quick Definition

The distinction between implicit theories embedded in compositional practice ("composer theory," which leans toward the piecemeal and empirical) and explicit theories articulated in written treatises ("theorist theory," which inclines to the systematic a priori).

# Core Definition

"Where 'composer theory' leans toward the piecemeal and empirical, 'theorist theory' inclines to the systematic a priori" (p. 2). Composers have "implicit views about theoretical issues both small and large, which we can recover (if imperfectly) through a careful study of their music" (p. 22). The two interact dynamically: "explicit theories both affect and are affected by the implicit theories" (p. 22). Sometimes "a single person's implicit theory diverges from their explicit beliefs" (p. 22). This is analogous to the relationship between implicit linguistic competence and explicit grammar rules.

# Key Properties

1. Composer theory is implicit, piecemeal, and empirical
2. Theorist theory is explicit, systematic, and a priori
3. The two interact and influence each other
4. A person's implicit practice can diverge from their explicit beliefs
5. Corpus analysis can reconstruct implicit composer theory
6. Textbooks may be unreliable as evidence of actual norms

# Construction / Recognition

## To Distinguish the Two:
1. Compare what a composer does (revealed by corpus study) with what treatises say should be done
2. Look for systematic discrepancies between practice and precept
3. Identify cases where a composer's explicit statements contradict their music
4. Recognize that both types contribute to musical understanding

# Context & Application

Modernism shifted the value placed on explicit vs. implicit knowledge, as "musical culture came to value the ability to describe functional-harmonic grammar more than the ability to speak the language" (p. 2). Corpus analysis allows reconstruction of the "dialogue between explicit and implicit, a history of musical ideas written jointly by theorists and composers" (p. 23).

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 18-19): Prout's explicit rule that suspensions cannot mask parallel fifths vs. the "Old Masters'" implicit practice of allowing them in specific contexts.

**Example 2** (pp. 27-28): Bach's cadential seventh-resolution idiom -- implicit composer theory diverging from the explicit generalization that sevenths resolve down by step.

**Example 3** (p. 22): The analogy to English split infinitives -- an explicit grammatical rule with no grounding in actual linguistic behavior.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating textbooks as infallible descriptions of compositional practice
  **Correction**: Authors might be wrong about the music they describe; corpus analysis provides better evidence

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking either type of theory is inherently superior
  **Clarification**: Both contribute to understanding; neither is automatically correct

- **Confusion**: Mapping the distinction onto "correct vs. incorrect"
  **Clarification**: Both can be partially right and partially wrong about different aspects of practice
