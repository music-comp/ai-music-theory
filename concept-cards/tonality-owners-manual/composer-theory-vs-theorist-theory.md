# Quick Definition
The distinction between the implicit theories embedded in compositional practice ("composer theory") and the explicit theories articulated in written treatises ("theorist theory").

# Formal Definition
Composer theory is the intuitive, implicit understanding of musical structure revealed by compositional and improvisational behavior -- analogous to a natural speaker's grammatical competence. Theorist theory consists of the explicit, systematic statements found in treatises and textbooks. The two interact: explicit theories both affect and are affected by implicit practice. Sometimes a single person's implicit theory diverges from their explicit beliefs. Where composer theory "leans toward the piecemeal and empirical," theorist theory "inclines to the systematic *a priori*."

# Musical Context
Corpus analysis allows reconstruction of the dialogue between these two kinds of theory. The history of music is written jointly by theorists and composers, with implicit theories absorbed through imitation and implicit learning. Modernism shifted the economic value of implicit and explicit, as musical culture came to value the ability to *describe* functional-harmonic grammar more than the ability to *speak* the language.

# Examples
- Prout's explicit rule that suspensions cannot mask parallel fifths vs. the "Old Masters'" implicit practice of allowing them (Figure 1.3.1)
- The prohibition on split infinitives: an explicit rule with no grounding in actual linguistic behavior -- analogous to some music-theoretical rules
- Bach's cadential seventh-resolution idiom: implicit "composer theory" that diverges from the explicit generalization that sevenths resolve down by step

# Common Confusions
- Neither type of theory is inherently superior -- both contribute to understanding
- Textbooks are unreliable as sole evidence of musical norms, since authors might be wrong about the music they describe
- The distinction does not map onto "correct vs. incorrect" -- both can be partially right and partially wrong
