# Quick Definition

An anticipation recursively embellished by a neighbor tone, creating one of the most analytically ambiguous idioms in the Palestrina style. The same melodic formula can function as either a decorated anticipation or a passing tone depending on context.

# Core Definition

A decorated anticipation occurs when an anticipation is recursively embellished by a neighbor tone, so the anticipation does not simply sound and hold but moves to a neighboring pitch before the harmony arrives (p. 214). This figure is analytically ambiguous because the same melodic formula can function as either a decorated anticipation or a passing tone, and the two readings can lead to very different harmonic interpretations -- including the presence or absence of parallel fifths (p. 214).

# Prerequisites

- **Anticipation** — The base nonharmonic tone being decorated
- **Neighbor tone** — The decorating element applied recursively

# Key Properties

1. Anticipation recursively decorated by a neighbor tone
2. Analytically ambiguous -- same figure can have different structural meanings
3. Choice of interpretation can determine presence/absence of parallel fifths
4. Occurs in the context of suspensions in Palestrina
5. Sometimes appears in eighth notes (fn. 13)
6. Demonstrates that nonharmonic analysis is interpretive, not mechanical

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify a Decorated Anticipation:
1. Locate a short melodic figure near a harmonic change
2. Consider whether the first note anticipates the upcoming chord
3. Check whether the intervening note functions as a neighbor to the anticipation
4. Evaluate whether an alternative reading (passing tone) is possible
5. Check for parallel fifths under each interpretation

# Context & Application

Decorated anticipations form one of the Palestrina style's most ambiguous idioms. They reveal that nonharmonic reduction is sometimes interpretive rather than mechanical, even in the strict Palestrina style. Passages can be "suspended between two possibilities, submitting to no fully satisfactory analysis" (p. 214, Fig. 5.1.5).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 214, Fig. 5.1.4 left): Palestrina's mass In duplicibus minoribus (II), Credo, mm. 163-165 -- the top voice B is most naturally read as an anticipation decorated by a lower neighbor A.

**Example 2** (p. 214, Fig. 5.1.4 right): Palestrina's mass De Beata Marie Virginis (I), Sanctus, mm. 19-20 -- the same figure must be read as a passing tone to avoid parallel B-A/E-D fifths.

**Example 3** (p. 214, Fig. 5.1.5): Palestrina's mass Dilexi quoniam, Kyrie, m. 9 -- a passage suspended between two possible analyses.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Mechanically applying the same reading to all instances of the melodic figure
  **Correction**: The surrounding voices determine which reading is appropriate -- the same figure can be a decorated anticipation or a passing tone

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking all nonharmonic tone identification is mechanical in the Palestrina style
  **Clarification**: Decorated anticipations show that even strict-style analysis involves interpretive judgment
