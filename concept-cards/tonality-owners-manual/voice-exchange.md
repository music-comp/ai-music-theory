# Quick Definition
A rearrangement of the notes within a single chord, where voices swap positions along equal and opposite paths that collectively sum to zero. Voice exchanges decompose any voice leading into a crossing-free component (preserving spacing) plus a rearrangement.

# Formal Definition
For diatonic thirds, there are two elementary voice exchanges: the small (two-step) exchange moving each voice by two scale steps (third down to root and root up to third), and the large (five-step) exchange moving each voice by five steps (third up to root and root down to third). Combining a voice exchange with a basic voice leading produces "antiparallel" motion -- contrary motion where voices move by almost the same amount (a fourth and a fifth). For triads, there are three pairwise voice exchanges: root-third, third-fifth, and root-fifth swaps.

# Musical Context
Voice exchanges are fundamental to functionally tonal composition. A single voice exchange combined with a single basic voice leading produces antiparallel motion, which is contrapuntally balanced (voices move by approximately equal distances). Fifth-related thirds are geometrically neighboring, allowing the most balanced antiparallel motion. The two-step voice exchange applied separately produces wedge sequences where voices move apart in near contrary motion. These patterns appear in Beethoven's Op. 26 finale, which systematically deploys the basic voice leading, both voice exchanges, and their combinations.

# Common Confusions
- Voice exchange is not the same as voice crossing in register; it is a rearrangement of notes among voices that may or may not produce an audible crossing
- More complex voice rearrangements can be generated by combining elementary voice exchanges with transpositions along the chord
- Schenkerian theory's surface-level voice exchanges can be understood as artifacts of voices moving within a simpler background
