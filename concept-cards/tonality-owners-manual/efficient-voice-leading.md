# Quick Definition

Voice leading that minimizes total voice displacement, achieved by keeping the center of gravity approximately fixed and the change in vertical configuration small -- reframing traditional prohibitions as affirmative goals.

# Core Definition

"The most efficient voice leading between two vertical configurations will necessarily minimize the change in center of gravity" (p. 88). Transpositionally related chords can be connected by efficient voice leading when their roots move by intervals related to the chord's evenness: "dyads at the tritone or near tritone (i.e., fourth or fifth), three-note chords when their roots move by major third or near major third (i.e., minor third or perfect fourth), four-note chords when their roots move by minor third, tritone, or intervals nearly equal to these" (pp. 88-89). These root motions correspond to 12/n, where n is the chord size.

# Key Properties

1. Minimizes total voice displacement
2. Preserves center of gravity as much as possible
3. Optimal root motions: dyads at tritone, triads at major third, seventh chords at minor third
4. On spiral diagrams, angular motion = center-of-gravity change
5. Contrary motion (change = 0) minimizes total motion
6. Similar motion increases total motion beyond what oblique motion requires
7. Explains the rarity of similar motion in Renaissance counterpoint

# Construction / Recognition

## To Achieve Efficient Voice Leading:
1. Keep center of gravity approximately fixed
2. Change the vertical configuration by only a small amount
3. Use contrary or near-contrary motion
4. Avoid similar motion (which increases total displacement)
5. For root-position triads, favor root motions near major third

# Context & Application

The configurational perspective reframes the traditional prohibition on "direct fifths and octaves": similar motion is rare "regardless of destination interval" (p. 93, Figure P3.8). This is because similar motion increases total voice displacement. The insight transforms negative counterpoint ("don't do X") into positive counterpoint ("do Y because it minimizes motion").

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 88-89, Figure P3.3): Center-of-gravity-preserving progressions including Schubert's major-third triadic shifts, Wagner's minor-third tetrachordal progressions, and ubiquitous fifth-progressions.

**Example 2** (p. 93, Figure P3.8): Data from Ockeghem, Josquin, and Palestrina showing similar motion is rare regardless of destination interval.

**Example 3** (p. 93, Figure P3.9): As center-of-gravity change increases, voice leading becomes more unbalanced; beyond oblique motion, total voice-leading size increases.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Thinking efficient voice leading is just about avoiding parallel fifths
  **Correction**: It is a broader principle about minimizing total voice displacement through center-of-gravity preservation

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking similar motion is prohibited specifically for perfect consonances
  **Clarification**: Similar motion is rare regardless of destination; "the general scarcity of similar motion is at least as striking as the difference between the perfect and imperfect consonances" (p. 93)
