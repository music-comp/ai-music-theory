# Quick Definition

A passage that prolongs dominant harmony, typically preparing a thematic return. Often uses rounds or repeating contrapuntal patterns over a dominant pedal -- essentially a round with transposition of zero.

# Core Definition

Standing on the dominant sustains dominant harmony (usually V or V7) for an extended period to build expectation for the tonic return (p. 154). In the Pathetique episode, the contrary-motion sequence breaks off for a standing-on-the-dominant in C minor, preparing the main theme's return. The technique commonly uses three-voice rounds dividing voices 2 + 1, adding passing tones to the two-step voice exchange (*0 on Figure 4.3.1), appearing as V-I or IV-I patterns within the prolonged dominant area (Figure 4.5.6, p. 178). "Such rounds differ from standard sequences only in that their harmonies do not transpose" (p. 179), linking them directly to the sequence framework.

6. Relates to Fenaroli and Prinner schemas

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify:
1. Look for extended dominant prolongation with internal voice-leading motion
2. Check for round or sequential patterns over a pedal
3. Verify that the harmonies oscillate locally without net transposition

# Context & Application

Standing-on-the-dominant commonly appears at the end of development sections, transitions, and episodes. The three-voice round used for this purpose dates back to the early sixteenth century (Figure 4.5.6, p. 178). Beethoven's Op. 10 no. 3, I, mm. 333ff presents a round at the end with upper voices embedding a two-voice canon in contrary motion with the bass (Figure 4.5.7, p. 179).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating standing-on-the-dominant as simply a sustained V chord
  **Correction**: It typically involves internal motion and voice-leading patterns that animate the static harmony
