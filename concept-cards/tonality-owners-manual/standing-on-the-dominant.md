# Quick Definition
A passage that prolongs the dominant harmony, typically preparing a return to the main theme. Often involves static harmony enlivened by a round or repeating contrapuntal pattern over the dominant pedal.

# Formal Definition
Standing on the dominant is a formal convention in which the music sustains dominant harmony (usually V or V7) for an extended period to build expectation for the tonic return. In the *Pathetique* episode, the contrary-motion sequence breaks off in favor of a standing-on-the-dominant in C minor, preparing the main theme's return. The technique commonly uses rounds dividing voices 2 + 1, adding passing tones to a two-voice exchange pattern to create V-I or IV-I motion within the prolonged dominant area.

# Musical Context
The standing-on-the-dominant commonly appears at the end of development sections, transitions, and episodes in sonata forms, rondos, and rounded binary forms. Common three-voice rounds used for standing-on-the-dominant add passing tones to the two-step voice exchange (marked *0 on Figure 4.3.1), appearing as V-I or IV-I patterns. These relate to the "Fenaroli" and "Prinner" schemas. The pattern dates back to the early sixteenth century.

# Common Confusions
- Standing on the dominant is not simply a sustained V chord; it typically involves internal motion and voice-leading patterns that animate the static harmony
- The technique relates directly to the round/sequence framework: it is essentially a round (transposition = 0) over a dominant pedal
