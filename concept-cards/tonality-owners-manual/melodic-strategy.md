# Quick Definition

The higher-level goals that guide melodic composition, such as stepwise descents between tonic-triad notes, understood as strategic aims pursued within the constraints of a local harmonic grammar -- analogous to strategic goals in sports pursued within the rules of the game.

# Core Definition

Melodic strategy reconceives the Schenkerian notion of long-range melodic structure as higher-order compositional goals pursued within a sequential harmonic framework. "If the harmonic grammar provides the rules of the game, then phrase-level melodic templates are the higher-level goals" (p. 409). Directed melodies "often articulate the doubly parallel motion represented by our spiral diagrams -- that is, the combination of big-T transposition along the scale, and little-t transposition along the chord" (p. 409). The nonharmonic system ensures out-of-chord notes resolve by step to in-chord notes, and registral limitations ensure within-chord notes outline close-position triads and seventh chords. The resulting background provides "melodic destinations, points of rest between which the surface moves" (p. 409).

# Prerequisites

- **Double transposition** -- Melodic strategy operates through the combination of big-T and little-t transposition
- **Quadruple hierarchy** -- The nested collectional levels within which strategic melodic motion occurs
- **Harmonic grammar** -- The local rules within which melodic strategy operates

# Key Properties

1. Directed melodies articulate coherent voice-leading structures
2. Simple melodies descend stepwise from one tonic-triad note to another
3. Complex melodies dance between multiple voices of an abstract background
4. Still more complex melodies move contrary to a background voice leading
5. Double transposition of thematic material becomes increasingly important as musical culture shifts from polyphonic to homophonic default
6. Melody is locally free but hierarchically structured

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Melodic Strategy:
1. Locate the main structural tones (typically tonic-triad notes)
2. Identify the overall direction of the melody (ascending, descending, arching)
3. Determine whether the surface melody tracks or contradicts the background voice leading
4. Check for double transposition of thematic objects between harmonic regions
5. Note whether the strategy produces ascending or descending motion at the surface level

# Context & Application

This concept allows Tymoczko to incorporate Schenkerian insights about long-range melodic coherence without adopting Schenkerian metaphysics about prolongation and representation. A single melodic strategy (e.g., stepwise descent) can produce very different harmonic effects depending on the chordal and scalar environment, as Chopin demonstrates in moving between diatonic modality, functional harmony, and chromaticism using the same fundamental approach.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 408): Mozart's Symphony No. 40, K.550, opening -- linear descent from D to F-sharp (5-hat to 7-hat) while reserving root-position dominant for the phrase end.

**Example 2** (pp. 410-411): Mozart's K.284, I -- ascending arpeggiation along the tonic triad D-F-sharp-A, achieved through "schematic building blocks that themselves exploit the fundamental geometry of musical possibility" (p. 411).

**Example 3** (p. 409): Double transposition of thematic material in three of eleven schemas in Gjerdingen's appendix (Do-Re-Mi, Sol-Fa-Me, Meyer).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Confusing melodic strategy with Schenkerian prolongation
  **Correction**: Strategy describes goals pursued within harmonic constraints; prolongation claims harmonic-contrapuntal identity across levels

- **Error**: Assuming every melody must articulate a stepwise descent
  **Correction**: Tymoczko argues for an expanded vocabulary of reductional targets beyond the Schenkerian catalogue (Section 9.4)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking melodic strategy implies that harmony is subordinate to melody
  **Clarification**: Both systems are independent; the art of functional composition consists in writing satisfying phrases while also satisfying harmonic grammar constraints

- **Confusion**: Believing the "slower melody" is consciously planned by composers
  **Clarification**: It may reflect implicit knowledge -- geometrical relationships that musicians discover through practice without explicit awareness
