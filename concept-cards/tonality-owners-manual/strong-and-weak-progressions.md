# Quick Definition

In the descending-thirds model, "strong" root progressions (descending third, descending fifth, ascending step) move rightward by one, two, or three positions, while their "weak" counterparts (ascending third, ascending fifth, descending step) move leftward.

# Core Definition

The descending-thirds arrangement of functional harmony privileges certain types of root progression: "since the 'strong' progressions (descending third, descending fifth, and ascending step) move rightward by one, two, or three steps, the model will permit more of these motions than their 'weak' counterparts (ascending third, ascending fifth, and descending step)" (p. 313). This asymmetry is a structural feature of the model: rightward motion (toward the dominant) is unrestricted, while leftward motion must follow specified arrows.

# Key Properties

1. Strong progressions: descending third (1 step right), descending fifth (2 steps right), ascending step (3 steps right)
2. Weak progressions: ascending third (1 step left), ascending fifth (2 steps left), descending step (3 steps left)
3. The model permits more strong than weak progressions
4. Functional tonality does not avoid ascending fifths *per se* -- it avoids ascending fifths *not involving the tonic triad* (p. 326)
5. IV--I and I--V (ascending fifths involving the tonic) are ubiquitous

# Construction / Recognition

## To Classify a Root Progression:
1. Determine the root interval and direction
2. Map to the descending-thirds chain: I--vi--IV--ii--vii^o^--V
3. Count positions moved: rightward = strong, leftward = weak
4. Note that the tonic-involving ascending fifths (IV--I, I--V) are special cases -- universally common despite being "weak"

# Context & Application

The strong/weak distinction is not absolute. While strong progressions dominate, some weak progressions (especially IV--I and I--V) are fundamental to functional tonality. The distinction helps explain why certain sequences are common (those using descending fifths) and others rare (those requiring sustained weak progressions).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 313): Descending fifth (e.g., V--I): moves 2 steps rightward -- the most common root motion.

**Example 2** (p. 313): Ascending step (e.g., IV--V, vi--vii^o^): moves 3 steps rightward -- strong but less common than descending fifths.

**Example 3** (p. 326): Ascending fifth (e.g., IV--I): weak by the model but ubiquitous in all functional genres when involving the tonic triad.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming all weak progressions are avoided in functional music
  **Correction**: IV--I and I--V are ascending fifths (weak) but are among the most fundamental functional progressions

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Equating "strong" with "common" and "weak" with "rare"
  **Clarification**: Strong progressions are favored *in general*, but specific weak progressions involving the tonic triad are extremely common
