# Quick Definition

A nonharmonic tone held over from a previous chord, creating a dissonance that resolves downward by step to a chord tone. Suspensions occupy a unique and ambiguous position in the nonharmonic system, functioning as "dissonant harmonic events" rather than mere decorations.

# Core Definition

A suspension is a dissonance created when a note from one harmony is sustained into the next, where it is dissonant, before resolving (typically downward) by step to a chord tone. Unlike other nonharmonic tones, suspensions can occupy an entire sounding consonance (p. 213), regularly license parallel fifths at the level of the harmonic skeleton (p. 217), and sometimes produce irreducible seventh chords (Renaissance sevenths). Tymoczko argues suspensions are "dissonant harmonic events" rather than mere decorations, as they tend to cluster near cadences and generate the tension released by the cadence itself (p. 218). The downward resolution of the suspension is "as close to an inviolable law of the style as one can find" (p. 226).

# Key Properties

1. Prepared: held over from previous chord where it was consonant
2. Dissonant with the new harmony
3. Resolves downward by step (nearly inviolable)
4. Can occupy an entire sounding consonance (unlike other nonharmonic tones)
5. Regularly licenses parallel fifths at the harmonic skeleton level
6. Clusters near cadences rather than being evenly distributed through phrases (Figs. 5.1.13-14)
7. Serves a cadential signaling role for musica ficta and the leading tone

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify a Suspension:
1. Locate a note dissonant with the prevailing harmony
2. Verify it was consonant in the preceding harmony (preparation)
3. Verify it resolves downward by step to a chord tone
4. Check that it typically occurs on a strong beat

# Context & Application

Suspensions have a cadential role as signals for musica ficta and the leading tone. In Renaissance music, suspension chains signal impending cadences even before the leading tone appears. Renaissance sevenths -- irreducible seventh chords produced by suspensions -- tend to have cadential function, with the most common form evolving into the baroque predominant seventh (p. 219). The 6/4-5/3 suspension pattern is ubiquitous in Palestrina, accounting for almost 10% of his suspensions (p. 222).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Claiming suspensions always "stand for" their tones of resolution
  **Correction**: In many cases (Renaissance sevenths, 6/4 chords), replacing the suspension with its resolution produces another dissonance or parallel fifths (p. 220)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Treating suspensions as merely decorative like other nonharmonic tones
  **Clarification**: Suspensions license parallel fifths at the skeleton level, cluster near cadences, and produce irreducible seventh chords -- they are "dissonant harmonic events" (p. 218)

- **Confusion**: Expecting suspensions to be evenly distributed through phrases (as in fourth-species counterpoint exercises)
  **Clarification**: In actual Palestrina music, suspensions cluster near cadences, serving as signals for impending closure (pp. 218-219)
