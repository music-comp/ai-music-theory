# Quick Definition
A nonharmonic tone held over from a previous chord, creating a dissonance that resolves downward by step to a chord tone. Suspensions occupy a unique and ambiguous position in the nonharmonic system.

# Formal Definition
A suspension is a dissonance created when a note from one harmony is sustained into the next, where it is dissonant, before resolving (typically downward) by step to a chord tone. Unlike other nonharmonic tones, suspensions can occupy an entire sounding consonance, regularly license parallel fifths at the level of the harmonic skeleton, and sometimes produce irreducible seventh chords. Tymoczko argues suspensions are "dissonant harmonic events" rather than mere decorations, as they tend to cluster near cadences and signal impending closure.

# Musical Context
Suspensions have a cadential role as signals for musica ficta and the leading tone. They generate the tension released by the cadence itself. Rather than being evenly distributed through phrases (as fourth-species counterpoint might suggest), suspensions cluster near cadences in Palestrina's music (Figures 5.1.13-14). The downward resolution of the suspension is as close to an inviolable law of the style as one can find.

# Common Confusions
- Suspensions are not simply decorative. They can produce irreducible seventh chords (Renaissance sevenths), mask parallel fifths in the harmonic skeleton, and have genuinely harmonic functions.
- The common claim that suspensions always "stand for" their tones of resolution is problematic. In many cases, replacing the suspension with its resolution produces a second dissonance or parallel fifths.
- Suspensions can license parallels both "on the surface" (Figure 5.1.12) and in the "harmonic skeleton" (Figure 5.1.10), creating an incoherent situation for the view that they are merely decorative.
