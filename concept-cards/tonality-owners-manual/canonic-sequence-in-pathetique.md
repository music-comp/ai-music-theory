# Quick Definition

The Ab major episode from Beethoven's Pathetique sonata (III, mm. 79-86), combining a canonic sequence, a near sequence, and a contrary-motion sequence in close succession -- a "brief tutorial in the many varieties of repetition" (p. 151).

# Core Definition

The passage opens with a canonic sequence whose melodic intervals alternate between voices: "one voice ascends by fourth and the other descends by fifth, with the motions switching hands after every dyad" (p. 151). Despite this alternation, the left hand always sounds the root and the right hand the third. On the 2-in-7 diagram, it moves clockwise combining the descending basic voice leading with alternating two- and five-step voice exchanges (Figure P4.1). At the bar level, this produces a transpositional sequence moving each voice down by step. Since "the diatonic scale has an odd number of notes, the voices exchange intervals when they return to the tonic" (p. 152), creating an impression of invertible counterpoint.

# Prerequisites

- **Canonic Sequence** — The primary sequence type in the opening
- **Antiparallel Motion** — The alternating fourth/fifth pattern
- **Voice Exchange** — Two- and five-step exchanges drive the sequence

# Key Properties

1. Three sequence types in close succession: canonic, near, contrary-motion
2. Antiparallel voice leading with intervals switching hands after every dyad
3. Left hand always sounds root, right hand always the third
4. Bar-level transpositional sequence (each voice down by step)
5. Odd scale size causes interval exchange at tonic return
6. Converging cadence creates half cadence then full cadence -> period structure

# Construction / Recognition

## To Analyze:
1. Map dyads onto the 2-in-7 diagram
2. Identify clockwise motion with alternating voice exchanges
3. Note that bar-level transposition is down by step
4. Track the converging cadence positions to explain the period structure

# Context & Application

The episode illustrates Beethoven's "love of patterns while hinting at a conception of functional harmony as inherently repetitive, its patterns disguised, distorted, and blurred" (p. 154). The middle section presents a near sequence on Eb, Db, Ab embedding the ascending basic voice leading in nonsequential triads (Figure P4.3). The final section presents a contrary-motion sequence moving hands an octave further apart with each iteration (Figure P4.4), breaking off for a standing-on-the-dominant in C minor. The passage is "simultaneously funny and profound" -- "embodied mathematics, drawing musical consequences from a small number of postulates" (p. 154).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Analyzing only one of the three sequence types
  **Correction**: The passage systematically presents all three types; recognizing this is key to understanding its structure

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Hearing the interval exchange as actual invertible counterpoint
  **Clarification**: Each hand plays the intervals of the other hand's antecedent but on the same notes; "contour and rhythm trump pitch-class content" creating an impression of voice exchange (p. 152)
