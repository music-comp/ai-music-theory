# Quick Definition
The general practice of eliminating "surface" musical activity to reveal a "background" that clarifies a piece's logic, encompassing nonharmonic, textural, and summarizing approaches.

# Formal Definition
Reductive analysis is the central practice of contemporary music analysis, involving the removal of surface activity to reveal underlying structures. Tymoczko distinguishes three kinds: nonharmonic reduction (removing nonharmonic tones), textural reduction (converting complex textures to chorale-like backgrounds), and summarizing reduction (finding long-range connections between nonadjacent events). These forms are poorly understood partly because scholarship has not always distinguished them, partly because earlier composers may not have shared modern reductive instincts, and partly because of difficult philosophical questions.

# Musical Context
Reductive analysis is fundamental to virtually all analytic endeavor in Western tonal music. It is most straightforward in strictly controlled styles like Palestrina and Bach. As music becomes more complex (Monteverdi, Mahler, Shostakovich), reduction becomes increasingly interpretive and less intersubjective. The nonharmonic system can be put to computational work by using it to "score" harmonic interpretations based on how well-behaved the voices are.

# Common Confusions
- Schenkerian theory treats diminution as less important notes "representing" more important notes "at a deeper structural level." Tymoczko argues this notion of representation is superfluous -- summarizing reduction is more like paraphrasing a movie plot.
- Reduction is theoretically feasible in much tonal music but does not follow that it is analytically or aesthetically advisable. The syntax may allow for a reduction that misrepresents the music's semantics.
- In Mahler's late music, nonharmonic reduction is "no longer something broadly intersubjective or syntactical, but rather autobiographical and interpretive."
