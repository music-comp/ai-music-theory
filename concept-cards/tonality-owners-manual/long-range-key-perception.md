# Quick Definition

The question of whether listeners can directly perceive the return to the tonic key after extended passages -- a question complicated by the fundamental difference between absolute-pitch listeners (who may hear keys directly) and relative-pitch listeners (who have only calculational access).

# Core Definition

There is "a fundamental and perhaps biological difference between two different groups: the small number of absolute-pitch listeners who are directly sensitive to keys, able to hear that a piece has returned to the tonic, and the much larger group of relative-pitch listeners who have access to this information only by way of explicit calculation" (p. 403). This makes us "wary of statements like: 'the most radical transformation of a theme is from dissonance to resolution [. . .] this is basically what happens in a sonata when the second group is recapitulated at the end in the tonic'" (p. 403, quoting Rosen 1988). Even if some listeners reliably identify tonal return through rhetoric, instrumentation, or score study, "the experience of dissonance is felt and immediate in a way that the calculation of keys is not" (p. 404).

# Key Properties

1. Absolute-pitch listeners may directly perceive key identity; relative-pitch listeners cannot
2. Even absolute-pitch listeners may not experience tonal return as genuine tension-release comparable to a dominant resolving
3. Repeated listening can provide cognitive access without genuine perceptual experience
4. Rhetoric, instrumentation, and musical convention may create a sense of "return" independent of actual key perception
5. Expert claims about hearing "vast stretches of music 'representing' or 'prolonging' a single note" may not be grounded in genuine perception (p. 404)
6. Both overconfidence and dismissal are risks: some claims are inflated, but theory can genuinely enrich experience

# Construction / Recognition

## To Evaluate Claims About Long-Range Perception:
1. Consider whether the listener has absolute pitch (potentially direct access) or relative pitch (calculational access)
2. Ask whether the claimed perception survives without score knowledge, repeated listening, or analytical training
3. Distinguish between knowing "that" a key has returned and *feeling* the return as experiential release
4. Consider alternative cues (rhetoric, dynamics, orchestration) that might produce a sense of "return"

# Context & Application

This discussion challenges deeply held assumptions in music theory, particularly Schenkerian claims about large-scale tonal prolongation. Tymoczko recommends taking seriously "those experiments suggesting that our powers of long-range musical perception are less impressive than theorists have traditionally believed" while also recognizing that "theory can sometimes change our experience" (pp. 404--406). The challenge is "to stretch our ears while remaining realistic about human limitation" (p. 406).

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 403--404): Rosen's claim about sonata recapitulation as "the most radical transformation from dissonance to resolution" -- challenged on perceptual grounds.

**Example 2** (p. 404): A non-absolute-pitch listener who memorizes a piece well enough to track key areas -- this is cognitive access, not direct perception.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming that because one can identify the key, one directly perceives tonal return as resolution
  **Correction**: Knowing "that" is different from feeling the return experientially

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking this argument dismisses all large-scale analysis as meaningless
  **Clarification**: Tymoczko argues for realistic skepticism, not nihilism; theory can genuinely enrich experience when done well
