# Quick Definition

A measure of key distance that minimizes the motion of pitch classes regardless of their scale-degree roles, making fifth-related major keys and relative major/minor the closest neighbors.

# Core Definition

Pitch-class-changing (or scale-degree-changing) key distance minimizes the total chromatic displacement of pitch classes "irrespective of their scale-degree roles" (p. 362). The nearest major keys are related by fifth (sharing six of seven notes), while the nearest major/minor pairs are in the relative relationship (Figure 8.1.2). On the spiral diagram, these voice leadings are represented as *loops* rather than slides, because abstract voices change their scale-degree roles. Distance is given by iterations of the diatonic scale's basic voice leading: "either lowers the leading tone (clockwise) or raises the fourth scale degree (counterclockwise)" (p. 363). This model dates back to Heinichen's "Musicalische Circul," one of the earliest geometrical representations of musical structure (Figure 8.1.6).

# Prerequisites

- **Basic voice leading of scales** -- The concept of scales as collections of abstract voices

# Key Properties

1. Nearest major keys: related by fifth (sharing 6 of 7 notes)
2. Nearest major/minor pairs: relative relationship (e.g., C major and A minor)
3. Represented as loops on the spiral diagram (voices change scale-degree roles)
4. Distance measured by iterations of the basic voice leading (lowering leading tone or raising fourth degree)
5. Links "key distance" to pitch-class content: nearby keys share many common tones
6. Most relevant when different material is presented in two keys (e.g., sonata exposition)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Calculate Pitch-Class-Changing Distance:
1. Compare the pitch-class content of the two keys
2. Count the number of basic voice-leading steps needed to transform one collection into the other
3. Each step lowers one leading tone (flatward) or raises one fourth degree (sharpward)
4. Fewer steps = closer keys

# Context & Application

This notion of key distance is "implicit in classical modulatory norms" (p. 362) and most useful when different material is presented in two keys -- "for example in the first and second themes of a textbook sonata" (p. 362) -- or when composers have a wide variety of instruments. It underlies the traditional circle of fifths and Heinichen's eighteenth-century circular models.

Two important warnings: (1) there is inherent ambiguity in identifying scalar voice leadings, since any change of scale can be nullified by an equal-and-opposite change in surface voices; (2) "changes in scale degree do not imply determinate trajectories through musical space: instead, pitch classes simply take on new scale-degree roles without occupying any intermediate points" (p. 365).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming this distance measure reflects how "similar" keys sound
  **Correction**: Nearby keys share many notes but may sound quite different; the measure is about pitch-class content, not perceptual similarity

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the two key-distance models are competing alternatives
  **Clarification**: They measure genuinely different things, both musically relevant; the choice depends on the compositional context
