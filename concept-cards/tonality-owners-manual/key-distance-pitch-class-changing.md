# Quick Definition
A measure of key distance that minimizes the motion of pitch classes regardless of their scale-degree roles, making fifth-related major keys and relative major/minor the closest neighbors.

# Formal Definition
Pitch-class-changing (or scale-degree-changing) key distance minimizes the total chromatic displacement of pitch classes irrespective of which scale-degree roles they occupy before and after the modulation. The nearest major keys are related by fifth (sharing six of seven notes), while the nearest major/minor pairs are in the relative relationship (e.g., C major and A minor) -- Figure 8.1.2. On the spiral diagram, these voice leadings are represented as *loops* rather than slides, because abstract voices change their scale-degree roles. Distance is measured by iterations of the diatonic scale's basic voice leading: lowering the leading tone (clockwise) or raising the fourth degree (counterclockwise).

# Musical Context
This notion of key distance is implicit in classical modulatory norms and most useful when different material is presented in the two keys (e.g., first and second themes of a sonata), or when composers have a wide variety of instruments. It links "key distance" to pitch-class content, with nearby keys sharing many common tones. It underlies the traditional circle of fifths and Heinichen's "Musicalische Circul" (Figure 8.1.6).

# Common Confusions
- There is inherent ambiguity in identifying scalar voice leadings: any change of scale can be nullified by an equal-and-opposite change in surface voice motion. The best we can say is "if scales move like this, then surface voices move like that."
- The two distance models are not competing alternatives but measure genuinely different things, both of which are musically relevant.
