# Quick Definition

The retrograde of the classical ascending 5-6 sequence, producing a descending chromatic line and generating the third loop family (I-V-bVII-IV) -- the one progression shared significantly by both classical and popular traditions.

# Core Definition

The descending 6-5 sequence "is the retrograde of the classical tradition's ascending 5-6 sequence, giving rise to a descending chromatic line" (p. 63). It generates the third loop family (I-V-bVII-IV). "Classical composers embed the progression within a broadly functional movement from tonic to dominant whereas popular music is more likely to use it as an alternative to tonic-dominant functionality" (p. 64). The diatonic version is a common Renaissance idiom that can be decorated with a voice exchange creating an outer-voice canon (pp. 79-80).

# Key Properties

1. Retrograde of the ascending 5-6 sequence
2. Produces descending chromatic line
3. Generates I-V-bVII-IV and its subsets
4. Classical: embedded within tonic-to-dominant motion
5. Rock: used as an alternative to functionality
6. The one loop family with significant classical presence

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify:
1. Look for a descending chromatic bass or inner-voice line
2. Check for alternation between root-position and first-inversion triads (6-5)
3. Note whether the context is functional (tonic to dominant) or standalone (rock loop)

# Context & Application

The sequence bridges classical and popular practice, appearing in Beethoven, Schubert, rock songs, and Renaissance music (Morley, Grieg).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating descending 6-5 and ascending 5-6 as the same sequence
  **Correction**: They are retrogrades with different harmonic implications

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the sequence is purely classical
  **Clarification**: It bridges classical and popular traditions, with different contextual uses
