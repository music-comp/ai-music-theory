# Quick Definition
A retrograde of the classical ascending 5-6 sequence, producing a descending chromatic line and generating the third loop family (I-V-bVII-IV).

# Formal Definition
The descending 6-5 sequence is the retrograde of the classical tradition's ascending 5-6 sequence, giving rise to a descending chromatic line through closely related triads. The progression I-V-bVII-IV represents one cycle of this pattern in rock contexts. In classical music, the same sequence typically articulates a descent from tonic to dominant; in popular music, it often returns plagally to the tonic after just four chords. Classical composers *embed* the progression within functional tonic-dominant motion, whereas popular music uses it as an *alternative* to functionality.

# Musical Context
This is the one loop family that plays a significant role in both classical and popular traditions. It appears in Beethoven (Op. 31 no. 1, the *Waldstein*), Schubert (late G major quartet), and many other composers, as well as in rock songs from "Natural Woman" to "Sweet Child O' Mine." The diatonic version (without chromatic alterations) is a common Renaissance idiom that appears in Morley's "April Is in My Mistress' Face" and Grieg's "In My Homeland."

# Common Confusions
- The descending 6-5 and ascending 5-6 are retrogrades of each other, not the same sequence in different directions
- Classical and rock uses of the same progression differ in how it connects to the broader harmonic context (embedded vs. alternative to functionality)
- The progression generates two previously encountered three-chord subsets: I-bVII-IV and I-V-IV
