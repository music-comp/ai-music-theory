# Quick Definition
The special voice-leading property of the minor third (interval class 3), which divides the octave into exactly four equal parts, making its most efficient voice leading to its tritone transposition the same size as its voice exchange -- a symmetry that underlies the Ludwig schema.

# Formal Definition
The minor third is balanced between the unison and the tritone, being exactly one fourth of an octave (3 semitones out of 12). This means the most efficient voice leading connecting a minor third to its tritone transposition is exactly the same size as its smaller voice exchange. Two contrary-motion voices restricted to this interval class alternate between these two possibilities: (E-flat4, C5) to (C4, E-flat5) to (A3, F-sharp5) to (F-sharp3, A5) and so on. The diatonic third, being approximately one quarter of an octave, inherits this symmetry approximately, allowing for near-sequential patterns where one voice sounds thirds while the other alternates thirds and steps.

# Musical Context
This mathematical property is the foundation for the Ludwig schema and explains why contrary-motion patterns based on thirds are so ubiquitous in tonal music. It connects voice-leading geometry to Beethoven's compositional practice, showing how abstract mathematical properties manifest in concrete musical idioms.

# Common Confusions
- The symmetry is exact for the chromatic minor third but only approximate for the diatonic third -- this approximation is what makes the Ludwig a near-sequence rather than an exact one
- The property applies to dyads (two-voice texture) and is then enriched with harmonic context to produce the full Ludwig schema
- This is not a historical observation but a mathematical fact that explains why certain patterns recur across styles
