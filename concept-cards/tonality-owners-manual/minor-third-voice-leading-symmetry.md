# Quick Definition

The special voice-leading property of the minor third (interval class 3), which divides the octave into exactly four equal parts, making its most efficient voice leading to its tritone transposition the same size as its voice exchange -- a symmetry that underlies the Ludwig schema.

# Core Definition

"The minor third is interesting insofar as it is balanced between these two extremes, being halfway between the unison and the tritone -- or in other words exactly one fourth of an octave" (p. 458). This means "the most efficient voice leading connecting it to its tritone transposition is exactly the same size as its smaller voice exchange" (p. 459). Two contrary-motion voices restricted to this interval class alternate between these two possibilities: "(E-flat4, C5) to (C4, E-flat5) to (A3, F-sharp5) to (F-sharp3, A5) and so on" (p. 459). The diatonic third, being "approximately one quarter of an octave, inherits this symmetry in an approximate way, allowing for sequences in which one voice sounds thirds while the other alternates thirds and steps" (p. 459). This approximate inheritance is what makes the Ludwig "a near-sequence rather than an exact one."

# Key Properties

1. Minor third divides the octave into exactly four equal parts (3 semitones out of 12)
2. Tritone transposition distance equals voice exchange distance for this interval
3. Two contrary-motion voices restricted to minor thirds alternate between transposition and voice exchange
4. Diatonic third inherits this symmetry approximately, producing near-sequences
5. This is a mathematical fact, not a historical or stylistic observation

# Construction / Recognition

## To Observe the Symmetry:
1. Start with a minor third (e.g., E-flat, C)
2. Apply a voice exchange: voices move to (C, E-flat) -- total motion = 6 semitones
3. Apply tritone transposition to the original: voices move to (A, F-sharp) -- total motion = 6 semitones
4. Note that both operations produce the same total voice-leading distance
5. In diatonic context, the third alternates between 3 and 4 semitones, producing near-equality

# Context & Application

This mathematical property is the foundation for the Ludwig schema and explains why contrary-motion patterns based on thirds are ubiquitous in tonal music. It connects abstract voice-leading geometry to Beethoven's compositional practice, providing the theoretical basis for the most characteristic aspect of his style.

**Example 1** (p. 459): Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel's "April" from Das Jahr -- exact contrary-motion sequence exploiting minor-third symmetry, outer voices always moving by minor third (Figure 10.1.1).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Thinking the symmetry is exact for diatonic thirds
  **Correction**: It is exact only for chromatic minor thirds; diatonic thirds inherit it approximately, producing near-sequences

- **Error**: Treating this as a purely historical observation about Beethoven
  **Correction**: It is a mathematical fact about interval class 3 that explains why certain patterns recur across styles

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the property applies to triads rather than dyads
  **Clarification**: It applies to two-voice texture (dyads), which is then enriched with harmonic context to produce the full Ludwig schema

- **Confusion**: Confusing the minor-third property with the diminished-seventh chord's symmetry
  **Clarification**: They are related -- the dim7 chord stacks minor thirds -- but the property here concerns the voice-leading behavior of the dyadic minor third
