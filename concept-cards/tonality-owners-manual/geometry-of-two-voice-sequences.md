# Quick Definition
The classification of all harmonically consistent two-voice sequences using the dyadic spiral diagram: without voice exchanges, odd steps produce canonic sequences and even steps produce transpositional ones; with voice exchanges, this relationship reverses.

# Formal Definition
The isomorphism between sequence and voice leading allows spiral diagrams to explore sequential possibility. For two-voice harmonically consistent repeating contrapuntal patterns: without voice exchanges, an odd number of steps = canonic, even = transpositional; with an odd number of voice exchanges, even steps = canonic, odd = antiparallel/contrary-motion. The most common no-exchange pattern is single-step motion (the basic voice leading); with voice exchanges, the most common uses the two-step exchange (producing simple rounds on its own, or antiparallel fourth-fifth motion combined with one step). Larger motions on the circle produce less balanced intervals and are correspondingly rare.

# Musical Context
This classification exhausts the harmonically consistent two-voice possibilities. The most common canonic sequence is the basic voice leading (one clockwise step), appearing in a substantial proportion of baroque and classical sequences. Classical sequences usually feature descending fifths, while earlier styles more frequently use ascending fifths with passing tritones. The two-step voice exchange combined with one step produces the *Pathetique*-style antiparallel motion. Sequences with larger motions (five or seven steps) make awkward melodic leaps and are rare.

# Common Confusions
- The spiral diagram proves that transpositional, canonic, and contrary-motion are the only harmonically consistent two-voice sequence types; no others exist
- Adding voice exchanges flips the even/odd relationship between steps and sequence type
- Geometry justifies including permutation alongside transposition as a sequence-generating operation
