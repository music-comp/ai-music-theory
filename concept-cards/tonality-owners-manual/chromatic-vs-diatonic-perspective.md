# Quick Definition
Two contrasting analytical frameworks: the chromatic perspective treats pitch classes as the fundamental units, while the diatonic perspective embeds them within scale-based hierarchies, with each offering distinct advantages for understanding tonal music.

# Formal Definition
The chromatic perspective models triadic voice leadings directly in twelve-tone chromatic space, as in neo-Riemannian theory (Cohn 1997). It must distinguish between major and minor triads' voice-leading behavior (minor triad fifths ascend by semitone, major triad fifths by two semitones). The diatonic perspective postulates an intervening diatonic scale, allowing a unified description: every triad's fifth ascends by *scale step* to become the next root. The diatonic perspective also reveals hierarchical structure (chords inside scales inside chromatic space) that the chromatic perspective obscures. However, the chromatic perspective better captures the connection between Beethoven's procedures and nineteenth-century chromaticism. The two analyses thus highlight Beethoven's position at the fulcrum between classical and Romantic eras.

# Musical Context
Before writing this book, Tymoczko thought of scale degrees and pitch classes as ontologically different (scale degrees labeling movable positions, pitch classes labeling fixed notes). He now views them as "fundamentally analogous," with musical objects participating in complex hierarchies where any collection's output can serve as the input of the next (Figure P8.4).

# Examples
- Beethoven's Ninth, scherzo (Figure P8.1): Chromatically, major and minor triads move differently; diatonically, all triads move the same way (fifth up by scale step).
- "Doe, a deer": The same reasoning that analyzes this as a single diatonic pattern moving up by scale step (rather than by alternating semitones and whole tones) applies to Beethoven's passage.

# Common Confusions
- Neither perspective is "correct" -- they offer complementary views of the same musical phenomena. The choice depends on what aspects of the music one wishes to highlight.
- The diatonic perspective does not deny chromaticism; it embeds it within a hierarchical framework where scales themselves move chromatically.
