# Quick Definition

Any collection of notes can be associated with two scales: an external "enclosing scale" (chromatic, diatonic, or contextually relevant) and the "intrinsic scale" formed from its own notes compressed into a single octave.

# Core Definition

"The deep point is that any collection of notes, from chord to motive to set, can be associated with two different scales: an external or *enclosing scale* -- chromatic, diatonic, or some other contextually relevant collection -- and the *intrinsic scale* formed from its own notes" (p. 41). Transposition along the enclosing scale preserves intervals as measured along that scale; transposition along the intrinsic scale "generalize[s] the notion of chordal inversion," preserving intervals as measured along the chord (p. 41). "The intrinsic scale is a missing link between traditional tonal theory and modern set theory, obscured by the habit of conceiving sets in exclusively chromatic terms" (p. 41).

# Prerequisites

- **Transposition Along a Collection** -- Understanding transposition along different collections is essential

# Key Properties

1. Every collection has both an intrinsic and an enclosing scale
2. Transposing along the enclosing scale is "ordinary" transposition
3. Transposing along the intrinsic scale generalizes chordal inversion
4. The intrinsic scale preserves voicing (spacing pattern in chordal steps)
5. Traditional theory often concerned itself with the intrinsic scale (e.g., close/open position)
6. Modern set theory largely focused on the enclosing (chromatic) scale
7. The intrinsic scale connects these two traditions

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify the Two Scales:
1. Take a chord or collection of notes
2. The enclosing scale is the contextually relevant larger collection (chromatic, diatonic, etc.)
3. Compress the chord's notes into a single octave to form the intrinsic scale
4. Transposing along the intrinsic scale shifts each voice by the same number of chordal steps

# Context & Application

The traditional method of forming inversions by moving bass to soprano destroys characteristic spacing (Figure P2.5). The intrinsic-scale method preserves it, producing a series of voicings sharing the same abstract registral arrangement (Figure P2.6). This approach is familiar in guitar pedagogy, used to construct inversions keeping the hand in roughly the same position. It also plays a role in traditional figured-bass theory.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 38, Figure P2.5 vs. P2.6): Traditional inversions (moving bottom note to top) destroy spacing; intrinsic-scale inversions preserve it.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Forming inversions by moving the bass note to the soprano (traditional method)
  **Correction**: This destroys characteristic spacing; use transposition along the intrinsic scale instead to preserve voicing

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking "inversion" always means pitch-class inversion (turning intervals upside down)
  **Clarification**: Tymoczko distinguishes pitch-class inversion from registral inversion (rearranging notes in register); the intrinsic scale relates to registral inversion
