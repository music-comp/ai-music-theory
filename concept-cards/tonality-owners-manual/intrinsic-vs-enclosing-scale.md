# Quick Definition
Any collection of notes can be associated with two scales: the external "enclosing scale" (chromatic, diatonic, etc.) within which it sits, and the "intrinsic scale" formed from its own notes.

# Formal Definition
The enclosing (or extrinsic) scale is the contextually relevant larger collection -- chromatic, diatonic, or otherwise -- through which a chord or motive moves. The intrinsic scale is formed from the notes of the chord or motive itself, compressed into a single octave. To transpose along the enclosing scale preserves intervals as measured along that scale; to transpose along the intrinsic scale generalizes the notion of chordal inversion, preserving intervals as measured along the chord. The intrinsic scale is described as "a missing link between traditional tonal theory and modern set theory."

# Musical Context
Recent music theory has largely concerned itself with transposition along the enclosing scale, whereas traditional theory often concerned itself with the intrinsic scale -- either implicitly (as a means of obtaining efficient voice leading) or explicitly (close and open position). The intrinsic scale connects chords related by pitch-class inversion and voiced the same way; such spacing-preserving progressions invariably preserve the distance between at least one pair of voices. This connects to neo-Riemannian theory.

# Common Confusions
- "Inversion" in this context can mean either pitch-class inversion (turning intervals upside down) or registral inversion (rearranging notes in register) -- Tymoczko carefully distinguishes these
- The traditional method of forming inversions by moving bass to soprano destroys characteristic spacing; the intrinsic-scale method preserves it (Figure P2.5 vs. P2.6)
- The intrinsic scale is obscured by the habit of conceiving sets in exclusively chromatic terms
