# Quick Definition
The prima prattica: the strict contrapuntal style codified in the music of Palestrina and his contemporaries, featuring rigorous control of nonharmonic tones through the SNAP system.

# Formal Definition
The first practice (prima prattica) represents the first great era of nonharmonic control, based on the SNAP system of suspensions, neighbors, anticipations, and passing tones. Though often said to originate with Josquin, this style stabilized in the music of Palestrina and contemporaries such as Lassus and Byrd. It is characterized by almost complete systematicity in dissonance treatment: nonharmonic tones rarely obscure the entire duration of a consonance, and virtually all dissonances conform to the SNAP categories. The sole exception is the cambiata as the surviving incomplete neighbor.

# Musical Context
The first practice served as the standard of contrapuntal correctness against which the seconda prattica (second practice) of Monteverdi and others was defined. Giovanni Artusi's critique of Monteverdi represents the most famous defense of first-practice norms. The Artusi-Monteverdi debate fundamentally concerns whether music should be judged by inviolable rules or by aesthetic effects -- a tension that persisted through the twentieth century.

# Common Confusions
- The first practice is not purely consonant harmony. It retains idioms that stubbornly resist nonharmonic reduction, including Renaissance sevenths and certain 64 chords. As Tymoczko puts it, Palestrina wrote "just before and just after an imaginary consonant era."
- Counterpoint textbooks tend to take Palestrina's practice as representative of a broader and more varied tradition, but Josquin, Palestrina, and other composers actually speak somewhat different musical dialects.
