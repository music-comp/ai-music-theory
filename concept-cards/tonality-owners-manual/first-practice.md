# Quick Definition

The prima prattica: the strict contrapuntal style codified in Palestrina's music, featuring rigorous control of nonharmonic tones through the SNAP system. The first great era of nonharmonic control.

# Core Definition

The first practice represents the first great era of nonharmonic control, based on the SNAP system (p. 211). Though often said to originate with Josquin, it stabilized in the music of Palestrina and contemporaries such as Lassus and Byrd. It is characterized by almost complete systematicity in dissonance treatment: nonharmonic tones rarely obscure the entire duration of a consonance, and virtually all dissonances conform to the SNAP categories. The sole exception is the cambiata. Palestrina was "one of the great proofreaders in human history" (p. 212).

# Key Properties

1. Based on the SNAP system (suspensions, neighbors, anticipations, passing tones)
2. Virtually all nonharmonic tones can be labeled and removed algorithmically (p. 213)
3. Nonharmonic tones rarely obscure the entire duration of a consonance
4. Consonances are "real sonic objects usually sounding at some point during their lifespan" (p. 213)
5. The cambiata is the sole surviving incomplete neighbor
6. Despite its systematicity, it retains idioms (Renaissance sevenths, 6/4 chords) that resist reduction

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify First-Practice Style:
1. Check that dissonances conform to SNAP categories
2. Verify consonances are audible at some point during their duration
3. Look for the characteristic cadential role of suspensions
4. Confirm the absence of incomplete neighbors (except cambiata)
5. Note that the style may still contain irreducible Renaissance sevenths

# Context & Application

The first practice served as the standard against which Monteverdi's seconda prattica was defined. The Artusi-Monteverdi debate concerns whether music should be judged by inviolable rules or aesthetic effects. Tymoczko argues Artusi was not simply wrong: "the classical tradition incorporated some of Monteverdi's specific innovations, but subjected them to rules as inviolable as those of the prima prattica" (p. 235). The "idealized consonant style of the counterpoint textbooks never actually existed" -- Palestrina wrote "just before and just after an imaginary consonant era" (p. 220).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating the first practice as purely consonant harmony
  **Correction**: It retains Renaissance sevenths and 6/4 chords that resist nonharmonic reduction (p. 220)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Equating Palestrina's style with all Renaissance counterpoint
  **Clarification**: Ockeghem, Josquin, and Palestrina speak different musical dialects -- counterpoint textbooks take Palestrina's practice as representative of a broader, more varied tradition (p. 211)
