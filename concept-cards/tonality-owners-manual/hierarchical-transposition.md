# Quick Definition

A model of musical structure in which transformations operate at multiple nested levels simultaneously -- chords moving inside scales that are themselves moving -- producing hierarchically self-similar patterns.

# Core Definition

Hierarchical transposition occurs when a repeating contrapuntal pattern at one structural level (e.g., triads moving within a diatonic scale) is combined with a repeating pattern at a higher level (e.g., diatonic scales moving within the chromatic aggregate). The result is a nested pair of basic voice leadings operating at different structural levels. Tymoczko uses the notation T for diatonic transposition and **T** for chromatic transposition, with subscripts indicating the size of the motion. This perspective supersedes a "flat" view in which scales of various sizes sit side-by-side, each mapping scale degrees directly to pitch classes, in favor of a hierarchical view where "any collection's output can serve as the input of the next" (p. 360, Fig. P8.4). This idea is "simple when written on the page but is extremely difficult to implement at the speed of music, whether in improvisation or at the slower pace of notation" (p. 360).

# Key Properties

1. Two or more basic voice leadings operate simultaneously at different structural levels
2. T represents diatonic transposition, **T** represents chromatic transposition
3. Replaces a "flat" model (scales mapped directly to pitch classes) with a hierarchical model
4. Any collection's output can serve as the input of the next in the hierarchy
5. Provides a unified perspective on phenomena from modulation to subchordal voice leading
6. Generalizes easily to any chord in any scale

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Hierarchical Transposition:
1. Identify a repeating contrapuntal pattern at the chordal level (e.g., triads connected by ascending diatonic step)
2. Check whether the underlying scale is also moving according to a repeating pattern
3. Use spiral diagrams to visualize motion at both levels simultaneously
4. Compare with a neo-Riemannian analysis -- if the neo-Riemannian requires different transformations for major and minor triads, a hierarchical analysis may offer a simpler unified explanation

# Context & Application

This concept generalizes the traditional understanding of modulation to encompass any situation where collections move inside larger collections. The hierarchical view is contrasted with neo-Riemannian theory, which models the same passages with nonhierarchical chromatic transformations operating directly on chromatic triads without mediation by the diatonic collection. Neither approach is correct as a matter of simple fact, but Tymoczko's hierarchical analysis offers standard harmonic procedures, a unified explanation of voice leadings, and easy generalization to any chord in any scale (p. 360). Music theory can help musicians internalize this abstract perspective, "explaining how to replace specific idioms with more general techniques applying to all levels of the collectional hierarchy" (p. 360).

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 357-359, Fig. P8.1): Beethoven's Ninth Symphony, second movement, mm. 143-176 -- eighteen descending-third progressions arranged in four-chord groups, with chordal voice leading taking clockwise steps on the triadic spiral while scalar voice leading moves counterclockwise on the scale spiral (Fig. P8.3).

**Example 2** (p. 360, Fig. P8.4): Flat vs. hierarchical models of chord and scale -- the flat model maps scales directly to pitch classes, while the hierarchical model allows collections to serve as inputs for higher-level collections.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Equating hierarchical transposition with simple modulation
  **Correction**: It describes any situation where transformations at one level are embedded within transformations at another, including cases that do not involve traditional key changes

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the hierarchical view is a perceptual claim about what listeners hear
  **Clarification**: It is a conceptual framework for organizing transformations at multiple levels; listeners do not necessarily hear all levels simultaneously
