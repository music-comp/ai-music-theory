# Quick Definition
A model of musical structure in which transformations operate at multiple nested levels simultaneously -- chords moving inside scales that are themselves moving -- producing hierarchically self-similar patterns.

# Formal Definition
Hierarchical transposition occurs when a repeating contrapuntal pattern at one structural level (e.g., triads moving within a diatonic scale) is combined with a repeating pattern at a higher level (e.g., diatonic scales moving within the chromatic aggregate). The result is a nested pair of basic voice leadings operating at different structural levels. Tymoczko uses the notation T for diatonic transposition and **T** for chromatic transposition, with subscripts indicating the size of the motion. This perspective supersedes a "flat" view in which scales of various sizes sit side-by-side, each mapping scale degrees directly to pitch classes, in favor of a hierarchical view where any collection's output can serve as the input of the next (Figure P8.4).

# Musical Context
This concept generalizes the traditional understanding of modulation to encompass any situation where collections move inside larger collections. It provides a unified perspective on phenomena from modulation to subchordal voice leading. The idea is simple to state but extremely difficult to implement at the speed of music, whether in improvisation or notation.

# Examples
- Beethoven's Ninth Symphony, second movement (mm. 143--176, Figure P8.1): Eighteen descending-third progressions arranged in four-chord groups. The chordal voice leading takes clockwise steps in the triadic spiral (third-related triads connected by ascending diatonic step), while the scalar voice leading moves counterclockwise in the scale spiral (fifth-related scales connected by descending chromatic step) -- Figure P8.3.
- Chopin's D major prelude: Sequential units articulate I--IV--V--[vi or I]--V/V--V progressions ascending by fifth along the D major scale.

# Common Confusions
- Hierarchical transposition is not simply "modulation": it describes any situation where transformations at one level are embedded within transformations at another, including cases that do not involve traditional key changes.
- The hierarchical view is a conceptual framework, not a perceptual claim: listeners do not necessarily hear all levels simultaneously.
