# Quick Definition

The expressive range of the scherzo extends far beyond the "playful and humorous" of its etymological meaning, encompassing characters from sparkling and witty to tragic, heroic, and diabolical, with rapid tempo as the only consistently shared feature.

# Core Definition

The restriction of the scherzo to the playful and humorous is not factual. Even the tempo is not decisive -- Beethoven's own metronome indications for rapid middle movements range around dotted quarter-note = 100, which is his average scherzo tempo. However, these middle movements, with only the rapid tempo in common, differ very widely in expressive qualities. A survey of scherzos by Beethoven, Schubert, Mendelssohn, Schumann, Chopin, Brahms, Tchaikovsky, Berlioz, Bruckner, Mahler, Reger, Debussy, and Ravel reveals an enormous range of character.

# Prerequisites

- **Scherzo form**: Understanding the structural framework within which character operates

# Key Properties

- Rapid tempo is the common element, but expressive quality varies enormously
- Character types include: vivacious, sparkling, brilliant, witty, enthusiastic, ecstatic, ardent, fiery, energetic, vehement, impassioned, dramatic, tragic, heroic, gigantesque, diabolical, grotesque
- Beethoven seldom uses the title "Scherzo" for movements in minor
- Among Brahms's eight chamber music scherzos, five are in minor
- Mode, metre, and tempo alone do not determine whether a movement is a scherzo

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify the character through tempo markings, dynamics, articulation, and texture
2. Do not assume a scherzo must be playful -- assess the actual expressive quality
3. Check whether the movement is titled "Scherzo" or carries another label (Allegro, Vivace, Presto)
4. Note that character evolved significantly from early (lighter) to later (more intense) scherzos

# Context & Application

The character of the scherzo evolved significantly. While early scherzos tended toward lighter moods, later composers expanded the expressive range dramatically. Beethoven's later works, and those of Brahms, Bruckner, and Mahler, use the scherzo as a vehicle for intense and complex expression. The wide latitude for character means that analysis must assess each scherzo individually rather than applying generic assumptions.

# Common Errors

- Assuming a scherzo must be playful or humorous
- Assuming a scherzo must be in major mode
- Applying generic character assumptions without examining the specific work

# Common Confusions

- **"Scherzo" = joke**: The etymological meaning is misleading; the genre encompasses the full range of human expression.
- **Mode as indicator**: Minor-mode scherzos are common (especially in Brahms); Beethoven's avoidance of the title "Scherzo" for minor movements is his personal convention, not a rule.
