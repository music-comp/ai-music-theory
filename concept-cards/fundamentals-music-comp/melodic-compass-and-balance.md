# Quick Definition

The range (compass) of a melody and the compositional principle of balancing wide or narrow compass through complementary movement in subsequent phrases, particularly between antecedent and consequent.

# Core Definition

Melodic compass refers to the total range spanned by a melody. Schoenberg observes that "there are many melodies whose compass is very small" -- sometimes remaining within a fifth or sixth. When the antecedent remains within a narrow compass, "the consequent ascends to a climax," providing compensating breadth. Conversely, "melodies whose compass at the beginning is very broad are likely to achieve balance by returning to a middle register." The principle holds that "much variety can be achieved within a relatively small compass, though the extension of the compass is often a defence against monotony" (Ch. 6, p. 25).

# Key Properties

1. Compass = the total range spanned by a melody
2. Many effective melodies have very small compass (within a fifth or sixth)
3. Narrow compass in antecedent often leads to climactic expansion in consequent
4. Broad compass at the beginning usually requires return to middle register for balance
5. After a climax, the melody typically recedes, balancing the compass
6. Small compass does not preclude effective variation -- register, direction, and contour provide variety
7. Extension of compass serves as a defense against monotony
8. Balance must be considered across the whole period, not just the antecedent

# Construction / Recognition

## To Manage Compass

1. Determine the compass of the antecedent (narrow or broad)
2. If narrow (within a fifth or sixth), plan the consequent to ascend to a climax, providing compensating breadth
3. If broad at the beginning, plan a return to middle register in subsequent phrases
4. After any climactic point, provide a receding motion for balance
5. Use register, direction, and contour changes to create variety within a limited compass

## To Recognize Compass Balance

1. Measure the total range of the melody or melodic section
2. Compare the compass of antecedent and consequent
3. Look for compensating motion: narrow followed by broad, or high followed by middle/low
4. Identify climactic points and subsequent receding motion

# Context & Application

The management of melodic compass is an essential element of period construction. The composer must plan the compass across both halves of the period to create a satisfying shape. The principle of balance -- compensating high with low, narrow with broad -- applies equally to antecedent construction and to the relationship between antecedent and consequent. This is distinct from symmetry; balance means that extremes of register are compensated by return to a central area.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using a uniformly narrow or uniformly broad compass across the entire period
  **Correction**: Plan compass changes between antecedent and consequent for balance

- **Error**: Failing to recede after a climactic expansion
  **Correction**: After reaching a climactic point, allow the melody to return to a central register

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: A narrow melodic compass is a weakness
  **Clarification**: Some of the finest melodies operate within a fifth or sixth; small compass does not preclude variety

- **Confusion**: Balance means symmetry
  **Clarification**: Balance means that extremes of register are compensated by return to a central area, not that antecedent and consequent must mirror each other

- **Confusion**: Compass management applies only to the antecedent
  **Clarification**: The compass of the whole period, not just the antecedent, must be considered when planning the theme
