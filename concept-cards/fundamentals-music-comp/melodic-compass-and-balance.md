# Quick Definition
The range (compass) of a melody and the compositional principle of balancing wide or narrow compass through complementary movement in subsequent phrases, particularly between antecedent and consequent.

# Formal Definition
Melodic compass refers to the total range spanned by a melody. Schoenberg observes that many melodies have very small compass, sometimes remaining within a fifth or sixth. When the antecedent remains within a narrow compass, the consequent often ascends to a climax, providing compensating breadth. Conversely, melodies whose compass at the beginning is very broad are likely to achieve balance by returning to a middle register. Much variety can be achieved within a relatively small compass, though extension of the compass is often a defense against monotony.

# Construction/Recognition
- Narrow compass in antecedent (within a fifth or sixth) often leads to climactic expansion in the consequent
- Broad compass at the beginning usually requires return to middle register for balance
- After a climax, the melody typically recedes, balancing the compass
- Small compass does not preclude effective variation -- register, direction, and contour offer variety
- Extension of compass serves as a defense against monotony

# Musical Context
The management of melodic compass is an essential element of period construction. The composer must plan the compass across both halves of the period to create satisfying shape. The principle of balance -- compensating high with low, narrow with broad -- applies equally to antecedent construction and to the relationship between antecedent and consequent.

# Common Confusions
- A narrow melodic compass is not a weakness; some of the finest melodies operate within a fifth or sixth.
- Balance does not mean symmetry; it means that extremes of register are compensated by return to a central area.
- The compass of the whole period, not just the antecedent, must be considered when planning the theme.
