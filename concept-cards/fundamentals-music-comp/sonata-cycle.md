# Quick Definition
A multi-movement work (sonata, quartet, symphony, concerto) of two or more movements of differing character, unified by key relationships (first and last movements share the same tonic) and motival connections that may be evident or disguised with the utmost subtlety.

# Core Definition
The concept of the sonata implies a cycle of two or more movements of differing character. Contrast of key, tempo, metre, form, and expressive character distinguish the various movements. Unity is provided by key relationships (the first and last movements use the same tonic, and intervening movements are related to this tonic) and through motival relationships, which may be clearly evident or disguised with the utmost subtlety. Before Haydn, all movements were normally in a single tonality; the Viennese classic masters introduced more variety by using other related keys for middle movements.

# Prerequisites
- Small ternary form
- Theme and variations

# Key Properties
- Two or more movements of differing character
- Normally three or four movements, though examples range from two to seven
- First and last movements usually in rapid tempo
- Intervening movements: slow (Allegretto to Adagio/Largo/Grave) and moderately fast (minuet/scherzo)
- First movement (and often last) typically uses sonata-allegro form
- Last movement frequently in rondo form
- Unity through key relationships and motival connections
- Viennese classics introduced related keys for middle movements

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the number of movements and their tempos
2. Note key relationships: first and last movements share the tonic
3. Identify the form of each movement (sonata-allegro, rondo, variations, minuet/scherzo)
4. Look for motival connections between movements (may be subtle)
5. Note contrasts of key, tempo, metre, form, and character

# Context & Application
The sonata cycle is the governing structural principle for the great majority of sonatas, string quartets, symphonies, and concertos since Haydn. Its flexibility allows for enormous variety in the character, number, and ordering of movements.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1: Allegro (f), Adagio (F), Menuetto (f), Prestissimo (f) -- standard four-movement plan
- Beethoven, Op. 54: starts "In tempo d'un Menuetto" -- unconventional first movement
- Beethoven, Op. 26: begins with Andante con Variazioni
- Beethoven, Op. 131: seven movements including an Adagio fugue, transitions, and varying tempos
- Beethoven, Op. 111: rare example of slow last movement (Adagio molto)
- Beethoven, Op. 31/3: absence of slow movement permits both minuet and scherzo

# Common Errors
- Assuming all movements must be in the same key (middle movements use related keys)
- Treating the number and ordering of movements as fixed rather than flexible
- Neglecting motival connections between movements

# Common Confusions
- The term "sonata" refers to the multi-movement cycle, not to the sonata-allegro form used within individual movements
- While three or four movements are normal, Beethoven's examples range from two to seven
- The Viennese classic masters' innovation was using different but related keys for middle movements, not the multi-movement concept itself
