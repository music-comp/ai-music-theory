# Quick Definition
A multi-movement work (sonata, quartet, symphony, concerto) of two or more movements of differing character, unified by key relationships and motival connections.

# Formal Definition
The concept of the sonata implies a cycle of two or more movements of differing character. Contrast of key, tempo, metre, form, and expressive character distinguish the various movements. Unity is provided by key relationships (the first and last movements use the same tonic, and intervening movements are related to this tonic) and through motival relationships, which may be clearly evident or disguised with the utmost subtlety.

# Construction/Recognition
- Normally three or four movements, though examples range from two to seven
- First and last movements are usually in rapid tempo
- Intervening movements fall into two types: slow (Allegretto to Adagio/Largo/Grave) and moderately fast (minuet or scherzo)
- Before Haydn, all movements were normally in a single tonality; the Viennese classic masters introduced related keys for middle movements
- The first movement (and often the last) typically uses sonata-allegro form
- The last movement is frequently a rondo form
- Occasionally a movement is a Theme with Variations

# Musical Context
The sonata cycle is the governing structural principle for the great majority of sonatas, string quartets, symphonies, and concertos since Haydn. Its flexibility allows for enormous variety in the character, number, and ordering of movements.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1: Allegro (f, 2/2), Adagio (F, 3/4), Menuetto (f, 3/4), Prestissimo (f)
- Beethoven, Op. 54: starts "In tempo d'un Menuetto" (unconventional first movement)
- Beethoven, Op. 26: begins with "Andante con Variazioni"
- Beethoven, Op. 27/2: opens with "Adagio sostenuto"
- Beethoven, Op. 111: slow last movement ("Adagio molto"), a rare exception
- Beethoven, Op. 31/3: absence of slow movement permits both a minuet and a scherzo
- Beethoven, Op. 131: seven movements
- Beethoven, Op. 132: five movements including a movement in Lydian mode

# Common Confusions
- "Sonata form" (the form of a single movement) should not be confused with the "sonata cycle" (the multi-movement structure)
- The number and ordering of movements is not fixed; Beethoven's practice shows extraordinary variety
- The term "first movement form" for sonata-allegro is somewhat misleading, as this form may also be used for last movements
