# Quick Definition
The necessary changes to the transition passage in the recapitulation section, redirecting its harmonic course from the original contrasting key to the tonic region.

# Formal Definition
At an appropriate point the transition turns toward a different region, often the subdominant (major or minor), and continues indirectly to the upbeat chord, usually by means of sectional transpositions of the original material. The transition undergoes the most significant modification in the recapitulation because its original harmonic destination (the contrasting key) must be replaced with the tonic key.

# Construction/Recognition
Common strategies for modifying the transition in recapitulation:
- Changing the harmonic direction at a specific point while keeping earlier material intact
- Transposing sections of the transition down a fifth (or up a fourth) to arrive at the tonic instead of the dominant
- Adding sequence-like repetitions to redirect the modulation
- Shortening or lengthening the transition as needed
- In some cases, completely replacing the transition with new material
- Adding accidentals to prepare for different transposition levels

# Musical Context
The transition modification is the single most essential change in any recapitulation. Without it, the subordinate group would return in the wrong key, defeating the tonal resolution that is the formal purpose of the recapitulation.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: transition repeated unchanged except for a two-measure reduction and minor modification of the last measure; the E chord's significance changes from I of E to V in A
- Beethoven, Op. 22-IV: transition starts exactly as before; in the fifth and sixth measures (m. 134-5) an added sequence-like repetition changes the modulation's course
- Beethoven, Op. 18/6-IV: transition undergoes modification from the very beginning; accidentals in the first four measures (m. 132) prepare for transposition up a third instead of the expected fourth; two measures added in m. 145
- Beethoven, Op. 13-III: original transition (m. 18-24) completely disappears; instead, the second half of the theme provides motive-forms for a new transitional segment (m. 129-34)

# Common Confusions
- The transition often changes at a specific "pivot point" partway through, not from its very beginning; recognizing where the divergence occurs is key to analysis
- Transposing the entire transition down a fifth does not always work because the harmonic relationships may not be symmetrical
- In minor key movements, the transition modifications may be much more extensive than simple transposition
