# Quick Definition
The modifications applied to the transition in the recapitulation, where it is usually heightened and lengthened to provide the only contrast to the prevailing tonic region -- redirected to lead to the tonic rather than to a contrasting key, but compensating with enhanced dramatic effect.

# Core Definition
Since no modulation is necessary in the recapitulation, one might expect the transition to disappear. On the contrary, its effect is usually heightened, and it is often lengthened. Unless the subordinate group contains modulatory elements, the transition now provides the only contrast to the tonic region which governs the entire recapitulation and coda. Thus its value as a contrast becomes more significant. At an appropriate point the transition turns toward a different region, often the subdominant (major or minor), and continues indirectly to the upbeat chord.

# Key Properties
- Usually heightened and lengthened, not eliminated
- Provides the only contrast to the prevailing tonic region
- Redirected: often turns toward subdominant (major or minor)
- Continues indirectly to the upbeat chord through sectional transpositions
- In simpler cases, redirection is the only change
- In more complex cases, reconstruction may be extensive
- Minor key movements may require particularly far-reaching changes

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify where the transition begins in the recapitulation
2. Compare with the exposition's transition to find the point of divergence
3. Note the new harmonic direction (typically toward subdominant)
4. Track how the transition reaches the upbeat chord for the subordinate group in tonic
5. Note any lengthening, shortening, or reconstruction

# Context & Application
The transition's heightened role in the recapitulation is one of the most important artistic features of sonata-allegro form. Without the tonal journey from tonic to dominant, the transition must find other means of providing contrast -- through harmonic color, dynamic intensification, or structural elaboration.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I: transition begins to change at m. 255; from m. 258 on, repeated partly a fifth lower and partly a fourth higher
- Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I: new transitional segment replaces m. 13-20, but arrives at exactly the same point (m. 21 = m. 155); remainder unchanged and untransposed
- Beethoven, Op. 7-I: leading theme diverted in fifteenth measure toward subdominant region; transition eight measures shorter
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I (m. 109): transition reconstructed using same motive-forms above a different harmonic skeleton
- Beethoven, Op. 10/1-I (m. 191-214): transition substantially intact, now leads to V of f (v) instead of original destination

# Common Errors
- Eliminating the transition in the recapitulation because modulation seems unnecessary
- Failing to redirect the transition to reach the tonic region
- Making the transition identical to the exposition version, missing the opportunity for enhanced contrast

# Common Confusions
- The transition's heightened importance in the recapitulation is counterintuitive; despite less modulation being needed, the transition is usually lengthened
- The subdominant region is often used as an intermediate destination, providing tonal color before returning to tonic
- In some cases, the transition is shortened (Op. 7-I); in others, it is completely reconstructed (Op. 2/1-I); the treatment depends on the individual work
