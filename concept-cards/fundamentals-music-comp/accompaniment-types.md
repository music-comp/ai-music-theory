# Quick Definition
Schoenberg identifies several distinct accompaniment types: chorale-like, figurated (broken chords), intermittent, afterbeat, and complementary, each with specific textural and rhythmic characteristics.

# Formal Definition
Beyond the basic chorale-like and figurated types, Schoenberg identifies several additional accompaniment categories. Intermittent accompaniment presents harmony only once per measure or once per several measures, either sustained or as a short chord. Afterbeat harmony places chords on weak beats, including syncopations. Complementary rhythm fills gaps in the melody's movement to maintain the motus (regular subdivision of the measure). These types may appear in combination, and many hybrid cases exist in practice.

# Construction/Recognition
- Intermittent: harmony once per measure or less; sustained (Op. 2/3-I, m. 97-108) or short chord at beginning of measure (Op. 10/1-I, m. 1-3) or during rests in the melody (Op. 2/2-III, m. 1-2)
- Afterbeat: chords on weak beats, including syncopations (Op. 2/1-I, m. 2-8; Op. 2/2, Scherzo; Op. 14/1-I, m. 1-3)
- Complementary: one voice fills gaps in another's movement, maintaining motus (Op. 14/2-I, m. 1-4; Op. 109-I, m. 1-8; Exs. 67a, b)
- Hybrid cases combining multiple types (Op. 10/3-IV, m. 17-22; Op. 31/2-III, m. 17-21)
- Some complementary cases derive from semi-contrapuntal devices (Exs. 45d, 66a, 67c)

# Musical Context
The variety of accompaniment types available allows the composer to match the accompaniment precisely to the character and needs of the theme. Intermittent accompaniment provides maximum transparency, afterbeat harmony creates rhythmic vitality, and complementary rhythm ensures continuous motion. The choice of type should be governed by the demands of the theme's character, the harmonic clarity required, and the instrument's capabilities.

# Examples
- Intermittent sustained: Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I, m. 97-108; Ex. 58h
- Intermittent short chord: Op. 10/1-I, m. 1-3; Op. 10/2-I, m. 1-4; Exs. 58c, f
- Afterbeat: Op. 2/1-I, m. 2-8, 41-46; Op. 2/2, Scherzo; Op. 14/1-I, m. 1-3
- Complementary: Op. 14/2-I, m. 1-4, 26-27; Op. 109-I, m. 1-8; Exs. 67a, b
- Hybrid: Op. 10/3-IV, m. 17-22; Op. 54-I, m. 1-2-5-6

# Common Confusions
- These types are not rigid categories; many passages combine features of multiple types or shift between them.
- Intermittent accompaniment does not mean weak accompaniment; even sparse harmonization can be highly effective.
- Complementary rhythm is not the same as counterpoint; it fills rhythmic gaps without necessarily having thematic independence.
