# Quick Definition
Piano style does not require strict preservation of the number of voices as in four-part chorale harmony; the number may vary freely, and individual voices may be doubled in octaves provided the basic voice leading is free from parallel octaves.

# Formal Definition
Piano style allows flexible treatment of the number of voices, unlike the strict four-part writing of chorale harmony. A piece that starts in three or four parts will use that number rather consistently, but fewer voices may appear (as if some rested) and more may enter for crescendo, accentuation, climax, or other special effects. If the basic voice-leading is free from parallel octaves, each individual voice can be doubled in octaves, especially if the doubling is consistently maintained. This principle applies to bass, melody, and even inner voices.

# Construction/Recognition
- Number of voices may vary throughout a piece
- Fewer voices: create transparency (as if some voices rest)
- More voices: create fullness for climax, accent, or crescendo
- Octave doubling permitted if basic voice leading avoids parallels
- Consistent doubling is preferred over sporadic doubling
- Bass doubling creates variety or contrast through register change
- Melody doubling is common
- Inner voice doubling may appear incidentally for special effect

# Musical Context
The freedom of voice leading in piano accompaniment reflects the practical realities of keyboard writing. Unlike choral or orchestral writing where each part is assigned to a specific performer, piano writing can freely add or subtract voices as the musical context demands. This flexibility is essential for achieving the dynamic range and textural variety that piano music requires.

# Common Confusions
- Freedom from strict voice count does not mean freedom from good voice leading; parallel octaves in the basic framework remain to be avoided.
- Doubling in octaves is not the same as parallel octaves; doubling means consistent reinforcement of a single voice, not independent voices moving in parallel.
- The number of voices changing is a feature, not a defect, of piano style.
