# Quick Definition
The principle that motive features differ in structural importance, and that effective variation requires preserving the more important features while changing the less important ones, with the relative importance depending on the compositional objective.

# Formal Definition
Variation requires changing some of the less-important features and preserving some of the more-important ones. Determining which features are more important depends on the compositional objective. Preservation of rhythmic features effectively produces coherence, though monotony cannot be avoided without slight changes. Through this selective preservation and alteration, a variety of motive-forms adapted to every formal function can be produced.

# Construction/Recognition
The hierarchy is context-dependent, but general principles apply:
- **Rhythm** tends to be the most cohesion-producing feature; preserving rhythm while changing other elements maintains recognizability
- **Contour/shape** can be significant even when rhythm and intervals change
- **Intervals** can be altered substantially while maintaining motive identity if rhythm is preserved
- **Harmony** provides the framework within which melodic variation operates

The composer determines the hierarchy based on what the passage needs: unity, variety, development, or contrast.

# Musical Context
This principle is central to practical composition. When constructing a theme, the composer must decide which features of the basic motive to treat as essential (preserved) and which as variable (subject to change). The decision shapes the character and direction of the entire piece.

# Examples
- Ex. 32: rhythm strictly preserved while intervals and direction change, producing closely related motive-forms
- Beethoven's Fifth Symphony: the rhythmic profile (short-short-short-long) is the primary preserved feature
- Brahms's Fourth Symphony: the intervallic content (thirds) is the primary preserved feature
- Beethoven Op. 2/3-IV: the contour (upward sweep and return) is the primary preserved feature

# Common Confusions
- There is no fixed, universal hierarchy of feature importance; it depends on the specific motive and compositional context
- Preserving rhythm is generally the most effective way to maintain coherence, but it is not always the most important feature
- "Less important" does not mean "unimportant"; every feature contributes to the motive's identity
