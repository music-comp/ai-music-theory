# Quick Definition

The overall shape or directional profile of a motive, which can remain significant and recognizable even when specific rhythmic treatment and intervals change.

# Core Definition

"The features of a motive are intervals and rhythms, combined to produce a memorable shape or contour which usually implies an inherent harmony" (Ch. 3, p. 8). "Often a contour or shape is significant, although the rhythmic treatment and intervals change" (Ch. 3, p. 8). Contour refers to the directional profile of the motive -- whether it moves up, down, by leap, by step, or in characteristic patterns of ascent and descent.

# Prerequisites

- **motive** -- Contour is one of the primary features of the motive

# Key Properties

1. Contour is the directional profile (shape) of the motive
2. It combines with rhythm and intervals to produce a memorable shape
3. Contour can remain significant even when rhythm and intervals change
4. Contour usually implies an inherent harmony
5. It is one of three primary motive features alongside rhythm and intervals
6. A motive may be recognized by contour alone through extensive variation

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify the directional pattern: does the motive ascend, descend, arch, zigzag?
2. Note characteristic directional changes (e.g., "upward sweep followed by a return")
3. Trace contour persistence through passages where rhythm and intervals change
4. Recognize that contour provides continuity through distant variations

## Characteristic Contour Types

- An upward leap (Ex. 12a)
- Stepwise upward movement (Ex. 16)
- An upward sweep followed by a return (Beethoven Op. 2/3-IV)
- Arch shapes, descending patterns, zigzag patterns

# Context & Application

Contour is particularly useful for generating distant motive-forms that still maintain a perceivable connection to the original. When rhythm and intervals have been substantially altered, contour may be the last remaining recognizable feature. This makes contour a powerful tool for developing variation over extended passages.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 8, Ex. 12a): An upward leap as the characteristic contour of a sonata theme.

**Example 2** (p. 8, Ex. 16): Movement up by step as the defining contour identity.

**Example 3** (p. 8): Beethoven Op. 2/3-IV -- "the upward sweep followed by a return within it which pervades" the entire movement, demonstrating contour as the most persistent motive feature.

**Example 4** (p. 8, Ex. 13): Brahms's Fourth Symphony -- descending-third contour as the generating pattern.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Describing contour only as "up" or "down"
  **Correction**: Contour includes the characteristic pattern of directional changes (e.g., "sweep and return")

- **Error**: Ignoring contour when analyzing heavily varied passages
  **Correction**: Contour may be the primary connecting feature in distant motive-forms

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Equating contour with specific intervals
  **Clarification**: A motive can preserve its contour (directional profile) while changing its intervals entirely

- **Confusion**: Treating contour as secondary to rhythm and intervals
  **Clarification**: In some compositions, contour is the most stable and recognizable element
