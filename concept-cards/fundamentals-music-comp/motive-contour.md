# Quick Definition
The overall shape or directional profile of a motive, which can remain significant and recognizable even when specific rhythmic treatment and intervals change.

# Formal Definition
The features of a motive are intervals and rhythms, combined to produce a memorable shape or contour which usually implies an inherent harmony. Often a contour or shape is significant, although the rhythmic treatment and intervals change. Contour refers to the directional profile of the motive -- whether it moves up, down, by leap, by step, or in characteristic patterns of ascent and descent.

# Construction/Recognition
Contour is recognized by the overall directional pattern of the motive rather than its specific intervals or rhythms. Characteristic contour types include:
- An upward leap (as in Ex. 12a)
- Stepwise upward movement (as in Ex. 16)
- An upward sweep followed by a return (as in Beethoven Op. 2/3-IV)
- Arch shapes, descending patterns, zigzag patterns

A contour can serve as the primary identifying feature of a motive, persisting through variations that change rhythm and specific intervals.

# Musical Context
Contour is one of the three primary motive features alongside rhythm and intervals. In some compositions, contour is the most stable and recognizable element, providing continuity even as other features undergo extensive variation. This makes contour particularly useful for generating distant motive-forms that still maintain a perceivable connection to the original.

# Examples
- Ex. 12a: upward leap as characteristic contour
- Ex. 16: movement up by step as contour identity
- Beethoven Op. 2/3-IV: upward sweep followed by a return -- this contour pervades the entire movement
- Brahms's Fourth Symphony: descending-third contour as the generating pattern

# Common Confusions
- Contour is not the same as the specific intervals; a motive can preserve its contour while changing its intervals
- Contour is not merely "up or down"; it includes the characteristic pattern of directional changes
- A motive may be recognized by contour alone, even when rhythm and intervals have been substantially altered
