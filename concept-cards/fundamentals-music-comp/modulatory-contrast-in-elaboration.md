# Quick Definition
The principle that the elaboration section provides harmonic contrast to the exposition's stability through roving, modulating harmony that passes through remote key regions, with a tendency toward flat-side keys.

# Formal Definition
The harmony of the exposition is essentially stable: apart from transitions, everything stands solidly within the region of a definite tonality. This requires a different kind of contrast in the elaboration. Variety requires roving, modulating harmony as a contrast to the generally stable nature of the exposition. Movement from region to region is usually effected through the closer key relationships, though the most remote regions may be reached in the process. There is a tendency to favour regions employing more flats or fewer sharps than the principal key, perhaps because the subordinate group almost always lies on the dominant side (more sharps or fewer flats).

# Construction/Recognition
- Essentially modulatory character contrasts with the essentially stable exposition
- More remote regions are used than in the exposition
- Movement between regions typically follows closer key relationships step by step
- Tendency toward flat-side keys (compensating for the sharp-side subordinate group)
- This flat-side tendency is particularly evident in approaching the retransition
- Occasional "roving" passages pertain to no definite region
- Approach chords (especially dominants) emphasized more than tonics
- Deceptive progressions are useful for maintaining modulatory momentum

# Musical Context
The modulatory character of the elaboration is not arbitrary but functionally related to the stability of the exposition. The two sections complement each other: one provides harmonic stability, the other harmonic variety. This complementary relationship is the formal raison d'etre of the elaboration.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I: reaches the "surprisingly remote region of Ab" from E major; the elaboration includes Eb (bIII) region
- Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I: follows the circle of fifths through sequential treatment
- Beethoven, Op. 28-I: passes through G (IV), g, regions a fourth lower, d, a; residues dwell on III as dominant of relative minor
- Mozart, K.V. 464-I: harmonic subtlety fully evident throughout the elaboration

# Common Confusions
- The modulatory character does not mean random key changes; movement between regions typically follows logical harmonic paths
- The flat-side tendency is a general observation, not an absolute rule
- "Roving" passages (pertaining to no definite region) are interspersed among more stable passages; the entire elaboration is not uniformly unstable
