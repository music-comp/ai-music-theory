# Quick Definition

The process of gradually eliminating characteristic features of a motive until only uncharacteristic residues remain, which no longer demand continuation, thereby enabling structural closure.

# Core Definition

Liquidation consists in gradually eliminating characteristic features, until only uncharacteristic ones remain, which no longer demand a continuation. Often only residues remain, which have little in common with the basic motive. In conjunction with a cadence or half cadence, this process can be used to provide adequate delimitation for a sentence (Schoenberg, p. 39).

The liquidation is generally supported by a shortening of the phrase. Two-measure phrases are reduced or condensed to one measure; four-measure phrases may condense to two, and sometimes to still smaller units: half-measures. Schoenberg distinguishes reduction (merely omitting part of the model) from condensation (compressing the content of the model, whereby even the order of features may be somewhat changed) (p. 39, footnote 1).

The concept is introduced in Chapter 3 through variation methods (reduction, omission) and receives its full treatment in Chapter 8 as the primary mechanism for concluding sentence structures.

# Key Properties

1. Progressive elimination of characteristic features
2. Retention of only uncharacteristic features that no longer demand continuation
3. Supported by progressive phrase shortening (2-measure to 1-measure to half-measure)
4. Works in conjunction with cadence or half cadence
5. Reduction: omitting part of the model
6. Condensation: compressing content, possibly reordering features
7. Residues often have little in common with the basic motive
8. Counteracts the tendency toward unlimited extension

# Construction / Recognition

## To Apply Liquidation

1. Identify the characteristic features of the motive (distinctive intervals, rhythms, contours)
2. Progressively remove or simplify these features in successive phrases
3. Shorten phrase units progressively (two-measure to one-measure to half-measure)
4. Continue until only uncharacteristic material remains (conjunct motion, repeated notes, generic figures)
5. Support the process with cadential harmony

## To Recognize Liquidation

1. Compare successive phrases with the original motive
2. Track which characteristic features disappear at each stage
3. Note the progressive shortening of phrase units
4. Identify the point where only uncharacteristic residues remain
5. Confirm cadential arrival

# Context & Application

Liquidation is Schoenberg's central concept for explaining how sentences achieve closure. It is the mechanism by which the "tendency toward unlimited extension" that development creates is counteracted. The process is not destructive but purposeful: the residues serve a structural function. Nearly all sentence continuations in the literature employ residues of the basic motive in their closing measures. The process also appears in cadential passages of consequents, where the melody reduces characteristic features to uncharacteristic ones (cadence contour).

Homophonic music can be called the style of "developing variation" (p. 43), and Ex. 61b illustrates how all motive-forms develop gradually out of the first two or three notes, with liquidation in m. 7-8 reducing to half-measure residues.

# Examples

- **Exs. 52a, b** (p. 39): Two-measure phrases reduced to one measure in m. 5-6; Ex. 52b further reduces to half-measure units

- **Ex. 52c** (p. 39): Four measures condensed to two (m. 9-10, 11-12), then to one measure

- **Ex. 61b** (Brahms, p. 43): One-measure phrases of m. 3-4 reduced to half-measure residues in m. 7-8, confirmed by accompaniment phrasing; all motive-forms develop from the first two or three notes

- **Literature examples** (Exs. 57-61, pp. 42-43): In almost all examples, the continuation after the repetition of the first phrase employs condensed phrases giving way to cadence contour, using only residues of the basic motive

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Omitting the process of liquidation, leaving characteristic features active at the cadence
  **Correction**: Characteristic features demand continuation; they must be progressively eliminated for the cadence to function

- **Error**: Liquidating too abruptly, jumping from full motival complexity to simple residues
  **Correction**: The process should be gradual, with progressive elimination and phrase shortening

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking liquidation is mere simplification
  **Clarification**: It specifically involves progressive removal of characteristic (identifying) features until only uncharacteristic (generic) ones remain

- **Confusion**: Confusing reduction and condensation
  **Clarification**: Reduction omits part of the model; condensation compresses content and may reorder features

- **Confusion**: Thinking liquidation makes music uninteresting
  **Clarification**: The residues serve a structural function, and the process itself can be compositionally compelling

- **Confusion**: Using "liquidation" in its everyday sense
  **Clarification**: Schoenberg's term carries specific technical meaning: the progressive elimination of characteristic features
