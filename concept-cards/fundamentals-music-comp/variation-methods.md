# Quick Definition
The specific techniques for altering the rhythm, intervals, harmony, and melody of a motive to produce new motive-forms while maintaining recognizable relationships to the original.

# Formal Definition
All features of rhythm, interval, harmony, and contour are subject to various alterations. Frequently, several methods of variation are applied to several features simultaneously, but such changes must not produce a motive-form too foreign to the basic motive. Schoenberg catalogs the methods systematically across four domains.

# Construction/Recognition
**Rhythmic variation:**
1. Modifying the length of notes (Ex. 17)
2. Note repetitions (Exs. 11h, i, k, l, n)
3. Repetition of certain rhythmic patterns (Exs. 17l, m, 18c)
4. Shifting rhythms to different beats (Ex. 23)
5. Addition of upbeats (Ex. 22)
6. Changing the metre -- a device seldom usable within a piece (Ex. 24)

**Intervallic variation:**
1. Changing the original order or direction of notes (Ex. 19)
2. Addition or omission of intervals (Ex. 21)
3. Filling up intervals with ancillary notes (Exs. 18, 20 ff.)
4. Reduction through omission or condensation (Ex. 21)
5. Repetition of features (Exs. 20h, 22a, b, d)
6. Shifting features to other beats (Ex. 23)

**Harmonic variation:**
1. Use of inversions (Exs. 25a, b)
2. Additions at the end (Exs. 25c-i)
3. Insertions in the middle (Ex. 26)
4. Substituting a different chord or succession (Exs. 27a-i)

**Melodic adaptation:**
1. Transposition (Ex. 28)
2. Addition of passing harmonies (Ex. 29)
3. "Semi-contrapuntal" treatment of the accompaniment (Ex. 29)

# Musical Context
These methods are the practical toolkit for composition. Schoenberg presents them systematically using a single broken-chord motive (Exs. 17-29) to show the range of possibilities. He recommends thorough exploration of these resources as essential for acquiring technical skill and developing a rich inventive faculty.

# Examples
- Exs. 17-29: comprehensive demonstration of all variation methods applied to a single basic motive
- Each example category shows multiple specific applications
- The broken-chord basis makes the relationship to the original clearly audible

# Common Confusions
- Multiple methods can be combined simultaneously, but too many changes at once risk making the result "too foreign to the basic motive"
- Changing metre is listed as a variation method but is "seldom usable within a piece"
- Schoenberg prefers "ancillary notes" to "embellishing" or "ornamental" notes to avoid aesthetically misleading connotations
- These methods apply to all musical parameters, not just melody
