# Quick Definition

A structural form consisting of a theme followed by several variations upon it, where variation serves as the structural principle for the entire piece, representing an approach to the logic of larger compositions.

# Core Definition

The term "variation" has multiple meanings in music: it creates motive-forms for constructing themes, produces contrast in middle sections, and provides variety in repetitions. But in "Theme and Variations" it is the structural principle for a whole piece. Production of an entire piece merely through the application of variation is an approach to the logic of larger compositions. The number of variations depends on whether it is a movement in a cyclic work (fewer) or an independent piece (more). The form originated perhaps in the custom of repeating a pleasant theme several times, avoiding decline of interest by introducing embellishments and additions.

# Prerequisites

- **Small ternary form**: Themes are typically binary or ternary
- **Developing variation**: The underlying technique of systematic variation

# Key Properties

- A theme is presented, followed by a series of variations
- Each variation preserves the main structural features of the theme
- The course of events should not be changed, even if the character changes
- Number and order of segments remain the same
- Proportions and structural relations are preserved
- Metre and tempo may change; measure count may be systematically multiplied by two or three
- Every variation must possess the same formal self-sufficiency and coherence as the theme
- Often concluded by a coda, finale, or fugue
- The viewpoint as to which features are "main" is subject to change between variations

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify the theme and its structural features (form, segments, proportions)
2. Trace each variation's preservation of these structural features
3. Identify the motive of variation for each variation
4. Note changes of metre, tempo, or multiplied measure count
5. Check for the concluding section (coda, finale, fugue)
6. Assess the organization of the set (character contrasts, climactic build, groupings)

# Context & Application

The classic masters made it a point that the theme should be recognizable in the variation. The form appears as an independent piece and as a movement within cyclic works. The number of variations varies from a handful (when the theme is complex) to thirty-three (Diabelli Variations). The form's flexibility makes it both an excellent pedagogical tool and a vehicle for the most sophisticated compositional achievement.

# Examples

- Beethoven, Op. 26-I, Op. 14/2-II, Op. 111-II: Movements in cyclic works (fewer variations)
- Beethoven, 32 Variations in c minor: Independent piece, passacaglia-like theme
- Beethoven, 33 Diabelli Variations, Op. 120: Independent piece, extensive set
- Bach, Goldberg Variations: Independent piece based on composer's own theme
- Brahms, String Quartet Op. 67-IV: Variation movement on composer's own theme

# Common Errors

- Changing the course of events (segment order, proportions) within a variation
- Creating variations that lack formal self-sufficiency
- Failing to maintain recognizability of the theme

# Common Confusions

- **"Variation" as technique vs. "Theme and Variations" as form**: Variation is used throughout all music; the form is a specific structural pattern.
- **Essential features**: The viewpoint determining what is "essential" may change between variations, allowing different aspects of the theme to be emphasized.
