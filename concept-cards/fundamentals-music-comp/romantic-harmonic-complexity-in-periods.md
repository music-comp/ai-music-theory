# Quick Definition

Romantic composers, particularly Brahms, employ more complex harmonic language in periods, exploiting the multiple meaning of harmonies and compensating for this complexity with simpler motival and rhythmic construction.

# Core Definition

The rapid development of harmony since the beginning of the nineteenth century has been the great obstacle to the acceptance of every new composer from Schubert on. Frequent deviation from the tonic region into more or less foreign regions seemed to obstruct unity and intelligibility. However, composers of this style, instinctively feeling the danger of incoherence, counteracted the tension in one plane (the complex harmony) by simplification in another plane (the motival and rhythmic construction). This also explains the unvaried repetitions and frequent sequences of Wagner, Bruckner, Debussy, Franck, Tchaikovsky, Sibelius, and many others (Schoenberg, p. 26).

Brahms's examples are especially interesting because of their prolific exploitation of the multiple meaning of harmonies. By the time of Mahler, Reger, Strauss, and Ravel, far-reaching harmony no longer seriously endangered comprehensibility.

# Key Properties

1. Complex harmony compensated by simpler motivic and rhythmic construction
2. Exploitation of the multiple meaning of harmonies (harmonic ambiguity)
3. Frequent deviation from the tonic into foreign regions
4. Unvaried repetitions and frequent sequences balance harmonic complexity
5. Quasi-contrapuntal variation of accompanying voices over unchanged melody
6. Pedal points for constructive retardation of harmonic movement

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify passages where harmony deviates significantly from the tonic region
2. Check whether the motival and rhythmic construction is simplified to compensate
3. Look for exploitation of harmonic ambiguity (chords interpretable in multiple keys)
4. Note unvaried repetitions or frequent sequences that balance complex harmony
5. Observe quasi-contrapuntal variation of accompaniment as a variation technique

# Context & Application

Schoenberg presents this principle not as criticism but as analytical explanation of how Romantic composers managed increasingly complex harmonic language. The principle of compensating complexity in one dimension with simplicity in another is a fundamental insight into compositional balance that applies beyond Romantic music.

# Examples

- **Brahms, Ex. 51a** (f-minor, p. 25): In m. 1-6, few harmonies belong diatonically to f-minor; most could be understood as Neapolitan sixth of c-minor. The deviation toward E-flat in m. 3 is surprising -- E-flat is treated like a tonic on VII. In m. 7-8, Brahms identifies m. 1-2 and 5-6 as pertaining to c-minor (the v-region of f-minor).

- **Brahms, Ex. 51c** (p. 26): Repetition (m. 17-32) unchanged in melody and harmony, varied only by quasi-contrapuntal treatment of accompanying lower voices

- **Brahms, Ex. 51f** (p. 26): Pedal point at beginning (m. 1-2) and end (m. 10-14); tendency of the smallest notes produces variant in m. 5

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Attempting complex Romantic harmony without compensating simplification elsewhere
  **Correction**: Complex harmony must be balanced by simpler motival and rhythmic construction to maintain comprehensibility

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking complex Romantic harmony is careless or undisciplined
  **Clarification**: Romantic composers deliberately compensated harmonic complexity with simplification in other dimensions

- **Confusion**: Believing that the principle of compensating complexity is merely historical
  **Clarification**: It is a fundamental compositional principle applicable to all styles
