# Quick Definition
Romantic composers, particularly Brahms, employ more complex harmonic language in periods, exploiting the multiple meaning of harmonies and balancing this complexity with simpler motival and rhythmic construction.

# Formal Definition
The rapid development of harmony since the beginning of the nineteenth century posed challenges to thematic intelligibility. Romantic composers, instinctively feeling the danger of incoherence from frequent deviation into foreign regions, counteracted the tension in the harmonic plane by simplification in the motival and rhythmic plane. This principle of compensating complexity in one dimension with simplicity in another also explains the unvaried repetitions and frequent sequences found in Wagner, Bruckner, Debussy, Franck, Tchaikovsky, and Sibelius. Brahms's examples are especially interesting because of their prolific exploitation of the multiple meaning of harmonies.

# Construction/Recognition
- Complex harmony balanced by simpler motivic and rhythmic construction
- Frequent use of harmonic reinterpretation (multiple meaning of harmonies)
- Unvaried repetitions and sequences compensating for harmonic tension
- Deviation from the tonic region into more or less foreign regions
- Use of pedal points for constructive retardation of harmonic movement
- Quasi-contrapuntal variation of accompanying voices over unchanged melody and harmony

# Musical Context
Schoenberg identifies a historical principle: as harmony grew more complex, composers instinctively simplified other dimensions to maintain comprehensibility. By the time of Mahler, Reger, Richard Strauss, and Ravel, far-reaching harmony no longer seriously endangered comprehensibility. The pedal point serves a constructive purpose: retarding harmonic progress, emphasizing the end of a modulation, and balancing remote motival variation. A pedal point without such purpose should be avoided; a moving, melodic bass line is always a greater merit.

# Examples
- Brahms, Ex. 51a (f-minor): In m. 1-6, few harmonies belong diatonically to f-minor; most could be understood as Neapolitan sixth of c-minor. The deviation toward E-flat in m. 3 is surprising -- treated like a tonic on VII. In m. 7-8, Brahms identifies m. 1-2 and 5-6 as pertaining to c-minor (v-region of f-minor).
- Brahms, Ex. 51c: Repetition (m. 17-32) unchanged in melody and harmony, varied by quasi-contrapuntal treatment of accompanying lower voices
- Brahms, Ex. 51f: Pedal point at the beginning (m. 1-2) and end (m. 10-14); tendency of the smallest notes produces a variant in m. 5

# Common Confusions
- Complex harmony in Romantic periods is not a sign of carelessness but requires deliberate compensating simplification in other dimensions.
- The pedal point should have a constructive purpose (retardation, balance) and not be used merely for its sound effect.
- Schoenberg's discussion of Romantic harmony is not criticism but analytical explanation of how later composers managed increasingly complex harmonic language.
