# Quick Definition
A unifying rhythmic-melodic figure that organizes the accompaniment, analogous to the basic motive of the theme but treated primarily through simple rhythmic repetition and adaptation to the harmony rather than extensive development.

# Formal Definition
As a unifying device, the accompaniment must be organized in a manner similar to the organization of a theme: by utilization of a motive, the motive of the accompaniment. The motive of the accompaniment can seldom be worked out with as much variety and development as that of a melody or theme. Its treatment consists, rather, of simple rhythmic repetition and adaptation to the harmony. Its special form must be so constituted that it can be modified, liquidated, or abandoned as the nature of the theme demands. Movement of the harmony in equal notes (regular harmonic change) is itself a primitive kind of motive of the accompaniment.

# Construction/Recognition
The motive of the accompaniment is recognized by:
- A characteristic rhythmic-melodic figure used systematically in the accompanying voices
- Treatment primarily through rhythmic repetition (not extensive motivic development)
- Adaptation to changing harmonies while maintaining rhythmic identity
- Flexibility to be modified, liquidated, or abandoned when the theme demands
- May include auxiliaries, appoggiaturas, changing notes (Op. 2/3-II, m. 11 ff.; Op. 7-IV, m. 64 ff.)
- Sometimes includes semi-contrapuntal movement of one voice (Op. 10/1-I, m. 56 ff.)
- More than one motive may appear when bass is separated from middle voices (Exs. 64e, f)
- The motive should be maintained for at least several measures or phrases

# Musical Context
The motive of the accompaniment provides textural continuity binding the accompaniment into a coherent layer. It is introduced in Chapter 5 as an essential element of theme construction and treated fully in Chapter 9. Even if varied, it should not disappear completely during the body of a theme. However, near cadences or half cadences, harmonic requirements often force modification or liquidation of the motive. A single motive may suffice for primitive cases, but changing character, construction, or harmonic density may justify multiple figures, especially in Mozart's Rococo style.

# Common Confusions
- The motive of the accompaniment is not the same as the theme's basic motive; it serves textural unity rather than thematic identity and is treated differently
- "Motive-like repetitions" in the accompaniment are simpler than motivic development in the melody
- The accompaniment motive should be consistent but flexible; near cadences, modification or liquidation is normal
- Liquidation or abandonment near cadences is expected, not a compositional weakness
- The obsolete Alberti bass is an example of a motive of accompaniment that works only in the most primitive cases
