# Quick Definition

A unifying rhythmic-melodic figure that organizes the accompaniment, treated primarily through simple rhythmic repetition and adaptation to the harmony, which must be flexible enough to be modified, liquidated, or abandoned as the theme demands.

# Core Definition

As a unifying device, the accompaniment must be organized in a manner similar to the organization of a theme: by utilization of a motive, the motive of the accompaniment. The motive of the accompaniment can seldom be worked out with as much variety and development as that of a melody or theme. Its treatment consists, rather, of simple rhythmic repetition and adaptation to the harmony. Its special form must be so constituted that it can be modified, liquidated, or abandoned, as the nature of the theme demands (Schoenberg, p. 52).

Movement of the harmony in equal notes (regular harmonic change) is itself a primitive kind of motive of the accompaniment. More than one motive may appear, especially when the bass is separated from the middle voices and carries out its own system (Exs. 64e, f). The motive should be maintained for at least several measures or phrases; even if varied, it should not disappear completely during the body of the theme (p. 55).

# Prerequisites

- **Motive** -- The accompaniment motive parallels the thematic motive in organizational function
- **Accompaniment function** -- The motive serves the broader function of the accompaniment

# Key Properties

1. Organizes the accompaniment as a unifying device
2. Treated through simple rhythmic repetition and harmonic adaptation (not extensive development)
3. Must be flexible: can be modified, liquidated, or abandoned
4. May include auxiliaries, appoggiaturas, changing notes
5. May include semi-contrapuntal movement of one voice
6. More than one motive may appear (especially with separated bass)
7. Should be maintained for at least several measures or phrases
8. Near cadences, modification or liquidation is often necessary

# Construction / Recognition

1. Establish a rhythmic-melodic figure that can be adapted to changing harmonies
2. Design it to be flexible enough for modification or liquidation
3. Maintain it consistently through the body of the theme
4. Adapt it to harmonic changes while preserving rhythmic identity
5. Modify or liquidate near cadences as harmonic requirements demand
6. Consider whether the bass needs its own separate motivic system

# Context & Application

The motive of the accompaniment provides textural continuity. It is introduced in Chapter 5 as essential to theme construction and treated fully in Chapter 9. Even Mozart's Rococo style, which uses multiple figures, maintains motivic coherence. The obsolete Alberti bass is an example of a motive of accompaniment that works only in the most primitive cases. A single motive suffices for simple harmony; changing character, construction, or harmonic density may justify multiple figures.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Developing the accompaniment motive with the same complexity as the thematic motive
  **Correction**: Accompaniment motive treatment is primarily rhythmic repetition and harmonic adaptation

- **Error**: Abandoning the accompaniment motive too early
  **Correction**: Maintain it for at least several measures or phrases; near cadences, modification or liquidation is appropriate

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the accompaniment motive is the same as the thematic motive
  **Clarification**: They serve different functions; the accompaniment motive provides textural unity, not thematic identity

- **Confusion**: Believing a single accompaniment motive must persist throughout
  **Clarification**: More than one figure may appear; modification or liquidation near cadences is normal

- **Confusion**: Equating the Alberti bass with all accompaniment motives
  **Clarification**: The Alberti bass is an obsolete, primitive example; the concept is much broader
