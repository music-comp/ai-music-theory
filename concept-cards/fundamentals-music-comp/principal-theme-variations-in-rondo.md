# Quick Definition
The practice of varying the principal theme upon each recurrence in a rondo, ranging from simple changes of sonority and register to contrapuntal interchange of voices, ornamental embellishment, and interpolated figurations -- variation being "one of the distinguishing features of higher art."

# Core Definition
From a structural standpoint, changes in the principal theme when it recurs are not necessary. But variation for its own sake is one of the distinguishing features of higher art. In the larger forms an unchanged repetition seldom occurs. The melodic outline and the thematic structure are usually preserved. The simplest type of variation is a change of sonority brought about by a different setting. In piano style, embellishments in the melody, changes of octave, subdivision of the accompaniment, and enrichment of the figuration are the usual variants.

# Key Properties
- Variation for its own sake is a mark of higher art
- Melodic outline and thematic structure usually preserved
- Types: change of sonority/setting, embellishment, octave changes, accompaniment subdivision, figuration enrichment, contrapuntal interchange, interpolation
- In piano style, variation possibilities differ from string or orchestral style
- Recapitulation in a "wrong key" is a more radical approach (Brahms)
- Unchanged repetition seldom occurs in larger forms

# Construction / Recognition
Types of variation applied to returning principal themes:
1. **Change of sonority/setting**: different voicing, register, or instrumentation
2. **Embellishment**: added ornamental tones in the melody
3. **Changes of octave**: placing the theme in a different register
4. **Subdivision of accompaniment**: breaking sustained chords into figuration
5. **Enrichment of figuration**: more elaborate passage work
6. **Contrapuntal interchange**: voices exchange roles
7. **Interpolation**: inserting additional material (imitations, figurations) within the theme
8. **"Wrong key" recapitulation**: beginning the return in a remote key before correcting to the tonic

# Context & Application
Variation of the returning principal theme is a primary means of maintaining interest across the multiple recurrences required by rondo form. Without variation, the repetitions would risk monotony. The degree of variation should preserve recognizability -- the listener must still perceive a return of the familiar theme.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: variations of upbeat in m. 41, 53, 100, 104, 112, 135; embellishments in m. 43, 102, 108, 137
- Beethoven, Op. 7-IV (m. 143): fourth appearance an octave higher, varied with unifying syncopations and inserted chromatic tones; omission of A'-section in first repetition (m. 51) is unusual
- Beethoven, Op. 22-IV: third appearance with quasi-contrapuntal interchange of voices (m. 112); last repetition (m. 165) has melodic line elaborated with triplets against duple accompaniment
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-IV: theme varied by interpolation of imitations (m. 57) and figurations (m. 85)
- Brahms, Second Symphony-III: recapitulation (m. 194) begins in F#, half step lower than original G; subtly modified to return through chromatic third relation to original level (m. 207)
- Beethoven, Op. 18/6-II: six different settings of the basic segment (m. 1-4), including transposition, figuration, ornamental additions

# Common Errors
- Varying the theme so much that it becomes unrecognizable as a return
- Failing to vary the theme at all across multiple appearances, producing monotony
- Using the same type of variation each time, creating a predictable pattern

# Common Confusions
- The melodic outline and thematic structure are usually preserved despite variation; the recurrence must remain recognizable
- Brahms's "wrong key" technique goes beyond ornamental variation; earlier composers sometimes hint at this with anticipatory quotations in the retransition
- In piano style, variation possibilities differ from string or orchestral style; piano relies more on embellishment and figuration than on tone color changes
