# Quick Definition
The practice of varying the principal theme upon each recurrence in a rondo, ranging from simple changes of sonority and register to more elaborate techniques such as contrapuntal interchange of voices, ornamental embellishment, and interpolated figurations.

# Formal Definition
From a structural standpoint, changes in the principal theme when it recurs are not necessary. But variation for its own sake is one of the distinguishing features of higher art. In the larger forms an unchanged repetition seldom occurs. The melodic outline and thematic structure are usually preserved. The simplest type of variation is a change of sonority brought about by a different setting. In piano style, embellishments in the melody, changes of octave, subdivision of the accompaniment, and enrichment of the figuration are the usual variants.

# Construction/Recognition
Types of variation applied to returning principal themes:
- **Change of sonority/setting**: different voicing, register, or instrumentation
- **Embellishment of melody**: added ornamental tones
- **Changes of octave**: placing the theme in a different register
- **Subdivision of accompaniment**: breaking up sustained chords into figuration
- **Enrichment of figuration**: more elaborate passage work
- **Contrapuntal interchange**: voices exchange roles
- **Interpolation**: inserting additional material (imitations, figurations) within the theme
- **Recapitulation in a "wrong key"**: beginning the return in a remote key before correcting to the tonic

# Musical Context
Variation of the returning principal theme is a primary means of maintaining interest across the multiple recurrences required by rondo form. Without variation, the repetitions would risk monotony.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: variations of the upbeat in m. 41, 53, 100, 104, 112, 135; embellishments of melody in m. 43, 102, 108, 137
- Beethoven, Op. 7-IV (m. 143): fourth appearance an octave higher, varied with unifying syncopations and inserted chromatic tones
- Beethoven, Op. 22-IV: third appearance presents quasi-contrapuntal interchange of voices (m. 112); last repetition (m. 165) has melodic line elaborated with triplets against duple accompaniment
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-IV: theme varied by interpolation of imitations (m. 57 ff.) and figurations (m. 85 ff.)
- Brahms, Second Symphony-III: recapitulation (m. 194) begins in F#, a half step lower than the original G; subtly modified to return through chromatic third relation to the original level in m. 207

# Common Confusions
- The melodic outline and thematic structure are usually preserved despite variation; the recurrence must remain recognizable
- Brahms's technique of beginning the recapitulation in a "wrong key" is a more radical approach that goes beyond mere ornamental variation; earlier composers (Mozart, Beethoven) sometimes hint at this with anticipatory quotations in the retransition
- In piano style, variation possibilities differ from string or orchestral style; the piano relies more on embellishment and figuration than on changes of tone color
