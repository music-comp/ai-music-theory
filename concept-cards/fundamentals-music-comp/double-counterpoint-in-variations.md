# Quick Definition

The technique of writing two voices that can exchange positions (upper becomes lower, lower becomes upper) while remaining musically valid, used in variations to create pairs or groups of related variations and to enrich recapitulations.

# Core Definition

Double counterpoint (at the octave) is the technique of writing two voices that function correctly in either vertical arrangement. In variation technique, this manifests in several ways: paired variations where the second interchanges the voices of the first; enrichment of recapitulations by interchanging melody and countermelody; and the production of groups of related variations that share material but present it in different voice configurations. The technique may also extend to triple or multiple counterpoint with three or more interchangeable voices.

- **Counterpoint in variations**: Understanding the broader contrapuntal context

# Key Properties

- Two voices that can be interchanged (upper becomes lower and vice versa)
- Paired variations: the second uses the same material with voices exchanged
- Triple or multiple counterpoint: three or more voices interchangeable
- Invertible at the octave: voices exchange at the octave
- Invertible at the twelfth: voices exchange at the twelfth (rarer, more restrictive)
- May appear within a single variation or as a relationship between paired variations
- Also used to group two or three related variations without change of character

# Construction / Recognition

1. Look for paired variations where the voices are exchanged
2. Check whether two voices can be interchanged while remaining musically valid
3. Note whether three or more voices are interchangeable (triple/multiple counterpoint)
4. Identify the interval of invertibility (octave, twelfth)
5. Distinguish from "quasi-contrapuntal inversion" (less strict)

# Context & Application

Double counterpoint provides a powerful means of generating related variations without repetition. By exchanging voice positions, the same musical material acquires a different character and texture. Brahms's Haydn Variations Op. 56a, Variation 4 (triple counterpoint invertible at octave and twelfth) and Variation 8 (mirror forms in multiple counterpoint) represent the summit of contrapuntal artistry in the variation form.

# Examples

- Beethoven, 32 Variations, Vars. 10-11: In double counterpoint at the octave
- Beethoven, 32 Variations, Vars. 20-21: Related pair in double counterpoint; extended to a group of three by Var. 22 (canonic imitation of simplified triplet figure)
- Beethoven, 32 Variations, Vars. 1-3: Related group using one idea in quasi-contrapuntal inversion
- Beethoven, Op. 26, Scherzo: Written-out repetition varied by voice interchange (m. 53-60)
- Brahms, Handel Variations, Var. 8: Based on double counterpoint at the octave
- Brahms, Haydn Variations Op. 56a, Var. 4: Triple counterpoint invertible at octave and twelfth
- Brahms, Haydn Variations Op. 56a, Var. 8: Mirror forms in multiple counterpoint

# Common Errors

- Confusing double counterpoint (invertible) with two-part counterpoint (not necessarily invertible)
- Assuming voice interchange changes nothing (it significantly alters character and texture)

# Common Confusions

- **Double counterpoint vs. two-part writing**: Double counterpoint specifically requires that the voices function correctly in either arrangement; not all two-part writing is invertible.
- **Quasi-contrapuntal inversion**: Refers to using one idea with inverted voice positions without necessarily strict invertible counterpoint.
