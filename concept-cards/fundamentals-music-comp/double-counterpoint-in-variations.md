# Quick Definition
The technique of writing two voices that can exchange positions (upper becomes lower, lower becomes upper) while remaining musically valid, used in variations to create pairs or groups of related variations and to enrich recapitulations.

# Formal Definition
Double counterpoint (at the octave) is the technique of writing two voices that function correctly in either vertical arrangement. In variation technique, this manifests in several ways: paired variations where the second interchanges the voices of the first; enrichment of recapitulations by interchanging melody and countermelody; and the production of groups of related variations that share material but present it in different voice configurations. The technique may also appear as "quasi-contrapuntal" pianistic imitations that contribute to coherence.

# Construction/Recognition
- Two voices that can be interchanged (upper becomes lower and vice versa)
- Paired variations: the second variation uses the same material with voices exchanged
- Triple or multiple counterpoint: three or more voices interchangeable
- Invertible at the octave: voices exchange at the octave
- Invertible at the twelfth: voices exchange at the twelfth (rarer, more restrictive)
- May appear as a technique within a single variation or as a relationship between paired variations

# Musical Context
Double counterpoint provides a powerful means of generating related variations without repetition. By exchanging voice positions, the same musical material acquires a different character and texture. This technique also appears in scherzo recapitulations, where it enriches the return of the theme. The ability to write in double counterpoint is a hallmark of compositional mastery.

# Examples
- Beethoven, 32 Variations in c minor, Variations 10-11: In double counterpoint at the octave
- Beethoven, 32 Variations in c minor, Variations 20-21: Related pair in double counterpoint, extended to a group of three by Variation 22 (canonic imitation of a simplified triplet figure)
- Beethoven, Op. 26, Scherzo: Written-out repetition varied by interchange of voices (m. 53-60) as in double counterpoint
- Brahms, Handel Variations, Variation 8: Based on double counterpoint at the octave
- Brahms, Haydn Variations Op. 56a, Variation 4: Triple counterpoint invertible at the octave and twelfth
- Beethoven, 32 Variations in c minor, Variations 1-3: Related group using one idea in quasi-contrapuntal inversion

# Common Confusions
- Double counterpoint is not the same as two-part counterpoint -- it specifically requires that the voices be interchangeable
- "Quasi-contrapuntal inversion" refers to the method of using one idea with inverted voice positions, not necessarily strict invertible counterpoint
- The interchange of voices changes the character of the music even though the notes are the same
