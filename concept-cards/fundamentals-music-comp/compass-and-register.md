# Quick Definition
The principle that a good melody generally remains within a reasonable compass (range), not straying too far from a central range, ensuring balance and singability.

# Formal Definition
A good melody generally remains within a reasonable compass, not straying too far from a central range. This principle works in conjunction with wave-like melodic motion: by keeping the melody within a limited range and centering it around a central register, the composer ensures that the melody remains balanced, graspable, and well-proportioned.

# Construction/Recognition
To maintain appropriate compass and register:
- Establish a central range early in the melody
- Limit excursions to extremes of high and low register
- Use excursions to extreme registers purposefully (e.g., for climax)
- Return to the central range after excursions
- Consider the overall range in relation to the tempo and character of the piece

# Musical Context
Compass and register are practical considerations in connecting motive-forms. When building phrases from motive-forms, the composer must ensure that successive transformations do not push the melody into extreme registers. This principle is related to singability: a melody that stays within a reasonable compass is more likely to feel natural and graspable.

# Examples
- Classical themes typically remain within an octave to a tenth for their main material
- Climactic moments may extend the compass temporarily before returning to the central range
- Exs. 30-34 demonstrate phrase construction that maintains reasonable compass

# Common Confusions
- "Reasonable compass" is relative to the instrument, the character of the piece, and the tempo
- Staying within a limited range does not mean avoiding large intervals; it means that the overall span is controlled
- Register is not the same as tessitura; register refers to the pitch area, while tessitura refers to the prevalent range of a vocal or instrumental part
