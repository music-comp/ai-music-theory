# Quick Definition
The standard tonal relationships governing the sonata-allegro exposition: principal theme in the tonic, subordinate group in the dominant (major keys) or relative major/minor dominant (minor keys), with the interchangeability of parallel major and minor permitting occasional appearance in minor v.

# Core Definition
Key relationships are substantially the same as in contrasting middle sections for the simpler forms. When the principal theme is major, the subordinate group nearly always lies in the key of V. Sometimes the interchangeability of parallel major and minor permits the appearance of part or all of the subordinate group in the key of v (minor), with the exception of codettas. In minor, the preferred keys are III (relative major) and v (minor). Since a theme which first appears in a major key does not always work satisfactorily in minor, and since the minor-major contrast may be essential, considerable modification may appear in the recapitulation.

# Key Properties
- Major keys: subordinate group nearly always in V
- Sometimes parts in v (minor dominant) through mode interchange, except codettas
- Minor keys: preferred keys are III (relative major) and v (minor)
- Departures possible, particularly after Beethoven
- Striking departures require "masterly adaptations of the form and the material"
- The exposition's harmony is essentially stable (contrasting with the elaboration)
- Key relationships create the fundamental tonal tension that drives the form

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the tonic key of the principal theme
2. Determine the key of the subordinate group (V, III, or v)
3. Note whether mode interchange affects any portion
4. Look for departures from standard relationships
5. Note how the key relationship affects the recapitulation requirements

# Context & Application
The standard key relationships are not arbitrary conventions but reflect the fundamental principle of related contrast. The dominant in major provides the closest but most directional relationship. In minor, the relative major provides a brighter contrast, while the minor dominant maintains mode unity. Departures from these norms are possible but require exceptional skill.

# Examples
- Standard major: subordinate group in V (most Beethoven major-key sonatas)
- Mode interchange: Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I and Op. 2/3-I: part of subordinate group in v (minor)
- Standard minor: subordinate group in III (Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I in f, subordinate group in Ab)
- Minor with minor v: Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I: peculiar case where first subordinate theme starts in region of minor v
- Striking departure: Brahms, Piano Quintet Op. 34 in f: subordinate theme in c#; recurs in f# -- "such relationships are infrequent; and they always require masterly adaptations"

# Common Errors
- Using too remote a key for the subordinate group without the skill to manage the consequences in the recapitulation
- Failing to maintain tonal stability within the subordinate group (apart from incidental modulation)
- Neglecting codettas in the proper key when the subordinate group temporarily uses minor v

# Common Confusions
- The subordinate group in V is the norm, not a rule; departures exist but are exceptional
- In minor, both III and v are standard choices; the choice affects recapitulation strategy
- Mode interchange (parts in minor v) does not mean the entire subordinate group is in minor; codettas typically remain in the major key
