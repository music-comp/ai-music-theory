# Quick Definition

Variation themes should employ simple, regular harmonic progressions -- often featuring mere interchange of I and V -- to provide a stable foundation that permits extensive harmonic variation in subsequent variations.

# Core Definition

The harmony of a variation theme should be simple and should not change too frequently or too irregularly. The use of mere interchange of I and V contributes to the simplicity of many themes. In the best themes, there may be only one or two harmonies per two measures. Even the Diabelli Waltz, whose first eight measures change harmony only once, proved suitable for thirty-three variations. Simple but characteristic harmony (such as a sequential middle section) contributes to the memorability of the theme.

# Prerequisites

- **Theme structural constitution**: Understanding the overall requirements for a good variation theme

# Key Properties

- Harmonic changes should be infrequent and regular
- Interchange of I and V is a common harmonic foundation
- One or two harmonies per two measures is typical
- Characteristic harmonic features (e.g., sequences) may appear but should remain simple
- The harmony provides the skeletal framework that variations can elaborate or alter
- Simple harmony allows greater scope for harmonic variation

# Construction / Recognition

1. Count the number of harmonic changes per measure or per two measures
2. Check whether the harmony is based on simple I-V interchange
3. Identify any characteristic harmonic features (sequences, etc.) and verify they remain simple
4. Compare to the Diabelli Waltz standard (one harmony change in eight measures)
5. Assess whether the harmonic simplicity provides room for later variation

# Context & Application

The harmonic simplicity of the theme serves a dual purpose: it makes the theme immediately comprehensible and memorable, and it provides a stable foundation against which harmonic variations register as meaningful departures. The most sophisticated variations (Diabelli, Handel) demonstrate how far harmonic variation can depart from a simple foundation while remaining recognizably connected.

# Examples

- Beethoven, 15 Variations in E-flat: Two harmonies to two measures
- Beethoven, Diabelli Variations Op. 120: Harmony changes only once in first eight measures
- Beethoven, 6 Variations in G: Simple but characteristic harmony, especially the sequence in the middle section
- Beethoven, Op. 26-I: Sequences contribute to memorability
- Brahms, Handel Variations: Simple I-based progression with mediant relationship (B-flat to D)

# Common Errors

- Writing a theme with too frequent harmonic changes
- Making harmonic changes irregular rather than regular
- Confusing "simple" with "uninteresting" (characteristic features are desirable if regular)

# Common Confusions

- **Simple vs. uninteresting**: Characteristic harmonic features are desirable -- a sequential middle section adds memorability while remaining simple.
- **Theme harmony vs. variation harmony**: The simplicity of the theme's harmony enables the sophistication of the variations' harmony. The two are in inverse proportion.
