# Quick Definition

True contrapuntal style appears in otherwise homophonic music when fugues or fugatos are incorporated, either as movement openings, as episodes within elaboration sections, or as concluding fugues in variation sets.

# Core Definition

A real contrapuntal style appears occasionally when fugues or fugatos are incorporated in otherwise homophonic music. Sometimes a movement begins with a fugato, though such sections are often repeated in more developed form while the remainder should generally be classified as homophonic. In the elaboration (development) sections of larger forms, there are frequently fugato episodes using imitations or invertible counterpoint. Some composers conclude sets of variations with real fugues, and some sonata finales are themselves fugues (Schoenberg, pp. 53-54).

Contrapuntal episodes, generally using imitations or invertible counterpoint, are frequently found in elaboration sections.

# Prerequisites

- **Accompaniment function** -- Contrapuntal treatment is the most complex form of "accompaniment"

# Key Properties

1. Fugato openings in otherwise homophonic movements
2. Fugato episodes in elaboration (development) sections
3. Concluding fugues in variation sets
4. Sonata finales as fugues
5. Contrapuntal episodes using imitations or invertible counterpoint
6. Such passages do not make the work "contrapuntal" overall; classification remains homophonic

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify fugal exposition technique (successive voice entries with a subject)
2. Locate imitative or canonic passages within elaboration sections
3. Note invertible counterpoint where voice parts exchange registers
4. Distinguish genuine fugato from semi-contrapuntal or quasi-contrapuntal writing
5. Classify the work as homophonic overall, with contrapuntal episodes

# Context & Application

Contrapuntal treatment within homophonic music represents textural enrichment and intensification. It typically appears at structurally significant moments: beginnings of movements (establishing seriousness), elaboration sections (heightening developmental tension), and conclusions (providing culmination). The contrapuntal texture provides maximum independence of voices.

# Examples

- **Fugato openings** (p. 54): Beethoven, Op. 10/2-III; Mozart, String Quartet in G, Finale; Beethoven, Op. 59/3, Finale

- **Fugato episodes in elaboration** (p. 54): Beethoven, Op. 59/1-I; Eroica Symphony, first and fourth movements; Brahms, Piano Quintet, third movement; Beethoven, Op. 101, Finale

- **Concluding fugues** (p. 54): Beethoven, Op. 120, Op. 35; Brahms, Op. 24

- **Sonata finales as fugues** (p. 54): Beethoven, Op. 106, Op. 110

- **Contrapuntal episodes** (p. 54): Op. 2/3-I, m. 115 ff.; Op. 22, Rondo, m. 81 ff.; Op. 81a-I, Coda; Op. 106-I E-flat section (unusually long canonic passage)

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Classifying a homophonic work as "contrapuntal" because it contains fugato episodes
  **Correction**: The overall classification remains homophonic; contrapuntal passages are episodes within it

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Equating fugato with fugue
  **Clarification**: A fugato uses fugal exposition technique without necessarily following through with full fugal development

- **Confusion**: Thinking contrapuntal passages in development sections are merely textural variety
  **Clarification**: They serve the development process, providing maximum voice independence for thematic working-out
