# Quick Definition
Every succession of tones produces unrest and problems; musical form is an attempt to treat this unrest by halting it, limiting it, or solving the problem through development.

# Core Definition
One single tone is not problematic because the ear defines it as a tonic, a point of repose. Every added tone makes this determination questionable. Every musical form can be considered as an attempt to treat this unrest either by halting or limiting it, or by solving the problem. A melody re-establishes repose through balance. A theme solves the problem by carrying out its consequences. The unrest in a melody need not reach below the surface, while the problem of a theme may penetrate to the profoundest depths.

# Prerequisites
None (this is a foundational theoretical concept)

# Key Properties
- A single tone is a point of repose (the ear defines it as tonic)
- Every added tone introduces unrest by making the tonic determination questionable
- Musical form = an attempt to treat unrest (halt, limit, or solve)
- Melody treats unrest by re-establishing balance (surface unrest)
- Theme treats unrest by carrying out consequences (deep unrest)
- In a theme, repose is scarcely reached or attempted early; the theme sharpens or deepens the problem

# Construction / Recognition
Identify the source of unrest: (1) harmonic tension from departures from the tonic, (2) rhythmic instability, (3) intervallic tension from unresolved leaps or dissonances. Then observe how the music addresses it -- through immediate resolution (melody-like), through deepening and eventual elaboration (theme-like), or through some combination.

# Context & Application
This concept underlies Schoenberg's entire theory of musical form. The degree and depth of unrest determines whether a passage functions as melody (surface unrest, quickly balanced) or theme (deep unrest, requiring extensive development). In a theme, a state of repose will scarcely be reached or attempted early; the theme will generally sharpen its problem or deepen it.

# Examples
- Beethoven Op. 53-I, Op. 57-I, Op. 31/1-I, Op. 31/2-I: themes that sharpen or deepen their problems rather than seeking early repose
- In a melody, the unrest need not reach below the surface
- In a theme, the problem may penetrate to the profoundest depths

# Common Errors
- Treating unrest as something to be avoided rather than the engine of musical motion
- Failing to distinguish surface unrest (melodic) from deep unrest (thematic)
- Resolving thematic unrest too quickly, producing a melodic rather than thematic effect

# Common Confusions
- Unrest is not "wrong" or something to be avoided; it is the fundamental engine of musical motion and form
- The distinction between "halting unrest" (melody) and "solving the problem" (theme) is one of degree and approach, not of quality
