# Quick Definition
The disciplined practice of evaluating one's own compositions for errors and weaknesses, essential for all composers regardless of talent, using the ear as the primary tool.

# Formal Definition
The sense of melody enables one who has it to do the right thing at once without the intervention of self-criticism, but even a master may stray onto the wrong track. When such a deviation occurs, one has to discover where and why one erred, and which is the right track. Self-criticism is therefore necessary to a composer, gifted or not. The best tool of a musician is his ear. Technical advice can be formulated more easily in the negative than in the positive.

# Construction/Recognition
Self-criticism involves systematic evaluation through: (1) listening -- playing or reading harmony and melody separately, (2) analyzing -- identifying significant features and whether they are developed, (3) eliminating non-essentials, (4) avoiding monotony, (5) watching the bass line, (6) making many sketches, and (7) watching harmony, root progressions, and bass line.

# Musical Context
Self-criticism is the practical complement to aesthetic and technical knowledge. A beginner who is not too self-assured and who knows technical maturity has not yet been reached will consider everything written as tentative. Later, the composer will be able to base composing exclusively on the sense of form.

# Common Confusions
- Self-criticism is not self-doubt; it is a disciplined analytical practice that even masters require
- Technical advice is more easily formulated negatively (what to avoid) than positively (what to do); this is a feature of compositional pedagogy, not a limitation
