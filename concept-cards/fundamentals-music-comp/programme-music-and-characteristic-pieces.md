# Quick Definition
Music composed to express specific extramusical associations, including programme music that narrates stories and characteristic pieces expressing particular moods, where the basic motive itself possesses a descriptive character.

# Formal Definition
From the viewpoint of pure aesthetics, music does not express the extramusical. But from the viewpoint of psychology, our capacity for mental and emotional associations is unlimited. Programme music goes so far as to narrate entire stories with musical symbols. Characteristic pieces express every conceivable mood. Descriptive music -- including programme music, stage music, ballet, film music, melodramas, and songs -- under pressure of strong and sudden contrasts, develops its forms in harmony with those emotions, events, and actions which it is supposed to illustrate. In such cases the basic motive itself possesses a descriptive character or mood.

# Construction/Recognition
In descriptive music, the basic motive is often shaped to illustrate an extramusical concept through rhythm, contour, texture, or figuration. The form develops according to the narrative or emotional program rather than purely abstract musical logic.

# Musical Context
A vast repertoire exists in this category, from Beethoven's symphonies to Wagner's operas to film music. Many composers have composed under the urge to express emotional associations. The practice of writing characteristic pieces was especially prominent in the Romantic era.

# Examples
- Beethoven's Pastorale Symphony, second movement: the murmuring brook illustrated by flowing accompaniment (Ex. 68a)
- Wagner's Magic Fire Music from Die Walkuere: flickering flames expressed through rich movement (Ex. 68b)
- Smetana: the source of the River Moldau (Ex. 68c)
- Bach's St. Matthew Passion: the rending of the curtain depicted in a recitative (Ex. 68d)
- Schubert's "The Weathervane": the swing of the weathervane with trills representing its creaking (Ex. 68e)
- Chopin and Schumann: Nocturnes, Ballades, Funeral Marches, Romances, Scenes from Childhood, Flower Pieces, Novelettes

# Common Confusions
- Schoenberg distinguishes between pure aesthetics (music does not express the extramusical) and psychology (our associative capacity is unlimited) -- both positions are valid from their respective viewpoints
- Descriptive music still requires coherent musical form; the program does not excuse incoherence
