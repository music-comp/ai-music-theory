# Quick Definition

The consequent is the second half of a period, functioning as a modified repetition of the antecedent, typically ending with a full cadence on I, V, or III.

# Core Definition

The consequent is a modified repetition of the antecedent, made necessary by the remote motive-forms in m. 3-4 of the antecedent. An unchanged and complete repetition is very rare; even in the simplest cases (Exs. 50b, c, e), at least the last measure is modified. The introduction of a full cadence usually requires earlier changes. If the period is a complete piece (e.g., children's songs), it must end with a perfect authentic cadence on I. If it is part of a larger form, it may end on I, V, or III (major or minor) (Schoenberg, p. 24).

Generally, one or two measures of the beginning will be retained, sometimes with more or less variation. Variation of the harmony can start as early as the first measure of the consequent or as late as the seventh measure.

# Key Properties

1. Modified repetition of the antecedent (not literal repetition)
2. Even the simplest cases modify at least the last measure
3. Harmonic deviation may begin as early as m. 1 or as late as m. 7
4. Must end with appropriate cadence according to function (I for complete pieces; I, V, or III for parts of larger forms)
5. The cadence must differ from the antecedent's, even when arriving on the same degree
6. Retaining one or two measures of the antecedent's beginning is general practice
7. Preserving the rhythm allows extensive changes in melodic contour

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct a Consequent

1. Begin by retaining one or two measures from the antecedent (with possible variation)
2. Plan the cadence: determine the target degree (I, V, or III) and cadence type
3. Introduce harmonic deviation at the appropriate point
4. Ensure the cadence differs from the antecedent's
5. Apply cadence contour: reduce characteristic features to uncharacteristic ones
6. Use rhythmic increase (more common) or decrease in the cadence approach

## To Recognize a Consequent

1. Identify the modified repetition of antecedent material
2. Locate the cadential goal and compare with the antecedent's
3. Note where the harmonic deviation begins

# Context & Application

The consequent serves a double function: providing the satisfying repetition that comprehensibility demands, while simultaneously introducing sufficient variation to maintain interest. The balance between preservation and variation is the central challenge. The preservation of rhythm allows extensive changes in melodic contour, which is a crucial principle: "THE PRESERVATION OF THE RHYTHM ALLOWS EXTENSIVE CHANGES IN THE MELODIC CONTOUR" (p. 25, Schoenberg's emphasis).

# Examples

- **Exs. 50b, c, e** (p. 24): Even in very simple cases, at least the last measure is modified

- **Exs. 45f, k, 48a, g, 50a** (p. 24): Harmonic deviation occurs in the seventh measure

- **Exs. 44f and 47c, e** (p. 24): Deviation occurs in the sixth measure

- **Ex. 45f** (p. 24): Variation consists in use of inversions of the same harmonies

- **Beethoven, Op. 10/3, Menuetto** (p. 24): Consequent starts on II, as a sequence of the antecedent (m. 9)

- **Ex. 47b** (p. 25): Consequent preserves only one rhythm, abandoning even slight variations for the sake of unity; this permits far-reaching changes in melodic contour

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Writing a literal repetition of the antecedent
  **Correction**: Even the simplest consequents modify at least the last measure for cadential purposes

- **Error**: Using the same cadence as the antecedent
  **Correction**: The consequent's cadence must differ from the antecedent's, even when arriving on the same degree

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the consequent must begin identically to the antecedent
  **Clarification**: The consequent may begin on a different degree (II, V) and may vary the opening from the start

- **Confusion**: Believing rhythmic variation is always necessary
  **Clarification**: Preserving the rhythm while changing the melodic contour is a powerful technique for maintaining coherence during variation
