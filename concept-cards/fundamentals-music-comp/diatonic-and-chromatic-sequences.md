# Quick Definition

Diatonic sequences use only notes of the prevailing key and maintain centripetal (tonic-centered) harmony, while chromatic sequences employ altered or substitute chords that may create centrifugal tendencies leading to modulation.

# Core Definition

A diatonic sequence can be executed without using other than diatonic tones; the harmony remains "centripetal" -- centered about the tonic region. Diatonic sequences express the tonality clearly and do not endanger balance in the continuation. When substitutes (altered or chromatic chords) are used, the tendency of the harmony may become "centrifugal" -- may produce modulations, to balance which becomes a problem (Schoenberg, p. 40).

Sequences in minor are even more apt to produce modulation than in major, because of the varying forms of the minor scale. In practice, sequences in minor are most often built around the natural or descending form, with strategic reintroduction of the leading tone to prevent premature modulation (p. 41).

# Key Properties

1. Diatonic sequences: all notes within the prevailing key; intervals may vary in quality
2. Chromatic sequences: altered or substitute chords create modulatory tendencies
3. Centripetal harmony: oriented toward the tonic
4. Centrifugal harmony: tendency to move away from the tonic
5. Minor key sequences are particularly prone to modulation due to three scale forms
6. Natural/descending minor form is preferred for sequences in minor
7. Strategic leading-tone reintroduction prevents premature modulation in minor

# Construction / Recognition

1. For diatonic sequences: keep all notes within the key; accept that intervals may vary in quality (major third may become minor third)
2. For chromatic sequences: use substitute chords carefully, respecting the quasi-melodic function of altered notes
3. In minor: build around the natural/descending form; reintroduce the leading tone strategically
4. Assess whether the result is centripetal (tonic-centered) or centrifugal (modulatory)

# Context & Application

The choice between diatonic and chromatic sequences has significant structural implications. Diatonic sequences are safer for students because they maintain tonal stability. Chromatic sequences offer greater harmonic richness but introduce the problem of balancing the resulting modulations. Substitutes should be used primarily to reinforce the logic of the progression, not merely to add color.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using chromatic sequences without managing the resulting modulations
  **Correction**: Balance centrifugal sequences with simpler motival construction or return to centripetal harmony

- **Error**: In minor, allowing the varying scale forms to produce unintended modulation
  **Correction**: Build around the natural/descending form; strategically reintroduce the leading tone

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking diatonic sequences transpose intervals exactly
  **Clarification**: A diatonic sequence stays within the key, so a major third may become a minor third

- **Confusion**: Thinking chromatic sequences are inherently wrong
  **Clarification**: They require careful management but offer greater harmonic richness
