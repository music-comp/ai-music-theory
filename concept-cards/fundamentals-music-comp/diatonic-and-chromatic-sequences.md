# Quick Definition
Diatonic sequences use only notes of the prevailing key and keep the harmony centripetal (centered on the tonic), while chromatic sequences employ altered or substitute chords that may create centrifugal harmonic tendencies leading to modulation.

# Formal Definition
A diatonic sequence can be executed without using other than diatonic tones; in such cases, the harmony remains "centripetal" -- centered about the tonic region. Diatonic sequences express the tonality clearly and do not endanger balance in the continuation. When substitutes (altered or chromatic chords) are used, the tendency of the harmony may become "centrifugal" -- producing modulations, the balancing of which becomes a compositional problem. Sequences in minor are even more apt to produce modulation than in major because of the varying forms of the minor scale; they are most often built around the natural or descending form, with strategic reintroduction of the leading tone to prevent premature modulation.

# Construction/Recognition
- Diatonic: all notes belong to the prevailing key; intervals between corresponding notes may vary in quality (major vs. minor third, etc.)
- Chromatic: substitutes, altered chords, or chromatic notes appear, potentially creating modulation
- Centripetal harmony: remains oriented toward the tonic
- Centrifugal harmony: tends to move away from the tonic toward foreign regions
- In minor keys: use natural/descending form for the sequence, strategically reintroduce the leading tone
- Substitutes should reinforce the logic of the progression

# Musical Context
The choice between diatonic and chromatic sequences has significant structural implications. Diatonic sequences are safer for students because they maintain tonal stability. Chromatic sequences offer greater harmonic richness but introduce the problem of balancing the resulting modulations. In minor keys, the three forms of the scale (natural, harmonic, melodic) create additional opportunities and risks for unintended modulation during sequential treatment.

# Common Confusions
- A diatonic sequence does not transpose intervals exactly (a major third may become a minor third); this is the nature of staying within the key.
- Chromatic sequences are not inherently wrong but require careful management of the modulations they create.
- In minor, the varying scale forms mean that even a "diatonic" sequence may inadvertently suggest modulation if the leading tone appears or disappears at the wrong moment.
