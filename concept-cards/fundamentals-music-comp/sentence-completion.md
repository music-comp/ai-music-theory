# Quick Definition

The sentence practice form consists of eight measures: the first four comprise a phrase and its repetition (tonic form/dominant form), while the continuation (m. 5-8) employs development and liquidation to reach a cadence.

# Core Definition

The sentence is a higher form of construction than the period. It not only makes use of the driving force of musical construction, but begins it at once. The practice form consists, in simpler cases, of eight measures, of which the first four comprise a phrase and its repetition. The technique applied in the continuation is a kind of development, comparable to the condensing technique of liquidation. The purpose of liquidation is to counteract the tendency toward unlimited extension (Schoenberg, p. 39).

The end of a sentence calls for the same treatment as the consequent of a period: it may close on I, V, or III, with a suitable cadence (full, half, Phrygian, plagal; perfect or imperfect) according to its function (p. 39).

# Prerequisites

- **Beginning the sentence** -- The first four measures (tonic form/dominant form) must be established before completion
- **Motive** -- The continuation operates on motive-forms
- **Connecting motive-forms** -- Development requires the ability to derive remote motive-forms

# Key Properties

1. Eight measures in the practice form
2. M. 1-4: phrase and its repetition (tonic form/dominant form)
3. M. 5-8: continuation using development and liquidation
4. The beginning already includes repetition, so continuation demands more remote motive-forms
5. Sequence-like procedures are very useful in the continuation
6. Liquidation progressively eliminates characteristic features
7. Phrase shortening supports liquidation (two-measure to one-measure to half-measure)
8. Closes with appropriate cadence on I, V, or III

# Construction / Recognition

## To Complete a Sentence (Practice Form)

1. Establish the opening phrase (m. 1-2)
2. Repeat with modification (m. 3-4) as dominant form or transposed repetition
3. Begin development in m. 5 using transformations of preceding motive-forms
4. Use sequence-like procedures for structured extension
5. Begin liquidation: progressively eliminate characteristic features
6. Shorten phrase units (two-measure to one-measure)
7. Close with appropriate cadence

## To Recognize a Sentence

1. Identify the twofold statement in the opening (phrase + repetition)
2. Locate the developmental continuation with more remote motive-forms
3. Note progressive phrase shortening and feature elimination
4. Confirm cadential closure

# Context & Application

The sentence form is much used in leading themes of sonatas and symphonies but is applicable also to smaller forms. Its distinguishing characteristic is that development begins immediately after the twofold opening statement, unlike the period where repetition is postponed until the consequent. A piece of music resembles a photograph album, displaying under changing circumstances the life of its basic idea -- its basic motive (p. 39).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating the continuation as another repetition of the opening phrase
  **Correction**: The opening already includes repetition; the continuation demands more remote motive-forms and development

- **Error**: Ending the sentence without liquidation
  **Correction**: Characteristic features must be progressively eliminated to enable cadential closure

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking all eight-measure themes are sentences
  **Clarification**: The sentence specifically requires an opening twofold statement followed by development and liquidation; periods have a different structure

- **Confusion**: Equating the sentence's development with sonata-form development
  **Clarification**: The principles are related but operate at different structural levels
