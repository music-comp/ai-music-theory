# Quick Definition
A sustained bass note (usually the dominant) held throughout the B-section, providing contrast through harmonic stasis against the moving bass of the A-section, while suggesting the contraposition of the dominant region.

# Core Definition
The pedal point on V, stationary in contrast to the moving bass of the A-section, suggests the contraposition of the dominant region to the tonic region. The pedal point provides both contrast (through its static quality) and coherence (through its harmonic connection to the tonic). In some cases, the mind retains the pedal point even when the bass is temporarily omitted, provided the upper voices express the harmony definitively.

# Key Properties
- Usually on the dominant (V)
- Stationary bass contrasts with the moving bass of the A-section
- Suggests contraposition of dominant region to tonic region
- Provides both contrast (stasis) and coherence (harmonic connection)
- The mind can retain the pedal even when the bass is temporarily omitted
- Can also be on the tonic (unusual, as in Op. 31/1-III)
- Each reappearance of V may be introduced by other harmonies (e.g., II)
- Progressive condensation of the repetition is possible above the pedal

# Construction / Recognition
A pedal point in the B-section is recognized by: (1) sustained or reiterated bass note, usually V, (2) harmonic activity above the pedal creating surface variety, (3) contrast with the active bass of the A-section, (4) eventual resolution or connection to the upbeat chord.

# Context & Application
The pedal point is one of the most common devices for organizing the B-section. It provides a stable foundation that allows motivic development above it while maintaining clear harmonic direction. The dominant pedal naturally prepares the return of the tonic.

# Examples
- Beethoven Op. 2/1-II: pedal on V, stationary vs. moving bass of A-section
- Beethoven Op. 2/2-IV: B-section again on a pedal
- Beethoven Op. 28-II: pedal on V, each V introduced by II, progressively condensed repetition
- Beethoven Op. 31/1-III: tonic pedal in B-section (because A-section uses dominant pedal) -- reversed I-V relation
- Haydn Ex. 102e: dominant pedal of tonic minor; bass omitted m. 13-17 on supposition that mind retains it

# Common Errors
- Using a pedal point without creating sufficient harmonic activity above it
- Failing to recognize that the tonic pedal can work when the surrounding sections use dominant pedals
- Neglecting to prepare the resolution of the pedal into the upbeat chord

# Common Confusions
- The pedal point is not always literally sustained; it may be reiterated or temporarily absent while retained in the listener's memory
- A tonic pedal in the B-section is unusual but effective when surrounding sections use a dominant pedal
