# Quick Definition
The historical expansion of what counts as melodious, driven by developments in harmony, vocal technique, and dramatic expression, such that earlier definitions of melody and "melodious" are no longer adequate.

# Formal Definition
Prior to the great changes which began in the first quarter of the nineteenth century, aestheticians could define melody in terms of beauty, expressiveness, simplicity, naturalness, tunefulness, singability, unity, proportion, and balance. But taking into account the development of harmony and its influence on every concept of musical aesthetics, earlier definitions of "theme" and "melody," "melodious" and "unmelodious," are no longer adequate. The ear of the modern musician has gradually acquired the capacity to comprehend the most remote harmonies as coherent elements of a tonality.

# Construction/Recognition
Criteria of the unmelodious remain: imbalance, incoherence, inadequate integration with the harmony or accompaniment, and incongruity of phrasing and rhythm. But intervals and progressions that would once have been considered unmelodious may now be natural. Time has changed both creative writing and our comprehension of earlier music.

# Musical Context
This evolution is traced through vocal literature: Schubert's remote modulations, Brahms's wide ranges and rhythmic complexity, Wagner's extreme vocal demands, Debussy and Strauss following Wagner, Italian composers absorbing Wagner's influence, Moussorgsky's folkloristic influence, and the extended tonality of the post-Wagnerian epoch.

# Examples
- Wagner: for dramatic purposes, often went beyond the limits of the voice (Ex. 78a)
- Moussorgsky (Ex. 87): melodic progressions influenced by Oriental folk-music that in turn influenced Western melodic writing
- Puccini (Ex. 88): pseudo-Chinese folklorism with exceptionally modern harmony
- Schoenberg songs (Ex. 91): extended tonality making remote intervals and progressions natural in melody
- Beethoven String Quartets (Exs. 96a, b): themes now heard as melodies, though structurally they are instrumental themes

# Common Confusions
- The evolution of melodic limits does not mean "anything goes"; criteria of the unmelodious (imbalance, incoherence) remain valid
- Historical perspective means we hear earlier music differently than its contemporaries did; Beethoven themes now sound like melodies to modern ears
