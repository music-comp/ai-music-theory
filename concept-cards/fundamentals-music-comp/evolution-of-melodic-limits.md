# Quick Definition
The historical expansion of what counts as melodious, driven by developments in harmony, vocal technique, and dramatic expression, such that earlier definitions of melody and "melodious" are no longer adequate.

# Core Definition
Prior to the great changes which began in the first quarter of the nineteenth century, aestheticians could define melody in terms of beauty, expressiveness, simplicity, naturalness, tunefulness, singability, unity, proportion, and balance. But taking into account the development of harmony and its influence on every concept of musical aesthetics, earlier definitions of "theme" and "melody," "melodious" and "unmelodious," are no longer adequate. The ear of the modern musician has gradually acquired the capacity to comprehend the most remote harmonies as coherent elements of a tonality.

# Key Properties
- Earlier definitions of melody were based on beauty, simplicity, naturalness, singability
- The development of harmony since the 19th century made those definitions inadequate
- The modern ear comprehends remote harmonies as coherent tonal elements
- Criteria of the unmelodious remain: imbalance, incoherence, inadequate integration with harmony, incongruity of phrasing
- Time has changed both creative writing and our comprehension of earlier music
- What was once considered "unmelodious" may now be heard as natural

# Construction / Recognition
The evolution can be traced through vocal literature: Schubert's remote modulations, Brahms's wide ranges, Wagner's extreme vocal demands, Debussy and Strauss following Wagner, Italian composers absorbing Wagner's influence, folkloristic influences (Moussorgsky, Puccini), and extended tonality (Schoenberg).

# Context & Application
This evolution means that historical context matters when evaluating melody. Beethoven string quartet themes that are structurally instrumental now sound like melodies to modern ears. The discussion has "perhaps only clarified the limits of the melodious, but has not determined what is unmelodious."

# Examples

# Common Errors
- Assuming "anything goes" because melodic limits have expanded
- Applying only 18th-century criteria to evaluate 19th- or 20th-century melody
- Ignoring that criteria of the unmelodious (imbalance, incoherence) remain valid

# Common Confusions
- The evolution of melodic limits does not mean "anything goes"; criteria of imbalance and incoherence remain valid
- Historical perspective means we hear earlier music differently than its contemporaries did
