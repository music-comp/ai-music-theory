# Quick Definition

The progressive shortening of a motivic pattern to its smallest identifiable fragments (residues), often treated in an ostinato-like manner over a dominant pedal point, as the final stage of liquidation before the recapitulation.

# Core Definition

Reduction to residues is the process whereby the motivic pattern used in sequential elaboration is progressively shortened to its smallest recognizable elements. After the sequence, the emphasis it created must be "neutralized" in order to introduce the recapitulation "in a manner at once 'surprising and expected', as Beethoven expressed it" (Ch. 16, p. 88). This is accomplished "by gradually depriving the motive-forms of their characteristic features and dissolving them into uncharacteristic forms, such as scales, broken chords, etc." (Ch. 16, p. 88). The residues -- typically fragments of one or two notes -- may be dwelt upon in an ostinato-like treatment over the dominant pedal.

# Prerequisites

- **developing-variation** -- Residues are the endpoint of the variation process applied to motives
- **spinning-out** -- Sequential elaboration creates the emphasis that reduction to residues must neutralize

# Key Properties

1. The pattern is reduced in stages: full pattern to half, to quarter, to individual notes
2. Residues are treated in an ostinato-like manner (repeated insistently)
3. Usually occurs over a dominant pedal point
4. Scale lines, broken chords, and other "uncharacteristic" forms may accompany the residues
5. Marks the transition from the modulatory instability of the B-section to the stability of the recapitulation
6. The combination of repose (pedal) and suspense (diminished pattern) creates the conditions for the theme's re-entrance
7. Residues are not random fragments -- they are the smallest still-recognizable elements of the original pattern

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct Reduction to Residues

1. Begin with the full motivic pattern from the sequential elaboration
2. Reduce the pattern by half (e.g., four measures to two)
3. Continue reducing (two measures to one, then to individual note groups)
4. Establish a dominant pedal point as the harmonic foundation
5. Treat the smallest residues in an ostinato-like manner over the pedal
6. Allow scale lines, broken chords, or other "neutral" forms to dissolve remaining characteristics
7. The passage should create anticipation for the recapitulation

## To Recognize Reduction to Residues

1. Identify progressive shortening of a previously established pattern
2. Look for ostinato-like repetition of very small fragments
3. Note the presence of a dominant pedal point
4. Observe dissolution of motivic character into neutral forms (scales, broken chords)
5. Confirm that the passage precedes a recapitulation or thematic return

# Context & Application

Reduction to residues is the culmination of the liquidation process, particularly prominent in the B-section (modulatory contrasting middle section) of scherzo forms and related ternary structures. After the sequential modulation has created emphasis and instability, the progressive reduction dissolves this emphasis, preparing the listener for the return of the theme. The technique creates a characteristic texture: minimal motivic content over static harmony, generating anticipation. The retarding effect of the pedal point is particularly appropriate at this juncture, as "it keeps at least the bass from progressing" (Ch. 16, p. 89).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 88): Beethoven, Op. 26, Scherzo. After the sequence, the four-measure pattern is reduced to two measures (m. 25-26); after a varied repetition, further reduced to five notes (m. 28); to four notes (m. 29-30); to two notes (m. 33 ff.); and to one note (m. 41 ff.). The upbeat harmony is reached at the beginning of the first reduction (m. 25) and continued through m. 44.

**Example 2** (p. 88, Ex. 122a, m. 25-29): Residues dwelt upon over the dominant in ostinato-like treatment, with the contour of the pattern "depreciated into the inconspicuous form of a descending scale line."

**Example 3** (p. 89): Beethoven, Symphony No. 4-III. Reduction from four measures to two, to one, to two-thirds of a measure.

**Example 4** (p. 89): Beethoven, String Quartet, Op. 18/6, Scherzo. Rhythm condensed to equal eighth-notes in m. 39, finally liquidated into scale lines and broken chords.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using random fragments instead of recognizable motivic residues
  **Correction**: Residues must be the smallest still-recognizable elements of the original pattern

- **Error**: Continuing modulatory motion during the reduction phase
  **Correction**: The dominant pedal should provide harmonic stasis; the purpose is to neutralize emphasis, not create more

- **Error**: Introducing new thematic material during the reduction
  **Correction**: The ostinato-like treatment of residues is the final stage of dissolution, not a new thematic idea

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Residues are random fragments
  **Clarification**: They are the smallest still-recognizable elements of the original pattern, systematically derived through progressive shortening

- **Confusion**: The ostinato-like treatment of residues is a new thematic idea
  **Clarification**: It is the final stage of dissolution of previous material

- **Confusion**: Reduction to residues is identical to pedal point technique
  **Clarification**: Reduction to residues is a motivic process that typically coincides with harmonic stasis (pedal point), but the two are separate techniques working together
