# Quick Definition
A passage that returns from a contrasting section (subordinate theme or elaboration) back to the principal theme or tonic region, typically consisting of liquidation over a dominant pedal point.

# Formal Definition
In rondos, the return from the subordinate theme to the principal theme, and in sonatas the return to the beginning after the exposition, are generally simple enough that no special segment is necessary. Functionally and psychologically more important are the retransitions after modulatory contrasts and Durchfuhrungen. Since the actual remodulation usually takes place as part of the preceding modulatory section, the retransition proper starts on the upbeat harmony and generally consists of little more than liquidation of the motival residues. The motive material often derives from subsidiary themes; sometimes references to features of the principal theme forecast its return.

# Construction/Recognition
- Begins on the upbeat harmony (usually the dominant) after remodulation has already occurred
- Consists primarily of liquidation of motival residues
- May use material from subsidiary themes or anticipatory references to the principal theme
- Often built over a dominant pedal point
- Dynamic and rhythmic changes frequently contribute to dramatic effect

# Musical Context
Retransitions are most significant after the elaboration (Durchfuhrung) in sonata-allegro forms, where they prepare the psychological return to the recapitulation. In rondos, simpler retransitions serve as bridges and spacers between sections.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I (m. 210-24) and Op. 10/1-I (m. 158-67): little more than liquidating devices around the dominant harmony
- Beethoven, Op. 7-I: extraordinarily, remodulation plus retransition occurs in only two measures (m. 187-8), after dwelling in the remote regions of a minor and d minor
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I (m. 93): motive material clearly anticipates the second measure of the principal theme
- Beethoven, Op. 90-I: a sixteenth-note figure (m. 130) is augmented to longer values and undergoes rhythmic metamorphoses, preparing the first three notes of the theme (m. 144)
- Eroica Symphony-I (m. 366): the famous horn entrance presents the tonic form of the main theme over sustained dominant harmony
- Beethoven, Op. 13-I: dominant pedal (m. 167-187) with frequent dynamic changes and sforzandi making this the dramatic climax of the movement

# Common Confusions
- The retransition is not the same as the remodulation; the actual harmonic return to the dominant usually happens within the preceding modulatory section, and the retransition proper begins on that dominant
- Simple retransitions in rondos (small bridging segments) should not be confused with the more dramatic and psychologically important retransitions after Durchfuhrungen
