# Quick Definition
The characteristic types of movements within a sonata cycle: fast outer movements (typically sonata-allegro or rondo), slow movements (Allegretto to Adagio/Largo), and moderately fast stylized dance forms (minuet or scherzo), with occasional substitution of variations or fugues.

# Core Definition
In general, the first and last movements are in rapid tempo. Intervening movements fall generally into two types, slow and moderately fast. The slow movements vary from Allegretto or Andantino to Adagio, Largo, or Grave. The moderately fast movements are ordinarily stylized dance forms, e.g., minuets or scherzos. Occasionally, one of the movements is a Theme with Variations. The last movement is frequently written in one of the rondo forms, though quite often it uses sonata-allegro form.

# Key Properties
- Fast outer movements: typically sonata-allegro (first), rondo or sonata-allegro (last)
- Slow movements: Allegretto/Andantino to Adagio/Largo/Grave
- Moderately fast movements: minuet or scherzo (stylized dance)
- Occasional substitutions: theme with variations, fugue
- A short slow introduction may precede the first movement's allegro
- Rare exceptions: slow last movement (Op. 111), unconventional first movement (Op. 54)
- Absence of slow movement may permit both minuet and scherzo (Op. 31/3)

# Construction / Recognition
1. First movement: usually sonata-allegro in rapid tempo (sometimes preceded by slow introduction)
2. Middle movements: one slow, one moderately fast (dance form)
3. Last movement: usually rondo or sonata-allegro
4. Look for departures from the norm: variations, fugue, unconventional tempo ordering

# Context & Application
The movement types within a sonata cycle provide the large-scale contrasts of character, tempo, and form that make the cycle a satisfying whole. The flexibility of the scheme allows for tremendous variety while maintaining a recognizable framework.

# Examples
- Standard four-movement: Allegro (sonata-allegro), Adagio, Minuet/Scherzo, Rondo
- Beethoven, Op. 26: begins with Andante con Variazioni instead of Allegro
- Beethoven, Op. 27/2: begins with Adagio sostenuto
- Beethoven, Op. 14/2-II: Andante movement is a Theme with Variations
- Beethoven, Op. 74-IV: finale is a Theme with Variations
- Beethoven, Op. 109: finale is Variations
- Beethoven, Op. 110: finale is a Fugue
- Beethoven, Op. 111: Adagio molto as rare slow last movement

# Common Errors
- Treating the movement plan as rigid when it is highly flexible
- Neglecting the contrast principle that governs the choice and ordering of movement types
- Assuming every cycle must have exactly four movements

# Common Confusions
- The "standard" plan is a norm, not a rule; Beethoven's works show enormous variety
- The designation "first movement form" for sonata-allegro does not mean it can only be used in first movements
- Scherzos sometimes move at an exceedingly rapid pace, not just "moderately fast"
