# Quick Definition
The standard categories of movements within a sonata cycle: fast outer movements, a slow movement, and a moderately fast stylized dance movement, with considerable flexibility in ordering and character.

# Formal Definition
In general, the first and last movements are in rapid tempo, though exceptions exist. Intervening movements fall generally into two types: slow (varying from Allegretto or Andantino to Adagio, Largo, or Grave) and moderately fast (ordinarily stylized dance forms, e.g., minuets or scherzos). The latter sometimes move at an exceedingly rapid pace. A short introduction in slow tempo followed by the customary allegro is not uncommon.

# Construction/Recognition
Typical movement types and their associated forms:
- **First movement**: Usually sonata-allegro form in fast tempo; occasionally preceded by a slow introduction
- **Slow movement**: Andante forms (aba, abab), sometimes theme and variations
- **Dance movement**: Minuet or scherzo with trio; moderately fast to very rapid
- **Finale**: Frequently a rondo form; sometimes sonata-allegro; occasionally variations or fugue
- The last movement is frequently written in one of the rondo forms (variations like Op. 109 or a fugue as in Op. 110 are exceptional)

# Musical Context
The variety of movement types within a sonata cycle provides the large-scale contrast of character, tempo, and mood that sustains listener interest across an extended work. The standard types evolved through the practice of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven, but exceptions are numerous.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 14/2: Allegro (G, 2/4), Andante (C, 2/2), Scherzo (G, 3/8)
- Beethoven, Op. 59/1: Allegro (F, 4/4), Allegretto (Bb, 3/8), Adagio (f, 2/4), Allegro (F, 2/4) -- note the unusual placement of the slow movement third
- Beethoven, Op. 130: six movements including Adagio ma non troppo-Allegro, Presto, Allegro assai, Adagio molto espressivo, and Finale-Allegro
- Beethoven, Op. 26-I: "Andante con Variazioni" as first movement
- Beethoven, Op. 111: only two movements, the second being "Adagio molto"

# Common Confusions
- The "standard" arrangement of movements is a generalization, not a rule; Beethoven's practice shows that virtually any ordering is possible
- A scherzo may move at an "exceedingly rapid pace," making it faster than many first movements
- The slow movement may be placed second or third in the cycle; both orderings are standard
