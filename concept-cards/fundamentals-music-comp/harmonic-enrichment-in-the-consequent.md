# Quick Definition
The consequent typically requires richer harmonic treatment than the antecedent, particularly in the approach to its cadence, with harmonic deviation beginning anywhere from the first to the seventh measure.

# Formal Definition
The consequent's need for a different (usually full) cadence creates harmonic requirements that distinguish it from the antecedent. Harmonic variation can start as early as the first measure of the consequent or as late as the seventh. The variation may consist of using inversions of the same harmonies, beginning on a different degree, enriching the cadence through more frequent chord changes, or introducing harmonic substitutions. More frequent changes of harmony and increasing use of small notes support the melodic approach to a climax.

# Construction/Recognition
- Harmonic deviation starting at the first measure: Ex. 45f uses inversions of the same harmonies
- Consequent beginning on a different degree: II (Beethoven, Op. 10/3, Menuetto), V (Exs. 44b, 48d)
- Intensification through harmonic concentration: two harmonies per measure in the cadence approach (Ex. 46d, m. 6-7)
- Remote variation and richer movement in cadential measures (Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV, m. 7-8)
- Enriched cadence beginning in the harmonic variation of m. 5 (Ex. 48d)

# Musical Context
Harmonic enrichment serves both structural and expressive purposes. Structurally, the full cadence requires a more elaborate harmonic approach than the antecedent's half cadence. Expressively, the richer harmony contributes to the sense of culmination and closure. The Romantic composers take harmonic enrichment much further, with Brahms exploiting the multiple meaning of harmonies in ways that far exceed classical practice.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-II: More frequent changes of harmony and increasing use of small notes support the melodic approach to a climax in m. 6
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: M. 7-8 produce necessary intensification through remote variation and richer movement
- Ex. 46d: Two harmonies used in both m. 6 and m. 7 (intensification through concentration)
- Brahms (Ex. 51a): Prolific exploitation of multiple meaning of harmonies; few harmonies belong diatonically to f-minor; most could be understood as Neapolitan sixth of c-minor

# Common Confusions
- Harmonic enrichment is not simply adding more chords; it involves the strategic placement of harmonic variety to serve the cadential function.
- The consequent does not need to be harmonically different from the very beginning; deviation can occur as late as the seventh measure.
- Using inversions of the same harmonies counts as harmonic variation, even though the root progression may be identical.
