# Quick Definition

The consequent typically requires richer harmonic treatment than the antecedent, particularly in the approach to its cadence, with harmonic deviation beginning anywhere from the first to the seventh measure.

# Core Definition

The consequent's need for a different (usually full) cadence creates harmonic requirements that distinguish it from the antecedent. Harmonic variation can start as early as the first measure of the consequent or as late as the seventh. The variation may consist of using inversions of the same harmonies, beginning on a different degree, enriching the cadence through more frequent chord changes, or introducing harmonic substitutions. More frequent changes of harmony and increasing use of small notes support the melodic approach to a climax (Schoenberg, pp. 22, 24).

# Prerequisites

- **Antecedent of the period** -- The consequent is a modification of the antecedent
- **Consequent of the period** -- Harmonic enrichment is a technique applied within the consequent
- **Half cadence and full cadence in periods** -- The need for a different cadence drives the harmonic changes

# Key Properties

1. Harmonic deviation can begin as early as m. 1 or as late as m. 7 of the consequent
2. Using inversions of the same harmonies counts as harmonic variation
3. The consequent may begin on a different degree (II, V, etc.)
4. More frequent chord changes intensify the cadence approach
5. Two harmonies per measure (harmonic concentration) produces cadential intensification
6. Remote variation and richer movement often appear in the final measures

# Construction / Recognition

1. Retain the first one or two measures of the antecedent (with possible variation)
2. Introduce harmonic deviation at the appropriate point (depending on how much variation is needed)
3. For minimal deviation: use inversions of the same harmonies
4. For moderate deviation: begin on a different degree (II as sequence, or V)
5. Intensify the cadence through concentration (more harmonies per measure)
6. Support the melodic approach to a climax with increased harmonic activity

# Context & Application

Harmonic enrichment serves both structural and expressive purposes. Structurally, the full cadence requires a more elaborate harmonic approach than the antecedent's half cadence. Expressively, the richer harmony contributes to the sense of culmination and closure. Romantic composers take harmonic enrichment much further, with Brahms exploiting the multiple meaning of harmonies in ways that far exceed classical practice.

# Examples

- **Beethoven, Op. 2/1-II** (p. 22): More frequent changes of harmony and increasing use of small notes support the melodic approach to a climax in m. 6

- **Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV** (p. 22): M. 7-8 produce necessary intensification through remote variation and richer movement

- **Ex. 46d** (p. 24): Two harmonies used in both m. 6 and m. 7 (intensification through concentration)

- **Ex. 45f** (p. 24): Variation consists in use of inversions of the same harmonies

- **Beethoven, Op. 10/3, Menuetto** (p. 24): Consequent begins on II as a sequence of the antecedent

- **Brahms, Ex. 51a** (p. 25): Prolific exploitation of multiple meaning of harmonies; few harmonies belong diatonically to f-minor

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Changing the harmony too drastically in the first measure, losing the sense of modified repetition
  **Correction**: The consequent should begin as a recognizable repetition; deviation can be subtle at first

- **Error**: Failing to intensify the cadence through harmonic concentration
  **Correction**: Use more frequent chord changes in the final measures to create effective closure

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking harmonic enrichment means simply adding more chords
  **Clarification**: It involves strategic placement of harmonic variety to serve the cadential function

- **Confusion**: Believing deviation must begin early
  **Clarification**: Deviation can occur as late as the seventh measure; even the simplest cases modify at least the last measure

- **Confusion**: Equating inversions with different harmonies
  **Clarification**: Using inversions of the same harmonies counts as harmonic variation; the root progression may be identical
