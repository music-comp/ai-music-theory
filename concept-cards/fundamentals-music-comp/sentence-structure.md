# Quick Definition
The sentence is a higher form of thematic construction than the period, in which the first four measures comprise a phrase and its immediate repetition, followed by a continuation that employs development and liquidation techniques.

# Formal Definition
The sentence is a higher form of construction than the period. It begins with a phrase and its repetition (tonic form and dominant form, as established in Chapter V), which already includes repetition within the first four measures. Because repetition has already occurred, the continuation demands more remotely varied motive-forms. The practice form consists of eight measures: the first four comprising the phrase and its repetition, and the remaining four employing development and liquidation techniques that lead to a cadence. The sentence form is much used in leading themes of sonatas and symphonies but is applicable to smaller forms as well.

# Construction/Recognition
- M. 1-2: Initial phrase (tonic form)
- M. 3-4: Modified repetition (dominant form or comparable twofold statement)
- M. 5-8: Continuation using development, condensation, and liquidation
- Condensed phrases in the continuation (two-measure phrases reduced to one measure, or four to two)
- Cadential conclusion on I, V, or III with appropriate cadence type
- The end calls for the same treatment as the consequent of a period

# Musical Context
A piece of music resembles a photograph album displaying the life of its basic idea under changing circumstances. The sentence makes a particularly strong structural statement because it begins with immediate repetition and then drives forward through development and liquidation. Sentences from masterworks often differ considerably from the practice form, with irregular lengths caused by remote motive-forms requiring extra repetitions, unequal unit sizes, or insertions.

# Examples
- Exs. 52a, b: Two-measure phrases reduced to one measure in m. 5-6
- Ex. 52c: Four measures condensed to two (m. 9-10 and 11-12), with further condensation to one measure
- Mozart, Overture to The Marriage of Figaro (Ex. 59f): Only seven measures
- Longer sentences caused by remote motive-forms, unequal unit connections, or insertions

# Common Confusions
- The sentence is not simply "another name for a period"; it has a fundamentally different structure -- immediate repetition followed by development, versus postponed repetition.
- The practice form of eight measures is an abstraction; masterwork sentences regularly deviate in length.
- "Higher form" does not mean "better" -- it means more complex in its developmental demands.
