# Quick Definition
The first division of a sonata-allegro, presenting principal and subordinate themes in contrasting but related tonalities, connected by a transition, and closed by codettas or a closing theme -- essentially stable in harmony, providing the thematic material for subsequent elaboration.

# Core Definition
The exposition resembles the first division of the larger rondos in several respects. The statements of the principal and subordinate themes are in contrasting but related tonalities. A transition connects them in all but the simplest cases. A distinct contrast of character helps to distinguish the various themes, though analysis will demonstrate their inter-connexion through common use of basic motive-features. It differs from the rondos in that the principal theme is not repeated prior to the elaboration, and there is no return to the principal key. Instead, a closing section, still in a contrasting region, completes the exposition.

# Key Properties
- Principal and subordinate themes in contrasting but related tonalities
- Transition connects the themes (except in simplest cases)
- Distinct contrast of character distinguishes themes
- Themes interconnected through common basic motive-features
- No return to principal theme or key before the elaboration (unlike rondo)
- Closing section (codettas or closing theme) in contrasting region completes the exposition
- Harmony is essentially stable (apart from transitions)
- Closing theme may derive from principal theme, from preceding material, or be entirely distinct

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the principal theme or group in the tonic region
2. Find the transition (may be independent or derived from principal theme)
3. Locate the subordinate group in the contrasting key (usually V in major, III or v in minor)
4. Note the closing section or codettas marking the end
5. Verify that the principal theme does not return before the end of the exposition

# Context & Application
The exposition establishes all the thematic material that will be elaborated in the middle section and recapitulated in the final section. Its essentially stable harmony provides the necessary contrast with the modulatory elaboration. The richness and variety of themes exposed in this section determine the possibilities for the elaboration.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: eight-measure sentence as principal theme; transposition provides transition material; subordinate theme in m. 21
- Beethoven, Op. 7-I: extensive subordinate group with six distinct sections (m. 41-136), each cadence overlapping the next
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-I: subordinate group of 102 measures after a principal theme of only 22 measures
- Beethoven, Op. 28-I: principal theme of 39 measures; subordinate group of 124 measures

# Common Errors
- Returning to the principal theme before the closing of the exposition (that would be rondo procedure)
- Making the exposition's harmony too modulatory, leaving nothing for the elaboration to contrast
- Failing to establish the subordinate key firmly enough

# Common Confusions
- The exposition resembles the rondo's first division but differs in not returning the principal theme before the elaboration
- A closing theme may derive from the principal theme, but it functions as a closing, not as a return
- The "stability" of the exposition is relative -- transitions modulate, and the subordinate group is in a different key -- but overall, each section stands in a definite tonal region
