# Quick Definition
An accompaniment style where all voices move in the same rhythm (homorhythmically), as in a chorale or hymn setting, which is more common in choral music than in instrumental writing.

# Formal Definition
Chorale-like accompaniment is a style in which all voices sing or play the same rhythm according to the text or musical phrasing. This is the most straightforward approach to accompaniment, with all parts moving together homophonically. It is rather seldom used in instrumental music but appears more often in homophonic choral music. In instrumental contexts, the voice leading remains essentially chorale-like even when broken-chord figuration is applied, making the chorale style the foundation underlying more elaborate accompaniment types.

# Construction/Recognition
- All voices move in the same rhythm (homorhythmic texture)
- Block chords or sustained harmonies
- Voice leading follows standard chorale-harmony principles
- More common in choral than instrumental music
- Serves as the underlying framework for broken-chord figuration

# Musical Context
Though rarely used in pure form in instrumental music, chorale-like accompaniment provides the harmonic and voice-leading foundation for all other accompaniment types. Broken-chord figuration (the most common piano accompaniment) is essentially chorale harmony distributed across time. Understanding chorale-like accompaniment is therefore prerequisite to understanding more elaborate accompaniment techniques.

# Common Confusions
- Chorale-like accompaniment is not primitive or unsophisticated; it is used effectively in masterpieces for specific expressive purposes.
- Even when broken chords replace block chords, the underlying voice leading remains essentially chorale-like.
- "Chorale-like" refers to the homorhythmic texture, not necessarily to the character of a church hymn.
