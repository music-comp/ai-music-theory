# Quick Definition

An accompaniment style where all voices move in the same rhythm (homorhythmically), more common in choral music than instrumental writing, serving as the foundational voice-leading framework for all other accompaniment types.

# Core Definition

Chorale-like accompaniment is a style in which all voices sing or play the same rhythm according to the text or musical phrasing. It is rather seldom used in instrumental music but appears more often in homophonic choral music. In instrumental contexts, the voice leading remains essentially chorale-like even when broken-chord figuration is applied (Schoenberg, p. 52). This makes the chorale style the underlying foundation for more elaborate accompaniment types.

# Key Properties

1. All voices move in the same rhythm (homorhythmic texture)
2. Block chords or sustained harmonies
3. Voice leading follows standard chorale-harmony principles
4. More common in choral than instrumental music
5. Serves as the underlying framework for broken-chord figuration
6. Rarely used in pure form in instrumental music

# Construction / Recognition

1. Write all voices in the same rhythm
2. Follow standard voice-leading principles (chorale harmony)
3. Ensure smooth voice connections and proper resolution
4. Recognize that broken-chord figuration is essentially chorale harmony distributed in time

# Context & Application

Though rarely used in pure form in instrumental music, chorale-like accompaniment provides the harmonic and voice-leading foundation for all other accompaniment types. Broken-chord figuration (the most common piano accompaniment) is essentially chorale harmony distributed across time. Understanding chorale-like accompaniment is therefore prerequisite to understanding more elaborate techniques.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Ignoring voice-leading principles when writing chorale-like accompaniment
  **Correction**: Standard chorale-harmony voice leading applies strictly

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking chorale-like accompaniment is primitive or unsophisticated
  **Clarification**: It is used effectively in masterpieces for specific expressive purposes

- **Confusion**: Equating "chorale-like" with the character of a church hymn
  **Clarification**: The term refers to homorhythmic texture, not necessarily to liturgical character

- **Confusion**: Thinking broken chords have nothing to do with chorale writing
  **Clarification**: Even when broken chords replace block chords, the underlying voice leading remains essentially chorale-like
