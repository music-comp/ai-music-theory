# Quick Definition
The use of contrapuntal techniques (canon, inversion, combinative counterpoint) within the minuet form, representing a fusion of homophonic-melodic and contrapuntal approaches to musical construction.

# Formal Definition
Although many movements in the classic literature combine homophonic and contrapuntal techniques, there is a fundamental difference between them. The homophonic-melodic treatment depends basically on development of a motive by variation. The contrapuntal treatment does not vary the motive, but displays the possibilities of combination inherent in the basic theme or themes. In rare cases, both techniques are genuinely fused, with motives admitting canonic imitations and inversions while also undergoing the usual variation of basic motive-forms.

# Construction/Recognition
- Canonic imitation: one voice follows another at a fixed interval and time delay
- Inversion: motives appear simultaneously with their mirror forms
- Combination: multiple themes presented together in prime, octave, or other intervals
- Double counterpoint: interchange of voice positions (upper and lower)
- Reduction: deriving accompanying figures from the basic motives by simplification
- Look for the coexistence of contrapuntal display and homophonic motive variation

# Musical Context
Contrapuntal treatment in the minuet is relatively rare but produces some of the most sophisticated examples of the form. The fusion of homophonic variation with contrapuntal combination represents a high point of compositional technique. Most minuets remain primarily homophonic, with the contrapuntal examples standing as exceptional achievements.

# Examples
- Mozart, String Quartet in A, K.V. 464 (Ex. 116): A rare example of real fusion of homophonic and contrapuntal techniques. Three motives (a, b, c) all circumscribe a fourth (Ex. 117). Subjects a and b admit canonic imitations and inversions, appearing simultaneously with their inversions. Combination appears in prime, octave, and lower sixth. In m. 59-60 the combination is inverted in the upper seventh, and b accompanies a in canonic imitation.
- Haydn, String Quartet, Op. 76/2-III (Ex. 115): Canonic imitation shifts the ending of the first segment, creating ambiguity in phrase structure (5+6 or 4+4+3)

# Common Confusions
- Contrapuntal treatment in the minuet does not replace motive variation -- in the best examples, both coexist
- Canonic imitation can shift perceived phrase boundaries, creating structural ambiguity
- The presence of imitative entries does not by itself constitute a "contrapuntal" minuet -- genuine fusion requires the display of combinative possibilities
