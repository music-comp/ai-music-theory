# Quick Definition
The fundamental compositional balance between introducing variety (contrast) and maintaining connection to the basic motive (coherence), which governs the construction of m. 3-4 in the antecedent and all subsequent thematic development.

# Formal Definition
In antecedent construction, m. 3-4 must present motive-forms that are simultaneously contrasting (remote from the original motive) and coherent (connected to it). The coherence may consist in rhythmic similarity, intervallic derivation, or shared contour features, while the contrast may involve change of register, direction, rhythmic activity, or melodic contour. The balance between these poles determines the intelligibility and interest of the theme. Coherence without contrast produces monotony; contrast without coherence endangers comprehensibility.

# Construction/Recognition
- Coherence more evident than contrast: change of register, direction, or contour only (Exs. 44f, h, i, j, l, 45f, 46a, f, 47b, c, d, e)
- Contrast through increase of small notes while maintaining motivic connection (Exs. 44a, b, c, d, g, k, 45a, b, c, d, 46e)
- Contrast through decrease of small notes, producing reduction (Exs. 43a, 45i, 46g)
- When coherence is not obvious, a connective bridges the gap
- Compass management: broad openings balance by returning to middle register; narrow openings may expand in the consequent

# Musical Context
This principle extends beyond antecedent construction to all aspects of composition. Schoenberg observes that variety needs no justification -- "it is a merit in itself" -- but that comprehensibility must not be sacrificed. Even extreme rhythmical contrast (as in Mozart's Exs. 45a, f) can succeed when the coordination of notes with harmony is masterful. Much variety can be achieved within a relatively small compass, though extension of compass often defends against monotony.

# Examples
- Haydn (Ex. 44f): Even continuous sixteenth-notes achieve contrast through differentiated figuration and harmony in m. 3-4
- Mozart (Ex. 45a): Extreme rhythmical contrast between first and second phrases, with abrupt juxtaposition that does not produce imbalance
- Melodies with small compass (Exs. 45f, 50a, b): Show that variety can be achieved within a fifth or sixth
- Melodies with broad compass (Exs. 44a, c, d, 45a): Achieve balance by returning to middle register

# Common Confusions
- Contrast and coherence are not opposites to be chosen between; they must coexist simultaneously.
- Extreme contrast can work when other elements (rhythm, harmonic coordination) provide the necessary coherence.
- A narrow melodic compass does not preclude effective contrast; register, direction, and rhythm offer many dimensions for variation.
