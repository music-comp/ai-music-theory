# Quick Definition
The contrasting thematic section in the sonata-allegro exposition, characterized by a different key, contrasting motive-forms, loose structure with direct repetition and juxtaposition, spinning out, evasion of cadences, and closing codettas -- its character, length, and complexity determined only by the demands of the individual composition.

# Core Definition
The most important single factor is contrast with the principal group: a different key or region, contrasting motive-forms, distinct rhythmic characteristics, and different types of thematic construction and articulation. There are nearly always enough differentiated ideas here to justify the term "group." Features include loose structure (direct repetition, juxtaposition, overlap), spinning out (chain-like derivation leading to sequences and liquidation), evasion of definite cadences until the end, and codettas or a closing theme marking the end of the exposition.

# Key Properties
- Contrast with principal group in key, character, motive-forms, rhythm, and construction
- Loose structure: direct repetition of segments, juxtaposition, overlap
- Spinning out: chain-like derivation leading to sequences and liquidation
- Evasion of definite cadences until the end (maintains harmonic momentum)
- Codettas or closing theme mark the end of the exposition
- Key: usually V (major), III or v (minor)
- Character, construction, complexity, and length determined by individual composition
- May range from eight measures (Op. 14/1-III) to 124 measures (Op. 28-I)

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the contrasting key region (V, III, or v)
2. Note the loose construction: repetition, juxtaposition, overlap
3. Look for spinning out and chain-like interconnexion
4. Note the evasion of definite cadences maintaining momentum
5. Identify codettas or a closing theme marking the exposition's end
6. Trace motivic connections back to the basic motive

# Context & Application
The subordinate group's character is determined only by the demands of the individual composition. Beyond the requirements of contrasting key, character, and thematic material, and sufficient closing material, only the composer's imagination determines the events. The evidence from Beethoven shows extreme variety: Op. 10/3-I has a 102-measure subordinate group after a 22-measure principal theme.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 7-I: six distinct sections (m. 41-136); from m. 82, each cadence overlaps the beginning of the next section, linking contrasting formulations and maintaining momentum
- Beethoven, Op. 10/1-I: lyrical four-measure phrase (m. 56) repeated in tonic-dominant relationship; two more varied phrases with scale lines replacing broken chords; extended cadential approaches from m. 72-94; codettas (m. 95-105) derived from transition end
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-I: 102 measures; ambiguous section from m. 23-53 could be transition or first subordinate theme; another theme (m. 54) resembles a codetta; extensive modulation (m. 67-93); three contrasted codettas
- Beethoven, Op. 28-I: subordinate group of 124 measures after principal theme of 39 measures
- Brahms, Piano Quintet Op. 34: subordinate theme in c# (in f minor movement); recurs in f# -- "such relationships are infrequent"

# Common Errors
- Making all subordinate themes identical in character and construction
- Failing to evade cadences, stopping the harmonic momentum
- Creating subordinate themes that are entirely unrelated to the basic motive

# Common Confusions
- The role of certain passages may be ambiguous (transition or subordinate theme?); this ambiguity is not a defect
- The enormous range of length and complexity makes generalization impossible
- Departures from standard key relationships (like Brahms's Piano Quintet) require "masterly adaptations of the form and the material"
