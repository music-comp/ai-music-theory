# Quick Definition
The second main thematic section of the sonata-allegro exposition, in a contrasting key, characterized by loose structure, spinning out, evasion of definite cadences, and sufficient closing material to mark the end of the exposition.

# Formal Definition
The most important single factor is contrast with the principal group: a different key or region, contrasting motive-forms, distinct rhythmic characteristics, and different types of thematic construction and articulation. There are nearly always enough differentiated ideas to justify the term "group." The character, construction, complexity, and length are determined only by the demands of the individual composition.

# Construction/Recognition
Expected features include:
- **Loose structure**: direct and immediate repetition of segments, juxtaposition of contrasting segments (often with overlap), little recurrence of earlier features within the section
- **Spinning out**: derivation of succeeding motive-forms from preceding ones, leading to sequences, condensation, and liquidation; chain-like interconnexion
- **Evasion of definite cadences** until the end of the exposition, contributing to harmonic momentum and joining remotely related material
- **Codettas or a closing theme** at the end, marking the end of the exposition
- **Key relationships**: in major, subordinate group nearly always in V; in minor, preferred keys are III (relative major) and v (minor)

# Musical Context
The subordinate group provides the primary thematic and tonal contrast in the exposition. Its looser construction and different character ensure clear differentiation from the principal group, while the underlying motivic derivation from the basic material maintains the movement's unity.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 7-I: extensive group with six distinct sections (m. 41-136); overlapping cadences link contrasting formulations and maintain momentum
- Beethoven, Op. 10/1-I: lyrical four-measure phrase (m. 56) repeated, then varied with scale lines replacing broken chords; extended cadential approaches from m. 72 to m. 94
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-I: subordinate group of 102 measures after a principal theme of only 22 measures; ambiguous role of the section from m. 23-53
- Beethoven, Op. 28-I: subordinate group of 124 measures with 39-measure principal theme
- Brahms, Piano Quintet Op. 34 in f: subordinate theme in c#, recapitulated in f# -- an infrequent but masterful departure from standard relationships

# Common Confusions
- The subordinate group is determined by the demands of the individual composition, not by a formula; enormous variety in length, complexity, and character is the norm
- The distinction between transition and first subordinate theme can be ambiguous (as in Op. 10/3-I), and this ambiguity may be intentional
- The interchangeability of parallel major and minor permits part or all of the group to appear in the minor dominant (v) rather than major dominant (V)
