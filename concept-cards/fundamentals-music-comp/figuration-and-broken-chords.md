# Quick Definition

An accompaniment technique using arpeggiated or broken chord patterns that fulfill piano style requirements while maintaining essentially chorale-like voice leading underneath.

# Core Definition

The requirements of piano style are better fulfilled through the use of broken chords, although the voice leading remains essentially chorale-like. A broken-chord accompaniment will use one or more special figures systematically -- the motive of the accompaniment. The method of repeating full chords derives from the same circumstance: the desire to revive the short-lived tone of the piano (Schoenberg, p. 52).

In general, the accompaniment makes use of shorter notes than the melody, though the contrary also occurs. The motive of the accompaniment often includes auxiliaries, appoggiaturas, changing notes. The broken chords may be distributed in open position. The Romantic style of transcription for piano sometimes makes the instrument "roar" through elaborate left-hand figurations -- a technique "much abused during the nineteenth century" (p. 52).

# Prerequisites

- **Chorale-like accompaniment** -- Figuration is chorale harmony distributed across time; understanding the underlying framework is essential
- **Accompaniment function** -- Figuration serves the broader accompaniment function

# Key Properties

1. Chords distributed across time as arpeggios or patterns
2. Voice leading remains essentially chorale-like underneath
3. Generally uses shorter notes than the melody (though contrary exists)
4. A consistent figure (motive of the accompaniment) is used systematically
5. May lie in lower, middle, or upper voices
6. May be distributed in open position
7. Repeated full chords are a related technique (reviving piano tone)
8. May include auxiliaries, appoggiaturas, changing notes

# Construction / Recognition

1. Begin with proper chorale-style voice leading
2. Distribute the chord tones across time in a consistent pattern
3. Establish a rhythmic figure (motive of the accompaniment)
4. Adapt the figure to changing harmonies while preserving the pattern
5. Maintain the figure systematically through the passage
6. Verify the underlying voice leading by extracting the chorale framework

# Context & Application

Figuration is the bridge between the abstract ideal of chorale harmony and the practical reality of piano writing. The piano's tone decays quickly, so broken chords help sustain harmonic presence. The Romantic style sometimes took this further, with elaborate figurations not present in the orchestral original. The student should maintain control of the underlying voice leading even when employing elaborate surface figuration.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Writing elaborate figuration without ensuring proper underlying voice leading
  **Correction**: The chorale-like voice-leading framework must be sound beneath any figuration

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking broken chords are mere decoration
  **Clarification**: They are a practical solution to the piano's tonal decay, not merely ornamental

- **Confusion**: Equating the Alberti bass with all figuration
  **Clarification**: The Alberti bass is one (obsolete) form; figuration encompasses a vast range of techniques
