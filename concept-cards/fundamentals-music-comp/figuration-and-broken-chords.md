# Quick Definition
An accompaniment technique using arpeggiated or broken chord patterns that better fulfill the requirements of piano style while maintaining essentially chorale-like voice leading underneath.

# Formal Definition
Figuration through broken chords is the most common method of creating piano accompaniment. The requirements of piano style are better fulfilled through broken chords than through sustained block chords, because the piano's tone decays quickly and broken chords help sustain harmonic presence. The voice leading remains essentially chorale-like, as comparison between figuration and its underlying chorale extract reveals. A broken-chord accompaniment will use one or more special figures systematically -- constituting the motive of the accompaniment. The method of repeating full chords derives from the same circumstance: the desire to revive the short-lived tone of the piano.

# Construction/Recognition
- Chords distributed across time as arpeggios or other patterns rather than sounded simultaneously
- Generally uses shorter notes than the melody (though the contrary also occurs)
- May lie in lower, middle, or upper voices
- Can be distributed in open position
- The underlying voice leading remains chorale-like
- A consistent figure (motive of the accompaniment) is used systematically
- Related technique: repeated full chords (also revives piano tone)

# Musical Context
Figuration is the bridge between the abstract ideal of chorale harmony and the practical reality of piano writing. The Romantic style of piano transcription sometimes takes this further, making the piano "roar" through elaborate left-hand figurations not present in the orchestral original -- a technique that was "much abused during the nineteenth century." The student should maintain control of the underlying voice leading even when employing elaborate surface figuration.

# Common Confusions
- Broken-chord figuration is not mere decoration; it is a practical solution to the piano's tonal decay.
- Elaborate figuration does not excuse poor voice leading; the underlying chorale structure must be sound.
- The Alberti bass is an example of broken-chord figuration that Schoenberg considers "obsolete" in its simplest form but acknowledges as representative of the type.
