# Quick Definition

The technique of establishing a thematic segment (pattern) and then transposing it to other harmonic degrees (sequence) as the principal means of carrying out modulation in the scherzo's contrasting middle section.

# Core Definition

Harmonically, the segments of the contrasting middle section should be built as patterns for sequences. Thematically, they should possess a certain degree of independence; the combination of motive-forms may resemble that in the antecedent of a period or the middle section of a ternary form. Motive-forms from the A-section must be modified or adapted to the modulatory procedure. The order may be changed, and some forms may be repeated within the segment (e.g., abac, abcc, ahbc, aaab, etc.). The pattern must be built so that the end of the last sequence offers an opening for the return to an appropriate upbeat harmony.

# Key Properties

- Pattern: a clearly delimited segment, typically 2-4 measures, combining A-section motive-forms
- Sequence: transposition of the pattern to a new harmonic degree
- The pattern may begin on V, v, III, i, iii, bIII, ii, iv, VI, or other degrees
- Variation of the sequence is common and desirable (not strict transposition)
- The principal voice may shift to a different voice (double counterpoint)
- The emphasis of sequence (repetition) creates obligations that must be neutralized through liquidation
- If the sequence ends too remotely, further modulation through reduced patterns is needed

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify the pattern (clearly delimited motivic segment)
2. Trace the transposition to successive degrees
3. Note any variation of the sequence
4. Check whether voice positions shift between pattern and sequence
5. Verify that the sequential process ends on or near the upbeat harmony
6. If too remote, look for additional reduced sequences

# Context & Application

The emphasis contributed by a sequence (as a form of repetition) creates obligations. It is necessary to neutralize these obligations through liquidation to introduce the recapitulation "at once surprising and expected," as Beethoven expressed it. The sequential process is the most common single feature shared across classical scherzos, though its application varies enormously from work to work.

# Examples

- Ex. 122: Twenty-five illustrations from one A-section, beginning on various degrees
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 4-III: Many sequences of a four-measure pattern
- Beethoven, String Quartet Op. 18/1, Scherzo: Three-measure pattern in flat mediant sequenced in tonic minor; two-measure pattern with three sequences leading to the dominant
- Brahms, Sextet Op. 36, Scherzo: Imitative sequences as dialogue between voices

# Common Errors

- Making the sequence a strict transposition (variation is normal and desirable)
- Failing to plan the endpoint of the sequential process
- Using too many sequences without beginning liquidation

# Common Confusions

- **Pattern vs. theme**: The pattern is derived from A-section material but adapted for modulation; it is not a direct quote.
- **Strict vs. varied sequence**: Variation of the sequence is standard practice, not an exception.
