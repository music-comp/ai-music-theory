# Quick Definition
A traditional distinction between variations that preserve the theme's complete structure (formal) and those that preserve only the harmony and melody outline while transforming the character (character variations) -- a distinction Schoenberg finds misleading, since every variation necessarily possesses both form and character.

# Formal Definition
A distinction is often made between formal variations and character variations. But there is no reason to suppose that a variation can be so formal as not to possess character. On the contrary, it is precisely the character which contributes variety. Every composer, in sketching motives for variation, will consider the necessity of providing sufficient contrast of character. The distinction is better understood as a matter of degree: how closely the variation preserves the structure, proportions, and harmonic framework of the theme.

# Construction/Recognition
- Formal variations: preserve the theme's structure, proportions, phrase lengths, and harmonic progression closely
- Character variations: may depart more freely from the theme's structure; changes of tempo, metre, and character may be more far-reaching
- Both types use the motive of variation as a unifying principle
- Both types maintain some essential connection to the theme (structural, harmonic, or melodic)
- The contrast between types often appears within a single set of variations

# Musical Context
Schoenberg's insistence that "there is no reason to suppose that a variation can be so formal as not to possess character" challenges a commonly drawn distinction in music theory pedagogy. His point is that character (through the motive of variation and its rhythmic, dynamic, and textural qualities) is what gives each variation its identity and prevents the set from becoming monotonous. The distinction between formal and character variations is better understood as a spectrum than a binary classification.

# Examples
- Beethoven, 6 Variations Op. 34: Great differences of tempo and character between variations, with changes of metre; the theme is adagio, variations alternate between allegro, allegretto, tempo di menuetto, marcia, etc.
- Brahms, Handel Variations: Mix of lyric, rhythmic, Hungarian, musette-like variations within a single set
- Beethoven, 32 Variations in c minor: Mostly "formal" in preserving the passacaglia bass, but with strong character contrasts through major-minor interchange and rhythmic transformation

# Common Confusions
- The formal/character distinction is not a rigid taxonomy -- every variation has both formal and character aspects
- "Character variation" does not mean the theme is unrecognizable; some essential features are always preserved
- Schoenberg considers "character" to be what contributes variety -- it is a positive quality, not a departure from correctness
