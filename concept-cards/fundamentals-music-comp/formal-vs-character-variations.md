# Quick Definition

A traditional distinction between variations that closely preserve the theme's structure (formal) and those that transform the character while preserving only the outline (character variations) -- a distinction Schoenberg finds misleading, since every variation necessarily possesses both form and character.

# Core Definition

A distinction is often made between formal variations and character variations. But Schoenberg challenges this: "there is no reason to suppose that a variation can be so formal as not to possess character." On the contrary, it is precisely the character which contributes variety. Every composer, in sketching motives for variation, will consider the necessity of providing sufficient contrast of character. The distinction is better understood as a spectrum of how closely the variation preserves the structure, proportions, and harmonic framework of the theme.

# Key Properties

- Formal variations: preserve the theme's structure, proportions, phrase lengths, and harmonic progression closely
- Character variations: may depart more freely; changes of tempo, metre, and character may be more far-reaching
- Both types use the motive of variation as a unifying principle
- Both types maintain some essential connection to the theme
- Character is what contributes variety and prevents monotony
- The contrast between types often appears within a single set

# Construction / Recognition

1. Assess how closely the variation preserves the theme's structure
2. Note changes of tempo, metre, or character
3. Determine whether the variation fits more toward the "formal" or "character" end of the spectrum
4. Remember that both categories overlap -- every variation has both formal and character aspects

# Context & Application

Schoenberg's critique of the formal/character distinction is an important theoretical intervention. He insists that character is a positive quality essential to every variation, not a departure from correctness. The distinction is useful as a descriptive tool (noting degree of structural preservation) but misleading as a prescriptive taxonomy.

# Examples

- Beethoven, 6 Variations Op. 34: Great differences of tempo and character; variations in different keys (F, D, B-flat, G, E-flat, c minor), tempi (adagio, allegro, allegretto, tempo di menuetto, marcia allegretto), and metres -- an extreme example of character variation
- Brahms, Handel Variations: Mix of lyric or cantabile (5, 6, 11); impassioned (9, 20); rhythmic (7, 8); Hungarian (13); musette-like (22)
- Beethoven, 32 Variations in c minor: Mostly "formal" in preserving the passacaglia bass, but with strong character contrasts through major-minor interchange

# Common Errors

- Treating formal and character variations as mutually exclusive categories
- Writing "formal" variations that lack any distinguishing character
- Assuming "character variation" means the theme is unrecognizable

# Common Confusions

- **Formal vs. character as taxonomy**: Not a rigid taxonomy; every variation has both form and character.
- **Character as departure**: Schoenberg views character as a positive quality that contributes variety, not as a departure from correctness.
