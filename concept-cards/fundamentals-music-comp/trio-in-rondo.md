# Quick Definition
The central contrasting section (C) in a large rondo form, typically a ternary structure in a more remote key than the B-section, often etude-like or contrapuntal in character.

# Formal Definition
The trio section is often itself ternary, and it expresses a definite contrasting key, usually somewhat more remote than that of the B-section. The character of the c-section contrasts with both the A- and B-sections. Quite often it is etude-like; not infrequently it is contrapuntal in the sense that the motive or theme undergoes little internal variation, but instead is placed in various combinations with itself and other material.

# Construction/Recognition
- Usually a ternary form with its own internal repetitions
- Expresses a contrasting key more remote than the B-section's key
- Often etude-like: passage work with a persistent rhythmic or technical figure
- Often contrapuntal: theme placed in various voice combinations (resembling double counterpoint)
- May take over the transition function at its beginning, dispensing with a separate connective
- Its ending is typically modified to serve as a retransition back to the tonic

# Musical Context
The trio provides the deepest level of contrast in the large rondo form, functioning analogously to the trio of a scherzo or the elaboration of a sonata-allegro. Its more remote key and distinct character differentiate it from the B-section.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: trio (m. 57-99) is etude-like in tonic minor with internal repetitions like a minuet; final repetition (m. 88) changes into retransition
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-IV: trio (m. 35-45) in flat submediant region; both hands participate in etude-like character
- Beethoven, Op. 13-III: trio (m. 79) consists of six versions of the first phrase; contrapuntal interplay of two voices; principal notes filled in with eighth-notes to produce descending scale lines (m. 99-106)
- Beethoven, Op. 28-IV: pseudo-contrapuntal trio (m. 79-101); upper voice of first four measures appears successively in upper, middle, and lower voices in tonic, dominant, and tonic minor regions; treatment resembles double counterpoint

# Common Confusions
- The trio in a rondo is not always a dance-derived form as in the minuet-trio tradition; it may be etude-like or contrapuntal
- "Contrapuntal" here means the theme is placed in various combinations rather than undergoing intensive motivic development
- The trio section may blend characteristics of both trio and Durchfuhrung, making classification ambiguous
