# Quick Definition
The central contrasting section (C) in a large rondo form, typically a ternary structure in a more remote key than the B-section, often etude-like or contrapuntal -- providing the deepest level of contrast within the rondo.

# Core Definition
The trio section is often itself ternary, and it expresses a definite contrasting key, usually somewhat more remote than that of the B-section. The character of the c-section contrasts with both the A- and B-sections. Quite often it is etude-like; not infrequently it is "contrapuntal" in the sense that the motive or theme undergoes little internal variation, but instead is placed in various combinations with itself and other material. There is no connective to introduce the trio in some cases; instead, the first segment of the trio takes over the transition function.

# Prerequisites
- Large rondo form
- Small ternary form

# Key Properties
- Usually a ternary form with its own internal repetitions
- Contrasting key more remote than the B-section's key
- Often etude-like: passage work with a persistent rhythmic or technical figure
- Often contrapuntal: theme placed in various voice combinations (resembling double counterpoint)
- May take over the transition function at its beginning
- Ending typically modified to serve as retransition back to the tonic
- Provides the deepest level of contrast in the rondo

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the C-section's contrasting key (more remote than B's key)
2. Note whether the texture is etude-like, contrapuntal, or both
3. Check for ternary organization with internal repetitions
4. Look for the transition function at the trio's beginning
5. Identify the retransition at the trio's end, leading back to the principal theme

# Context & Application
The trio provides the deepest level of contrast in the large rondo form, functioning analogously to the trio of a scherzo or the elaboration of a sonata-allegro. Its more remote key and distinct character differentiate it from the B-section. The etude-like or contrapuntal character provides textural contrast beyond harmonic contrast.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: trio (m. 57-99) etude-like in tonic minor with internal repetitions; final repetition (m. 88) changes into retransition; trio theme recurs in coda (m. 161)
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-IV: trio (m. 35-45) in flat submediant; both hands participate in etude-like character
- Beethoven, Op. 13-III: trio (m. 79) consists of six versions of the first phrase with contrapuntal interplay; principal notes filled in with eighth-notes to produce descending scale lines (m. 99-106)
- Beethoven, Op. 28-IV: pseudo-contrapuntal trio (m. 79-101); upper voice appears successively in upper, middle, and lower voices in tonic, dominant, and tonic minor regions; treatment resembles double counterpoint

# Common Errors
- Making the trio key too close to the B-section's key, failing to provide deeper contrast
- Writing a trio that is merely another subordinate theme rather than a section with distinct character
- Failing to modify the trio's ending to serve as a retransition

# Common Confusions
- The trio in a rondo is not always a dance-derived form as in the minuet-trio tradition; it may be etude-like or contrapuntal
- "Contrapuntal" here means the theme is placed in various combinations rather than undergoing intensive motivic development
- The trio section may blend characteristics of both trio and Durchfuhrung, making classification ambiguous
