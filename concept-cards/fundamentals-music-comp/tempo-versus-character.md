# Quick Definition
Tempo contributes to but does not determine musical character; pieces sharing the same tempo marking can possess vastly different expressive qualities.

# Formal Definition
It is fallacious to think that tempo indications determine character. In classical music, at least, this is not true. An adagio is slow and an allegro is fast -- this contributes something, but not everything, to the expression of a character. The multiplicity of characters possible within any single tempo category demonstrates that tempo is a necessary but insufficient condition for establishing character.

# Construction/Recognition
To appreciate the independence of character from tempo, compare works that share tempo markings but differ in character. The differences manifest in the thematic material, continuations, subordinate themes, accompaniment patterns, rhythmic features, and textures -- all elements beyond tempo.

# Musical Context
This distinction is essential for performers and composers alike. A performer who relies on tempo alone to convey character will produce a superficial interpretation. A composer who believes choosing a tempo is sufficient to establish character will produce undifferentiated music.

# Examples
- Compare the Presto Agitato of Op. 27/2-III with the Allegro Vivace of Op. 31/1-I -- both rapid, yet entirely different in character
- Compare the Allegro of Op. 31/2-I with the Allegro of Op. 31/3-I
- Compare the Assai Allegro of Op. 57-I with the Presto alla Tedesca of Op. 79-I

# Common Confusions
- Students often assume that choosing a tempo marking is the primary step in establishing character, when in fact it is only one contributing factor among many
- The same tempo marking can serve hundreds or thousands of different characters
