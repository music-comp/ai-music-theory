# Quick Definition

The necessary counterbalance to logic and coherence in musical form: parts of a piece differ in content, character, mood, tonality, size, and structure, presenting an idea from various viewpoints and producing the contrasts on which variety is based.

# Core Definition

The parts of a piece "differ more or less in content, character and mood, in tonality, size and structure. These differences permit presentation of an idea from various viewpoints, producing those contrasts on which variety is based" (Ch. 5, p. 20). However, "variety must never endanger comprehensibility or logic" (Ch. 5, p. 20). The demands of logic, coherence, and comprehensibility must be fulfilled "in relation to the need for contrast, variety and fluency of presentation" (Ch. 1, p. 2).

# Key Properties

1. Contrast arises from differences in content, character, mood, tonality, size, and structure
2. Variety is the cumulative effect of distributed contrasts
3. Variety must never endanger comprehensibility or logic
4. Rapid succession of contrasts obstructs the listener's grasp
5. Pieces in rapid tempo exhibit lesser degree of variety
6. Changing every feature produces "something foreign, incoherent, illogical" (Ch. 3, p. 8)

# Construction / Recognition

1. Introduce contrasts through changes in musical parameters (rhythm, melody, harmony, register, dynamics, texture)
2. Limit the degree of contrast to maintain comprehensibility
3. In rapid tempos, use less variety -- rapidity obstructs understanding
4. Use delimitation, subdivision, and simple repetition to control variety
5. Balance the need for freshness against the need for coherence of motive-forms

# Context & Application

Contrast and variety are the necessary complement to logic and coherence. Without them, music would be monotonous; with too much, it becomes incoherent. Schoenberg treats this balance as equally important in popular and classical music, citing the classical masters' search for a "popular touch" in their themes and Mattheson's 1739 advice that "a theme should have a certain something which the whole world already knows" (Ch. 5, p. 20).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 1): Dance forms achieve contrast through changes of metre, tempo, and rhythmic characteristics.

**Example 2** (p. 1): Sonata form achieves contrast through the complexity of interrelationships between parts.

**Example 3** (Ch. 3, p. 8): "Changing every feature produces something foreign, incoherent, illogical. It destroys the basic shape of the motive."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Introducing too many contrasts in rapid succession
  **Correction**: Comprehensibility requires limitation of variety, especially in rapid tempos

- **Error**: Changing every feature of a motive simultaneously
  **Correction**: This destroys the motive's identity; change some features while preserving others

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing contrast is the opposite of coherence
  **Clarification**: Both are necessary and must be balanced; contrast without coherence produces "nonsense"

- **Confusion**: Equating more variety with better music
  **Clarification**: "Only so much variation as character, length and tempo require should be admitted" (Ch. 5, p. 20)
