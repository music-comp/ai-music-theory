# Quick Definition
The three categories of motive repetition -- exact, modified, and developed -- which range from strict preservation of all features to progressive transformation through variation.

# Formal Definition
A motive is used by repetition. The repetition may be:
- **Exact**: preserves all features and relationships. Transpositions to a different degree, inversions, retrogrades, diminutions, and augmentations are exact repetitions if they preserve strictly the features and note relations.
- **Modified**: created through variation; provides variety and produces new material (motive-forms) for subsequent use. Some modifications are merely local "variants" with little or no influence on the continuation.
- **Developed**: a motive-form is developed further through subsequent variation, creating a chain of progressive transformation.

# Construction/Recognition
Exact repetition is identified by the strict preservation of all intervallic and rhythmic relationships, even when the motive is transposed, inverted, retrograded, diminished, or augmented. Modified repetition changes some features while preserving others. Developed repetition builds upon modified forms cumulatively.

# Musical Context
The choice among types of repetition depends on the structural function of the passage. Opening presentations often use exact or near-exact repetition for clarity. Continuations and developments use modified and developed repetition to generate forward motion. The distinction between "variants" (local modifications without consequences) and true variations (modifications that drive further development) is critical.

# Common Confusions
- Transposition, inversion, retrograde, diminution, and augmentation count as "exact" repetition because they preserve the internal relationships of the motive
- "Modified" repetition is not the same as "developed" repetition; development implies cumulative, progressive transformation
- Local "variants" are a subcategory of modified repetition with no structural consequences
