# Quick Definition
The most effective factor in creating contrast in the B-section is harmony: the B-section contraposes a closely related region to the tonic established by the A-section, providing both contrast and coherence simultaneously.

# Core Definition
The A-section establishes a tonic; the B-section contraposes another region (one of the more closely related). This provides both contrast and coherence. The B-section may begin on various degrees (V, i, v, ii, iii, II, IV, iv, vi), and all but one can be joined back to an A-section ending on I. The use of minor tonic and minor subdominant effectively contrasts with the return of major in the recapitulation. The principle of "multiple meaning" allows chords to be reinterpreted between regions.

# Key Properties
- Harmony is the most effective contrast factor
- B-section contraposes a closely related region
- B-sections can begin on: V, i, v, ii, iii, II, IV, iv, vi
- Minor tonic and minor subdominant effectively contrast with major recapitulation
- The principle of multiple meaning allows harmonic reinterpretation
- The usual relationship is tonic (A) vs. dominant region (B)
- This usual relationship can be reversed (as in Op. 31/1-III)
- B-section typically ends on V to prepare tonic return

# Construction / Recognition
Harmonic contrast is achieved through: (1) establishing a different harmonic region from the A-section's tonic, (2) using pedal points (especially on V) to suggest a contrasting region, (3) using minor tonic or subdominant for modal contrast, (4) ending on V to prepare the recapitulation. The student should exploit all degrees to expand harmonic knowledge.

# Context & Application
The student should try B-sections beginning on all degrees to expand harmonic knowledge, even at the risk of overburdening so small a form. This develops harmonic resources and skill for larger and more complex forms.

# Examples
- Beethoven Op. 2/1-II: mere interchange of V and I contrasts with I-V at beginning; pedal on V suggests dominant region
- Beethoven Op. 31/1-III: B-section starts on tonic pedal, ends on tonic, because A-section and recapitulation use dominant pedals -- the usual I-V relation is reversed
- Haydn Ex. 102b: B-section begins on bIII, reinterpreted as III of tonic minor via multiple meaning
- Ex. 105: ten B-sections on V, i, v, ii, iii, II, IV, iv, vi -- in 1, 3, 7, 8, 9 minor tonic/subdominant contrast with ensuing major recapitulation

# Common Errors
- Using harmonies too remote to maintain coherence
- Failing to end the B-section on a harmony that prepares the recapitulation
- Using only one harmonic approach in all exercises rather than exploring different degrees

# Common Confusions
- Harmonic contrast need not mean remote modulation; even a simple I-V interchange can provide effective contrast
- The reversal of the usual I-V relationship (as in Op. 31/1-III) is rare but demonstrates flexibility
- The student should explore all degrees even if it overburdens the form -- the goal is developing skill
