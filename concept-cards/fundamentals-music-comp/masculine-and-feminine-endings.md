# Quick Definition
An ending on a strong beat is called "masculine," and an ending on a weak beat is called "feminine." Too frequent use of the same kind of ending is often monotonous.

# Formal Definition
Masculine and feminine endings are classifications of phrase endings based on their metric placement. A masculine ending falls on a strong beat (downbeat or other metrically accented position), producing a sense of definiteness and closure. A feminine ending falls on a weak beat (upbeat or metrically unaccented position), producing a softer, more open quality. The choice between them affects both the character of the phrase and the larger structural articulation.

# Construction/Recognition
- Masculine: final note or chord on a strong beat (beat 1 or beat 3 in common time)
- Feminine: final note or chord on a weak beat (beat 2 or beat 4, or an offbeat)
- Variety between the two types across phrases prevents monotony
- Feminine endings in the antecedent can serve as a unifying characteristic when used consistently

# Musical Context
In period construction, the type of ending affects the character of the caesura and the cadence. Consistent use of one type can serve as a unifying device (as in Beethoven, Op. 2/1-II, where feminine endings in m. 2, 4, and 8 are unifying characteristics). However, exclusive use of either type across an extended passage risks monotony and should be avoided.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-II (Adagio): Feminine endings in m. 2, 4, and 8 serve as unifying characteristics of the melody

# Common Confusions
- These terms describe metric placement, not expressive character; a "masculine" ending is not inherently stronger in expression than a "feminine" one.
- The terms are borrowed from literary prosody and refer purely to metric accent, not to gendered qualities of the music.
