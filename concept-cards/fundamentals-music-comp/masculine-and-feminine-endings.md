# Quick Definition

An ending on a strong beat is called "masculine," and an ending on a weak beat is called "feminine"; too frequent use of the same kind of ending is often monotonous.

# Core Definition

An ending on a strong beat is called "masculine," and on a weak beat, "feminine." Too frequent use of the same kind of ending is often monotonous (Schoenberg, p. 23). The choice between masculine and feminine endings affects both the character of individual phrases and the larger structural articulation of periods.

# Prerequisites

- **Phrase** -- Masculine/feminine refers to the metric placement of phrase endings

# Key Properties

1. Masculine: final note or chord on a strong beat (downbeat or metrically accented position)
2. Feminine: final note or chord on a weak beat (upbeat or metrically unaccented position)
3. Too frequent use of the same type becomes monotonous
4. Consistent use of one type across phrases can serve as a unifying device
5. The terms derive from literary prosody and refer purely to metric accent

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify whether the phrase ending falls on a strong or weak beat
2. Strong beat = masculine; weak beat = feminine
3. Vary the types across phrases to avoid monotony
4. Consider using consistent ending type for unification (deliberately)

# Context & Application

In period construction, the type of ending affects the character of the caesura and the cadence. Consistent use of one type can serve as a unifying device, as in Beethoven, Op. 2/1-II, where feminine endings in m. 2, 4, and 8 are unifying characteristics of the melody.

# Examples

- **Beethoven, Op. 2/1-II, Adagio** (p. 22): Feminine endings in m. 2, 4, and 8 serve as unifying characteristics of the melody

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using the same ending type (always masculine or always feminine) throughout
  **Correction**: Vary the types to avoid monotony

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking "masculine" means stronger or more powerful
  **Clarification**: The terms describe metric placement, not expressive character; they derive from literary prosody

- **Confusion**: Thinking one type is inherently better
  **Clarification**: Both are legitimate; the choice depends on character and variety needs
