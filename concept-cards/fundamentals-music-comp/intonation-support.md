# Quick Definition
The harmonic support that assists a singer in finding correct pitch, which is almost indispensable at the beginning of a vocal melody, especially since singers lack an absolute pitch reference.

# Formal Definition
Support of intonation through harmony is almost indispensable, especially at the beginning. Unless a singer possesses a memory for absolute pitch, the singer has no yardstick whatever for intonation. Chromaticism, augmented intervals (or successions of them), and tones or tone successions which cannot be related to the harmony -- especially if outside the tonality -- offer difficulties.

# Construction/Recognition
When writing vocal melody: (1) provide clear harmonic support at the beginning, (2) ensure that melodic intervals can be related to the underlying harmony, (3) avoid chromatic passages, augmented intervals, and tones outside the tonality unless the harmony makes them comprehensible, (4) note that successive leaps with a compass of a seventh or ninth should be avoided.

# Musical Context
The trained singer should be able to sing the intervals of the tempered scale, which deviates from natural acoustical intervals. Chromaticism is difficult partly because natural semitones differ in size from tempered ones, a fact which causes choirs to "get off pitch." Sparingly used, chromaticism need not be avoided entirely -- chromatic passing tones and embellishing notes can easily be understood.

# Common Confusions
- Intonation difficulty is not the same as interval difficulty; a large interval may be easy to intonate if the harmony supports it, while a small chromatic interval may be very difficult without harmonic support
- The problem is not chromaticism per se but chromaticism that cannot be related to the underlying harmony
