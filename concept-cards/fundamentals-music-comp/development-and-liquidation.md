# Quick Definition
The continuation of a sentence employs two complementary techniques: development (growth, augmentation, extension, expansion of motival material) and liquidation (reduction, condensation, intensification leading to closure), which together shape the second half of the sentence.

# Formal Definition
The technique applied in the continuation of a sentence is a kind of development, comparable in some respects to the condensing technique of liquidation. Development implies not only growth, augmentation, extension, and expansion, but also reduction, condensation, and intensification. The purpose of liquidation is to counteract the tendency toward unlimited extension that development naturally creates. The two processes are complementary: development drives the material forward with increasing complexity, while liquidation progressively eliminates characteristic features to enable cadential closure.

# Construction/Recognition
- Development phase: motive-forms are transformed, combined, and varied more remotely
- Transition: the phrase units begin to shorten (condensation)
- Liquidation phase: characteristic features are progressively eliminated
- The two processes may overlap or succeed each other
- Reduction: omitting part of the model
- Condensation: compressing the content, potentially reordering features
- Intensification: increasing harmonic activity or rhythmic density as part of the developmental process

# Musical Context
This dual process is the driving force of the sentence's continuation and distinguishes it from the consequent of a period. The sentence's beginning already includes repetition (tonic form/dominant form), so the continuation must go further -- into genuine development -- before finding closure through liquidation. In masterpieces, this has given rise to a great variety of structures, from compact eight-measure sentences to extended irregular forms.

# Examples
- Ex. 61b (Brahms): A clear illustration of "developing variation" -- all motive-forms and phrases develop gradually out of the first two or three notes, with liquidation in m. 7-8 reducing to half-measure residues

# Common Confusions
- Development and liquidation are not opposing forces but complementary aspects of the same continuation process.
- "Development" in this context refers to the technique within a sentence, not the "development section" of sonata form (though the principles are related).
- Liquidation is not the absence of development; it is a specific technique that brings development to a purposeful conclusion.
