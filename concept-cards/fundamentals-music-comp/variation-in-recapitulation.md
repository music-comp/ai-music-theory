# Quick Definition
The modifications applied to the subordinate group and transition in the recapitulation of rondo forms, including transposition to the tonic, transition redirection (often through the subdominant), and occasional omissions, additions, or reconstruction -- with special complications in minor-key movements.

# Core Definition
Since the subordinate group is repeated only once, after a number of intervening contrasts, variation is not strictly necessary. In fact, too much variation, especially at the beginning of the subordinate theme, could easily prevent recognition. But in order to repeat the subordinate material in the tonic region the transition requires changes. At an appropriate point the transition turns toward a different region, often the subdominant (major or minor), and continues indirectly to the upbeat chord, usually by means of sectional transpositions of the original material. In minor, treatment is more complicated, especially when the subordinate group is in major.

# Key Properties
- Minimum change: transposition of subordinate group to tonic region
- Transition must be redirected (often through subdominant)
- Too much variation at the beginning can prevent recognition
- In simpler cases, no further changes beyond transposition
- More complex cases may include omissions, additions, or reconstruction
- Minor key: subordinate theme usually recurs in tonic major, then turns to minor at a later point
- Elaborate changes are not common in rondos (more so in sonata-allegro)

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify how the transition has been redirected to reach the tonic
2. Note whether the subordinate theme is simply transposed or reconstructed
3. In minor-key movements, check whether the subordinate theme appears in tonic major or minor
4. Look for added modulatory passages that compensate for the loss of tonal contrast
5. Note any omissions or additions compared to the exposition

# Context & Application
The recapitulation of the subordinate group in rondo forms is simpler than in sonata-allegro forms, since rondo movements typically have less tonal distance to bridge. However, minor-key movements present special challenges because repeating a major-mode subordinate theme in tonic minor changes its character.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: transition reduced by two measures; subordinate theme rhythm modified; theme also slightly shortened
- Beethoven, Op. 7-IV: entire subordinate group recapitulated without major changes except transition redirection (m. 113)
- Beethoven, Op. 22-IV: transition starts as original; added sequence-like repetition (m. 134-5) changes modulation course
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-IV: transition modified and extended; original subordinate theme entirely omitted, replaced by modulatory passage on the basic motive
- Beethoven, Op. 13-III: original transition completely disappears; subordinate material reconstructed quite freely, with only a hint of c minor (m. 159)
- Beethoven, Op. 18/4-IV: B-section reconstructed in parallel major; ternary organization disappears; long retransition using principal theme motive-forms (m. 137-62)

# Common Errors
- Varying the subordinate theme's beginning too much, preventing recognition
- Failing to redirect the transition to reach the tonic region
- In minor, simply transposing a major-mode subordinate theme to minor without considering character changes

# Common Confusions
- Variation at the beginning of the subordinate theme is especially risky for recognition; variation is safer later in the group
- The elaborate changes seen in some examples (Op. 13-III, Op. 18/4-IV) are not typical; simpler transposition is more common in rondos
- In minor-key movements, the subordinate theme often appears in tonic major first, then turns to minor -- not simply transposed to tonic minor
