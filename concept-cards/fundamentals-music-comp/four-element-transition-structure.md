# Quick Definition
The four structural elements that ordinarily comprise a transition: establishment of the transitional idea, modulation, liquidation of motival characteristics, and establishment of a suitable upbeat chord.

# Formal Definition
The structure of a transition ordinarily includes four elements: (1) establishment of the transitional idea (through repetition, often sequential); (2) modulation (often in several stages); (3) liquidation of motival characteristics; and (4) establishment of a suitable upbeat chord. These aspects may overlap in varying degrees.

# Construction/Recognition
1. **Establishment**: The transitional idea is stated and confirmed through repetition, often sequential
2. **Modulation**: Harmonic movement away from the tonic, frequently accomplished in several stages
3. **Liquidation**: Progressive elimination of characteristic motival features, reducing the material to residues
4. **Upbeat chord**: Arrival at and emphasis of a dominant or other chord that functions as an upbeat to the ensuing subordinate theme

These four elements may overlap and interpenetrate rather than appearing as discrete, clearly separated stages.

# Musical Context
This four-element framework applies to transitions in sonata-allegro expositions, rondo forms, and other large forms. It governs both independent transitions and those derived from previous thematic material.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I: the transition starts m. 32 with a four-measure segment (establishment), partial repetition a third higher modulates to V of the dominant (modulation, m. 42), over a pedal point the scale line is liquidated by interrupting the descent twice (liquidation), m. 42-57 dwell on the dominant (upbeat chord)
- Beethoven, Op. 31/2-I: initial segment consists of two phrases above a pedal point on Bb (m. 18-21), first phrase repeated in variation (m. 22), then an episode over a pedal point on V of the dominant region is liquidated in m. 27-30

# Common Confusions
- The four elements are not always clearly delineated; they frequently overlap, and some may be very brief or combined with others
- The "upbeat chord" is not necessarily V of the new key; it is whatever harmony best prepares the ear for the entry of the subordinate theme
- "Liquidation" here refers specifically to the progressive removal of distinctive motival features, not to the disappearance of all musical content
