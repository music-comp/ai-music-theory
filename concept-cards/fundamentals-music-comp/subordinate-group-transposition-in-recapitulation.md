# Quick Definition
The technique of transposing the subordinate group from its original contrasting key to the tonic region in the recapitulation, requiring appropriate modifications of the transition and sometimes far-reaching reconstruction, especially in minor-key works.

# Formal Definition
Since the subordinate group is repeated only once after a number of intervening contrasts, variation is not strictly necessary. But in order to repeat the subordinate material in the tonic region, the transition requires changes. At an appropriate point the transition turns toward a different region, often the subdominant (major or minor), and continues indirectly to the upbeat chord, usually by means of sectional transpositions of the original material. In the simpler cases there are no further changes and the ensuing material is merely transposed to the tonic region, perhaps with minor ornamental variations.

# Construction/Recognition
- The transition is redirected harmonically to lead to the tonic rather than the original contrasting key
- The subordinate theme material is transposed to the tonic region
- In simpler cases, transposition is the only significant change
- In more complex cases, there may be omissions, additions, or complete reconstruction
- **Minor key complication**: When the subordinate theme was originally in major, repeating it in tonic minor would change its character; it therefore usually recurs in tonic major, with some later return to minor
- Too much variation at the beginning of the subordinate theme could prevent recognition

# Musical Context
This process is essential to the formal logic of both rondo and sonata-allegro forms. The recapitulation resolves the tonal contrast of the exposition by bringing all material into the tonic orbit. The transition bears the greatest burden of modification.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: transition repeated with two-measure reduction and minor modification; subordinate theme rhythm modified
- Beethoven, Op. 7-IV: entire subordinate group recapitulated without major changes except transition redirection (m. 113)
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-IV: transition modified and extended; the theme from m. 17 omitted entirely and replaced by a modulatory section on the basic motive
- Beethoven, Op. 13-III (minor key): original transition completely disappears; subordinate material reconstructed quite freely; only a hint of c minor heard (m. 159) in a much extended closing section
- Beethoven, Op. 18/4-IV: B-section reconstructed in parallel major; ternary organization disappears; a long retransition using principal theme motive-forms is added to relieve uninterrupted tonic emphasis

# Common Confusions
- The transition undergoes the most significant changes, not necessarily the subordinate theme itself
- In minor key movements, the problems are more complex because repeating major-key material in minor changes its character
- The more elaborate changes (omissions, additions, reconstruction) are less common in rondos than in sonata-allegro forms
