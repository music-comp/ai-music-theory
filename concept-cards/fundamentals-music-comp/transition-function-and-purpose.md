# Quick Definition
A connecting passage between principal and subordinate themes that serves both to introduce a contrast and to be itself a contrast, belonging to the group of subsidiary ideas.

# Core Definition
The purpose of a transition is not only to introduce a contrast; it is, itself, a contrast. It may begin, after the end of the main theme, with new thematic formulations; or the end of the main theme may be modified thematically and harmonically into a connecting segment. A transition, especially if it is an independent section, belongs to the group of subsidiary ideas. Transitions appear at various places in larger forms: between principal and subordinate themes (modulating to a different region); as retransitions (returning to the tonic); or, in the recapitulation, reformulated as a roundabout way from tonic to tonic.

# Key Properties
- Dual function: introduces contrast AND is itself a contrast
- Belongs to subsidiary (not principal) ideas
- Modulatory character -- moves from one tonal region to another
- Position between stable thematic sections
- Uses liquidation and motival reduction
- May be independent (new theme) or derived from main theme
- In recapitulation, reformulated as tonic-to-tonic passage

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify by its position between stable themes and its modulatory character
- Look for the four-element structure: establishment, modulation, liquidation, upbeat chord
- Fluid change of region and prompt elimination of characteristic features distinguish it from stable themes
- May begin with new thematic material or with elements from the preceding theme
- Short rhythmic figures are characteristic, lending themselves to modulation and liquidation

# Context & Application
In simpler forms, the chief contrast is furnished by harmony alone. In larger forms, a modulatory passage may be organized into an independent section -- the transition -- which connects the main theme with another stable contrasting idea, the subordinate theme. The transition is essential for providing contrast in the recapitulation, where it offers the only relief from the tonic region governing the entire section.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I: transition starts m. 32, built from an inversion of m. 9-10 of the main theme; partial repetition a third higher modulates to V of the dominant (m. 42)
- Beethoven, Op. 13-III: transition (m. 18-21) consists of little more than a modulatory progression
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-IV: a very short rondo contains all normal constituent parts in miniature; transition (m. 9), despite internal repetition, is only seven measures

# Common Errors
- Writing transitions as mere neutral connective filler rather than as genuine sources of contrast
- Failing to liquidate motival characteristics before the subordinate theme enters
- Omitting the upbeat chord preparation, making the subordinate theme entry unconvincing

# Common Confusions
- A transition is not merely a bridge or passage of connective filler; it is itself a source of contrast and often contains its own thematic material
- Transitions in the recapitulation serve a different harmonic function (tonic to tonic) than in the exposition (tonic to contrasting region), yet they remain valuable for the contrast they provide
- The modification, reduction, or omission of the transition in the recapitulation is discussed separately (pp. 193-5)
