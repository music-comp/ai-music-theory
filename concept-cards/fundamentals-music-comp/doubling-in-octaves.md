# Quick Definition
The practice of reinforcing a voice by duplicating it an octave higher or lower, permitted in piano style when the basic voice leading is free from parallel octaves and especially effective when consistently maintained.

# Formal Definition
If the basic voice-leading is free from parallel octaves, each individual voice can be doubled in octaves. This doubling is especially effective when consistently maintained throughout a passage. It can be applied to any voice: bass, melody, or inner voices. Bass doubling can produce variety or contrast through change of register. Melody doubling is common and enhances the projection of the principal line. Inner voice doubling appears more incidentally, for special effects.

# Construction/Recognition
- Prerequisite: basic voice leading must be free from parallel octaves
- Most effective when maintained consistently through a passage
- Bass doubling: creates register variety and contrast
- Melody doubling: enhances melodic projection
- Inner voice doubling: used incidentally for special effects
- Sporadic doubling may appear for individual accents or emphases

# Musical Context
Doubling in octaves is a fundamental technique of piano writing that distinguishes it from the strict four-part writing of chorale harmony. It allows the piano to achieve the dynamic range and textural weight that orchestral instruments achieve through multiple players. The contrast between doubled and undoubled passages can itself be a structural device, as when bass doubling in one phrase creates contrast with an undoubled passage in the next.

# Common Confusions
- Doubling in octaves is not the same as parallel octaves between independent voices; doubling reinforces a single voice, while parallel octaves are a voice-leading error between different voices.
- Consistent doubling is more convincing than sporadic doubling, which can sound arbitrary.
- Doubling does not change the harmonic content; it reinforces what is already present.
