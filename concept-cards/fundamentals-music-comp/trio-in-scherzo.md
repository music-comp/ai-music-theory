# Quick Definition

The contrasting middle section at the movement level that follows the scherzo, functioning identically to the trio in the minuet but with additional techniques for connecting the trio to the scherzo's repetition, including transitional liquidation and mediating passages.

# Core Definition

The relation between scherzo and trio is the same as that between minuet and trio. In many cases, the repetition of the scherzo follows a complete ending of the trio. In other cases, the recapitulation of the trio is liquidated into a transition, introducing an upbeat chord for the scherzo's repetition. Often a little segment may be inserted between trio and scherzo. Scherzo movements are sometimes enlarged to the dimensions of a rondo by interpolating the trio twice, alternating with three appearances of the scherzo.

# Prerequisites

- **Scherzo form**: Understanding the ternary structure at the section level
- **Trio**: Understanding the concept of the trio as a contrasting second dance

# Key Properties

- Provides contrast to the scherzo in tonality, character, or both
- May end completely before the scherzo returns, or may transition into it
- Transitional techniques: liquidation of trio's recapitulation into upbeat chord; inserted connecting segments; reintroduction of scherzo motive on modulatory harmony
- May be interpolated twice for rondo-like structure (Scherzo-Trio-Scherzo-Trio-Scherzo)
- Some composers introduce two different trios (Schumann, Piano Quintet Op. 44)
- The handling of the transition from trio to scherzo is a significant compositional challenge

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify the trio as a complete, contrasting section following the scherzo
2. Assess the tonal and character contrast with the scherzo
3. Examine the transition from trio back to scherzo (complete ending, liquidation, connecting segment)
4. Check for rondo-like expansion (double trio insertion)
5. Look for mediation between contrasting themes at the transition point

# Context & Application

The techniques used to connect the trio back to the scherzo reveal important principles of mediation between contrasting materials. The extraordinary example from Brahms's Sextet Op. 36 shows how a mediating passage can prepare the return of the scherzo while still deriving from the trio's material, demonstrating sophisticated compositional craft. The recognition of the need for adequate contrast between trio and scherzo (even when both are in the same key) is an important compositional principle.

# Examples

- Beethoven, Op. 2/3 and String Quartet Op. 18/1: Trio's recapitulation liquidated into transition introducing upbeat chord
- Beethoven, Fifth Symphony, m. 224-35: Transitional liquidation
- Beethoven, Op. 26 and String Quartet Op. 18/2: Scherzo motive reintroduced on modulatory harmony
- Beethoven, Seventh Symphony, m. 221-234: Reminiscence of the trio; transposition reintroduces F in last two measures
- Beethoven, String Quartet Op. 18/6, m. 65-68: Tonic minor segment for contrast between trio and scherzo, both in B-flat major
- Brahms, Sextet Op. 36: Eight-measure mediating passage (m. 227) whose first half prepares the scherzo while the second derives from the trio; construction in twos and fours prepares the return of 2/4 metre
- Beethoven, Op. 74, Op. 95, Symphonies 4, 7: Trio interpolated twice (rondo-like)
- Schumann, Piano Quintet Op. 44: Two different trios

# Common Errors

- Treating the da capo as a mere repetition without considering the transition from trio
- Failing to provide adequate contrast when trio and scherzo are in the same key
- Neglecting thematic mediation at the transition point

# Common Confusions

- **Trio principles are the same**: The trio in a scherzo follows the same principles as in a minuet; the difference lies in the techniques for connecting back to the scherzo.
- **Rondo-like expansion**: A scherzo with two trio insertions is an expansion of the basic form, not a different form.
