# Quick Definition
Techniques for expanding smaller parts within larger forms through sequential repetition, extensions, liquidations, and broadened connectives, producing derivatives of the basic motive that serve a co-ordinate rather than contrasting structural function.

# Core Definition
Smaller parts in larger forms may be expanded by means of internal repetitions, sequences, extensions, liquidations, and broadening of connectives. The number of parts may be increased by supplying codettas, episodes, etc. In such situations, derivatives of the basic motive are formulated into new thematic units. Their structural function, however, is co-ordinate rather than contrasting. This expansion technique is distinct from the generation of contrast through genuinely new thematic material.

# Prerequisites
- Understanding of motive and motive-forms
- Familiarity with phrase and sentence construction
- Knowledge of sequences and liquidation processes

# Key Properties
- Expansions use derivatives of the basic motive, not entirely new material
- The structural function of expanded material is co-ordinate (supportive), not contrasting
- Techniques include: sequential repetition, extensions, liquidations, broadened connectives
- Additional parts (codettas, episodes) may increase the number of sections
- Distinguished from the generation of contrast through genuinely opposed thematic ideas

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify recurring motivic patterns within a section that extend its length
2. Distinguish between sequential repetitions (transposed) and varied repetitions (modified)
3. Note whether new thematic units are co-ordinate (supporting) or contrasting
4. Map the internal structure using measure counts (e.g., 3+3+4+2+2)
5. Look for extensions, liquidations, and broadened connectives as expansion mechanisms

# Context & Application
Large forms develop through the generating power of contrasts, but within individual sections, expansion through internal repetition is the primary means of increasing size. The co-ordinate function of internally repeated material distinguishes it from the contrasting function of transitions, subordinate themes, and elaboration sections. Understanding this distinction is essential for composing sections of appropriate length and weight within larger forms.

# Examples
- In sonata-allegro expositions, subordinate theme groups often expand through internal repetition of short segments joined by juxtaposition
- Beethoven's codetta sections frequently expand through sequential repetition of cadential figures
- Extensions and liquidations within a transition expand its length while maintaining its modulatory function

# Common Errors
- Confusing co-ordinate expansion with contrasting material -- internally repeated material serves a supporting function
- Mechanical repetition without variation, producing monotony rather than expansion
- Failing to distinguish between expansion of existing sections and addition of new contrasting sections

# Common Confusions
- Internal repetitions in larger forms (Ch 18) concern the expansion of sections within complex structures, distinct from the irregular phrase construction produced by internal repetitions discussed in Ch 14
- Sequential repetition (transposition) should be distinguished from varied repetition (modification)
- The presence of internal repetitions does not automatically produce irregular construction; they may be balanced to produce regular phrases
