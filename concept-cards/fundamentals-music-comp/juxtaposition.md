# Quick Definition
The technique of placing distinct thematic formulations side by side without connective development, characteristic of the loose construction found in subordinate theme groups.

# Formal Definition
In subordinate themes, there are frequently a number of distinct formulations, each established and then abandoned to make way for the next -- the group of subordinate themes. These segments are joined by mere juxtaposition rather than by developmental processes, connective transitions, or organic outgrowth from one another. This stands in contrast to the principal theme, where ideas are connected through variation and motivic development.

# Construction/Recognition
- Distinct thematic segments are placed adjacent to each other without transitional connectives
- Each segment typically has its own character, rhythm, or motive-form
- The boundary between juxtaposed segments is usually clear, sometimes marked by an overlap
- There is little or no recurrence of earlier features within the section
- The technique produces a "chain" of contrasting ideas rather than a continuously developed argument

# Musical Context
Juxtaposition is a hallmark of subordinate theme groups in sonata-allegro expositions. It provides variety and contrast through the multiplication of distinct thematic ideas rather than through the intensive development of a single idea.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I: derivatives of the principal theme are juxtaposed without any connective in m. 76
- Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I: a different two-measure segment (m. 39-40) is juxtaposed after the preceding material; other distinct formulations appear in m. 61, 69, 73
- Mozart, K.V. 465-I (coda): segments are juxtaposed in succession -- two three-measure and two two-measure segments follow the opening

# Common Confusions
- Juxtaposition is not the same as contrast; it specifically refers to the manner of joining -- placing ideas side by side without connective material -- rather than to the nature of the ideas themselves
- Juxtaposition may include overlap (where the end of one segment coincides with the beginning of the next), which is a refinement rather than a contradiction of the technique
