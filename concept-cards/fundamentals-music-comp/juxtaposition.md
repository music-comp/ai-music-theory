# Quick Definition

The technique of placing distinct thematic formulations side by side without connective development, characteristic of the loose construction found in subordinate theme groups.

# Core Definition

In subordinate themes, "mere repetition and juxtaposition often replace development and elaboration" (Ch. 18, p. 103). There are "frequently a number of distinct formulations, each established and then abandoned to make way for the next -- the group of subordinate themes" (Ch. 18, p. 103). These segments are joined by mere juxtaposition rather than by developmental processes, connective transitions, or organic outgrowth from one another. This technique produces a chain of contrasting ideas rather than a continuously developed argument, and constitutes evidence of the subordinate character of these themes.

# Key Properties

1. Distinct thematic segments are placed adjacent to each other without transitional connectives
2. Each segment typically has its own character, rhythm, or motive-form
3. The boundary between juxtaposed segments is usually clear, sometimes marked by an overlap
4. There is little or no recurrence of earlier features within the section
5. The technique produces a chain of contrasting ideas rather than a continuously developed argument
6. Juxtaposition is evidence of subordination -- it distinguishes subordinate from principal themes
7. Within the subordinate group, individual themes contrast with each other as well as with the principal theme

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct Using Juxtaposition

1. Create a short thematic segment (typically 2-4 measures)
2. Establish the segment through direct repetition (possibly sequential or slightly varied)
3. Abandon the segment and introduce a new, distinct formulation
4. Repeat the establish-and-abandon pattern for subsequent formulations
5. Avoid developmental connectives between segments -- simply place them side by side
6. Ensure each segment has its own distinct character

## To Recognize Juxtaposition

1. Identify a succession of distinct thematic ideas without developmental transitions
2. Note the establish-and-abandon pattern: each idea repeated, then replaced
3. Observe the absence of motivic development connecting the segments
4. Confirm the passage occurs in a subordinate thematic context

# Context & Application

Juxtaposition is a hallmark of subordinate theme groups in sonata-allegro expositions, rondos, and other larger forms. It provides variety and contrast through the multiplication of distinct thematic ideas rather than through the intensive development of a single idea. "Aesthetically, the most important type of contrast is that of construction, since it is evidence of subordination" (Ch. 18, p. 103). The looser construction of subordinate themes depends on "immediate repetition of relatively short segments, joined to others by mere juxtaposition; and a lesser degree of internal development" (Ch. 18, p. 104).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 103): Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I. In m. 21-25, a phrase appears three times. A little segment is juxtaposed in m. 26; a new concluding segment is again juxtaposed in m. 33 and repeated. Codettas begin in m. 41.

**Example 2** (p. 103): Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I. The small segment in m. 59-62 is twice sequentially repeated. A two-measure segment (m. 70) is sequenced twice. In m. 76, derivatives of the principal theme are juxtaposed without any connective. A cadential segment (m. 84) and its repetition bring the subordinate section to a close.

**Example 3** (p. 104): Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I. A six-measure segment (m. 27-32) is repeated quasi-sequentially. A different two-measure segment (m. 39-40) is juxtaposed, repeated, and reduced. Other distinct formulations appear in m. 61, 69, 73.

**Example 4** (p. 106): Mozart, K.V. 465-I (coda). Two three-measure and two two-measure segments are juxtaposed in succession after the opening segment of the coda.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using developmental connectives between subordinate theme segments
  **Correction**: Subordinate themes are characteristically joined by mere juxtaposition, not by transitional development

- **Error**: Making all juxtaposed segments too similar in character
  **Correction**: Each segment should be distinct in character, rhythm, or motive-form to provide the multiplicity of ideas that defines the subordinate group

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Juxtaposition is the same as contrast
  **Clarification**: Juxtaposition specifically refers to the manner of joining -- placing ideas side by side without connective material -- rather than to the nature of the ideas themselves

- **Confusion**: Juxtaposition and overlap are contradictory
  **Clarification**: Overlap (where the end of one segment coincides with the beginning of the next) is a refinement of juxtaposition, not a contradiction of it

- **Confusion**: Juxtaposition implies weakness or lack of craft
  **Clarification**: It is a deliberate constructional technique that provides evidence of subordination and multiplies thematic variety
