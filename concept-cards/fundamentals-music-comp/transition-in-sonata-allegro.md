# Quick Definition
The modulatory passage connecting principal and subordinate themes in a sonata-allegro exposition, which may use an independent theme or transform the main theme, and which becomes a valuable source of contrast in the recapitulation.

# Formal Definition
Occasionally there is no independent transition. But a well-developed modulatory passage between the principal and subordinate themes becomes a valuable source of contrast in the recapitulation. Both the transformation of the main theme into a transition and the transition with an independent theme are equally useful, the choice depending in part on the amount of internal repetition incorporated in the principal theme. The more extended transitions normally close with liquidation and emphasis of a suitable upbeat chord or region.

# Construction/Recognition
- May be independent (new thematic material) or derived from the principal theme
- In very extended works (symphonies, concertos), the transition may require more than one theme
- Normally closes with liquidation and emphasis of a suitable upbeat chord
- In the recapitulation, the transition is modified to redirect from tonic to tonic rather than from tonic to contrasting key
- Its value as contrast becomes more significant in the recapitulation, where it may provide the only departure from the tonic region
- May be omitted in the simplest cases (Op. 10/2, Op. 10/3)

# Musical Context
The transition serves the dual function of harmonic modulation and thematic contrast. In the recapitulation, since no modulation to a new key is necessary, one might expect the transition to disappear; on the contrary, its effect is usually heightened and it is often lengthened.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 10/2 and Op. 10/3: no independent transition
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: principal theme transposed to provide transition material
- Beethoven, Op. 7-I: varied repetition of opening motto inaugurates the transition
- In very extended works, the transition may require more than one theme

# Common Confusions
- The transition's role in the recapitulation is paradoxically more important than in the exposition; without the need for modulation, it provides essential contrast to the otherwise unbroken tonic
- The choice between independent and derived transition depends partly on the principal theme's structure, not merely on the composer's preference
- Even when the transition is absent (as in Op. 10/2-3), the movement proceeds directly from principal to subordinate themes
