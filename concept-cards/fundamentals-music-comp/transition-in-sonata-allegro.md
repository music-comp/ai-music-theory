# Quick Definition
The modulatory passage connecting the principal and subordinate groups in a sonata-allegro exposition, drawing on the transition principles of Chapter 18 -- occasionally absent (Op. 10/2, Op. 10/3), but when present, providing valuable contrast especially in the recapitulation.

# Core Definition
No special discussion of this feature is necessary beyond Chapter XVIII. A considerable number of characteristic examples have been discussed there. Occasionally there is no independent transition (Op. 10/2 and Op. 10/3). But a well-developed modulatory passage between the principal and subordinate themes becomes a valuable source of contrast in the recapitulation. Both the transformation of the main theme into a transition, and the transition with an independent theme are equally useful, the choice depending in part on the amount of internal repetition incorporated in the principal theme. In very extended works (symphonies, concertos), the transition may require more than one theme. The more extended transitions normally close with liquidation and emphasis of a suitable upbeat chord or region.

# Key Properties
- May be absent in simpler cases (Op. 10/2, Op. 10/3)
- When present, provides valuable contrast in the recapitulation
- Both independent theme and derived transitions equally useful
- Choice depends on amount of internal repetition in principal theme
- In extended works, may require more than one theme
- Normally closes with liquidation and upbeat chord emphasis
- Becomes especially important in recapitulation, where it provides the only relief from tonic

# Construction / Recognition
1. Apply the four-element structure from Ch 18
2. Choose between independent theme or derived transition based on principal theme character
3. Close with liquidation and suitable upbeat chord
4. In extended works, consider multiple transitional themes
5. Plan for modifications needed in the recapitulation

# Context & Application
The sonata-allegro transition draws entirely on the principles established in Chapter 18. Its value becomes especially clear in the recapitulation, where, since no modulation to a contrasting key is necessary, the transition provides the only contrast to the tonic region governing the entire recapitulation and coda.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 10/2 and Op. 10/3: no independent transition; principal theme leads directly to subordinate group
- All examples discussed in Chapter 18 apply to sonata-allegro transitions
- In symphonies and concertos, transitions may be more extensive and use multiple themes

# Common Errors
- Omitting the transition when it would provide valuable recapitulation contrast
- Using a transition that is too stable, failing to achieve the modulatory purpose
- Neglecting to plan for the recapitulation modifications when writing the exposition transition

# Common Confusions
- The absence of a transition in some works (Op. 10/2, Op. 10/3) does not mean transitions are optional in general; they become valuable sources of contrast in the recapitulation
- "No special discussion is necessary" refers to form, not importance; the transition's role in the recapitulation is crucial
- In extended works, a single transitional theme may be insufficient
