# Quick Definition
The quality of being performable by the voice, which forms the foundation of all melodiousness; singability implies relatively long notes, smooth register linkage, stepwise motion, avoidance of difficult intervals, tonal adherence, and cautious dissonance.

# Core Definition
Singability, in a more popular sense, implies relatively long notes; smooth linkage of the registers; movement in waves, more stepwise than by leaps; avoidance of augmented and diminished intervals; adherence to the tonality and its most closely related regions; employment of the natural intervals of a key; gradual modulation; and a cautious use of dissonance. The concept of the melodious is intimately related to the concept of singability. The nature and technique of the primordial musical instrument, the voice, determines what is singable.

# Key Properties
- Relatively long notes allowing the voice to develop full sound
- Smooth linkage of registers (avoiding sudden register jumps)
- Movement in waves, more stepwise than by leaps
- Avoidance of augmented and diminished intervals
- Adherence to tonality and closely related regions
- Employment of natural intervals of a key
- Gradual modulation
- Cautious use of dissonance
- The voice needs time to develop full sound; rapid staccato notes are difficult

# Construction / Recognition
Test singability by: (1) checking whether the melody could be sung in one breath per phrase, (2) evaluating the smoothness of register transitions, (3) checking for augmented or diminished intervals that lack harmonic support, (4) assessing whether leaps are compensated by stepwise motion, (5) examining whether modulations are gradual and tonally grounded.

# Context & Application
The concept of the melodious in instrumental melody developed as a free adaptation from the vocal model. Even instrumental melody should ideally be such that it could be sung, "if only by a voice of incredible capacity." The boundaries of singability have expanded historically, but the concept remains the foundation of all melodiousness.

# Examples
- Beethoven String Quartet Op. 132-III (Ex. 96c): has only one melody symptom -- singability -- yet makes the impression of a melody, demonstrating the power of this single quality
- Brahms contradicted his own rule that "The melody of a song should be such that one could whistle it" in Ex. 74d
- Many Beethoven piano sonata melodies could be sung by any singer: Op. 2/2-II, Op. 7-IV, Op. 10/1-II, Op. 10/3-III, Op. 13-II

# Common Errors
- Writing melody that cannot be related to any vocal model
- Neglecting singability in instrumental writing
- Confusing difficulty with unsuitability; trained singers can perform difficult passages

# Common Confusions
- Singability does not mean the melody must literally be vocal music; it is a structural criterion applicable to instrumental music as well
- Singability is the classical ideal; later music may deliberately violate it for expressive purposes
- The boundaries of singability have expanded; what was unsingable in the eighteenth century may be performable today
