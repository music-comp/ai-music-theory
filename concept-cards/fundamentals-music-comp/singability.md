# Quick Definition
The principle that a phrase approximates what one could sing in a single breath, providing a natural, physiological basis for determining structural unit length.

# Formal Definition
The term phrase means, structurally, a unit approximating to what one could sing in a single breath. This criterion provides a physical, intuitive measure for the appropriate length and shape of the smallest structural unit. It connects the abstract concept of musical structure to the embodied experience of music-making.

# Construction/Recognition
A phrase that feels "singable" has an appropriate length (not too long for a breath), a natural contour (with a sense of direction and arrival), and sufficient internal coherence to feel like a complete thought. Testing whether a melodic idea can be sung in one breath is a practical check on phrase construction.

# Musical Context
Singability is related to the classical ideal of the "popular touch" in themes. Schoenberg notes that the eighteenth-century "ars nova" detached itself from the contrapuntal style partly through the ideal that themes should be immediately graspable. Mattheson (1739) is quoted as saying that composers should "hide the fact that they write great music" and that "a theme should have a certain something which the whole world already knows."

# Examples
- The breath-length criterion applies across tempos: in slow tempos, fewer measures constitute a phrase; in fast tempos, more measures may be needed
- Classical themes by Beethoven consistently demonstrate singable phrase construction

# Common Confusions
- Singability does not mean the phrase must literally be vocal music; it is a structural criterion applicable to instrumental music as well
- The "breath" criterion is approximate, not absolute -- different singers have different breath capacities
- Singability is a classical ideal; later music may deliberately violate it for expressive purposes
