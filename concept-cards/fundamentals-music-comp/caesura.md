# Quick Definition

A caesura is a point of musical punctuation -- comparable to a comma or semicolon -- that divides the antecedent from the consequent in a period, carried out simultaneously in both melody and harmony.

# Core Definition

The caesura is a type of musical punctuation comparable to a comma or semicolon, which divides an eight-measure period into an antecedent and consequent of four measures each. It is carried out in both melody and harmony (Schoenberg, p. 22). The caesura typically occurs at the end of the antecedent (m. 4 in an eight-measure period), and in the great majority of cases the antecedent ends on V at the caesura, usually approached through a half or full cadence, but sometimes through mere interchange of I and V.

# Prerequisites

- **Phrase** -- The caesura marks the boundary between phrases within the period
- **Motive** -- Melodic marking of the caesura involves transformation of motive-forms

# Key Properties

1. Simultaneously realized in both melody and harmony
2. Typically occurs at m. 4 in an eight-measure period
3. Most commonly on V (half cadence, full cadence, or I-V interchange)
4. Melodically often marked by a decrease in smaller note values
5. Frequently approached by receding from an earlier climactic point
6. Contour at the caesura often descends below the register of the beginning
7. In contrapuntal textures (e.g., Bach), may be concealed by continuous voice movement

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct a Caesura

1. Arrive at V (or occasionally I) at the end of the antecedent
2. Approach through half cadence, full cadence, or I-V interchange
3. Mark the caesura melodically by decreasing note values or simplifying rhythmic activity
4. Support the caesura with a contrasting contour that often descends below the opening register
5. Recede from any preceding climactic point

## To Recognize a Caesura

1. Locate the structural breathing point between antecedent and consequent
2. Confirm simultaneous articulation in both melody and harmony
3. Identify the harmonic arrival (most commonly V)
4. Note any melodic decrease, register descent, or retreat from a climax

# Context & Application

The caesura provides the essential structural articulation that defines the period form. Without a clear caesura, the distinction between antecedent and consequent becomes blurred, and the form may be perceived as a continuous phrase rather than a period. In Bach's contrapuntal writing, the caesura may be concealed by continuous motion, though period-like repetition of motive-forms remains evident.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Failing to articulate the caesura in both melody and harmony simultaneously
  **Correction**: Both dimensions must participate; a purely melodic or purely harmonic caesura is insufficient

- **Error**: Always approaching the caesura with a formal half cadence
  **Correction**: Simple interchange of I and V can suffice; a formal cadence is not always required

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking a caesura is simply a rest or pause
  **Clarification**: A caesura is a structural articulation carried out in both melody and harmony, not merely a silence

- **Confusion**: Believing the caesura must always be obvious
  **Clarification**: In contrapuntal textures like Bach's, continuous motion may conceal the caesura while the period-like structure remains present

- **Confusion**: Assuming the caesura must always be on V
  **Clarification**: V is most common, but the caesura can also arrive on I
