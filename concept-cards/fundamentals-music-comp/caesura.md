# Quick Definition
A caesura is a point of musical punctuation -- comparable to a comma or semicolon in language -- that divides the antecedent from the consequent in a period, carried out in both melody and harmony.

# Formal Definition
The caesura is a structural division point within the period, typically occurring at the fourth measure in an eight-measure period. It functions as a type of musical punctuation, executed simultaneously in both the melodic line and the harmonic progression. The caesura marks the boundary between the antecedent and consequent, creating the necessary structural breathing point before the modified repetition begins.

# Construction/Recognition
- Occurs at the end of the antecedent, typically m. 4 in an eight-measure period
- Must be realized in both melody and harmony simultaneously
- Often marked melodically by a decrease in smaller note values
- Frequently approached by receding from an earlier climactic point
- The melodic contour at the caesura often descends below the register of the beginning
- In Bach's contrapuntal style, the caesura may be concealed by continuous voice movement

# Musical Context
The caesura provides the essential structural articulation that defines the period form. Without a clear caesura, the distinction between antecedent and consequent becomes blurred, and the form may be perceived as a continuous phrase rather than a period. The caesura is typically supported by harmonic arrival on V (half cadence) or by interchange of I and V.

# Common Confusions
- A caesura is not simply a rest or a pause; it is a structural articulation carried out in both melody and harmony.
- The caesura is not always obvious -- in contrapuntal textures like Bach's, continuous motion may conceal it while the period-like structure remains present.
- A caesura on V does not necessarily require a formal half cadence; simple interchange of I and V can suffice.
