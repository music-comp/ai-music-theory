# Quick Definition
The closing portion of the elaboration that neutralizes modulatory momentum, liquidates motival obligations, and prepares the return to the recapitulation -- often through a dominant pedal point, bridging upbeat-like passages, or in more complex works, a series of codetta-like segments repeatedly approaching the upbeat chord.

# Core Definition
The close of the elaboration must be handled in such a manner as to neutralize modulatory momentum and liquidate motival obligations created within the section, and at the same time prepare the listener for the return to the recapitulation. The reduction of motive-forms to minimum content, and the presence of relatively long sections stressing the dominant or some other suitable upbeat chord, are characteristic. Often a bridging upbeat-like passage is inserted. Since this point is the junction between two main divisions, a contrast of rhythm or dynamics, or both, and of register generally reinforces the desired contrast.

# Key Properties
- Neutralizes modulatory momentum
- Liquidates motival obligations
- Prepares listener for recapitulation return
- Motive-forms reduced to minimum content
- Relatively long sections stress the dominant or upbeat chord
- Bridging upbeat-like passages often inserted
- Contrast of rhythm, dynamics, and register at the junction
- In complex works: series of codetta-like segments approaching the upbeat chord
- When recapitulation begins on a non-tonic chord, a different upbeat chord may be needed

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify where the dominant (or upbeat chord) is reached after the last modulatory segment
2. Note the liquidation of remaining motive-forms
3. Look for the dominant pedal point or codetta-like segments
4. Check for contrasts of rhythm, dynamics, and register preparing the recapitulation
5. In complex works, map the series of approaches to the upbeat chord

# Context & Application
The retransition in sonata-allegro is the most dramatic and psychologically important application of the retransition concept. In more complex compositions, the simple dominant pedal point is replaced with a series of segments resembling codettas that repeatedly approach the upbeat chord, with internal modulation that returns each time. The Eroica Symphony exemplifies this elaborate approach.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I (m. 135), Op. 13-I (m. 187), Op. 14/2-I (m. 121): bridging upbeat-like passages inserted
- Eroica Symphony-I: dominant reached in m. 338, some sixty measures before the recapitulation (m. 398); recurs briefly in m. 354; finally established in m. 378
- Fifth Symphony-I: similar elaborate treatment between m. 190 and 248
- Beethoven, Op. 10/2-I: customary dominant emphasis curtailed because the recapitulation does not begin with the tonic (begins in D, submediant major)
- Beethoven, Op. 31/3-I: similar curtailment due to non-tonic recapitulation beginning

# Common Errors
- Making the retransition too abrupt, failing to neutralize the modulatory momentum
- Dwelling too long on the dominant, dissipating the dramatic tension
- Neglecting the contrast of rhythm, dynamics, and register at the junction point

# Common Confusions
- The retransition is not simply "arriving at the dominant"; in complex works, the dominant may be reached many measures before the recapitulation, with elaborate approaches in between
- When the recapitulation begins on a non-tonic chord, the customary dominant emphasis may be curtailed or a different upbeat chord used
- The "series of codetta-like segments" in elaborate retransitions resembles codettas except that they approach the upbeat chord instead of the tonic
