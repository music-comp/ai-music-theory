# Quick Definition
The passage at the end of the elaboration that neutralizes modulatory momentum and prepares the listener for the recapitulation, typically through emphasis of the dominant and liquidation of motival residues.

# Formal Definition
The close of the elaboration must be handled in such a manner as to neutralize modulatory momentum and liquidate motival obligations created within the section, and at the same time to prepare the listener for the return to the recapitulation. The reduction of motive-forms to minimum content, and the presence of relatively long sections stressing the dominant or some other suitable upbeat chord, have been mentioned in the foregoing analyses.

# Construction/Recognition
- Reduces motive-forms to minimum content
- Emphasizes the dominant (or other suitable upbeat chord) through pedal points or repeated approaches
- Often a bridging upbeat-like passage is inserted
- A contrast of rhythm, dynamics, or register reinforces the junction between elaboration and recapitulation
- In more complex compositions, a series of codetta-like segments repeatedly approaches the upbeat chord, with internal modulation that returns to the dominant
- When the recapitulation begins on a chord other than the tonic, a different upbeat chord may be necessary

# Musical Context
The retransition is the structural junction between the two main divisions of the sonata-allegro. Its effectiveness determines whether the recapitulation arrives with the psychological impact of homecoming or merely follows mechanically.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I (m. 135): bridging upbeat-like passage inserted before recapitulation
- Beethoven, Op. 13-I (m. 187): descending eighth-note figure acts as retransition
- Eroica Symphony-I: dominant reached in m. 338, some sixty measures before the recapitulation (m. 398); it recurs briefly in m. 354 and is finally established in m. 378 ff.
- Fifth Symphony-I: similar extended treatment between m. 190 and 248
- Beethoven, Op. 10/2-I: customary dominant emphasis curtailed because recapitulation begins in D (submediant major) rather than F (tonic)
- Mozart, K.V. 464-I: end of retransition (m. 162) overlaps the beginning of recapitulation

# Common Confusions
- The retransition is not a separate formal section but the closing portion of the elaboration; it functions as both conclusion of the middle section and preparation for the return
- The dominant may be established long before the actual recapitulation; the extended dominant emphasis in the Eroica (60 measures) is extreme but illustrative
- When the recapitulation begins in a key other than the tonic (as in Op. 10/2-I), the standard dominant preparation is modified accordingly
