# Quick Definition
The concept that the basic motive contains, in embryonic form, the elements of every subsequent musical figure in a piece -- functioning simultaneously as the "smallest common multiple" and the "greatest common factor" of all musical ideas.

# Formal Definition
Inasmuch as almost every figure within a piece reveals some relationship to the basic motive, it is often considered the "germ" of the idea. Since it includes elements, at least, of every subsequent musical figure, one could consider it the "smallest common multiple." And since it is included in every subsequent figure, it could be considered the "greatest common factor."

# Construction/Recognition
The germ-motive is recognized by tracing relationships backward from any figure in a piece to the opening motive. Every subsequent figure should reveal some connection -- whether in rhythm, intervals, contour, or harmony -- to the basic motive. The more thoroughly the motive permeates the piece, the stronger the organic unity.

# Musical Context
This organic conception of the motive reflects Schoenberg's view of composition as analogous to biological growth. The motive is not merely a theme to be stated and restated, but a seed from which the entire musical organism develops. This concept is closely related to developing variation, where each new motive-form grows from previous ones.

# Examples
- Beethoven's Fifth Symphony: the four-note motive permeates virtually every figure in the first movement
- Brahms's Fourth Symphony: the opening succession of thirds generates material throughout
- Beethoven Op. 2/3-IV: the upward-sweep-and-return contour pervades the movement

# Common Confusions
- The "germ" metaphor does not mean the motive must be stated in its original form throughout; it means its elements are present in derived forms
- The mathematical analogies ("smallest common multiple," "greatest common factor") are metaphorical, illustrating the motive's dual role as source and subset
- Not every note in a piece is literally derived from the motive, but every significant figure should reveal some relationship to it
