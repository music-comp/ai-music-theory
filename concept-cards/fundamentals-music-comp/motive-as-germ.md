# Quick Definition

The concept that the basic motive contains, in embryonic form, the elements of every subsequent musical figure in a piece -- functioning simultaneously as the "smallest common multiple" and the "greatest common factor" of all musical ideas.

# Core Definition

"Inasmuch as almost every figure within a piece reveals some relationship to it, the basic motive is often considered the 'germ' of the idea. Since it includes elements, at least, of every subsequent musical figure, one could consider it the 'smallest common multiple'. And since it is included in every subsequent figure, it could be considered the 'greatest common factor'" (Ch. 3, p. 8).

# Prerequisites

- **motive** -- Must understand the motive and its features before grasping its role as generative seed

# Key Properties

1. The basic motive is the "germ" from which the entire piece grows
2. Almost every figure in the piece reveals some relationship to it
3. It is the "smallest common multiple" -- it includes elements of every subsequent figure
4. It is the "greatest common factor" -- it is included in every subsequent figure
5. The metaphor implies organic growth, not mechanical construction

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify the basic motive at the opening of the piece
2. Trace relationships from any figure in the piece back to the opening motive
3. Look for shared features -- rhythm, intervals, contour, harmony -- connecting figures to the basic motive
4. Note that relationships may be indirect: figure C derives from figure B, which derives from the basic motive
5. The more thoroughly the motive permeates the piece, the stronger the organic unity

# Context & Application

This organic conception reflects Schoenberg's view of composition as analogous to biological growth. The motive is not merely a theme to be stated and restated but a seed from which the entire musical organism develops. This concept is closely related to developing variation, where each new motive-form grows progressively from previous ones, creating a chain of organic development.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 8): Beethoven's Fifth Symphony -- the four-note motive permeates virtually every figure in the first movement.

**Example 2** (p. 8): Brahms's Fourth Symphony -- the opening succession of thirds generates material throughout.

**Example 3** (p. 8): Beethoven Op. 2/3-IV -- the upward-sweep-and-return contour pervades the entire movement.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Expecting every note to be literally derived from the motive
  **Correction**: The relationship is to significant figures, not to every individual note

- **Error**: Stating the motive only in its original form throughout the piece
  **Correction**: The germ generates varied forms; literal restatement is just one possibility

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Taking the mathematical analogies literally
  **Clarification**: "Smallest common multiple" and "greatest common factor" are metaphorical, illustrating the motive's dual role as source and subset

- **Confusion**: Believing the germ concept means the motive must appear literally in every passage
  **Clarification**: Its elements are present in derived forms; the connection may be indirect through chains of variation
