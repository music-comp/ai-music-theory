# Quick Definition

The technique of embellishing the principal (essential) tones of a theme with neighbouring tones, scale lines, or broken chords, creating the melodic content of a variation while preserving the theme's underlying structure.

# Core Definition

In the motives for at least the first few variations, it is common practice to circumscribe the principal tones with neighbouring tones. Often tones of the theme are included in parts of scale lines or broken chords. Such elements must fit the expressed harmony. The principal tones may appear at the same point in the figure (more primitive treatment) or be distributed more freely within the half-measure or measure (more fluent and varied treatment). The rhythmic displacement of principal tones relative to their position in the theme is a significant feature of this technique.

# Prerequisites

- **Production of the motive of variation**: The skeleton must be derived before circumscription can be applied

# Key Properties

- Principal tones of the theme are embellished with upper and lower neighbours
- Scale lines and broken chords may connect or surround principal tones
- All circumscribing elements must fit the expressed harmony
- Primitive treatment: principal tones always at the same point in the figure
- Sophisticated treatment: principal tones distributed freely, even rhythmically displaced
- The technique preserves the theme's tonal outline while adding melodic interest
- Also appears in thematic construction generally (e.g., Eroica scherzo)

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify the principal tones of the theme's skeleton
2. Add neighbouring tones, scale lines, or broken chords around each principal tone
3. Ensure all added tones fit the expressed harmony
4. Decide on the rhythmic placement of principal tones (fixed position = primitive; free = sophisticated)
5. Maintain consistent rhythmic organization throughout the variation

# Context & Application

Circumscription is the most fundamental technique of melodic variation. It operates at the intersection of the theme's skeleton and the motive of variation, generating the surface melody of each variation. The technique is not limited to variation sets -- the Eroica scherzo theme is described by Schoenberg as a circumscription of the ascending tonic and descending dominant arpeggios.

# Examples

- Beethoven, Diabelli Variations: Variations 2, 9, 11, 12, 14, 18, 28 derive the motive from circumscription of principal tones; observe rhythmic displacement
- Ex. 126b: Melody notes circumscribed with appoggiaturas and intervening sixteenth-notes; main notes always at the same point (primitive treatment)
- Ex. 126c: Greater fluency by avoiding mechanical reiteration; main notes freely distributed within the half-measure
- Ex. 126d: Even greater variety using notes of various lengths
- Beethoven, Eroica Scherzo (Ex. 121): Theme is a circumscription of ascending tonic and descending dominant

# Common Errors

- Adding circumscribing tones that do not fit the expressed harmony
- Always placing principal tones at the same point (produces mechanical, primitive results)
- Confusing circumscription with arbitrary ornamentation

# Common Confusions

- **Circumscription vs. ornamentation**: Circumscription is disciplined elaboration of specific principal tones; ornamentation is decorative addition without structural derivation.
- **Fixed vs. free placement**: Placing principal tones always at the same position in the figure is the more primitive approach; rhythmic displacement is the more sophisticated one.
