# Quick Definition
Melody written for the voice, governed by the physical constraints of the vocal instrument: register limitations, intonation requirements, and the principle that singability is the foundation of all melodiousness.

# Formal Definition
A melody could hardly include unmelodious elements; the concept of the melodious is intimately related to the concept of singability. The nature and technique of the primordial musical instrument, the voice, determines what is singable. Singability implies relatively long notes, smooth linkage of the registers, movement in waves (more stepwise than by leaps), avoidance of augmented and diminished intervals, adherence to the tonality and its most closely related regions, employment of the natural intervals of a key, gradual modulation, and a cautious use of dissonance. Additional restrictions derive from the registers of the voice and from the difficulties of intonation.

# Construction/Recognition
Write vocal melody observing: (1) relatively long notes allowing the voice to develop full sound, (2) predominantly stepwise motion with limited leaps, (3) smooth register connections, (4) avoidance of augmented and diminished intervals, (5) adherence to tonality, (6) gradual modulation, (7) cautious dissonance, (8) harmonic support for intonation especially at the beginning, (9) awareness of register capabilities.

# Musical Context
Vocal melody is the model from which all concepts of melodiousness derive. Prior to the nineteenth century, aestheticians could define melody in terms of beauty, expressiveness, simplicity, naturalness, and singability. The development of harmony since then has made earlier definitions inadequate. Composers from Wagner onward progressively expanded what the voice was asked to do, often going beyond traditional vocal limits for dramatic purposes.

# Examples
- Schubert's Winterreise Op. 89: unusually sudden and remote modulation (Ex. 73a), a leap of a tenth (Ex. 73b), and a realistic "Stormy Morning" so rapid it becomes difficult to sing (Ex. 73c)
- Brahms: range spanning an eleventh within two measures (Ex. 74a); syncopated rhythm obscuring phrasing (Ex. 74b); "The Forge" forcing staccato accentuation on the voice (Ex. 74c)
- Wagner (Ex. 78a): frequent use of highest register, sudden jumps to lowest register, difficult dissonant intervals
- Italian tradition: Rossini's rapid small notes (Ex. 81), Verdi's chromatic passages in Otello (Ex. 86)

# Common Confusions
- The limits of vocal melody have expanded historically; what was "unmelodious" to earlier aestheticians may be accepted today
- Imbalance, incoherence, and inadequate integration with harmony remain criteria of the unmelodious regardless of era
