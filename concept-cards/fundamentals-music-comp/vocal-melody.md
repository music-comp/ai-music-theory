# Quick Definition
Melody written for the voice, governed by the physical constraints of the vocal instrument: register limitations, intonation requirements, and the principle that singability is the foundation of all melodiousness.

# Core Definition
A melody could hardly include unmelodious elements; the concept of the melodious is intimately related to the concept of singability. The nature and technique of the primordial musical instrument, the voice, determines what is singable. The concept of the melodious in instrumental melody has developed as a free adaptation from the vocal model. Additional restrictions derive from the registers of the voice and from the difficulties of intonation. Instrumental melodies admit much more freedom, but the vocal model remains foundational.

# Key Properties
- Singability is the foundation of all melodiousness
- Vocal melody implies: relatively long notes, smooth register linkage, movement in waves (more stepwise than by leaps), avoidance of augmented and diminished intervals, adherence to tonality and closely related regions, natural key intervals, gradual modulation, cautious dissonance
- The voice needs minimal time to develop full sound; rapid notes and staccatos are difficult
- Sudden register changes endanger unity of tone quality
- The limits of vocal melody have expanded historically, especially since Wagner

# Construction / Recognition
Write vocal melody observing: (1) relatively long notes, (2) predominantly stepwise motion, (3) smooth register connections, (4) avoidance of augmented and diminished intervals, (5) adherence to tonality, (6) gradual modulation, (7) cautious dissonance, (8) harmonic support for intonation especially at the beginning, (9) awareness of register capabilities.

# Context & Application
Prior to the nineteenth century, aestheticians could define melody in terms of beauty, expressiveness, simplicity, naturalness, and singability. The development of harmony since then has made earlier definitions inadequate. Composers from Wagner onward progressively expanded what the voice was asked to do, often going beyond traditional vocal limits for dramatic purposes. Imbalance, incoherence, and inadequate integration with harmony remain criteria of the unmelodious regardless of era.

# Examples
- Schubert's Winterreise Op. 89: sudden remote modulation (Ex. 73a), a leap of a tenth (Ex. 73b), rapid tempo difficult to sing (Ex. 73c)
- Brahms: range spanning an eleventh within two measures (Ex. 74a); syncopated rhythm obscuring phrasing (Ex. 74b); "The Forge" forcing staccato on the voice (Ex. 74c)
- Wagner (Ex. 78a): frequent use of highest register, sudden jumps to lowest register, difficult dissonant intervals, a leap of a ninth at section ending
- Verdi in Otello (Ex. 86): chromatic scale and surprising metre change surpass Rossini in difficulty

# Common Errors
- Writing vocal melody without considering register constraints
- Providing insufficient harmonic support for intonation
- Using augmented or diminished intervals without harmonic justification

# Common Confusions
- The limits of vocal melody have expanded historically; what was "unmelodious" to earlier aestheticians may be accepted today
- Imbalance, incoherence, and inadequate integration with harmony remain criteria of the unmelodious regardless of era
