# Quick Definition
A theme resembles a scientific hypothesis that does not convince without tests and proof; it poses a problem that demands elaboration, development, and contrast as its consequences.

# Core Definition
A theme resembles rather a scientific hypothesis which does not convince without a number of tests, without presentation of proof. The formulation of a theme assumes that there will follow "adventures," "predicaments," which ask for solution, for elaboration, for development, for contrast. A theme is not independent or self-determined; it is strictly bound to consequences which have to be drawn, and without which it may appear insignificant. The harmony of a theme is often active, "roving," unstable, though even complex themes still move around a tonic or a definite contrasting region.

# Key Properties
- Three-dimensional: interval, harmony, and rhythmic development
- Sharpens or deepens its problem rather than resolving quickly
- Juxtaposes remotely coherent segments without connectives
- Leaps directly to remote developments of the basic motive
- Harmony is often "roving" and unstable
- Strictly bound to consequences; appears insignificant without development
- Despite complexity, still moves around a tonic or definite contrasting region
- Organization cannot be so loose that one feels a lack of structure

# Construction / Recognition
A theme is recognized by: (1) rhythmic development alongside intervallic and harmonic development, (2) juxtaposition of segments without smooth connectives, (3) remote motivic developments, (4) roving or unstable harmony, (5) the sense that something must follow, (6) a state of repose scarcely reached or attempted early.

# Context & Application
This concept is fundamental to understanding sonata form and other developmental forms. The theme is the catalyst for everything that follows in a development section. The "hypothesis" analogy means the theme makes a claim that the rest of the piece must substantiate.

# Examples
- Beethoven Op. 53-I: the theme's harmony is active and roving but still moves around a tonic
- Beethoven Op. 57-I: the theme poses a "problem" that the entire first movement must solve
- Beethoven Op. 10/1-I, m. 9, 17: the theme leaps directly to remote developments of the basic motive
- Brahms First Symphony-I, Piano Quartet Op. 60-I: complex themes with roving harmony still moving around a tonic

# Common Errors
- Treating a theme as a failed melody rather than a different structural type
- Creating a theme so loosely organized it lacks structure
- Neglecting the consequences a theme demands

# Common Confusions
- A theme is not a failed melody; it serves a fundamentally different structural purpose
- The "hypothesis" analogy means the theme makes a claim that must be substantiated, not that it is tentative
- "Theme" is one of the most misused terms in music; Schoenberg uses it for specific structures in sonatas and symphonies
