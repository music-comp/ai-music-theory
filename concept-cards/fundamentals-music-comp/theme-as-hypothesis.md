# Quick Definition
A theme resembles a scientific hypothesis that does not convince without tests and proof; it poses a problem that demands elaboration, development, and contrast as its consequences.

# Formal Definition
A theme resembles rather a scientific hypothesis which does not convince without a number of tests, without presentation of proof. The formulation of a theme assumes that there will follow "adventures," "predicaments," which ask for solution, for elaboration, for development, for contrast. A theme is not independent or self-determined; it is strictly bound to consequences which have to be drawn, and without which it may appear insignificant.

# Construction/Recognition
A theme is recognized by: (1) three-dimensionality (interval, harmony, and rhythmic development), (2) the tendency to sharpen or deepen its problem rather than resolve it quickly, (3) juxtaposition of remotely coherent segments without connectives, (4) "roving," unstable harmony, (5) leaping directly to remote developments of the basic motive, (6) the sense that something must follow for the passage to be complete.

# Musical Context
This concept is fundamental to understanding sonata form and other developmental forms. The theme is the catalyst for everything that follows in a development section. Despite remote harmonies, even complex themes still move around a tonic or a definite contrasting region; the organization cannot be so loose that one feels a lack of structure.

# Examples
- Beethoven Op. 53-I: the theme's harmony is active and roving but still moves around a tonic
- Beethoven Op. 57-I: the theme poses a "problem" that the entire first movement must solve
- Beethoven Op. 10/1-I, m. 9, 17: the theme leaps directly to remote developments of the basic motive
- Brahms First Symphony-I, Piano Quartet Op. 60-I: complex themes still moving around a tonic or definite contrasting region

# Common Confusions
- A theme is not a failed melody; it serves a fundamentally different structural purpose
- The "hypothesis" analogy means that a theme makes a claim that the rest of the piece must substantiate, not that it is tentative or uncertain
- "Theme" is one of the most misused terms in music; Schoenberg uses it for specific structures found in sonatas and symphonies
