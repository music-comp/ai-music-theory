# Quick Definition
In the antecedent of a period, measures 3-4 introduce more remote (contrasting) motive-forms rather than immediate repetition, providing the contrast that justifies the consequent's repetition of the entire antecedent.

# Formal Definition
Since the consequent will function as a modified repetition of the antecedent, the antecedent itself must be completed with more remote motive-forms in m. 3-4 -- forms that contrast with m. 1-2 while maintaining coherence. This distinguishes the antecedent from the opening of a sentence, where m. 3-4 are a modified repetition of m. 1-2. The degree of remoteness varies from minimal contrast (mere change of register, direction, or contour) to substantial rhythmic and intervallic transformation.

# Construction/Recognition
- Coherent contrast through increase of small notes (tendency of the smallest notes): Exs. 44a, b, c, d, g, k, 45a, b, c, d, and 46e
- Coherent contrast with minimal surface change (register, direction, contour): Exs. 44f, h, i, j, l, 45f, 46a, f, and 47b, c, d, e
- Coherent contrast through decrease of smaller notes (reduction): Exs. 43a, 45i, 46g
- When coherence between basic and remote motive-forms is not obvious, a connective may bridge the gap (Exs. 44l, 45c, d, g, 46c, 47d)
- Abrupt juxtaposition need not produce imbalance (Ex. 45a)
- Motive-forms built from broken chords assure sound relationship between melody and harmony

# Musical Context
The creation of appropriate remote motive-forms is the central compositional challenge of antecedent construction. The contrast must be sufficient to justify the upcoming repetition in the consequent, but coherent enough to maintain intelligibility. Continuous study of examples from musical literature is essential for mastering this balance.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3, Rondo: Chain-like construction in m. 3-4 where motive-forms overlap like links of a chain
- Exs. 44f (Haydn): Even with continuous sixteenth-notes, the figuration and harmony of m. 3-4 are clearly differentiated from m. 1-2
- Mozart examples (Exs. 45a, f): Extreme rhythmical contrast between first and second phrases
- Mozart (Exs. 45b, g): Co-ordination of small notes with harmony as ancillary notes achieves exceptional perfection

# Common Confusions
- Remote motive-forms are not random contrasting material; they must derive from the basic motive through recognizable (if distant) transformation.
- Too many small notes in m. 3-4 may produce a crowded effect, even though the tendency of the smallest notes encourages their increase.
- The degree of contrast achievable in m. 3-4 depends on the student's ability; Mozart's perfection in coordinating small notes with harmony is not expected of beginners.
