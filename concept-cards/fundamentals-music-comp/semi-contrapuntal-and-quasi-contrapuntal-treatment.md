# Quick Definition
Semi-counterpoint involves free melodic movement of accompanying voices with motival and thematic implications, while quasi-counterpoint merely embellishes, melodizes, and vitalizes otherwise unimportant harmonic voices without genuine thematic significance.

# Formal Definition
Semi-counterpoint has motival and even thematic implications but is not based on combinations such as multiple counterpoint, canonic imitations, etc. -- only on a free melodic movement of one or more voices. Quasi-counterpoint is often little more than a way of embellishing, melodizing, and vitalizing otherwise unimportant voices in the harmony. In real homophonic music there is always one main voice; the addition of imitations, canonic or free, is principally a method of accompanying this main voice. The distinction lies in the degree of thematic significance: semi-counterpoint carries motivic weight, while quasi-counterpoint serves purely textural purposes.

# Construction/Recognition
- Semi-contrapuntal: free melodic movement with motival implications (Op. 2/2-I, m. 11-16 and imitations in m. 32 ff.; Op. 2/3-IV, m. 57 ff.)
- Quasi-contrapuntal: embellishing sustained tones or imitating only phrasing, not thematic content (Op. 14/2-I, m. 49 ff.; Op. 31/1-II, m. 16 ff.; Op. 28-IV, m. 28-31 and 33-35)
- Semi-contrapuntal countermelody: an added melodic line (Op. 26-II, m. 45 ff.; Op. 28-I, m. 183 ff.; Op. 31/1-III, m. 17 ff.)
- Countermelodic phrases as bridge-like fillers between phrases of the principal voice (Op. 13-II, m. 37 ff.)
- Countermelodies embellishing a repetition (Op. 10/3-II: compare m. 17-19 with 21-23)
- Ostinatos may be understood as semi-contrapuntal (Brahms, First Symphony-IV; Finale of Variations on a Theme of Haydn)

# Musical Context
Both techniques occupy the middle ground between pure homophony and real counterpoint. They vitalize the texture without requiring the formal rigor of fugal or canonic writing. Both are illustrated in Beethoven, Op. 10/1-I: in m. 48-55, both upper voices are so well organized that it is difficult to determine which is main voice and which is countermelody (semi-contrapuntal); in m. 56-85, the bass moves more melodically than harmony requires without becoming a true countermelody (quasi-contrapuntal).

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 10/1-I, m. 48-55: Semi-contrapuntal -- both upper voices equally well organized
- Beethoven, Op. 10/1-I, m. 56-85: Quasi-contrapuntal -- bass moves more melodically than necessary
- Beethoven, Op. 14/2-I, m. 49 ff.: Quasi-contrapuntal -- lowest voice imitates only the phrasing of the melody
- Beethoven, Op. 31/1-II, m. 16 ff.: Auxiliaries disguised as imitations, embellishing sustained harmony
- Beethoven, Op. 28-IV, m. 28-31, 33-35: Definitely not contrapuntal since they present only one harmony
- Exs. 54, 55, 56: Illustrations of semi-contrapuntal treatment with characteristic rhythmic figures from imitation

# Common Confusions
- Semi-counterpoint and quasi-counterpoint are distinct: the former has motivic/thematic implications, while the latter is purely ornamental.
- Imitations in quasi-counterpoint may look like contrapuntal devices but serve an entirely different purpose (decoration vs. thematic development).
- Not all melodic bass movement is contrapuntal; it may be quasi-contrapuntal if it lacks genuine thematic independence.
- Adding a countermelody to a repetition is a form of semi-contrapuntal variation, not true contrapuntal writing.
