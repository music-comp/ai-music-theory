# Quick Definition
Melodies characteristically proceed in waves -- moving upward, then downward, seldom continuing long in one direction -- with varying amplitudes, as demonstrated by tonal contour graphs.

# Formal Definition
Melodies proceed in waves, a fact readily observed in tonal contour analysis. The amplitude of these waves varies. A melody seldom moves long in one direction. Though the student was advised to avoid repeating the culmination point (the climax), contour graphs show that many good melodies do repeat it. However, Schoenberg advises the student not to attempt this.

# Construction/Recognition
Examine the tonal contour of a melody by graphing its pitch over time. Look for: (1) wave-like alternation of ascending and descending motion, (2) varying amplitude, (3) limited duration of movement in any single direction, (4) placement of the climax (usually toward the end), (5) whether the culmination point is repeated. The wave pattern is a fundamental characteristic of well-constructed melody.

# Musical Context
This principle applies across styles and periods. Contour analysis of melodies by Bach, Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven consistently reveals wave-like motion. The principle connects to the broader ideal of balance in melody: a line that rises must eventually fall, creating the self-regulating quality that makes melody self-contained.

# Common Confusions
- Wave motion does not mean mechanical alternation; the waves are irregular and varied in amplitude
- Repeating the climax point is observed in good melodies but remains risky for students; Schoenberg advises against attempting it
