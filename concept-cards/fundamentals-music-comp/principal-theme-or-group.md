# Quick Definition
The opening thematic material of a sonata-allegro exposition, varying from a simple period or sentence to a group of distinct themes -- nearly always more thematic than melodic, with flexible and irregular internal organization forecasting the metamorphoses to come.

# Core Definition
The structure may vary widely -- from a straightforward period or sentence, through something resembling a ternary form, to a group of distinct themes joined in the most subtle fashion. Often a return to the A-theme merges into a transition. The ideas of the principal group are nearly always more thematic than melodic and the internal organization tends to be flexible and irregular, forecasting the metamorphoses to which the ideas will later be subjected. If the structure is too symmetrical, subsequent freedom of articulation may be impeded. A striking, motto-like beginning is a useful feature.

# Prerequisites
- Sonata-allegro form
- Sentence and period construction
- Motive and motive-forms

# Key Properties
- Varies from simple sentence/period to a group of distinct themes
- More thematic than melodic (contrast with lyric subordinate theme)
- Internal organization: flexible and irregular
- Too much symmetry impedes subsequent freedom of articulation
- A striking, motto-like beginning is useful
- The return of A may merge into the transition
- Free articulation: irregular phrase lengths, multiple distinct motive formulations
- Connective possibilities vary: strong cadential isolation or complete merger without break

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the opening thematic material in the tonic region
2. Note whether the structure is a sentence, period, ternary form, or group of themes
3. Look for flexible, irregular internal organization
4. Check for a motto-like beginning
5. Note the variety of motive formulations and their articulation
6. Identify where the principal theme ends and the transition begins (may merge)

# Context & Application
The principal theme establishes the basic motives that will be developed throughout the movement. Its flexible construction is essential for providing material amenable to elaboration. A feeling for the full flexibility of the form can only be gained by thorough study of a great many examples from many composers.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: simple sentence of eight measures; transposition provides transition material
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I: two distinct ideas (motto-like broken chords and lyrical continuation m. 9); repeated in shortened form (m. 21)
- Beethoven, Op. 7-I: motto-like beginning (m. 1-4), series of two-measure phrases, overlapping varied repetition, extension through passage work
- Beethoven, Op. 14/1-I: twelve measures, but may be considered a prototype of the "group" with four distinct motive formulations
- Beethoven, Op. 28-I: thirty-nine measures above a tonic pedal point, with ten-measure theme repeated an octave higher and continued
- Motto-like beginnings: Op. 2/2-I, Op. 7-I, Op. 10/1-I, Op. 10/2-I, Op. 22-I, Op. 95-I

# Common Errors
- Making the principal theme too symmetrical, impeding subsequent developmental freedom
- Creating a principal theme that is too melodic and too regular for elaboration purposes
- Failing to include a sufficient variety of motive formulations

# Common Confusions
- "Thematic" vs. "melodic" is a crucial distinction; principal themes are built for development, not for singing
- The principal theme may be a single sentence or a complex group; neither extreme is inherently superior
- The boundary between principal theme and transition may be unclear when the A-theme return merges into transitional material
