# Quick Definition
The bass line is a "second melody" subject to many of the same requirements as the principal melody: rhythmic balance, variety of contour, avoidance of monotonous repetitions, and effective use of inversions and chromatic progression.

# Formal Definition
The bass was previously described as a "second melody." This means that it is subject to somewhat the same requirements as the principal melody. It should be rhythmically balanced, should avoid the monotony of unnecessary repetitions, should have some variety of contour, and should make full use of inversions (especially of seventh chords). Chromatic progression can be as melodically advantageous in the bass as in the principal melody. Semi- and quasi-counterpoint help to make the bass interesting.

# Construction/Recognition
Evaluate the bass line by: (1) playing it separately from the melody and harmony, (2) checking for rhythmic balance, (3) identifying unnecessary repetitions, (4) evaluating the variety of its contour, (5) checking whether inversions (especially seventh chord inversions) are used effectively, (6) looking for opportunities for chromatic movement, (7) assessing whether semi-contrapuntal techniques are employed.

# Musical Context
The bass line is often neglected by beginners who focus exclusively on the melody and harmonic progression. Treating the bass as a "second melody" elevates its importance and leads to richer, more satisfying textures. This principle applies at all levels of composition.

- Exs. 44g, i, j, 48a, and 58g: examples of semi- and quasi-counterpoint that help make the bass interesting
- Watch the harmony, watch the root progressions, watch the bass line -- Schoenberg's final admonition combines all three concerns

# Common Confusions
- The bass is not merely the lowest note of each chord; it is a melodic line in its own right
- "Second melody" does not mean the bass should always be tuneful; it means it should be crafted with the same care as the principal melody
