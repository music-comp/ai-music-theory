# Quick Definition
A melody is independent and self-determined, requiring no addition, continuation, or elaboration -- in contrast to a theme, which is strictly bound to the consequences that must follow it.

# Formal Definition
All the restrictions and limitations of melody -- its tendency toward regularity, simple repetitions, symmetry, close harmonic relationships, continuation rather than elaboration, and slightly varied motive-forms -- produce that independence and self-determination because of which a melody requires no addition, continuation, or elaboration. A theme, on the contrary, is not independent and self-determined; it is strictly bound to consequences which have to be drawn, and without which it may appear insignificant.

# Construction/Recognition
A melody is independent when it: (1) achieves balance without needing further development, (2) contains within itself all the interest and variety it needs, (3) could stand alone without any subsequent elaboration or development. A theme, by contrast, feels incomplete or insignificant if not followed by development.

# Musical Context
This distinction has profound implications for form. Melodies tend to appear in forms that do not require extensive development (songs, ternary forms, rondos). Themes tend to appear in forms that demand development (sonata-allegro form). The melody's condensed, self-sufficient nature is aesthetically valuable: "there is no nobler contrast than to speak lightly of a great grief."

# Examples
- Beethoven String Quartet Op. 132-III (Ex. 96a, b): themes that nonetheless make the impression of melodies due to their self-sufficient quality
- Contrast with Beethoven Op. 53-I, Op. 57-I: themes that feel incomplete without their subsequent development
- Heine's verse "Aus meinen grossen Schmerzen / Mach ich die kleinen Lieder" illustrates the melodic ideal of condensation

# Common Confusions
- Independence does not mean simplicity; a melody can be harmonically and rhythmically sophisticated while still being self-contained
- A theme that "appears insignificant" without development is not a failure of the theme; it is the nature of thematic writing to require continuation
