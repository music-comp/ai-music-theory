# Quick Definition
A melody is independent and self-determined, requiring no addition, continuation, or elaboration -- in contrast to a theme, which is strictly bound to the consequences that must follow it.

# Core Definition
All the restrictions and limitations of melody -- its tendency toward regularity, simple repetitions, symmetry, close harmonic relationships, continuation rather than elaboration, and slightly varied motive-forms -- produce that independence and self-determination because of which a melody requires no addition, continuation, or elaboration. A theme, on the contrary, is not independent and self-determined; it is strictly bound to consequences which have to be drawn, and without which it may appear insignificant.

# Key Properties
- Melodic independence arises from: regularity, simple repetitions, symmetry, close harmony, continuation, slightly varied motive-forms
- A melody needs nothing beyond itself; it is complete
- A theme without its consequences may appear insignificant
- The melody's condensed, self-sufficient nature is aesthetically valuable
- Independence is produced by limitation, not by freedom

# Construction / Recognition
A melody is independent when it: (1) achieves balance without needing further development, (2) contains within itself all the interest and variety it needs, (3) could stand alone without any subsequent elaboration. A theme feels incomplete or insignificant without development.

# Context & Application
This distinction has profound implications for form. Melodies tend to appear in forms not requiring extensive development (songs, ternary forms, rondos). Themes tend to appear in forms demanding development (sonata-allegro). The melody's condensed, self-sufficient nature is aesthetically valuable: "there is no nobler contrast than to speak lightly of a great grief."

# Examples
- Beethoven String Quartet Op. 132-III (Exs. 96a, b): themes that make the impression of melodies due to self-sufficient quality
- Contrast with Beethoven Op. 53-I, Op. 57-I: themes that feel incomplete without subsequent development
- Heine's "Aus meinen grossen Schmerzen / Mach ich die kleinen Lieder" illustrates the melodic ideal of condensation

# Common Errors
- Assuming independence means simplicity; a melody can be harmonically and rhythmically sophisticated while self-contained
- Adding unnecessary development to a passage that is already complete as melody
- Treating a theme's apparent insignificance (without development) as a defect

# Common Confusions
- Independence does not mean simplicity; a melody can be sophisticated while self-contained
- A theme that "appears insignificant" without development is not a failure; it is the nature of thematic writing to require continuation
