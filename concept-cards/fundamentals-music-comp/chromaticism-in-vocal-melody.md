# Quick Definition
Chromatic movement in vocal lines presents intonation challenges because natural semitones differ in size from tempered ones, but can be used sparingly when the harmony supports comprehension.

# Formal Definition
Chromaticism is difficult in vocal melody partly because the natural semitones differ in size from the tempered ones -- a fact which causes choirs to "get off pitch." Sparingly used, chromaticism need not be avoided. Chromatic notes functioning as circumscribing or embellishing notes can easily be understood. However, more complex chromatic passages require highly developed technical skill, and some chromatic usages are difficult to justify musically.

# Construction/Recognition
When using chromaticism in vocal melody: (1) ensure chromatic tones function as passing, neighboring, or embellishing notes that can be understood in context, (2) avoid extended or harmonically unsupported chromatic passages, (3) be aware that choirs are particularly susceptible to pitch drift from chromaticism, (4) reserve complex chromaticism for highly skilled singers.

# Musical Context
The development of harmony from the Wagnerian and post-Wagnerian epoch expanded the use of chromaticism in melody. The ear of the modern musician gradually acquired the capacity to comprehend remote harmonies as coherent elements of a tonality. To composers of this epoch, the use of chromatic intervals and progressions in melodies became as natural as scalewise movement was to their predecessors.

# Common Confusions
- The problem with chromaticism in vocal melody is not aesthetic but practical: the voice lacks fixed pitch references and must rely on harmonic context
- Tempered and natural semitones differ in size, making even simple chromatic passages an intonation challenge for singers
