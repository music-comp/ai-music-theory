# Quick Definition

The pedagogical practice of basing phrases on clear harmonic skeletons, beginning with simple progressions (especially I and V) that express the key clearly, before adding melodic elaboration.

# Core Definition

"When the melodic idea consists entirely or largely of notes outlining a single harmony, or a simple succession of harmonies, there is little difficulty in determining and expressing the harmonic implications" (Ch. 2, p. 3). With such a clear harmonic skeleton, "even rather elaborate melodic ideas can be readily related to their inherent harmony." For opening phrases, I and V are especially useful since they express the key most clearly. Almost any simple harmonic progression can serve as the basis.

# Prerequisites

- **phrase** -- The harmonic simplification method is applied to the construction of phrases

# Key Properties

1. Phrases should begin from a clear harmonic basis (the "harmonic skeleton")
2. I and V are especially useful for opening phrases as they express the key most clearly
3. The method proceeds in graduated stages from simpler to more complex elaboration
4. Non-chordal notes contribute fluency but must not obscure or contradict the harmony
5. The danger of over-elaboration is real: too many small notes can overburden the melody
6. Simple does not mean uninteresting -- considerable melodic variety is possible over a single harmony

# Construction / Recognition

## Schoenberg's Graduated Practice Method

1. **Stage 1**: Begin with a single harmony (e.g., tonic of F major) and create phrases using only chord tones (Ex. 5)
2. **Stage 2**: Introduce different note values for rhythmic variety while remaining on chord tones (Exs. 6-7)
3. **Stage 3**: Add simple non-chordal notes (passing notes, note repetitions) for fluency (Exs. 8-9)
4. **Stage 4**: Progress to more elaborate embellishments (appoggiaturas, changing notes) only after mastering simpler levels (Exs. 10-11)

## To Recognize the Principle

1. Identify whether a melody can be reduced to a simple underlying harmonic progression
2. Check if non-chordal notes resolve properly and do not contradict the harmony
3. Assess whether the harmonic skeleton remains audible beneath the elaboration

# Context & Application

This graduated approach is central to Schoenberg's pedagogy. He recommends that students "stimulate their inventive faculties by making a great many sketches of phrases based on a predetermined harmony." He acknowledges that "at first, such attempts may be stiff and awkward, but, with patience, the co-ordination of the various elements will rapidly become smoother, until real fluency and even expressiveness is attained" (Ch. 2, p. 4). The method is pedagogical in nature, not a compositional rule -- actual compositions may use complex harmonies.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Beginning composition with complex harmonies before mastering simple ones
  **Correction**: Follow the graduated stages -- master chord-tone melodies before adding embellishment

- **Error**: Adding too many small notes that "overburden the melody" and "obscure the harmony"
  **Correction**: Non-chordal notes should contribute fluency without contradicting the underlying harmony

- **Error**: Inventing melody without awareness of its harmonic implications
  **Correction**: "The composer should never invent a melody without being conscious of its harmony" (Ch. 2, p. 3)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Assuming harmonic simplification is a compositional rule rather than a pedagogical method
  **Clarification**: It is a practice method; actual compositions may use complex harmonies freely

- **Confusion**: Thinking "simple" means "uninteresting"
  **Clarification**: Considerable melodic variety is possible over a single harmony, as shown in Exs. 5-7

- **Confusion**: Treating non-chordal notes as inherently problematic
  **Clarification**: Non-chordal notes contribute fluency and interest -- the issue is only excess or misuse
