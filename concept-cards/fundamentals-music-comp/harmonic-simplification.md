# Quick Definition
The practice of basing phrases on clear harmonic skeletons, beginning with simple progressions (especially I and V) that express the key clearly, before adding melodic elaboration.

# Formal Definition
When the melodic idea consists entirely or largely of notes outlining a single harmony, or a simple succession of harmonies, there is little difficulty in determining and expressing the harmonic implications. With such a clear harmonic skeleton, even rather elaborate melodic ideas can be readily related to their inherent harmony. For opening phrases, I and V are especially useful since they express the key most clearly. Almost any simple harmonic progression can be used as a basis.

# Construction/Recognition
The practice method Schoenberg prescribes:
1. Begin with a single harmony (e.g., tonic of F major) and create phrases using only chord tones (Ex. 5)
2. Introduce different note values for rhythmic variety while remaining on chord tones (Exs. 6-7)
3. Add simple non-chordal notes (passing notes, note repetitions) for fluency (Exs. 8-9)
4. Progress to more elaborate embellishments (appoggiaturas, changing notes) only after mastering simpler levels (Exs. 10-11)

# Musical Context
This graduated approach to phrase construction is central to Schoenberg's pedagogy. Students should stimulate their inventive faculties by making many sketches at each level. "At first, such attempts may be stiff and awkward, but, with patience, the co-ordination of the various elements will rapidly become smoother, until real fluency and even expressiveness is attained."

# Examples
- Ex. 5: contours created by various arrangements of chord tones on a single harmony

- Ex. 7: combining different note values while confined to chord tones
- Exs. 8-9: adding passing notes to the basic chord-tone phrases
- Exs. 10-11: elaborate embellishments that risk obscuring the harmony

# Common Confusions
- Harmonic simplification is a pedagogical method, not a compositional rule; actual compositions may use complex harmonies
- "Simple" does not mean "uninteresting"; considerable melodic variety is possible over a single harmony
- The danger of over-elaboration is real: too many small notes can "overburden the melody" and "obscure the harmony"
