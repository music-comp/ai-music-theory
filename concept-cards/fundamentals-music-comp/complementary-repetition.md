# Quick Definition
The technique of repeating the opening phrase of a sentence with a contrasting harmonic formulation (dominant form answering tonic form), analogous to the dux-comes relationship in fugue, providing variety within unity.

# Formal Definition
Complementary repetition refers to the relationship between the tonic form and dominant form of a sentence's opening, modeled on the dux (leader) and comes (follower) of fugal practice. Through its slightly contrasting formulation, this kind of repetition provides variety in unity. The rhythm and contour of the melody are preserved in the repetition; an element of contrast enters through the changed harmony and the necessary adaptation of the melody.

# Construction/Recognition
To create a complementary repetition:
1. Compose a tonic form (mm. 1-2) based on simple harmonic progressions (I, I-V, I-V-I, I-IV, or I-II)
2. Repeat the melodic rhythm and contour in mm. 3-4
3. Shift the harmonic basis to the dominant area according to the correspondence patterns
4. Adapt the melody only enough to conform with the changed harmony

The melody in the dominant form may follow the contour exactly (Ex. 40a), or treat the contour more freely while preserving rhythm (Exs. 40b, c). More extensive melodic variation is possible (Ex. 41) but should not obscure the repetition.

# Musical Context
Complementary repetition is one of the most characteristic features of classical sentence construction. It achieves the ideal balance between the need for repetition (intelligibility) and the need for variety (avoiding monotony) by changing the harmonic dimension while preserving the melodic-rhythmic dimension.

# Examples
- Ex. 40a: dominant form follows contour of tonic form exactly
- Exs. 40b, c: other dominant forms preserving rhythm while treating contour more freely
- Ex. 41: dominant forms varied more than the harmonic change strictly requires
- Exs. 41b, c: tonic forms based on four harmonies, making true answer difficult -- only main harmonies (I-V) answered with V-I

# Common Confusions
- Complementary repetition is not literal transposition; it involves harmonic reinterpretation with melodic adaptation
- The analogy to fugal dux/comes is structural, not procedural; sentence construction is homophonic, not contrapuntal
- A tonic form with too many harmonies cannot be answered literally; only the main harmonies are reflected in the dominant form
- The degree of melodic variation in the dominant form is flexible, but the rhythmic profile should be preserved
