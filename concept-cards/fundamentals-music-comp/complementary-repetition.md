# Quick Definition

The technique of repeating the opening phrase of a sentence with a contrasting harmonic formulation (dominant form answering tonic form), analogous to the dux-comes relationship in fugue, providing variety in unity by preserving rhythm and contour while changing the harmonic basis.

# Core Definition

"In many classical examples one finds a relationship between first and second phrase similar to that of dux (tonic form) and comes (dominant form) in the fugue. This kind of repetition, through its slightly contrasting formulation, provides variety in unity. In the repetition, the rhythm and contour of the melody are preserved. An element of contrast enters through the changed harmony and the necessary adaptation of the melody" (Ch. 5, pp. 21-22).

# Key Properties

1. Based on the dux (leader) / comes (follower) relationship from fugal practice
2. Provides "variety in unity" through contrasting harmonic formulation
3. Rhythm and contour of the melody are preserved in the repetition
4. Contrast enters through changed harmony and melodic adaptation
5. The melody adapts only enough to conform with the changed harmony
6. More extensive melodic variation is possible but should not obscure the repetition

# Construction / Recognition

## To Create Complementary Repetition

1. Compose a tonic form (mm. 1-2) based on simple progressions (I, I-V, I-V-I, I-IV, or I-II)
2. Preserve the melodic rhythm and contour in mm. 3-4
3. Shift the harmonic basis to the dominant area following the correspondence patterns:

| Tonic Form | Dominant Form |
|------------|---------------|
| I          | V             |
| I-V        | V-I           |
| I-V-I      | V-I-V         |
| I-IV       | V-I           |
| I-II       | V-I           |

4. Adapt the melody only enough to conform with the changed harmony
5. The melody may follow the contour exactly (Ex. 40a) or more freely (Exs. 40b, c)

## Important Guideline

"To answer literally a tonic form with too many harmonies is impracticable. Here, only the main harmonies, I-V, could be answered, with V-I" (Ch. 5, p. 22).

# Context & Application

Complementary repetition is one of the most characteristic features of classical sentence construction. It achieves the ideal balance between repetition (intelligibility) and variety (avoiding monotony) by changing the harmonic dimension while preserving the melodic-rhythmic dimension. Schoenberg notes that even in short passages, "a definite and regular accompaniment is employed to animate the harmony and to express a specific character" (Ch. 5, p. 22).

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 21-22, Exs. 35a, b, 53b): First phrase uses only I; second phrase uses only V.

**Example 2** (p. 21, Exs. 52b, c): I-V answered by V-I. Also Beethoven Op. 31/2-III, String Quartets Op. 59/2-III and Op. 131-IV.

**Example 3** (p. 21, Ex. 36): Tonic form I-V-I answered by dominant form V-I-V.

**Example 4** (pp. 21-22, Ex. 40a): Dominant form follows contour of tonic form exactly.

**Example 5** (p. 22, Exs. 40b, c): Dominant forms preserving rhythm while treating contour more freely.

**Example 6** (p. 22, Ex. 41): Dominant forms "varied more than the harmonic change requires."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Answering a harmonically complex tonic form literally
  **Correction**: "To answer literally a tonic form with too many harmonies is impracticable" -- answer only the main harmonies

- **Error**: Varying the dominant form more than necessary
  **Correction**: The melody should adapt "only enough to conform with the harmony" unless deliberate additional variation is intended

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking complementary repetition is literal transposition
  **Clarification**: It involves harmonic reinterpretation with melodic adaptation, not mechanical transposition

- **Confusion**: Equating the fugal analogy with fugal procedure
  **Clarification**: The dux/comes analogy is structural, not procedural; sentence construction is homophonic, not contrapuntal

- **Confusion**: Believing "dominant form" means the phrase is in the dominant key
  **Clarification**: "Dominant form" means the phrase uses dominant-area harmony within the tonic key
