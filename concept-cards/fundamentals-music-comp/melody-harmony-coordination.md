# Quick Definition
The principle that in homophonic-harmonic music the essential content is concentrated in the principal voice, which implies an inherent harmony, and that the composer should never invent a melody without being conscious of its harmony.

# Formal Definition
In homophonic-harmonic music, the essential content is concentrated in one voice, the principal voice, which implies an inherent harmony. The mutual accommodation of melody and harmony is difficult at first, but the composer should never invent a melody without being conscious of its harmony. When the melodic idea consists entirely or largely of notes outlining a single harmony or a simple succession of harmonies, there is little difficulty in determining and expressing the harmonic implications.

# Construction/Recognition
Begin with a clear harmonic skeleton -- even elaborate melodic ideas can be readily related to their inherent harmony when the skeleton is clear. For opening phrases, I and V are especially useful since they express the key most clearly. Almost any simple harmonic progression can serve as a basis. The relationship between melody and harmony can be observed at several levels of elaboration.

# Musical Context
This principle is foundational to Schoenberg's approach to phrase construction. He recommends that students practice by making many sketches of phrases based on predetermined harmony, beginning with phrases that outline a single chord and gradually adding non-chordal notes. The coordination of melodic, rhythmic, and harmonic factors is a skill that develops with practice.

# Common Confusions
- Melody does not exist independently of harmony in homophonic music; every melody implies a harmonic framework
- Being "conscious of harmony" does not mean the harmony must be simple -- it means the composer must understand the harmonic implications of the melodic line
- Elaborate embellishments can obscure the harmony if not carefully handled
