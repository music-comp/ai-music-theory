# Quick Definition
Modifications to the melody in recapitulations, including ornamental elaboration, register transfer, contrapuntal additions, and even complete reconstruction at cadential points -- all constrained by the need to preserve the melodic contour for recognition.

# Core Definition
The contour of the melody should not be entirely changed, except at points where essential structural changes occur. Ornamental variation -- breaking down longer notes through the use of passing notes, circumscribing notes, tone repetitions -- need not change the contour essentially. Excessive rhythmic changes or shifts of accent are likely to prevent recognition. A changed cadence may require considerable reformulation, or even complete reconstruction. The melodic outline and thematic structure are usually preserved.

# Key Properties
- Melodic contour should be preserved for recognizability
- Ornamental elaboration (passing notes, circumscription, tone repetition) is safe
- Excessive rhythmic changes or accent shifts risk preventing recognition
- Register transfer (octave higher) creates climactic effect
- Voice transfer (melody to different voice) creates textural variety
- Changed cadences may require free reconstruction
- Accompaniment variation should be less extensive than melodic variation
- Contrapuntal additions (imitation, counter-melody) are semi-contrapuntal in nature

# Construction / Recognition
1. Check whether the melodic contour is preserved despite surface changes
2. Identify ornamental elaboration that maintains contour
3. Note register changes (octave displacement for climactic effect)
4. Look for voice transfers (melody reassigned to different voice)
5. Identify cadential points where reconstruction is needed
6. Note contrapuntal additions in other voices

# Context & Application
The tension between recognition and variation is the central artistic challenge of the recapitulation. Too little variation produces monotony; too much prevents the listener from recognizing the return. Contour preservation is the primary constraint, while ornamental, registral, and textural changes provide freshness. The principles apply across all recapitulation contexts -- small ternary, rondo, and sonata-allegro.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: upbeat variations (m. 41, 53, 100, 104, 112, 135); melodic embellishments (m. 43, 102, 108, 137)
- Beethoven, Op. 7-IV (m. 143): theme an octave higher, varied with syncopations and inserted chromatic tones
- Beethoven, Op. 22-IV (m. 112): quasi-contrapuntal interchange of voices; last repetition (m. 165) has triplets against duple accompaniment
- Beethoven, Op. 31/1-III: main melody transferred to tenor voice with new quasi-melodic accompaniment in right hand
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-IV: theme varied by interpolation of imitations (m. 57) and figurations (m. 85)

# Common Errors
- Changing the melodic contour so drastically that the return is unrecognizable
- Shifting rhythmic accents, which prevents recognition more readily than pitch changes
- Making accompaniment changes more elaborate than melodic changes

# Common Confusions
- Contour preservation is the primary goal; ornamental changes that maintain contour are safe, while rhythmic changes altering the perceived pattern are risky
- Complete reconstruction is acceptable at cadential points where the harmonic destination has changed
- Transferring the melody to another voice is a form of variation, not a departure from the recapitulation
- Contrapuntal additions are often semi-contrapuntal rather than true counterpoint based on invertible combinations
