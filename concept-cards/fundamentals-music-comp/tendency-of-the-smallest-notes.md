# Quick Definition
A compositional principle observing that the smallest note values in any segment of a piece tend to generate increasing numbers of similarly small notes in subsequent passages, comparable to the momentum of acceleration in a falling body.

# Formal Definition
The tendency of the smallest notes is Schoenberg's observation that the smallest note values in any segment of a piece -- even in a motive or motive-form -- exert an influence on the continuation comparable to the momentum of acceleration in a falling body: the longer the movement lasts, the faster it becomes. Thus, if in the beginning only one sixteenth-note is used, very soon an increasing number will appear, growing often into whole passages of sixteenths. To restrict this tendency requires specified care.

# Construction/Recognition
- If the opening motive contains one sixteenth-note, expect increasing numbers in the continuation
- The tendency manifests as an almost natural acceleration of rhythmic activity
- One may acquiesce to this tendency or deliberately counteract it
- Too many small notes may produce a crowded effect
- The tendency can be observed in m. 3-4 of antecedents as a form of coherent contrast
- Decrease of small notes can serve as a deliberate counterbalance, often used to mark the caesura

# Musical Context
This principle operates at multiple structural levels. In antecedent construction, the tendency explains the common increase of rhythmic activity in m. 3-4 (Exs. 44a, b, c, d, g, k, 45a, b, c, d, 46e). In the consequent, the cadence contour may either follow the tendency (accelerando effect) or contradict it (ritardando effect). The principle also explains why Brahms's Ex. 51f shows a variant in m. 5 where a rhythmic figure shifts from the second beat to the first, producing near-continuous eighth-note flow.

# Examples
- Exs. 44a, b, c, d, g, k, 45a, b, c, d, and 46e: Increase of small notes visible in m. 3 (and sometimes m. 4) of antecedents
- Ex. 51f (Brahms): Rhythmic figure 'a' shifted from second beat to first beat in m. 5, resulting in almost continuous eighth-note flow
- Counterexamples showing decrease: Exs. 43a, 45i, 46g where motive-forms appear as reductions

# Common Confusions
- The tendency is not a rule to be slavishly followed; it is an observation about natural momentum that composers must consciously manage.
- Restricting the tendency is not a weakness but a compositional skill -- unchecked acceleration leads to overcrowding.
- The tendency applies to all segments, not just the antecedent; it influences continuation at every structural level.
