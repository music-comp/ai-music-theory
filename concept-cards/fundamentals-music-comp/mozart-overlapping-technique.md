# Quick Definition

A characteristic technique of Mozart in which segments overlap by dovetailing, so that the last measure of one segment coincides with the first measure of the next, compensating for extensions in phrase length with economical re-entry.

# Core Definition

"This coda offers an opportunity to discuss a technique characteristic of Mozart, the technique of overlapping joints which resembles dovetailing" (Ch. 18, p. 106). "It is often a result of extending, for instance, a segment of four measures to five, or even six." Mozart's "economical mind compensates for the extension by entering one measure early with the repetition" (Ch. 18, p. 107). Thus the end of one segment and the beginning of the next share a common measure. The structural length of each segment is one measure longer than the actual number of measures it occupies.

# Key Properties

1. A segment is extended beyond its expected length (e.g., four measures extended to five or six)
2. The repetition or next segment enters one measure early, overlapping with the final measure of the preceding segment
3. The shared measure functions simultaneously as the conclusion of one idea and the beginning of the next
4. Multiple successive segments may all overlap in this manner
5. The structural length of each segment is one measure longer than the actual number of measures it occupies
6. The technique reflects Mozart's characteristic economy of means
7. It contributes to seamless formal articulation by masking sectional divisions
8. The overlap could easily be avoided by bringing each segment to a definite end, but Mozart preferred the dovetailed construction

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct Overlapping Joints

1. Create a thematic segment of a given length
2. Extend it by one or two measures beyond the expected endpoint
3. Begin the next segment (or its repetition) one measure before the extended segment concludes
4. Ensure the shared measure can function both as conclusion and beginning
5. Apply the technique to successive segments for continuous flow
6. Use progressive reduction of segment lengths as the passage proceeds

## To Recognize Overlapping Joints

1. Identify a point where a new segment begins before the previous one has formally concluded
2. Confirm that the shared measure functions in both roles (ending and beginning)
3. Calculate the structural length (longer) versus the sounding length (shorter by one measure)
4. Look for multiple successive overlaps creating a continuous chain
5. Check whether the passage occurs in a coda or closing section

# Context & Application

This technique is particularly evident in Mozart's codas and closing sections, where multiple overlapping segments create a continuous flow that masks the sectional divisions. Schoenberg demonstrates that the overlap is a deliberate choice: "It is evident that the six measures could easily be brought to a definite end in the fourth measure and, instead of overlapping, the repetition could begin a measure later. But Mozart's economical mind compensates for the extension by entering one measure early with the repetition" (Ch. 18, pp. 106-107). The technique reflects Mozart's characteristic economy of means and contributes to the seamless quality of his formal articulations.

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 106-107, Ex. 128): Mozart, String Quartet in D, K.V. 575-IV (coda, m. 200). All six segments in this coda overlap:
- The second segment starts in m. 205 where the first ends
- The second segment is a repetition extended to eight or nine measures structurally, though the completion of the cadence is omitted in viola and cello
- The next segment (m. 213-19) is structurally seven measures, as its varied repetition (m. 219-25) proves
- The last two segments (m. 225-7, 227-9) are three measures each
- Schoenberg notes the technique could easily be avoided but Mozart preferred the dovetailed construction

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Creating overlap that functions only as a conclusion, not as a beginning
  **Correction**: The shared measure must convincingly serve both functions simultaneously

- **Error**: Overlapping segments of uniform length throughout a coda
  **Correction**: Use progressive reduction of segment lengths in combination with overlap

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Overlapping is the same as elision (which typically removes material)
  **Clarification**: In Mozart's technique, the shared measure retains the functions of both segments; nothing is removed

- **Confusion**: The structural length equals the sounding length
  **Clarification**: The structural length of an overlapping segment (e.g., seven measures) differs from its actual sounding length (e.g., six measures before the next segment begins)

- **Confusion**: This technique is the same as mere continuation
  **Clarification**: Overlapping specifically involves a new segment beginning before the old one has formally concluded -- it is a deliberate formal device, not casual continuation
