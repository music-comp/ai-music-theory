# Quick Definition
A characteristic technique of Mozart in which segments overlap by dovetailing, so that the last measure of one segment coincides with the first measure of the next, compensating for extensions in phrase length with economical re-entry.

# Formal Definition
The technique of overlapping joints resembles dovetailing. It is often a result of extending, for instance, a segment of four measures to five, or even six. Mozart's economical mind compensates for the extension by entering one measure early with the repetition. Thus the end of one segment and the beginning of the next share a common measure.

# Construction/Recognition
- A segment is extended beyond its expected length (e.g., four measures extended to five or six)
- The repetition or next segment enters one measure early, overlapping with the final measure of the preceding segment
- The shared measure functions simultaneously as the conclusion of one idea and the beginning of the next
- Multiple successive segments may all overlap in this manner
- The structural length of each segment is one measure longer than the actual number of measures it occupies

# Musical Context
This technique is particularly evident in Mozart's codas and closing sections, where multiple overlapping segments create a continuous flow that masks the sectional divisions. It reflects Mozart's characteristic economy of means and contributes to the seamless quality of his formal articulations.

# Examples
- Mozart, String Quartet in D, K.V. 575-IV (coda, m. 200): all six segments overlap. The second segment starts in m. 205 where the first ends. It is a repetition extended to eight or nine measures structurally. The next segment (m. 213-19) is structurally seven measures, as its varied repetition (m. 219-25) proves. The last two segments (m. 225-7, 227-9) are three measures each.
- The technique could easily be avoided by bringing each segment to a definite end and beginning the repetition a measure later, but Mozart preferred the dovetailed construction

# Common Confusions
- Overlapping is not the same as elision (which typically removes material); in Mozart's technique, the shared measure retains the functions of both segments
- The structural length of an overlapping segment (e.g., seven measures) differs from its actual sounding length (e.g., six measures before the next segment begins)
- This technique is distinct from mere continuation; it specifically involves a new segment beginning before the old one has formally concluded
