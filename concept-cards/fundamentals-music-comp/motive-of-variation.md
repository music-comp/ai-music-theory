# Quick Definition
A predetermined rhythmic-melodic figure systematically applied throughout a single variation, providing a unity that surpasses that of the theme; in higher forms, derived from the theme itself, thus connecting all variations intimately with the theme.

# Formal Definition
In classical music every variation shows a unity which surpasses that of the theme. This unity results from the systematic application of a motive of variation -- a specific rhythmic-melodic figure that is maintained consistently throughout the variation. In higher forms, the motive derives from the theme itself, thus connecting all the variations intimately with the theme. In the practice form, the motive should consist of a predetermined figure, modified no more than accommodation to the harmony and structure requires. The motive of variation is distinct from the theme's own motives; it is the specific figure used to elaborate the theme within each variation.

# Construction/Recognition
- A short figure, usually no longer than two measures, often a half-measure or less
- Maintained consistently throughout a single variation
- Must be adaptable to every movement of the harmony
- Must contain all the principal tones of the theme
- Its nature and length are limited by the number and distribution of principal tones and harmonies
- In higher forms, derived from features of the theme itself
- The rhythmic organization of the motive has great bearing on the character of the variation

# Musical Context
The motive of variation is the fundamental unit of variation technique. Each variation typically has its own motive of variation, though related variations may share or develop the same motive. The concept explains how variations achieve both unity within themselves and variety across the set: each variation is unified by its consistent motive, while different motives produce different characters across the set.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Variations in e minor: First eleven variations use predetermined figures as practice form
- Beethoven, 32 Variations in c minor: First three variations use a figure combining broken chords with tone repetitions derived from the repeated G of the theme's first three measures
- Brahms, Handel Variations: Motive of Variation 1 derived from the bass of the first measure, doubly diminished rhythmically; its complement furnishes the accompaniment figure; same three notes in triplets form the motive for Variation 2
- Beethoven, Diabelli Variations: Many variations (2, 9, 11, 12, 14, 18, 28) derive the motive from circumscription of principal tones

# Common Confusions
- The "motive of variation" is not the same as the "motive" of the theme -- it is a new figure used to elaborate the theme
- Each variation typically has its own motive of variation; it is not a single figure for the entire set
- The motive of variation must accommodate changes of harmony, not merely repeat at different pitch levels
