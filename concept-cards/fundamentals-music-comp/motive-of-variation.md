# Quick Definition

A predetermined rhythmic-melodic figure systematically applied throughout a single variation, providing a unity that surpasses that of the theme; in higher forms, derived from the theme itself, thus connecting all variations intimately with the theme.

# Core Definition

In classical music every variation shows a unity which surpasses that of the theme. This unity results from the systematic application of a motive of variation -- a specific rhythmic-melodic figure that is maintained consistently throughout the variation. In higher forms, the motive derives from the theme itself, thus connecting all variations intimately with the theme. In the practice form, the motive should consist of a predetermined figure, modified no more than accommodation to the harmony and structure requires. The motive of variation is distinct from the theme's own motives; it is the specific figure used to elaborate the theme within each variation.

# Key Properties

- A short figure, usually no longer than two measures, often a half-measure or less
- Maintained consistently throughout a single variation
- Must be adaptable to every movement of the harmony
- Must contain all the principal tones of the theme
- Its nature and length are limited by the number and distribution of principal tones
- In higher forms, derived from features of the theme itself
- The rhythmic organization has great bearing on the character of the variation
- Each variation typically has its own motive of variation

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify the figure that is systematically applied throughout the variation
2. Check that it contains all principal tones of the theme
3. Verify its adaptability to harmonic changes
4. Trace its derivation from the theme (in higher forms)
5. Note its rhythmic character (which determines the variation's character)
6. Assess its length relative to the distribution of principal tones

# Context & Application

The motive of variation is the fundamental unit of variation technique. Each variation typically has its own motive, though related variations may share or develop the same motive. The concept explains how variations achieve both unity within themselves (consistent motive) and variety across the set (different motives produce different characters).

# Examples

- Beethoven, Variations in e minor: First eleven variations use predetermined figures as practice form
- Beethoven, 32 Variations in c minor: First three variations use a figure combining broken chords with tone repetitions derived from the repeated G of the theme's first three measures
- Brahms, Handel Variations: Variation 1 motive derived from bass of first measure, doubly diminished rhythmically; its complement furnishes the accompaniment; same notes in triplets form the motive for Variation 2
- Beethoven, Diabelli Variations: Many variations (2, 9, 11, 12, 14, 18, 28) derive the motive from circumscription of principal tones

# Common Errors

- Confusing the motive of variation with the theme's own motive
- Applying the motive of variation mechanically without adapting to harmonic changes
- Using a single motive of variation for the entire set (each variation should have its own)

# Common Confusions

- **Motive of variation vs. theme motive**: The motive of variation is a new figure created to elaborate the theme, not the theme's original motivic material.
- **One motive per variation**: Each variation typically has its own motive of variation; it is not a single figure applied across the entire set.
- **Accommodation**: The motive must accommodate changes of harmony, not merely repeat at different pitch levels.
