# Quick Definition

In period construction, the antecedent typically ends on V through a half cadence, full cadence, or I-V interchange, while the consequent concludes with a full cadence, usually on I, establishing the harmonic contrast that defines the period.

# Core Definition

The cadential structure distinguishes the two halves of a period harmonically. The antecedent ends on V in the great majority of cases, approached through a half cadence, full cadence (to V as if in the dominant key), or mere interchange of I and V. Antecedents ending on I also exist. The consequent usually ends on I, V, or III (major or minor) with a full cadence -- perfect authentic, imperfect, Phrygian, or plagal, according to its function. Even when both halves arrive at the same degree, the cadence itself must differ (Schoenberg, pp. 22-24).

An ending on a strong beat is called "masculine," and on a weak beat, "feminine." Too frequent use of the same kind of ending is often monotonous (p. 23).

# Key Properties

1. Antecedent endings: V (most common), approached via half cadence, full cadence to V, or I-V interchange; I (less common)
2. Consequent endings: I (most common for complete pieces), V, or III (major or minor)
3. The consequent cadence must differ from the antecedent cadence, even when arriving on the same degree
4. Full cadence in the consequent usually requires earlier harmonic changes (deviations as early as m. 5 or 6)
5. Masculine endings (strong beat) and feminine endings (weak beat) should be varied to avoid monotony
6. If the period is a complete piece (e.g., children's songs), it must end with a perfect authentic cadence on I

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct Period Cadences

1. End the antecedent on V through half cadence, full cadence, or I-V interchange
2. Plan the consequent's cadence to differ from the antecedent's
3. For complete pieces, end the consequent with a perfect authentic cadence on I
4. For periods within larger forms, choose I, V, or III as appropriate
5. Begin harmonic deviation early enough to support the consequent's different cadence
6. Vary masculine and feminine endings to avoid monotony

## To Recognize Period Cadences

1. Identify the harmonic arrival at the caesura (end of antecedent)
2. Compare the cadence type at the end of the consequent with the antecedent's
3. Note whether the cadence approach differs even when the arrival degree is the same

# Context & Application

The cadential contrast between antecedent and consequent is one of the defining features of the period. The half cadence at the end of the antecedent creates harmonic openness that demands the consequent's resolution. Bach's examples show exceptional variety: one ends on III (relative major), others use quasi-Phrygian cadences to reach the dominant, and one has a plagal cadence with upper voices expressing the authentic cadence -- a procedure justified only by the motion of independent parts.

# Examples

- **Beethoven, Op. 2/1-II** (p. 22): Antecedent ends on V through interchange (m. 4); consequent ends on I with full cadence (m. 8)

- **Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV** (p. 22): Half cadence reinforces caesura on V (m. 4); full cadence on V in m. 8 "as if in the key of the dominant"

- **Bach examples** (Ex. 43, p. 22): One ends on III (relative major); Exs. 43c and e reach the dominant through quasi-Phrygian cadence; Ex. 43d has a plagal cadence though upper voices express authentic cadence

- **Beethoven, Op. 10/3, Menuetto** (p. 22): Antecedent and consequent of eight measures each

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using the same cadence for both antecedent and consequent
  **Correction**: Even when arriving on the same degree, the approach must be different

- **Error**: Using the same ending type (masculine or feminine) throughout
  **Correction**: Vary ending types to avoid monotony

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Equating a half cadence to V with a full cadence in the key of V
  **Clarification**: A half cadence arrives on V as dominant of the home key; a full cadence to V treats V as a temporary tonic

- **Confusion**: Thinking the consequent must always end on I
  **Clarification**: Only when the period is a complete piece; within larger forms, V or III are also valid

- **Confusion**: Believing exotic cadence types (Phrygian, plagal) are errors
  **Clarification**: Bach uses quasi-Phrygian and plagal cadences effectively, though the plagal cadence with authentic upper voices is justified only by independent part-writing
