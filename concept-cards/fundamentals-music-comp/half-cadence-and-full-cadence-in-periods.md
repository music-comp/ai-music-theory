# Quick Definition
In period construction, the antecedent typically ends with a half cadence on V (or simple interchange of I and V), while the consequent concludes with a full cadence, usually on I, establishing the fundamental harmonic contrast that defines the period.

# Formal Definition
The cadential structure of a period distinguishes its two halves harmonically. The antecedent ends on V in the great majority of cases, approached through a half cadence, full cadence (to V as if in the dominant key), or mere interchange of I and V. The consequent typically ends on I, V, or III (major or minor) with a full cadence -- perfect authentic, imperfect, Phrygian, or plagal, according to its function. Even when both halves arrive at the same degree, the cadence itself must differ.

# Construction/Recognition
- Antecedent endings on V: half cadence (most common), full cadence to V, or I-V interchange
- Antecedent endings on I: less common but possible
- Consequent endings: I (most common for complete pieces), V, or III (major or minor)
- The consequent cadence must differ from the antecedent cadence, even when arriving on the same degree
- Introduction of a full cadence in the consequent usually requires earlier harmonic changes (deviations as early as m. 5 or 6)
- An ending on a strong beat is "masculine"; on a weak beat, "feminine"

# Musical Context
The cadential contrast between antecedent and consequent is one of the defining features of the period. If the period is a complete piece (e.g., children's songs), it must end with a perfect authentic cadence on I. If it is part of a larger form, it may end on I, V, or III. The half cadence at the end of the antecedent creates harmonic openness that demands the consequent's resolution.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-II: Antecedent ends on V through interchange (m. 4); consequent ends on I with full cadence (m. 8)
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: Half cadence reinforces caesura on V (m. 4); full cadence on V in m. 8 ("as if in the key of the dominant")
- Bach examples (Ex. 43): One ends on III (relative major); others use quasi-Phrygian cadences to reach the dominant; one has a plagal cadence with the upper voices expressing the authentic cadence

# Common Confusions
- A half cadence and a full cadence to V are different things: a half cadence arrives on V as dominant, while a full cadence to V treats V as a temporary tonic.
- The consequent's cadence must be different from the antecedent's, even if both arrive on the same scale degree -- the approach must vary.
- Too frequent use of the same kind of ending (masculine or feminine) becomes monotonous.
