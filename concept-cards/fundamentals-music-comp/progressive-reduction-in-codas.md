# Quick Definition
The characteristic tendency of codas to feature segments that become progressively shorter and simpler, reducing both length and harmonic complexity toward the end of a movement.

# Formal Definition
As the length of segments decreases in a coda, so also the complexity of the cadences decreases. The last codettas may omit even the cadential subdominant. Interchange of V and I often gives way to mere repetitions of the tonic. The motive material is, for the most part, derived from previous themes, reformulated to conform to cadential harmony and effectively liquidated. This progressive reduction is a common but not invariable feature of codas.

# Construction/Recognition
The pattern of progressive reduction involves:
1. Initial segments with richly elaborated cadences, possibly reaching remote regions
2. Subsequent segments becoming shorter in length
3. Harmonic content simplifying from full cadences to V-I interchange to mere tonic repetition
4. Motival content progressively liquidated, with only residues remaining
5. The reduction does not occur invariably, and never with mechanical regularity

# Musical Context
Progressive reduction serves as the natural conclusion of a movement, bringing the musical discourse to rest through simultaneous diminution of harmonic, melodic, and rhythmic activity. It parallels the liquidation process used elsewhere in musical form.

# Examples
- Beethoven, String Quartet Op. 18/4-I: coda consists of four segments -- first (m. 208) is six measures quoting and liquidating the transition motive; second (m. 214-15) quotes and liquidates the opening phrase; third (m. 216-17) uses only residues; last reduces to repetition of the tonic chord
- Beethoven, Op. 57-I: segment lengths decrease as 6, 4, 3, 3, 1, 1 measures throughout a dramatic crescendo, followed by six measures of tonic diminishing to ppp
- Beethoven, Op. 28-I: the last motive-form (m. 446-7) is followed by four repetitions with ascending upbeats in a crescendo, then liquidation with decrescendo

# Common Confusions
- Progressive reduction does not occur with mechanical regularity; it is a tendency, not a rigid formula
- The reduction is not merely one of length but simultaneously of harmonic complexity and motival content
- A crescendo may accompany the reduction in length (as in Op. 57-I), creating dramatic tension even as the material simplifies
