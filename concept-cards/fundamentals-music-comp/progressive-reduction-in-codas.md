# Quick Definition
The characteristic tendency of codas to feature segments that become progressively shorter and simpler, reducing both length and harmonic complexity toward the end of a movement -- from richly elaborated cadences to mere tonic repetitions.

# Core Definition
As the length of segments decreases in a coda, so also the complexity of the cadences decreases. The last codettas may omit even the cadential subdominant. Interchange of V and I often gives way to mere repetitions of the tonic. The motive material is, for the most part, derived from previous themes, reformulated to conform to cadential harmony and effectively liquidated. This progressive reduction is a common but not invariable feature of codas, and never occurs with mechanical regularity.

# Key Properties
- Segments become progressively shorter
- Harmonic complexity decreases simultaneously
- Full cadences simplify to V-I interchange, then to mere tonic repetition
- Last codettas may omit even the cadential subdominant
- Motive material is derived from previous themes and progressively liquidated
- The reduction is a tendency, not a rigid formula
- A crescendo may accompany the reduction in length, creating dramatic tension

# Construction / Recognition
1. Initial segments: richly elaborated cadences, possibly reaching remote regions
2. Subsequent segments: becoming shorter in length
3. Harmonic content simplifying from full cadences to V-I interchange
4. Motival content progressively liquidated, with only residues remaining
5. Final segments: mere tonic repetition
- The reduction does not occur invariably, and never with mechanical regularity

# Context & Application
Progressive reduction serves as the natural conclusion of a movement, bringing the musical discourse to rest through simultaneous diminution of harmonic, melodic, and rhythmic activity. It parallels the liquidation process used elsewhere in musical form but operates on the larger scale of formal sections.

# Examples
- Beethoven, String Quartet Op. 18/4-I: coda of four segments -- first (m. 208) six measures quoting the transition motive; second (m. 214-15) quoting the opening phrase; third (m. 216-17) using only residues; last reducing to repetition of the tonic chord
- Beethoven, Op. 57-I: segment lengths decrease 6, 4, 3, 3, 1, 1 measures throughout a dramatic crescendo, followed by six measures of tonic diminishing to ppp upon a reminiscence of the principal rhythm
- Beethoven, Op. 28-I: the last motive-form (m. 446-7) followed by four repetitions with ascending upbeats in a crescendo, then liquidation with decrescendo

# Common Errors
- Applying progressive reduction with mechanical regularity rather than as a flexible tendency
- Reducing length without simultaneously simplifying harmonic content
- Making the reduction too predictable, removing dramatic interest

# Common Confusions
- Progressive reduction does not occur with mechanical regularity; it is a tendency, not a rigid formula
- The reduction is not merely one of length but simultaneously of harmonic complexity and motival content
- A crescendo may accompany the reduction in length (as in Op. 57-I), creating dramatic tension even as the material simplifies
