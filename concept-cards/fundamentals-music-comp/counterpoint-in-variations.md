# Quick Definition
The use of contrapuntal techniques in variations, ranging from the simple addition of voices to a cantus firmus (like species counterpoint exercises) to sophisticated devices such as canons, fugues, double counterpoint, and mirror forms.

# Formal Definition
In some respects all variations have a certain relation to counterpoint, if only to the preliminary exercises of species counterpoint -- adding one or more voices to a cantus firmus. In variations, the theme acts as cantus firmus; the acceleration of the motus (rhythmic activity) across successive variations may derive from this source. Every sort of contrapuntal treatment may be found, and sometimes the entire organization of a variation is based upon contrapuntal procedures. Structural changes may be very far-reaching when, for instance, a fugue is built upon a derivative of the theme.

# Construction/Recognition
- Basic: adding one or more voices to the theme treated as cantus firmus
- Free imitation: voices imitating motive-forms without strict rules
- Canon: strict imitation at a fixed interval and time delay
- Fugue or fughetta: full contrapuntal form built on a theme derivative
- Double counterpoint at the octave: two voices interchangeable in position
- Multiple counterpoint: three or more interchangeable voices
- Mirror forms: inversion, retrograde, or retrograde-inversion of themes
- Invertible counterpoint at the octave and twelfth

# Musical Context
Contrapuntal treatment in variations represents the intersection of two fundamental compositional techniques: variation and counterpoint. The progression from simple added voices to complex contrapuntal devices often mirrors the increasing intensity of the variation set. Many variation sets conclude with a fugue, representing the culmination of contrapuntal complexity.

# Examples
- Beethoven, 32 Variations in c minor: Most variations add voices to the basic scheme (passacaglia-like); free imitations in Variation 17; canon in Variation 22; Variations 10-11 and 20-21 in double counterpoint at the octave
- Beethoven, 15 Variations in E-flat, Op. 35: Variation 7 is canonic; set concluded with a Finale alla Fuga; first three variations are contrapuntal additions to the bass alone
- Beethoven, Diabelli Variations: Fugue (Variation 32), fughetta (Variation 24), canonic imitation (Variations 19, 20), free imitation (Variations 4, 11, 14, 20, 30)
- Brahms, Handel Variations: Finale is a fugue; Variation 6 is a canon; Variation 8 is based on double counterpoint at the octave; quasi-contrapuntal pianistic imitations in Variations 16, 18, 23, 24
- Brahms, Haydn Variations Op. 56a: Variation 4 is triple counterpoint invertible at the octave and twelfth; Variation 8 contains mirror forms in multiple counterpoint

# Common Confusions
- Not all contrapuntal treatment in variations is strict counterpoint -- quasi-contrapuntal pianistic imitations are a related but distinct technique
- The theme acting as "cantus firmus" does not mean it appears literally and unchanged -- it may be varied while still serving as the structural foundation
- A fugue conclusion to a variation set is not a separate form; it is the ultimate contrapuntal variation
