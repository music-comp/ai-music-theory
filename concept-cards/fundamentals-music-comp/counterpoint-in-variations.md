# Quick Definition

The use of contrapuntal techniques in variations, ranging from the simple addition of voices to a cantus firmus (like species counterpoint exercises) to sophisticated devices such as canons, fugues, double counterpoint, and mirror forms.

# Core Definition

In some respects all variations have a certain relation to counterpoint, if only to the preliminary exercises of species counterpoint -- adding one or more voices to a cantus firmus. In variations, the theme acts as cantus firmus; the acceleration of the motus (rhythmic activity) across successive variations may derive from this source. Every sort of contrapuntal treatment may be found, and sometimes the entire organization of a variation is based upon contrapuntal procedures. Structural changes may be very far-reaching when a fugue is built upon a derivative of the theme.

# Key Properties

- Basic: adding one or more voices to the theme treated as cantus firmus
- Free imitation: voices imitating motive-forms without strict rules
- Canon: strict imitation at a fixed interval and time delay
- Fugue or fughetta: full contrapuntal form built on a theme derivative
- Double counterpoint at the octave: two voices interchangeable in position
- Multiple counterpoint: three or more interchangeable voices
- Mirror forms: inversion, retrograde, or retrograde-inversion
- Invertible counterpoint at the octave and twelfth
- Quasi-contrapuntal pianistic imitations: contribute to coherence but are not strict counterpoint

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify added voices and their relationship to the theme
2. Check for imitative entries (free or strict)
3. Look for canonic procedures (strict imitation at fixed interval/delay)
4. Identify invertible counterpoint (voice positions interchangeable)
5. Note any fugal procedures (fugue, fughetta, finale alla fuga)
6. Distinguish genuine contrapuntal devices from quasi-contrapuntal imitations

# Context & Application

Contrapuntal treatment in variations represents the intersection of variation and counterpoint. The progression from simple added voices to complex contrapuntal devices often mirrors the increasing intensity of the variation set. Many sets conclude with a fugue, representing the culmination of contrapuntal complexity.

# Examples

- Beethoven, 32 Variations: Most variations add voices to the basic scheme (passacaglia-like); free imitations in Var. 17; canon in Var. 22; Vars. 10-11 and 20-21 in double counterpoint at the octave
- Beethoven, Op. 35: Var. 7 is canonic; concluded with Finale alla Fuga; first three variations are contrapuntal additions to the bass alone
- Beethoven, Diabelli Variations: Fugue (Var. 32), fughetta (Var. 24), canonic imitation (Vars. 19, 20), free imitation (Vars. 4, 11, 14, 20, 30)
- Brahms, Handel Variations: Finale is a fugue; Var. 6 is a canon; Var. 8 based on double counterpoint; quasi-contrapuntal imitations in Vars. 16, 18, 23, 24
- Brahms, Haydn Variations Op. 56a: Var. 4 is triple counterpoint invertible at octave and twelfth; Var. 8 contains mirror forms in multiple counterpoint

# Common Errors

- Confusing quasi-contrapuntal pianistic imitations with genuine strict counterpoint
- Treating the fugue conclusion as a separate form rather than the ultimate contrapuntal variation

# Common Confusions

- **Theme as cantus firmus**: The theme is not used literally and unchanged; it may be varied while still serving as the structural foundation.
- **Quasi-contrapuntal vs. strict**: Quasi-contrapuntal imitations contribute to coherence but lack the rigor of genuine invertible counterpoint or canonic procedure.
- **Fugue as variation**: A fugue concluding a variation set is the ultimate contrapuntal variation, not a departure from the variation form.
