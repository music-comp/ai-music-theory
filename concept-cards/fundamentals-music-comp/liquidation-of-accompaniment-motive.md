# Quick Definition

Near cadences or half cadences, harmonic requirements often force the modification, liquidation, or abandonment of the accompaniment motive, paralleling the liquidation process in the thematic voice.

# Core Definition

The motive of the accompaniment, as a unifying device, should be maintained for at least several measures or phrases. Even if varied, it should not disappear completely. But harmonic requirements in the vicinity of cadences or half cadences often force modification or liquidation of the motive of accompaniment. Changes of character or construction, or increase in the number of harmonies, may justify or even require modification of the accompaniment. This process takes a variety of forms (Schoenberg, pp. 55-56).

In many cases a single motive is used consistently throughout a section except for cadences. This is possible only in primitive cases where the harmony does not change much. A clear change in character or construction may justify multiple figures, especially in Mozart's Rococo style (p. 55).

# Prerequisites

- **Motive of the accompaniment** -- The motive must be established before it can be liquidated
- **Liquidation** -- The broader principle of liquidation applies to the accompaniment motive

# Key Properties

1. Accompaniment motive maintained for at least several measures or phrases
2. Should not disappear completely during non-cadential passages
3. Near cadences, modification or liquidation is often forced by harmonic requirements
4. Changes of character or increased harmonic density may require modification
5. Modification ranges from gradual simplification to outright abandonment and replacement
6. More than one figure may appear (especially in Mozart's Rococo style)
7. Parallels thematic liquidation in the melody

# Construction / Recognition

1. Maintain the accompaniment motive through the body of the theme
2. Near cadences, begin to modify or simplify the figure
3. Reduce it to its essential rhythmic or harmonic component
4. Abandon if the harmonic activity becomes incompatible with the figure
5. The process may take varied forms: gradual simplification, reduction, or replacement

# Context & Application

The liquidation of the accompaniment motive parallels thematic liquidation. Both processes work together toward structural closure. The accompaniment motive's simplification or disappearance near cadences is a natural result of increased harmonic complexity -- the motive may no longer accommodate rapid harmonic changes. This parallel process reinforces the cadential effect across all musical dimensions.

# Examples

- **Modification/liquidation near cadences** (pp. 55-56): Exs. 45f, 46f, 48c, 51a, 55a, b, 56b, 58c, 59g, h, 61d

- **Clear change requiring modification** (p. 55): Op. 14/2-I, compare m. 1-4 with m. 5 ff.

- **Climactic ascension requiring modification** (p. 55): Op. 31/1-II, m. 5 -- melody requires richer harmony, so accompaniment motive is modified

- **Reduction to maintain motus** (p. 55): Op. 31/3-III -- special broken-chord figure reduced in m. 3 ff. to no more than is necessary to keep up the motus

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Abandoning the accompaniment motive prematurely during non-cadential passages
  **Correction**: Maintain the motive through the body of the theme; modify or liquidate only near cadences

- **Error**: Forcing the accompaniment motive to continue unchanged into harmonically complex cadences
  **Correction**: Near cadences, modification or liquidation is natural and often necessary

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking liquidation of the accompaniment motive is a compositional failure
  **Clarification**: It is a structural necessity that parallels thematic liquidation

- **Confusion**: Equating replacement by a different figure with liquidation
  **Clarification**: Replacement (as in Mozart's Rococo style) is distinct from liquidation; both are legitimate
