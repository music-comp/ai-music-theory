# Quick Definition
The technique of adapting, varying, expanding, condensing, recombining, and carrying motive-forms through various keys or regions -- Schoenberg's preferred term for the process commonly called "development," which he rejected because the material does not truly develop in the sense of growth or evolution.

# Core Definition
The customary term "development" for this section is a misnomer. It suggests germination and growth which rarely occur. The thematic elaboration and modulatory "working out" (Durchfuhrung) produce some variation, and place the musical elements in different contexts, but seldom lead to the "development" of anything new. Motive-forms are adapted, varied, expanded, condensed, recombined, and carried through various keys or regions. The technique appears in contrasting middle sections of all ternary forms (minuet, scherzo) and reaches its fullest expression in the sonata-allegro.

# Key Properties
- "Development" is a misnomer; "elaboration" (Durchfuhrung) is preferred
- Material does not truly "develop" in the sense of growth or maturation
- Techniques: adaptation, variation, expansion, condensation, recombination
- Material carried through fluctuating, unstable harmonic situations
- Sequential treatment is the most common organizational device
- Process typically concludes with liquidation and a pedal point
- Freedom of structure does not imply absence of logic or balance
- Appears at all formal levels: scherzo middle section, rondo C-section, sonata-allegro

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify motive-forms from the exposition being modified
2. Note the modulatory harmonic context (essentially unstable)
3. Look for sequential organization as the primary structural device
4. Track the progressive shortening and simplification of segments
5. Identify the concluding liquidation and pedal point

# Context & Application
The concept of elaboration applies across multiple formal levels -- from the contrasting middle section of a minuet or scherzo to the Durchfuhrung of a sonata-allegro. The key insight is that the material is not "developed" toward a goal but rather "elaborated" -- displayed in various aspects and combinations. The importance and significance of this division was much overrated during the late nineteenth century. While it is usually considered the most characteristic feature of sonata-allegro form, in many important works it is short and sketch-like.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 3, Scherzo: first ten measures of the Durchfuhrung utilize motive-forms of m. 1-4 in chromatic ascension
- Beethoven, Op. 13-I: monothematic elaboration; sequential treatment with progressive reduction
- Beethoven, Op. 28-I: monothematic; sequence plus progressive reduction from eight to four to two to one measure
- Mozart, K.V. 465-I: built entirely from the initial motive-form, demonstrating the fullest exploitation through motival transformation
- Beethoven, Op. 10/2-I: chief material from incidental octaves at the end of the exposition -- formerly inconspicuous features becoming dominant

# Common Errors
- Treating the elaboration as an opportunity for free composition rather than working with exposed material
- Failing to provide modulatory contrast with the exposition's stability
- Making the elaboration too long or too important relative to other sections

# Common Confusions
- "Elaboration" and "development" are not synonymous in Schoenberg's usage -- elaboration is his preferred term
- The material does not "develop" in the sense of growing more mature; it is adapted and recombined
- Elaboration technique appears in scherzos and minuets as well as in sonata forms
- The section's importance was overrated in the late 19th century; in many works it is brief
