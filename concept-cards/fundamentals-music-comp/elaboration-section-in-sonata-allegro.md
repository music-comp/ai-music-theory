# Quick Definition
The contrasting middle section of the sonata-allegro form, essentially modulatory in character, devoted to the working out of previously exposed thematic material through a series of segments passing systematically through contrasting keys.

# Formal Definition
The elaboration is essentially a contrasting middle section. Because the exposition is basically stable, the elaboration tends to be modulatory. Because the exposition uses closely related keys, the elaboration usually includes more remote regions. Because the exposition "develops" a wealth of differing themes from a basic motive, the elaboration normally makes use of variants of previously "exposed" themes, seldom evolving new musical ideas. The customary term "development" is a misnomer: it suggests germination and growth which rarely occur.

# Construction/Recognition
- Consists of segments passing systematically through contrasting keys
- Ends with establishment of an appropriate upbeat chord or retransition
- Thematic material drawn from the exposition in any order
- Often a small number of formerly inconspicuous features dominate the entire division
- Earlier segments tend to be longer and more stable; later segments shorter with more rapid change of region
- Occasional "roving" passages pertain to no definite region
- Segments overlap freely; approach chords (dominants) emphasized more than tonics
- Tendency to favour regions with more flats or fewer sharps (compensating for the dominant-side subordinate group)
- Relative length varies widely: from approximately half to substantially equal to the exposition
- The beginning may relate to the end of the exposition through same region, juxtaposition, tonic minor/major, or interposed transition

# Musical Context
The elaboration provides the modulatory contrast that balances the stable tonalities of the exposition and recapitulation. Its importance was much overrated during the latter half of the nineteenth century. In many important works it is short and sketch-like. While usually considered the most characteristic feature, the Durchfuhrung's significance lies in its contrast function, not in its supposed developmental character.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 13-I: six-measure segment in relative minor of dominant, sequenced a step lower; reduced versions (4 measures) appear three times; dominant pedal (m. 167-87) with thematic references
- Beethoven, Op. 28-I: monothematic; principal theme in subdominant, sequenced, progressively reduced from 8 to 4 to 2 to 1 measure; residues reduced to mere rhythmic reminiscence (m. 240-56)
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: principal theme appears only in introduction and retransition; main body uses subordinate theme in sequential manner; pedal on V at m. 81
- Beethoven, Op. 10/1-I: built upon a theme not previously formulated, though related to transition and subordinate themes -- "a masterly imagination and intuition"
- Beethoven, Op. 10/2-I: chief material from incidental octaves at exposition's end; does not reach the usual dominant because recapitulation begins in D (submediant major)
- Mozart, K.V. 465-I: built entirely from the initial motive-form; gradual expansion of compass, motive shortening, and liquidation

# Common Confusions
- "Development" is a misnomer; the section works out existing material rather than growing new ideas
- The elaboration need not use the most important themes; formerly inconspicuous material may dominate
- Occasionally a new formulation appears that derives from the basic material but was never previously stated (Op. 10/1-I); this is exceptional, not typical
- The elaboration's importance was overrated in the late nineteenth century; many great movements have short, sketch-like elaborations
