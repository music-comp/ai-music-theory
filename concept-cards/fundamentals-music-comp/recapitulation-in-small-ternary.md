# Quick Definition
The A1-section that concludes the small ternary form, usually a modified repetition of the A-section that ends on the tonic, with changes ranging from slight variation to considerable reconstruction.

# Formal Definition
The recapitulation may be an unchanged repetition. More frequently it is changed, modified, or varied. Changes may be necessary to establish a definite ending, especially if the A-section ends on a degree other than I. Shortening (by elimination, reduction, or condensation) may help to avoid monotony. Lengthening (by insertion, interpolated repetitions, extension, or addition) may create emphasis. Modification of both melody and harmony may be necessary to accommodate such changes.

# Construction/Recognition
The recapitulation ends on the tonic if it completes an independent piece. Recognize it by: (1) return of A-section material, (2) cadential drive toward the tonic, (3) modifications to melody, harmony, or length compared to the A-section. Common modifications include: reduction (omitting measures), extension (adding sequences or repetitions), transposition to the tonic, ornamental variation, or changed cadence.

# Musical Context
The recapitulation is where the structural purpose of the small ternary form is fulfilled: the return of familiar material after contrast, with the closure that the tonic provides. The degree of modification reflects the composer's balance between recognition (maintaining the identity of the A-section) and freshness (avoiding monotony from literal repetition).

# Examples
- Beethoven Op. 2/1-II: recapitulation reduced to four measures; only m. 13 preserved (an octave higher), m. 14 turns to IV for cadence
- Beethoven Op. 2/2-II: seven-measure recapitulation combines reduction (omitting m. 3-5) and extension (adding m. 15-16 sequentially)
- Beethoven Op. 7-II: recapitulation extended to ten measures through sequences, repetitions, and a deceptive cadence requiring a second enriched cadence
- Beethoven Op. 28-II: omits m. 3-5, condenses m. 6-8 into two measures, balanced by insertion of two semi-sequential repetitions

# Common Confusions
- The recapitulation is seldom a literal repetition, even in the simplest examples
- Reduction and extension can occur simultaneously in a single recapitulation (e.g., omitting some measures while extending others)
- The final cadence of the recapitulation generally differs from that of the A-section, even if both lead to the same degree
