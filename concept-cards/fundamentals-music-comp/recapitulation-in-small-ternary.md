# Quick Definition
The A1-section that concludes the small ternary form, usually a modified repetition of the A-section that ends on the tonic, with changes ranging from slight variation to considerable reconstruction.

# Core Definition
The recapitulation may be an unchanged repetition. More frequently it is changed, modified, or varied. Changes may be necessary to establish a definite ending, especially if the A-section ends on a degree other than I. Shortening (by elimination, reduction, or condensation) may help to avoid monotony. Lengthening (by insertion, interpolated repetitions, extension, or addition) may create emphasis. Modification of both melody and harmony may be necessary to accommodate such changes. The principles of variation can be applied to all elements of the A-section, but with moderation, so as not to conceal the presence of a repetition.

# Key Properties
- Ends on tonic if completing an independent piece
- Seldom an unchanged repetition
- Final cadence generally differs from A-section cadence, even if both target the same degree
- Shortening methods: elimination, reduction, condensation
- Lengthening methods: insertion, interpolated repetitions, extension, addition
- Melody contour should not be entirely changed except at essential structural points
- Ornamental variation (passing notes, circumscribing notes, tone repetitions) need not change contour
- Excessive rhythmic changes or accent shifts prevent recognition
- Harmony variation: largely insertions and substitutions
- Accompaniment variation: figuration changes, contrapuntal additions, counter-melodies
- A changed cadence may require considerable reformulation or complete reconstruction
- The recapitulation may begin on a degree other than I (rare in small ternary)

# Construction / Recognition
Recognize the recapitulation by: (1) return of A-section material, (2) cadential drive toward tonic, (3) modifications to melody, harmony, or length. Common modifications: reduction (omitting measures), extension (adding sequences or repetitions), transposition to tonic, ornamental variation, changed cadence.

# Context & Application
The recapitulation fulfills the structural purpose of the form: return of familiar material after contrast, with tonic closure. The degree of modification reflects the balance between recognition (maintaining A-section identity) and freshness (avoiding monotony from literal repetition). In a small form, alterations should be used with discretion, maintaining balance with preceding sections.

# Examples
- Beethoven Op. 2/1-II: reduced to 4 measures; only m. 13 preserved (octave higher); m. 14 turns to IV for cadence
- Beethoven Op. 2/2-II: 7 measures combining reduction (omitting m. 3-5) and extension (adding m. 15-16 sequentially); m. 17 ascending reformulation of m. 6, placing m. 18-19 an octave higher
- Beethoven Op. 7-II: extended to 10 measures through sequences, repetitions, deceptive cadence requiring second enriched cadence
- Beethoven Op. 28-II: omits m. 3-5, condenses m. 6-8 into two measures, balanced by two semi-sequential repetitions of m. 18
- Beethoven Op. 31/1-III: main melody transferred to tenor, quasi-melodic accompaniment added in right hand
- Ex. 107a, b, c: recapitulations beginning on II, V, and I6/4 respectively

# Common Errors
- Making the recapitulation a literal repetition when modification would avoid monotony
- Varying so extensively that the recapitulation is no longer recognizable as a return
- Failing to adjust the cadence to end on the tonic when needed
- Using excessive rhythmic changes that prevent recognition

# Common Confusions
- The recapitulation is seldom a literal repetition, even in the simplest examples
- Reduction and extension can occur simultaneously (omitting some measures while extending others)
- The final cadence generally differs from the A-section's, even if both lead to the same degree
