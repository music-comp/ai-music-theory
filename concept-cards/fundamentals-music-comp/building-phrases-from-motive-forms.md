# Quick Definition

The practical process of combining derived motive-forms into complete phrases, ensuring that the results function as true musical units rather than mere mechanical assemblages.

# Core Definition

"In working out derivatives of a motive, it is important that the results have the character of true phrases -- of complete musical units" (Ch. 4, p. 13). Exs. 30-34 "show various methods of producing a large number of different phrases out of one basic motive. Some of them might be used to begin a theme, others to continue it; and some, especially those which do not begin with I, as material to meet other structural requirements, e.g. contrasts, subordinate ideas and the like" (Ch. 4, p. 12).

# Prerequisites

- **motive** -- Must have a basic motive to derive forms from
- **variation** -- Must understand variation to produce motive-forms
- **variation-methods** -- Must know specific techniques for producing varied forms
- **connecting-motive-forms** -- Must understand the three connecting principles

# Key Properties

1. Results must have the character of true phrases (complete musical units)
2. Different phrases serve different structural functions (opening, continuation, contrast)
3. Phrases beginning on I are suitable for theme openings
4. Phrases not beginning on I may serve contrasts or subordinate ideas
5. Each melody note should be either a harmony note or a conventional non-chordal note
6. Even stiff or overcrowded results have pedagogical value
7. The practice of sketching variations "should never be abandoned"

# Construction / Recognition

## Methods for Building Phrases (Exs. 30-34)

1. **Adding ancillary notes** while retaining all notes of the basic motive (Ex. 31)
2. **Preserving rhythm** while changing intervals and direction, with transposition to other degrees (Ex. 32) -- "often used in traditional music to produce entire themes"
3. **Combining rhythmic changes** with ancillary notes and interval/direction changes (Ex. 33) -- more far-reaching but may produce stiff results
4. **Rhythmic shifting and rearrangement** of features (Ex. 34) -- produces material for continuations and contrasts, but "may endanger comprehensibility"

## Verification Steps

1. Check that each note is a harmony note or conventional non-chordal note
2. Verify the phrase functions as a complete musical unit
3. Determine the appropriate structural function (opening, continuation, contrast)
4. Ensure the phrase maintains a perceivable relationship to the basic motive

# Context & Application

This is the practical application chapter where theoretical principles become compositional practice. Schoenberg emphasizes that even results that are "stiff and overcrowded" have pedagogical value: "the practice of making such sketches, which attempt various methods of variation, should never be abandoned" (Ch. 4, p. 13). The distinction between phrases suitable for beginnings versus continuations versus contrasts is crucial for theme construction in subsequent chapters.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Producing motive-form combinations that lack phrase character
  **Correction**: The results must function as "complete musical units," not merely as connected motives

- **Error**: Using all phrase constructions for theme openings
  **Correction**: Some are better suited for continuations, contrasts, or subordinate ideas

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Expecting all phrase sketches to be polished compositions
  **Clarification**: Even stiff results have value; the purpose is systematic exploration of variation resources

- **Confusion**: Treating far-reaching variations as equally usable as closely related ones
  **Clarification**: Remotely related forms "may endanger comprehensibility" and require careful placement
