# Quick Definition

A sustained or repeated note (usually the dominant in the bass) that retards harmonic progression, employed at the end of contrasting middle sections to prevent too rapid or distant harmonic development and to create simultaneous repose and suspense before the recapitulation.

# Core Definition

The pedal point is a retarding device used where the harmony must be prevented from developing too rapidly or too distantly. It should be employed where harmonic repose and suspense are needed simultaneously -- repose through cessation of modulatory movement, and suspense in anticipation of the theme's re-entrance. Usually the sustained note is in the bass (dominant pedal), with the upper voices carrying out a progression from V to V. As an inverted pedal, it can be a sustained or repeated note in another voice. The pedal can be developed into a pedal figure, where multiple voices participate in an ostinato-like formulation.

# Prerequisites

- **Liquidation in scherzo**: The pedal point typically follows or accompanies the liquidation process

# Key Properties

- A sustained or repeated note, typically the dominant, in the bass voice
- Upper voices may move freely above the sustained bass
- Marks the end of the modulatory contrasting middle section
- Creates simultaneous repose (cessation of modulation) and suspense (anticipation of return)
- May be elaborated into a pedal figure with ostinato-like treatment
- An inverted pedal places the sustained note in an upper voice
- Often reinforced by dwelling upon an upbeat chord (dominant, artificial dominant)
- Not a cadence -- it retards the harmony rather than resolving it

# Construction / Recognition

1. Look for a sustained or repeated note in the bass at the end of the B-section
2. Check that upper voices move above the sustained note
3. Verify the function: retarding (preventing further modulation), not cadential (resolving)
4. Note any elaboration into a pedal figure (ostinato-like formulation)
5. Check for inverted pedal (sustained note in upper voice)

# Context & Application

The pedal point typically appears at the transition between the contrasting middle section and the recapitulation, in both minuets and scherzos. It functions as a structural marker, signaling that the modulatory process has concluded and the return is imminent. The technique extends into sonata-allegro elaboration sections as well.

# Examples

- Beethoven, Op. 26, Scherzo: Upbeat harmony reached at m. 25 and continued through m. 44 on a sustained dominant
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-III: Motive reduced to three notes, then reassembled in chains of eighth-notes moving to the dominant
- Ex. 122f, m. 20-22: All three upper voices participate in an ostinato-like pedal figure
- Ex. 122g, m. 17-21: Part of the modulation carried out over a similar pedal figure

# Common Errors

- Treating the pedal point as a cadence (it retards, not resolves)
- Making the pedal too brief to create the necessary repose
- Forgetting that the pedal need not be literally sustained -- repeated notes and ostinato figures serve the same function

# Common Confusions

- **Pedal point vs. cadence**: A pedal retards harmonic progression; a cadence resolves it. The pedal prevents further development; the cadence closes.
- **Sustained vs. repeated**: The pedal need not be literally sustained; repeated notes or ostinato figures function identically.
- **Bass vs. inverted**: An inverted pedal (in an upper voice) has the same retarding function.
