# Quick Definition
A sustained or repeated note (usually the dominant in the bass) that retards harmonic progression, employed at the end of contrasting middle sections to prevent too rapid or distant harmonic development and to prepare for the recapitulation.

# Formal Definition
The pedal point is a retarding device used where the harmony must be prevented from developing too rapidly or too distantly. It should be employed at the point where harmonic repose and suspense are needed simultaneously -- repose through cessation of modulatory movement, and suspense in anticipation of the theme's re-entrance. Usually the sustained note is in the bass (dominant pedal), with the upper voices carrying out a progression from V to V. As an inverted pedal, it can be a sustained or repeated note in another voice. The pedal can be developed into a pedal figure, where multiple voices participate in an ostinato-like formulation.

# Construction/Recognition
- A sustained or repeated note, typically the dominant, in the bass voice
- Upper voices may move freely above the sustained bass
- Marks the end of the modulatory contrasting middle section
- Creates simultaneous repose (cessation of modulation) and suspense (anticipation of return)
- May be elaborated into a pedal figure with ostinato-like treatment
- An inverted pedal places the sustained note in an upper voice
- Often reinforced by dwelling upon an upbeat chord (dominant, artificial dominant)

# Musical Context
The pedal point typically appears at the transition between the contrasting middle section and the recapitulation. It functions as a structural marker, signaling to the listener that the modulatory process has concluded and the return of the main theme is imminent. The technique is shared between minuets and scherzos, and extends into sonata-allegro elaboration sections.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 26, Scherzo: Upbeat harmony reached at m. 25 and continued through m. 44 on a sustained dominant
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-III: Motive reduced to three notes, then reassembled in chains of eighth-notes moving to the dominant
- Ex. 122f, m. 20-22: All three upper voices participate in an ostinato-like pedal figure

# Common Confusions
- A pedal point is not a cadence -- it retards the harmony rather than resolving it
- The pedal point need not be literally sustained; repeated notes or ostinato figures serve the same function
- An inverted pedal (in an upper voice) functions the same way as a bass pedal
