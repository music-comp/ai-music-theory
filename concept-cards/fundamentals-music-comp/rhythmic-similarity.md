# Quick Definition
The use of shared or related rhythmic patterns as a unifying element when connecting motive-forms, one of the most effective means of producing coherence.

# Formal Definition
Rhythmic similarities act as unifying elements in the connection of motive-forms. As established in Chapter 3, preservation of rhythmic features effectively produces coherence. Rhythmic similarity is one of three principal factors (alongside common content and coherent harmony) that contribute to logic in the connection of motive-forms.

# Construction/Recognition
Rhythmic similarity is achieved by:
- Preserving the rhythmic profile of the basic motive while changing other features
- Using related rhythmic patterns that share key characteristics (e.g., similar attack patterns, similar groupings)
- Maintaining consistent rhythmic characteristics even when intervals and contour change

Ex. 32 demonstrates the principle most clearly: rhythm is strictly preserved while intervals and direction change, producing closely related motive-forms that are often used in traditional music to construct entire themes.

# Musical Context
Rhythm is the most cohesion-producing feature of a motive. When rhythm is preserved, even substantial changes in intervals and direction still produce recognizable relationships. This is why rhythmic similarity is listed as one of the three fundamental connecting principles. In traditional music, the technique of preserving rhythm while transposing to different degrees is a standard method for producing entire themes.

# Common Confusions
- Rhythmic similarity does not require identical rhythm; related patterns also unify
- Preserving rhythm does not prevent monotony by itself; slight rhythmic changes are still needed
- Rhythmic similarity is the most reliable connecting device, but it is not the only one
