# Quick Definition

The technique of gradually depriving motive-forms of their characteristic features and dissolving them into uncharacteristic forms (scales, broken chords, etc.) to neutralize the emphasis of the sequential process and prepare for the recapitulation.

# Core Definition

Liquidation is the technique of gradually depriving motive-forms of their characteristic features and dissolving them into uncharacteristic forms, such as scales, broken chords, and the like. The emphasis contributed by a sequence (a form of repetition) creates obligations. It is necessary to neutralize these obligations in order to introduce the recapitulation in a manner at once "surprising and expected," as Beethoven expressed it. Such unpretentious forms as scale lines, broken chords, and similar figures are neutral enough to cancel the emphasis of the sequential process.

# Prerequisites

- **Pattern and sequence in elaboration**: The sequential process that creates the obligations to be neutralized
- **Liquidation**: The general technique (from Ch. 8)

# Key Properties

- Progressive reduction of the pattern's length (e.g., four measures to two, to one, to fractions)
- Characteristic melodic intervals replaced by scale lines or broken chords
- The contour is "depreciated" into inconspicuous forms
- Often combined with dwelling on the dominant (ostinato-like treatment of residues)
- The end of liquidation is marked by a combination of repose and suspense
- A pedal point is appropriate at this point to prevent further harmonic progression
- The pedal may be developed into a pedal figure (multiple voices participating in ostinato-like formulation)

# Construction / Recognition

1. After the sequence, look for progressive reduction of the pattern's length
2. Note the replacement of characteristic intervals with scales, broken chords
3. Check for dwelling on the dominant or upbeat harmony
4. Look for ostinato-like treatment of residues
5. Identify the pedal point (bass or inverted)
6. The combination of repose and suspense signals readiness for recapitulation

# Context & Application

Liquidation in the scherzo is the same technique described in Chapter 8 for the sentence, applied at a larger structural scale. It bridges the modulatory section and the recapitulation, functioning as a transition from instability to stability. Once the upbeat harmony is reached, further modulation becomes unnecessary.

# Examples

- Beethoven, Op. 26, Scherzo: Four-measure pattern reduced to two (m. 25-26); varied repetition; further reduced to five notes (m. 28); to four notes (m. 29-30); to two notes (m. 33 ff.); to one note (m. 41 ff.). Upbeat harmony reached at m. 25 and continued through m. 44.
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 4-III: Four-measure pattern liquidated by reduction to two measures, one measure, and two-thirds of a measure
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 1-III, Menuetto: Gradual liquidation resembling the scherzo practice form
- Ex. 122u: Contour of the pattern (m. 9-12) depreciated into a descending scale line; m. 25-29 dwell on the dominant with ostinato-like residues
- Exs. 122b, c, d: Two-measure patterns reduced to two notes (indicated by brackets at m. 21 ff.)

# Common Errors

- Liquidating too abruptly (sounds forced)
- Liquidating too gradually (loses momentum)
- Confusing liquidation with mere shortening (liquidation specifically removes characteristic features)

# Common Confusions

- **Liquidation vs. shortening**: Liquidation specifically involves the removal of characteristic features, not just reduction of length.
- **Liquidation vs. retransition**: Liquidation is a motivic process (removing features); retransition is a harmonic process (returning to the tonic). They often overlap but are conceptually distinct.
