# Quick Definition
The technique of gradually depriving motive-forms of their characteristic features and dissolving them into uncharacteristic forms (scales, broken chords, etc.) to neutralize the emphasis of the sequential process and prepare for the recapitulation.

# Formal Definition
Liquidation is the technique of gradually depriving motive-forms of their characteristic features and dissolving them into uncharacteristic forms, such as scales, broken chords, and the like. The emphasis contributed by a sequence (a form of repetition) creates obligations. It is necessary to neutralize these obligations in order to introduce the recapitulation in a manner at once "surprising and expected," as Beethoven expressed it. Such unpretentious forms as scale lines, broken chords, and similar figures are neutral enough to cancel the emphasis of the sequential process.

# Construction/Recognition
- Progressive reduction of the pattern's length (e.g., four measures to two, to one, to fractions)
- Characteristic melodic intervals are replaced by scale lines or broken chords
- Rhythmic features may be simplified or made uniform
- The contour of the pattern is "depreciated" into inconspicuous forms
- Often combined with dwelling on the dominant (ostinato-like treatment of residues)
- The end of liquidation is marked by a combination of repose and suspense
- A pedal point is appropriate at this point

# Musical Context
Liquidation in the scherzo is the same technique described in Chapter 8 for the sentence, applied here at a larger structural scale. It bridges the modulatory section and the recapitulation, functioning as a transition from instability to stability. The technique requires taste and judgment: too abrupt a liquidation sounds forced, while too gradual a one loses momentum.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 26, Scherzo: Four-measure pattern reduced to two (m. 25-26); after varied repetition, further reduced to five notes (m. 28); to four notes (m. 29-30); to two notes (m. 33 ff.); to one note (m. 41 ff.). Upbeat harmony reached at m. 25 and continued through m. 44.
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 4-III: Four-measure pattern liquidated by reduction to two measures, one measure, and finally two-thirds of a measure
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 1-III, Menuetto: Gradual liquidation resembling the scherzo practice form
- Exs. 122b, c, d: Two-measure patterns reduced to two notes (indicated by brackets at m. 21 ff.)

# Common Confusions
- Liquidation is not mere shortening -- it specifically involves the removal of characteristic features
- Liquidation is not the same as a retransition; it is a motivic process, not merely a harmonic one
- The term applies both to small-scale sentence construction (Ch. 8) and to large-scale scherzo elaboration sections
