# Quick Definition
The practice of consciously identifying the significant features of the basic motive and determining whether those features -- rather than less characteristic ones -- are being developed in the composition.

# Core Definition
Be conscious of the significant features of the basic motive, and determine whether they, or the less characteristic, are developed. There may be empty segments with no real content, without significant melodic or rhythmic movement, even without harmonic change. Self-critical analysis identifies these weaknesses and ensures that the most important motivic features receive appropriate development.

# Prerequisites
- self-criticism: This is the second step in the self-criticism framework

# Key Properties
- Conscious identification of the basic motive's significant features
- Determining whether significant features (not less characteristic ones) are developed
- Detecting empty segments: passages with no real content
- Empty segments lack significant melodic movement, rhythmic movement, or harmonic change
- Connects to the broader concept of developing variation

# Construction / Recognition
When analysing one's work: (1) identify the significant features of the basic motive (intervals, rhythmic patterns, contour), (2) check whether subsequent development uses these significant features, (3) look for empty segments -- passages with no real content, (4) determine whether less characteristic features have been mistakenly developed at the expense of more important ones.

# Context & Application
This principle connects to Schoenberg's broader concept of developing variation: the significant features of the basic motive should be the source of all subsequent development. When less characteristic features are developed instead, the result lacks coherence and focus.

# Examples
- Ex. 105a: the repeated notes in the first eight measures are somewhat overdone, as are the parallel thirds in the last half -- features intended to demonstrate motival characteristics in recognizable form
- Empty segments may arise from passages that lack significant melodic movement, rhythmic movement, or harmonic change

# Common Errors
- Developing less characteristic features at the expense of significant ones
- Failing to notice empty segments
- Being unconscious of what the basic motive's significant features actually are

# Common Confusions
- "Significant features" are not necessarily the most prominent or obvious ones; they are the ones that define the motive's identity and offer richest development potential
- An empty segment is not the same as a rest or silence; it is a passage that gives the illusion of content without developing anything meaningful
