# Quick Definition

The fundamental tension in composition between the need for repetition (which produces unity and intelligibility) and the danger of monotony (which results from too much unvaried repetition), resolved through variation.

# Core Definition

"A motive appears constantly throughout a piece: it is repeated. Repetition alone often gives rise to monotony. Monotony can only be overcome by variation" (Ch. 3, p. 8). This tension is further elaborated in Chapter 5: "Intelligibility in music seems to be impossible without repetition. While repetition without variation can easily produce monotony, juxtaposition of distantly related elements can easily degenerate into nonsense, especially if unifying elements are omitted" (Ch. 5, p. 20).

# Prerequisites

- **motive** -- The motive is the unit that is repeated and varied

# Key Properties

1. Repetition is necessary for intelligibility
2. Unvaried repetition produces monotony
3. Monotony can only be overcome by variation
4. Too much variety (without repetition) produces incoherence ("nonsense")
5. Only as much variation as character, length, and tempo require should be admitted
6. The coherence of motive-forms should be emphasized
7. Delimitation, subdivision, and simple repetition control excessive variety
8. Rapid tempo reduces the permissible degree of variety

# Construction / Recognition

The composer must navigate between two extremes:

1. **Too much unvaried repetition** produces monotony
2. **Too much variety** without repetition produces incoherence
3. **The balance point** depends on tempo, character, and length
4. Use delimitation to set boundaries on variation
5. Use subdivision to break complex passages into graspable units
6. Use simple repetition to anchor the listener's comprehension
7. Emphasize the coherence of motive-forms even when introducing variety

# Context & Application

This tension is the driving force behind developing variation. The classical masters sought a "popular touch" in their themes, balancing accessibility with sophistication. Schoenberg quotes Mattheson (1739): "A theme should have a certain something which the whole world already knows" (Ch. 5, p. 20). Pieces in rapid tempo exhibit a lesser degree of variety because "rapidity obstructs one's grasp of an idea" (Ch. 5, p. 20).

# Examples

**Example 1** (Ch. 3, p. 8): Beethoven's Fifth Symphony -- note repetitions contribute distinctive characteristics while the rhythmic motive undergoes extensive variation.

**Example 2** (Ch. 5, p. 20): The eighteenth-century ideal of the "popular touch" in themes reflects the need for graspable repetition balanced with variety.

**Example 3** (Ch. 5, p. 20): The "ars nova" of the eighteenth century detaching itself from contrapuntal complexity in favor of more immediately intelligible repetition-based structures.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Avoiding repetition entirely to prevent monotony
  **Correction**: "Intelligibility in music seems to be impossible without repetition" -- the solution is variation, not elimination

- **Error**: Introducing too many contrasts in rapid succession
  **Correction**: Rapidity obstructs comprehension; fast tempos require less variety

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing repetition is inherently bad
  **Clarification**: Repetition is necessary for intelligibility; only unvaried repetition produces monotony

- **Confusion**: Equating monotony with simplicity
  **Clarification**: A simple idea can be varied endlessly; monotony arises from lack of variation, not from simplicity

- **Confusion**: Interpreting "nonsense" as a value judgment
  **Clarification**: Schoenberg uses "nonsense" to describe incoherent juxtaposition of unrelated elements, a technical observation
