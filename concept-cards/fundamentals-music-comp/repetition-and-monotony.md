# Quick Definition
The fundamental tension in composition between the need for repetition (which produces unity and intelligibility) and the danger of monotony (which results from too much unvaried repetition).

# Formal Definition
A motive appears constantly throughout a piece: it is repeated. Repetition alone often gives rise to monotony. Monotony can only be overcome by variation. Intelligibility in music seems to be impossible without repetition, yet repetition without variation can easily produce monotony. Conversely, juxtaposition of distantly related elements can easily degenerate into nonsense, especially if unifying elements are omitted.

# Construction/Recognition
The composer must navigate between two extremes:
- Too much unvaried repetition produces monotony
- Too much variety without repetition produces incoherence ("nonsense")

Only so much variation as character, length, and tempo require should be admitted. The coherence of motive-forms should be emphasized. Delimitation, subdivision, and simple repetition are the most useful means of controlling the tendency toward too rapid development.

# Musical Context
This tension is the driving force behind developing variation. Every compositional decision involves balancing repetition against variety. The balance point depends on context: pieces in rapid tempo require less variety because rapidity already obstructs the listener's grasp of ideas. Popular music requires more discretion (less variety) for immediate intelligibility, but this discretion is equally characteristic of classical masters.

# Examples
- Beethoven's Fifth Symphony: note repetitions contribute distinctive characteristics while the rhythmic motive is varied extensively
- Schoenberg cites the classical masters as exemplars of balancing repetition and variety
- The eighteenth-century ideal of the "popular touch" in themes reflects the need for graspable repetition

# Common Confusions
- Repetition is not inherently bad; it is necessary for intelligibility
- Monotony is not the same as simplicity; a simple idea can be varied endlessly
- The solution to monotony is variation, not the abandonment of repetition
- "Nonsense" in Schoenberg's usage means incoherent juxtaposition, not absurdity in the colloquial sense
