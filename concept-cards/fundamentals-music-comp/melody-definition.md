# Quick Definition
A melody is a self-contained, balanced musical line that re-establishes repose through balance, comparable to an apercu or aphorism in its rapid advance from problem to solution.

# Formal Definition
The concentration of the main idea in a single melodic line requires a special kind of balance and organization, only partly explicable in terms of technique. A melody re-establishes repose through balance. The unrest in a melody need not reach below the surface. A melody tends to achieve balance in the most direct way: it avoids intensifying the unrest, supports comprehensibility by limitation, facilitates lucidity through subdivision, and extends itself rather by continuation than by elaboration or development.

# Construction/Recognition
A melody tends toward regularity, simple repetitions, and even symmetry. It reveals distinct phrasing. It uses slightly varied motive-forms that achieve variety by presenting basic features in different situations. It remains within the closer harmonic relationships. It can be called two-dimensional, comprising chiefly interval and latent harmony, since rhythmic characteristics are less decisive.

# Musical Context
All restrictions and limitations of melody produce that independence and self-determination because of which a melody requires no addition, continuation, or elaboration. A melody is complete in itself. The concept of the melodious in instrumental melody developed as a free adaptation from the vocal model.

# Examples
- Beethoven String Quartet Op. 132-III: a structurally instrumental theme that nonetheless makes the impression of a melody (Ex. 96a)
- Contours from Bach English Suites (Ex. 97), Haydn String Quartets (Ex. 98), Mozart (Ex. 99), and Beethoven Piano Sonatas (Ex. 100) show melodies proceeding in waves
- Melodies seldom move long in one direction; their amplitude varies

# Common Confusions
- Melody is not simply any sequence of tones; it is specifically a self-contained, balanced formulation that resolves its own unrest
- The term "melody" is often used loosely for any tune, but Schoenberg gives it a precise technical meaning that distinguishes it from "theme"
