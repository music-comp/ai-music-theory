# Quick Definition
A melody is a self-contained, balanced musical line that re-establishes repose through balance, comparable to an apercu or aphorism in its rapid advance from problem to solution.

# Core Definition
The concentration of the main idea in a single melodic line requires a special kind of balance and organization, only partly explicable in terms of technique. A melody re-establishes repose through balance. The unrest in a melody need not reach below the surface. A melody tends to achieve balance in the most direct way: it avoids intensifying the unrest, supports comprehensibility by limitation, facilitates lucidity through subdivision, and extends itself rather by continuation than by elaboration or development. Since rhythmic characteristics are less decisive in a melody, it could be called two-dimensional, comprising chiefly interval and latent harmony.

# Prerequisites
No strict prerequisites, though understanding of motive, phrase, and harmony is helpful.

# Key Properties
- Two-dimensional: comprises chiefly interval and latent harmony (rhythmic development less decisive)
- Tends toward regularity, simple repetitions, and even symmetry
- Reveals distinct phrasing
- Uses slightly varied motive-forms in different situations
- Remains within closer harmonic relationships
- Extends by continuation rather than elaboration or development
- Independent and self-determined: requires no addition, continuation, or elaboration
- Proceeds in waves (each elevation countered by a depression)

# Construction / Recognition
A melody is recognized by: (1) distinct phrasing, (2) tendency toward regularity and symmetry, (3) slightly varied motive-forms rather than radical transformations, (4) closer harmonic relationships, (5) continuation rather than development, (6) self-contained balance, (7) wave-like contour with graduated approach to climax.

# Context & Application
The concept of the melodious in instrumental melody developed as a free adaptation from the vocal model. All restrictions and limitations of melody produce that independence and self-determination because of which a melody requires no addition, continuation, or elaboration. The melody is a simpler formulation than the theme, but simplicity here means concentration, not superficiality. "There is no nobler contrast than to speak lightly of a great grief."

# Examples
- Contours from Bach English Suites (Ex. 97), Haydn String Quartets (Ex. 98), Mozart (Ex. 99), and Beethoven Piano Sonatas (Ex. 100) show melodies proceeding in waves
- Beethoven String Quartet Op. 132-III (Ex. 96a, b): structurally instrumental themes that nonetheless make the impression of melodies
- Melodies seldom move long in one direction; their amplitude varies

# Common Errors
- Confusing any sequence of tones with a melody
- Attempting to develop a melody as though it were a theme
- Moving too long in one direction without wave-like compensation

# Common Confusions
- Melody is not simply any tune; Schoenberg gives it a precise technical meaning that distinguishes it from "theme"
- The simplicity of melody is a simplicity of formulation, not of content; profound ideas can be expressed in melodic form
- The term "melody" is often used loosely, but here it refers specifically to a self-contained, balanced formulation
