# Quick Definition

The technique of moving through keys by transposing a motivic pattern to successive degrees, used as the chief modulatory device in the contrasting middle section of the minuet.

# Core Definition

Sequential modulation is the process of transposing a clearly delimited motivic segment (pattern) through a series of harmonic degrees, thereby effecting a modulation. In the minuet's contrasting middle section, this is the primary means of harmonic movement. The pattern is typically a phrase of two to four measures, derived from A-section material but modified for modulatory purposes. The sequence follows a logical harmonic plan, often progressing through the circle of fifths. After the sequential modulation, the harmony typically settles on the dominant via a pedal point to prepare for the recapitulation.

# Key Properties

- A clearly delimited pattern is established (usually 2-4 measures)
- The pattern is transposed to successive degrees (e.g., iii-vi-II-V)
- Motive-forms from the A-section are adapted to the modulatory procedure
- The order of motive-forms may be changed, and some may be repeated within the segment
- The end of the sequential process requires neutralization through liquidation
- Remotely varied motive-forms are dangerous in sequential passages because modulation already introduces instability
- The sequence need not be exact; variation of the sequence is common and desirable

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify the pattern (clearly delimited motivic segment, typically 2-4 measures)
2. Trace the transposition to successive degrees
3. Check the harmonic plan (circle of fifths is common)
4. Note how A-section motive-forms have been adapted for modulation
5. Look for the liquidation that follows the sequential process
6. Verify the dominant pedal point preparation for recapitulation

# Context & Application

Sequential modulation is the most characteristic feature of the minuet's B-section, and the same technique is shared with many scherzos and more developed forms. It provides both structural clarity (through the repetition inherent in the sequence) and harmonic movement (through the transposition). The technique scales from the minuet to the sonata-allegro development section.

# Examples

- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-III: Two-measure phrase appears four times, moving through the circle of fifths (iii-vi-II-V)
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-III: Sequential modulation leads to the subdominant region
- Mozart, String Quartet in A, K.V. 464: Sequence of motive b accompanied by the interval of a fourth derived by reduction from both a and b

# Common Errors

- Using remotely varied motive-forms during sequential modulation (the instability is already high)
- Failing to plan the sequence so the last transposition connects to the upbeat harmony
- Omitting the liquidation after the sequential process

# Common Confusions

- **Sequential modulation vs. mere transposition**: Sequential modulation involves genuine movement through keys, not just pitch displacement.
- **Sequence variation**: The sequence need not be a strict transposition; variation is common and desirable.
