# Quick Definition
Modified sequences transpose only the melody or only the harmony (with the other element freely varied), while partial sequences transpose some features but vary others; both represent departures from strict sequential treatment toward freer compositional practice.

# Formal Definition
Sequential treatment is not always complete. It may be applied to either melody or harmony alone, with the remaining element more or less freely varied. Such cases may be called modified or partial sequences. In other cases, the general character of a transposed repetition may be present, though without literal transposition of any element. These instances, and others involving variation of some features, may be described as "sequentially controlled" -- the sequential principle guides the construction without dictating every detail.

# Construction/Recognition
- Modified sequence: one element (melody or harmony) is transposed while the other is freely varied
- Partial sequence: some features are transposed while others are varied
- Sequentially controlled: general character of transposition without literal transposition of any single element
- Look for the "impression" of sequential motion even when strict transposition is absent
- The freer the sequence, the more it resembles developing variation

# Musical Context
In actual practice, strict sequences are the exception rather than the rule. Most sequential passages in the literature employ modified, partial, or sequentially controlled treatments. This reflects the tension between the structural clarity that sequences provide and the need for harmonic and melodic flexibility. The student should understand strict sequences as a foundation but recognize that masterworks typically employ freer treatments.

# Common Confusions
- "Modified" and "partial" are not failures to achieve a strict sequence; they are compositional choices that offer greater flexibility.
- Sequentially controlled passages may not contain any literally transposed element yet still sound sequential in character.
- The distinction between a very free sequence and ordinary developing variation can be a matter of interpretation.
