# Quick Definition
The third main division of sonata-allegro form, restating the exposition's material with the subordinate group transposed to the tonic region, the transition often heightened and lengthened, and the composer's artistry applied through variation, omissions, extensions, additions, harmonic changes, and even reconstruction -- all while maintaining recognizability.

# Core Definition
As in the larger rondos, the minimum change in the recapitulation is the transposition of the subordinate group to the tonic region. Since no modulation is necessary, one might expect the transition to disappear here. On the contrary, its effect is usually heightened, and it is often lengthened. Unless the subordinate group contains modulatory elements, the transition now provides the only contrast to the tonic region which governs the entire recapitulation and coda. Thus its value as a contrast becomes more significant. A composer's artistry usually demands more than the minimum necessary changes. Variation, after all, is a merit in itself.

# Key Properties
- Minimum change: subordinate group transposed to tonic region
- Transition usually heightened and lengthened (not eliminated)
- Transition provides the only contrast to the tonic region in the recapitulation
- Composer's artistry demands more than minimum changes
- Possible modifications: reductions, omissions, extensions, additions, harmonic changes, modulations, register/setting changes, contrapuntal treatment, reconstruction
- Repetition must remain recognizable, especially where themes enter
- The "adventures" of themes during elaboration and functional changes require modification
- Variation is a merit in itself

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the return of the principal theme (may begin with literal repetition or immediate variation)
2. Note how the transition has been modified (usually heightened, lengthened, redirected to tonic)
3. Verify the subordinate group is transposed to the tonic region
4. Look for omissions, additions, extensions, or reconstruction
5. Check for contrapuntal or harmonic enrichment
6. Identify where the coda begins (if present)

# Context & Application
The recapitulation is not a mere restatement but an artistic reinterpretation of the exposition material. The functional changes due to placement in the form -- the tonic now governing everything rather than just the principal theme -- require adaptation. The transition's enhanced role as the sole source of tonal contrast makes it more important than in the exposition.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I: first 19 measures unchanged (m. 225-43); m. 20-31 omitted, replaced by 7-measure segment; transition changes from m. 255; remainder transposed to tonic; no coda
- Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I: leading theme exact (m. 139-46); codettas of m. 9-12 omitted; new transitional segment replaces m. 13-20; subordinate theme in c instead of g; coda inaugurated by deceptive progression to flat VI (m. 218)
- Beethoven, Op. 7-I: leading theme diverted in fifteenth measure toward subdominant; transition eight measures shorter; subordinate group in principal key with superficial changes
- Beethoven, Op. 10/2-I: curious recapitulation beginning in D (submediant major, m. 118); first twelve measures of principal theme in D, then modified to approach tonic

# Common Errors
- Simply transposing everything without any artistic variation
- Eliminating the transition, losing the only tonal contrast in the recapitulation
- Varying themes so much that they become unrecognizable
- Failing to account for the functional changes caused by the tonic-region context

# Common Confusions
- One might expect the transition to disappear in the recapitulation since no modulation is needed; instead, it is usually heightened and lengthened because it provides the only contrast to the prevailing tonic
- The recapitulation is not a literal repeat; the "adventures" during the elaboration and the changed formal context require modification
- Variation is valued for its own sake, not merely as a necessity
