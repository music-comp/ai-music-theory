# Quick Definition
The third main division of the sonata-allegro, restating the exposition's material with the subordinate group transposed to the tonic, typically with varied, extended, or reconstructed passages reflecting the themes' "adventures" during the elaboration.

# Formal Definition
As in the larger rondos, the minimum change in the recapitulation is the transposition of the subordinate group to the tonic region. Since no modulation is necessary, one might expect the transition to disappear; on the contrary, its effect is usually heightened, and it is often lengthened. Unless the subordinate group contains modulatory elements, the transition now provides the only contrast to the tonic region which governs the entire recapitulation and coda. A composer's artistry usually demands more than the minimum necessary changes.

# Construction/Recognition
Possible modifications include:
- **Transition**: effect heightened and often lengthened; may be redirected toward subdominant before reaching tonic
- **Variations**: reductions, omissions, extensions, additions, harmonic changes, changes of register and setting, contrapuntal treatment, even reconstruction
- **Recognition**: the repetition must remain recognizable, especially where themes enter
- **Functional changes**: the "adventures" of the themes during elaboration, and their changed position in the form, nearly always require modification
- The transition's value as contrast becomes more significant since it provides the only relief from the prevailing tonic

# Musical Context
The recapitulation resolves the tonal conflict of the exposition by bringing all material into the tonic orbit. The composer's challenge is to achieve this resolution while maintaining interest through artful variation and adaptation.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I: first 19 measures unchanged; m. 20-31 omitted, replaced by a seven-measure segment; transition changes from m. 255; remainder transposed to tonic with slight changes
- Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I: leading theme exact (m. 139-46) but codettas omitted; a new transitional segment replaces the original; subordinate group in c instead of g
- Beethoven, Op. 7-I: leading theme (m. 189) diverted in fifteenth measure toward subdominant; transitional passage eight measures shorter
- Beethoven, Op. 10/2-I: begins in D (submediant major) -- a curious start; principal theme returns in F only at m. 137; ambiguous segment from exposition is reorganized with a ten-measure modulatory insertion

# Common Confusions
- The recapitulation is not merely a transposed copy of the exposition; artful variation is expected and the transition's role changes fundamentally
- The transition paradoxically becomes more important in the recapitulation, not less, because it provides the only harmonic contrast to the prevailing tonic
- Beginning the recapitulation in a key other than the tonic (as in Op. 10/2-I) is unusual but not impossible
