# Quick Definition
Instrumental melodies admit much more freedom than vocal melodies in range, intervals, and figuration, but are still best governed by vocal principles, with "freedom prospering best when under control."

# Core Definition
The freedom of instrumental melodies is restricted by the technical limitations of the various instruments, which differ in nature and degree from those of vocal music, particularly with respect to compass. Nevertheless, an instrumental melody should ideally still be such that it could be sung, "if only by a voice of incredible capacity." The concept of the melodious in instrumental melody has developed as a free adaptation from the vocal model. "Freedom prospers best when under control."

# Key Properties
- Instrumental melodies have more freedom in range, intervals, and figuration
- The vocal model remains the ideal foundation
- "Freedom prospers best when under control"
- Many instrumental sections are themes, not melodies (the distinction matters)
- Some instrumental passages are etude-like, neither melody nor theme
- In classical music, the difference between vocal and instrumental melodies was not very great
- In opera, vocal versions of instrumental melodies were sometimes simplified or embellished

# Construction / Recognition
To assess whether an instrumental passage is a melody, consider whether it possesses the balance, self-containment, and wave-like motion characteristic of vocal melody. Many sections in instrumental music cannot be called melodies though they contain nothing unmelodious. Some are etude-like. Others must be classified as themes.

# Context & Application
In classical music, distinctly pianistic passages could become perfectly singable with slight changes (simplifying arpeggios, eliminating large leaps). In classical operas, when the voice repeated an instrumental melody from the prelude, the melody was sometimes simplified or embellished for the voice. Today the gap between instrumental and vocal melody is larger, partly because singers' abilities have not kept pace with instrumental technique.

# Examples
- Beethoven Rondo Op. 2/2-IV: the brilliant arpeggio in m. 1, when simplified, produces a perfectly singable melody (Ex. 92)
- Many Beethoven piano sonata movements contain melodies any singer could perform: Op. 2/2-II, Op. 7-IV, Op. 10/1-II, Op. 10/3-III, Op. 13-II
- Etude-like passages: Op. 7-IV m. 64; Op. 10/2-I m. 95 ff.; Op. 22-IV m. 72-79

# Common Errors
- Assuming instrumental freedom means freedom from all melodic principles
- Confusing etude-like passages or themes with melodies
- Ignoring the vocal model entirely in instrumental writing

# Common Confusions
- Instrumental freedom does not mean freedom from all melodic principles; the underlying vocal model remains the ideal
- Not every instrumental passage is a melody; many are themes, etude-like passages, or developmental figuration
