# Quick Definition
Character refers to the overall expressive quality of a musical piece or passage, encompassing the emotion it should produce, the mood in which it was composed, and the manner in which it must be played.

# Core Definition
The term "character," applied to music, refers not only to the emotion which the piece should produce and the mood in which it was composed, but also the manner in which it must be played. Character is a composite quality arising from the interaction of multiple musical elements -- tempo, accompaniment, rhythm, texture, and thematic material -- rather than from any single parameter alone. It is fallacious to think that tempo indications determine character. There is not one adagio character, but hundreds; not one scherzo character, but thousands.

# Prerequisites
No formal prerequisites. Character is a foundational concept that applies to all composition.

# Key Properties
- Character is composite: it arises from the combined effect of thematic material, accompaniment, rhythm, tempo, texture, and articulation
- Tempo contributes something but not everything to character
- The accompaniment plays an important role in establishing character
- Rhythmic features are a principal means of distinguishing characters
- All good music consists of many contrasting ideas within a single movement
- Heraclitus called contrast "the principle of development"

# Construction / Recognition
Character is established through the combined effect of: (1) thematic material and its continuation, (2) type of accompaniment, (3) rhythmic characteristics, (4) tempo, (5) texture, and (6) articulation. To recognize character, compare the totality of these elements rather than isolating any single feature.

# Context & Application
Every composition, even the smallest exercise, should be conceived with a special character in mind. A poem, story, play, or moving picture may provide the stimulus to express definite moods. The student should compose pieces that differ widely, especially in tempo, rhythm, and metre, to develop the capacity for producing the manifold types of contrast necessary for larger forms.

# Examples
- Beethoven's three "stormy" or "passionate" movements (Op. 27/2-III, Op. 31/2-I, Op. 57-I) share a general emotional category yet differ markedly in character through their thematic material and continuations
- Compare their respective subordinate themes: Op. 27/2-III m. 21 ff., Op. 31/2-I m. 41 ff., Op. 57-I m. 35 ff.
- Characteristic pieces by Chopin and Schumann: Nocturnes, Ballades, Funeral Marches, Romances, Novelettes

# Common Errors
- Relying on tempo alone to establish character
- Failing to conceive a specific character before beginning to compose
- Assuming a single element (e.g., minor key) defines the character

# Common Confusions
- Character is NOT determined solely by tempo -- this is a common fallacy that Schoenberg explicitly warns against
- There is not one "adagio character" but hundreds; not one "scherzo character" but thousands
- An adagio is slow, an allegro is fast -- this contributes something but not everything to character
