# Quick Definition

The B-section of the scherzo, which provides contrast through modulatory elaboration of A-section material, approaching the Durchfuhrung (elaboration) technique of the sonata allegro, with motive-forms passing through fluctuating, unstable harmonic situations.

# Core Definition

The main function of the B-section is to provide contrast. It is generally held that the B-section of a scherzo should be an elaboration (Durchfuhrung), but in fact it often resembles the B-section of the minuet, while many minuets possess a modulatory contrast. In the modulatory middle section, changes of shape and even of constitution occur as A-section material passes through fluctuating, unstable harmonic situations. This freedom of structure and motive treatment does not imply that regularity, logic, and balance can be ignored. Schoenberg rejected the term "development" on the grounds that little growth or evolution takes place; rather, motive-forms are adapted, varied, expanded, condensed, recombined, and carried through various keys.

# Key Properties

- Motive-forms from the A-section are modified and adapted for modulatory purposes
- The modulation is carried out at least partly by sequential treatment
- Harmony is fluctuating and unstable, contrasting with the A-section's stability
- The order of motive-forms may be changed; some may be repeated within segments
- Remotely varied motive-forms are dangerous due to harmonic instability
- The section concludes with liquidation and a pedal point preparing the recapitulation
- Modulation should proceed gradually, not in one great leap

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify the motive-forms and trace their derivation from the A-section
2. Follow the modulation plan (often sequential)
3. Check for pattern-sequence construction
4. Note the liquidation process toward the end
5. Identify the pedal point preparing the recapitulation
6. Assess the degree of elaboration (simple contrast vs. genuine Durchfuhrung)

# Context & Application

The modulatory contrasting middle section represents the scherzo's most distinctive structural feature, differentiating it from the minuet. Most classical scherzos have in common at least partial sequential treatment in the modulation. The diversity of construction is "inexhaustible" -- Schoenberg emphasizes that "there is such a wide latitude for the fantasy of the composer that only a trained mind can control it." Hence, practice-form composition must be supplemented with analysis of master works.

# Examples

- Beethoven, Op. 26, Scherzo: Progressive liquidation from four-measure pattern to two, five notes, four notes, two notes, one note
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 3, Scherzo: Chromatic ascension to supertonic using m. 1-4; second theme element quoted and sequenced; curious dwelling on V of the mediant for sixteen measures
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 4-III: Modulation by many sequences of a four-measure pattern, liquidated to two measures, one measure, and two-thirds of a measure
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 7-III: Circle of fifths from mediant major to subdominant; "recapitulation in the wrong key"
- Brahms, Sextet Op. 18, Scherzo: Contrast by imitations of leading motive, not by sequences
- Brahms, Sextet Op. 36, Scherzo: Imitative sequences as dialogue between upper and lower voices

# Common Errors

- Modulating in one great leap rather than gradually
- Ignoring regularity and logic because the section is "free"
- Using remotely varied motive-forms (already destabilized by modulation)

# Common Confusions

- **Elaboration vs. development**: Schoenberg preferred "elaboration" because the material does not truly "develop" in the sense of growth; it is adapted, varied, and recombined.
- **Scherzo B-section = Durchfuhrung**: Not always; some scherzo B-sections are simple contrasts, and some minuet B-sections are genuine elaborations.
