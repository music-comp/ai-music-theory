# Quick Definition
The harmony at the end of the B-section that leads to the recapitulation, called an "upbeat chord" because it functions like a rhythmic upbeat to the accentuated downbeat of the returning tonic -- regardless of its actual rhythmic placement.

# Core Definition
The B-section ends on a harmony which leads to the recapitulation. In classical music this harmony is the dominant, because it reintroduces the tonic in its tonality-defining sense. Its effect in such cases is comparable to that of an upbeat to the subsequently accentuated downbeat. Because of this function, such chords will be called "upbeat chords," regardless of their rhythmic placement. Other harmonies, and transformations of them, can also function as connectives.

# Key Properties
- Typically the dominant (V) in classical music
- Functions like a rhythmic upbeat to the recapitulation's "downbeat"
- Defined by harmonic function, NOT by rhythmic placement
- Sometimes postponed to the last eighth or sixteenth of the B-section
- A small connective figure often serves as bridge to recapitulation
- Other harmonies can serve as upbeat chords: ii, iii, and their derivatives
- If the recapitulation begins on a degree other than I, the upbeat chord must be chosen accordingly
- bIII can introduce a recapitulation beginning on V
- bVI can introduce a recapitulation beginning on II

# Construction / Recognition
The upbeat chord is identified by: (1) its position at the end of the B-section, (2) its function of leading to and preparing the recapitulation, (3) in classical music it is typically V, (4) a small connective figure often bridges from it to the recapitulation. When the recapitulation begins on a non-tonic degree, the upbeat chord must match.

# Context & Application
The upbeat chord is a critical structural juncture in the small ternary form. It is where the B-section's harmonic exploration resolves into preparation for return. The term "upbeat chord" is Schoenberg's specific coinage, defined by harmonic function rather than rhythmic position. This concept anticipates the retransition in larger forms.

# Common Errors
- Ending the B-section without a clear upbeat chord, leaving the transition to recapitulation unclear
- Using a non-functional harmony at the juncture
- Choosing an upbeat chord inappropriate for a recapitulation that begins on a non-tonic degree

# Common Confusions
- "Upbeat chord" does not refer to rhythmic placement; it is defined by harmonic function (leading to recapitulation) regardless of position in the measure
- The upbeat chord is not always V; other harmonies serve this function when the recapitulation begins on a non-tonic degree
- The connective figure between upbeat chord and recapitulation is a separate element
