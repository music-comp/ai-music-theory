# Quick Definition
A subordinate theme with a singable, song-like character (Gesangsthema), unified by persistent rhythm with freely changing intervals, and constructed loosely in the manner related to popular music.

# Formal Definition
Under the influence of Schubert, theorists began to call the subordinate theme Gesangsthema, or "lyric theme." The lyric or singable character is the result of a loose construction intimately related to that of popular music. The "looseness" consists in disregarding almost all features except the rhythmic ones, thus neglecting the profounder implications, and providing richness of content through the multiplication of themes. The nomenclature had a curious influence on composers, suggesting the creation of longer and longer lyric melodies.

# Construction/Recognition
- Unified by a persistent rhythm with freely changing intervals
- Multiple repetition, unvaried or slightly varied, of relatively long phrases
- Simple internal construction that is self-evident
- Song-like quality that may be more obvious in string writing than piano writing
- In piano style, the lyric quality is achieved through legato markings, sustained phrases, and consistent rhythmic patterns rather than through literally vocal melody

# Musical Context
The lyric theme forms one of the potential contrasts within the subordinate group. While Schubert's influence made it a hallmark of Romantic subordinate themes, lyric subordinate themes already appear in the works of Mozart and Beethoven. It was a mistake, however, to call all subordinate themes "lyric," since many are not lyric at all.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 53-I (m. 35): marked "dolce e molto legato," the same rhythmic pattern appears seven times
- Beethoven, Op. 57-I (m. 36 ff.): within each of three rhythmically identical two-measure phrases, the rhythmic motive appears three times; the lyric character becomes evident when compared with the "agitato" section following (m. 51)
- Beethoven, Op. 13-I (m. 51): an eight-measure theme appears three times
- Beethoven, Fifth Symphony-I (m. 63-93): continuous quarter-note movement for thirty-one measures
- Mozart, Symphony in g, K.V. 550-I (m. 44)
- Schubert examples: Piano Sonata Op. 143-I (m. 60 ff.); String Quartet in d-I (m. 61)
- Brahms examples: String Quartet Op. 51/2-I (m. 46); Third Symphony-I

# Common Confusions
- Not all subordinate themes are lyric; the identification of subordinate theme with Gesangsthema was "a mistake" according to Schoenberg
- The piano does not lend itself readily to the song-like quality; in piano sonatas, the lyric character is produced more through repetition of long phrases than through literally vocal melody
- The "popular touch" in lyric themes refers to the simple, rhythm-dominated construction, not to a lack of artistic quality
