# Quick Definition
A type of subordinate theme characterized by a loose, song-like construction unified by persistent rhythm while disregarding "profounder implications," originating as a Schubertian tradition of calling the subordinate theme "Gesangsthema" (song theme).

# Core Definition
Under the influence of Schubert, theorists began to call the subordinate theme Gesangsthema, or "lyric theme." This was a mistake, for there exist many subordinate themes which are not lyric at all. But this nomenclature had a curious influence on the minds of composers, suggesting the creation of longer and longer lyric melodies. The lyric, or singable, character is the result of a loose construction intimately related to that of popular music. The "looseness" consists in disregarding almost all features except the rhythmic ones, thus neglecting the profounder implications, and providing richness of content through the multiplication of themes.

# Key Properties
- Unified by persistent rhythm rather than motivic development
- Intervals change freely while rhythm remains constant
- Loose construction related to popular music style
- Multiple repetition of relatively long phrases (unvaried or slightly varied)
- Contrast with the principal theme achieved through repetition pattern rather than developmental logic
- Piano style achieves lyric quality through multiple repetition rather than true song-like writing

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for persistent rhythmic patterns maintained across multiple statements
- Note that intervals change freely while the rhythmic figure remains
- Identify multiple repetitions (unvaried or slightly varied) of relatively long phrases
- In piano music, the lyric quality appears as "dolce e molto legato" markings and sustained phrase patterns
- Compare the lyric subordinate theme with any following "agitato" section to clarify its character

# Context & Application
Lyric subordinate themes appear in the works of predecessors of Schubert because they form one of the potential contrasts. However, Schubert's influence made them a more deliberate and frequent feature. The piano does not lend itself readily to the song-like, so in piano sonatas the lyric quality is achieved through multiple repetition and consistent rhythmic patterns rather than through true vocal melody.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 10/1-I (m. 56): lyric subordinate theme
- Beethoven, Op. 53-I (m. 35): "dolce e molto legato" -- substantially the same rhythmic pattern appears seven times
- Beethoven, Op. 57-I (m. 36): within each of three rhythmically identical two-measure phrases, the rhythmic motive appears three times; lyric character becomes evident when compared with the "agitato" section following (m. 51)
- Beethoven, Op. 13-I (m. 51): an eight-measure theme appears three times
- Beethoven, Fifth Symphony-I (m. 63-93): continuous quarter-note movement persists for thirty-one measures; simple construction is self-evident
- Mozart, Symphony in g, K.V. 550-I (m. 44): lyric subordinate theme
- Schubert examples: Piano Sonata Op. 143-I (m. 60), String Quartet in d-I (m. 61), String Quartet Op. 163-I (m. 60)
- Brahms examples: String Quartet Op. 51/2-I (m. 46), String Quintet Op. 111-I (m. 26)

# Common Errors
- Assuming all subordinate themes must be lyric -- many subordinate themes are not lyric at all
- Writing piano lyric themes that are too vocal in character, ignoring the instrument's natural idiom
- Making the lyric theme too developmental, undermining its essential looseness

# Common Confusions
- Calling the subordinate theme "Gesangsthema" was historically a mistake that influenced composition practice; not all subordinate themes are or should be lyric
- The "popular" quality of lyric themes is a compositional feature, not a defect -- it provides necessary contrast through simplicity
- In piano music, the lyric quality is achieved differently than in string or vocal music: through multiple repetition and consistent rhythm rather than sustained melodic line
