# Quick Definition
The special problems arising in minor-key sonata-allegro recapitulations, particularly when subordinate themes originally in major must be restated -- requiring solutions ranging from recapitulation in tonic major (then turning to minor), to extensive reconstruction, to presenting the material in the subdominant or parallel major before returning to the tonic minor.

# Core Definition
The recapitulation in minor presents special problems, particularly when the subordinate group is in major. The interchange of mode upon repetition in the principal key is not always practical; and remaining too long without relief in the minor becomes tiresome. The adaptations made to solve these problems vary widely. To repeat the subordinate theme in minor would change its character and diminish the contrast. Therefore, it usually recurs in tonic major and at some later point turns to the minor.

# Key Properties
- Major-mode subordinate theme repeated in tonic minor changes character and diminishes contrast
- Subordinate theme usually recurs in tonic major, then turns to minor later
- Remaining too long in minor without relief becomes tiresome
- Solutions vary widely from case to case
- May involve recapitulation in the subdominant major (IV)
- May involve tonic major followed by connective passage to tonic minor
- Far-reaching changes often accompany such treatment
- The transition may be completely reconstructed

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify whether the subordinate theme was originally in a major key (III or V)
2. Note whether it recurs in tonic major, tonic minor, subdominant major, or another key
3. Track where the turn to tonic minor occurs
4. Look for far-reaching modifications in the transition and subordinate group
5. Note any added modulatory passages that compensate for the mode issue

# Context & Application
Minor-key sonata movements require the most creative adaptation in their recapitulations. The solutions found by Beethoven and Mozart demonstrate that no single formula applies; each case requires individual treatment based on the specific character of the themes and the needs of the movement.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: transition reconstructed (m. 109) with same motive-forms above different harmonic skeleton; entire subordinate group in principal key (f) without significant change; short codetta added
- Beethoven, Op. 10/1-I: transition (m. 191-214) leads to V of f (v); subordinate theme follows in F (IV!); fourteen measures in major provide contrast; then repeated in c; remainder unchanged
- Beethoven, Op. 13-I: original transition discarded (m. 195); theme diverted to V of f (iv); subordinate theme in f, soon diverted toward c; pervasive modifications of detail
- Beethoven, Op. 18/4-I: entire subordinate group in tonic major (C); return to e only at second codetta (m. 199)
- Mozart, K.V. 421-I: entire subordinate group in tonic minor; from beginning of transition (m. 84), hardly a phrase appears in original form; shifts of half-measure interchange accents; yet recognizability is not endangered

# Common Errors
- Simply transposing a major-mode subordinate theme to tonic minor without considering the character change
- Staying in tonic minor too long without relief, creating monotony
- Failing to provide a convincing turn from major to minor within the recapitulation

# Common Confusions
- There is no single formula for handling minor-key recapitulations; each case requires individual treatment
- "Tonic major" for the subordinate theme is the most common solution, with a later turn to minor
- Some recapitulations place the subordinate theme in the subdominant major (Op. 10/1-I) rather than tonic major
- Mozart's solution of recapitulating entirely in tonic minor with far-reaching modifications (K.V. 421-I) shows that the character change can be managed through sophisticated rewriting
