# Quick Definition
The special problems that arise when recapitulating subordinate themes originally in major within a minor-key movement, since transposing to tonic minor changes the theme's character and remaining too long in minor becomes tiresome.

# Formal Definition
The recapitulation in minor presents special problems, particularly when the subordinate group is in major. The interchange of mode upon repetition in the principal key is not always practical; and remaining too long without relief in the minor becomes tiresome. Since a theme which first appears in a major key does not always work satisfactorily in minor, and since the minor-major contrast between principal and subordinate themes may be essential, considerable modification of the subordinate group may appear in the recapitulation.

# Construction/Recognition
Strategies for handling minor-key recapitulations:
1. **Subordinate group in tonic minor**: straightforward transposition, but changes character (Mozart, K.V. 421-I)
2. **Subordinate group in tonic major**: preserves the major character; at some later point the music turns to minor (Op. 18/4-I)
3. **Subordinate group in subdominant major**: provides relief in major before returning to tonic minor (Op. 10/1-I)
4. **Subordinate group in subdominant minor then tonic minor**: arrives at tonic indirectly (Op. 13-I)
5. **Complete reconstruction**: far-reaching changes to harmony, rhythm, melodic line, even structure (Mozart, K.V. 421-I)

# Musical Context
Minor-key sonata movements present a fundamental asymmetry: the exposition typically presents the subordinate group in the relative major (III) or minor dominant (v), but in the recapitulation, direct transposition to the tonic minor may destroy the essential contrast between principal and subordinate themes.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: transition (m. 109) reconstructed with different harmonic skeleton; entire subordinate group recurs in the principal key (f) without significant change
- Beethoven, Op. 10/1-I: transition leads to V of f (v); subordinate theme follows in F (IV, major subdominant); 14 measures literal in major, then repeated in c; provides relief before the minor conclusion
- Beethoven, Op. 13-I: original transition discarded; subordinate theme in f (iv), soon diverted toward c; pervasive modifications of detail throughout
- Beethoven, Op. 18/4-I: entire subordinate group appears in tonic major (C); return to e occurs only at the second codetta (m. 199)
- Mozart, K.V. 421-I: entire subordinate group in tonic minor; from the transition onward (m. 84), hardly a phrase reappears in original form -- shifts of half-measure interchange primary and secondary accents; yet "the psychological effect is only that of a variant"

# Common Confusions
- There is no single correct solution; the adaptations vary widely from piece to piece
- Mozart's K.V. 421-I demonstrates that far-reaching changes need not endanger recognizability; "Mozart excelled in such subtle reconstruction"
- The problem is fundamentally about character preservation, not merely about key: a theme's major-mode identity may be essential to its meaning
