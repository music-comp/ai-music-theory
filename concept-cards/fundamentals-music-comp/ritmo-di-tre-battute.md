# Quick Definition

"Rhythm of three measures" -- a hypermetrical technique in which three written measures function as a single larger rhythmic unit, reducing the standard four-measure grouping (ritmo di quattro battute) to three.

# Core Definition

Ritmo di tre battute is a technique, notably used by Beethoven, in which three notated measures are heard and organized as a single metrical unit. Instead of the standard four-measure grouping (ritmo di quattro battute), the hypermetre operates in groups of three. This connects two sub-units (2+1 or 1+2) and reduces the four-measure pattern to three. The naturalness of such groupings is demonstrated by the fact that a phrase of six quarter-notes can be understood as resembling one measure of 3/2 time.

# Prerequisites

- **Uneven phrase lengths**: Understanding that phrases need not conform to four-measure norms

# Key Properties

- Three consecutive written measures function as one rhythmic unit
- Reduces standard four-measure hypermetre to three-measure hypermetre
- Sub-units connect as 2+1 or 1+2 within the three-measure group
- May be indicated by the composer (as Beethoven does in the Ninth Symphony)
- Especially associated with scherzo movements at rapid tempo
- The written bar lines remain unchanged; the perceived grouping shifts
- A hypermetrical concept operating above the level of individual measures

# Construction / Recognition

1. Look for passages where the musical accent pattern groups in threes across bar lines
2. In rapid tempo, individual measures may feel like beats within a larger three-beat unit
3. The composer may indicate the grouping explicitly (as Beethoven does)
4. Related markings: "si ha s'imaginar la battuta di 6/8" -- imagining a different bar length
5. Check whether melodic, harmonic, and rhythmic patterns confirm three-measure periodicity

# Context & Application

This technique creates large-scale rhythmic irregularity while maintaining internal regularity within each three-measure group. It demonstrates that irregular phrase lengths often have a natural explanation when reconceived at a different metrical level. Schoenberg uses this concept to show that what appears "irregular" at the notated level may be perfectly natural when understood hypermetrically.

# Examples

- Beethoven, Ninth Symphony, Scherzo (Ex. 109): Marked "ritmo di tre battute," organizing the music in three-measure groups
- Beethoven, Harp Quartet, Op. 74 (Ex. 108a): Marked "si ha s'imaginar la battuta di 6/8"
- Brahms, Piano Quartet in e minor, last movement (Ex. 108b): Opening theme is an a-b-a form of ten three-measure phrases, each resembling one measure of 3/2
- Ex. 110: Shows how the ritmo di tre battute reduces the four-measure ritmo di quattro battute to three

# Common Errors

- Confusing ritmo di tre battute with 3/4 time signature (it refers to hypermetrical grouping, not metre)
- Thinking the bar lines change (they remain as written; it is the perceived grouping that shifts)
- Treating three-measure grouping as inherently irregular rather than natural at the hypermetrical level

# Common Confusions

- **Ritmo di tre battute vs. 3/4 metre**: 3/4 organizes beats within a measure; ritmo di tre battute organizes measures within a hypermeasure.
- **Three-measure phrase vs. ritmo di tre battute**: A single three-measure phrase is simply uneven; ritmo di tre battute implies a sustained pattern of three-measure groupings.
