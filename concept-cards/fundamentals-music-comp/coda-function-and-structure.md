# Quick Definition
An extrinsic addition at the end of a movement, not strictly necessary for formal completeness, in which the composer "wants to say something more" -- typically featuring richly elaborated cadences that progressively reduce in length and complexity.

# Core Definition
Since many movements have no codas, it is evident that the coda must be considered as an extrinsic addition. The assumption that it serves to establish the tonality is hardly justified; it could scarcely compensate for failure to establish the tonality in the previous sections. In fact, it would be difficult to give any other reason for the addition of a coda than that the composer wants to say something more. This accounts for the observed diversity of size and shape.

# Prerequisites
- Liquidation technique
- Cadential harmony and processes

# Key Properties
- Extrinsic addition -- not structurally necessary
- Does not exist primarily to establish tonality
- Enormous diversity of size: from a short extension (Op. 2/1-I) to 135 measures (Eroica-I, nearly one-fifth of the movement)
- Usually starts with richly elaborated cadences containing modulatory deviations
- Segments decrease in length and harmonic complexity (progressive reduction)
- Last codettas simplify to V-I interchange or mere tonic repetition
- Motive material derived from previous themes, reformulated to cadential harmony
- Many codas grow out of a final repetition of the main theme
- Variation codas often contain more striking harmonic contrasts than the variations themselves

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the point where the recapitulation's material is complete
2. The coda begins with new elaborative activity, often quoting previous themes
3. Look for richly elaborated cadences, possibly with modulatory deviations
4. Note the progressive decrease in segment length and harmonic complexity
5. Final segments reduce to codettas with V-I or mere tonic repetitions
6. Mozart's overlapping technique (dovetailing) is characteristic of his codas

# Context & Application
Codas appear in sonata-allegro movements, rondos, and variation sets. Their purpose is expressive rather than structural -- the composer has more to say. In variation sets, codas usually contain more striking harmonic contrasts and modulatory deviations than the variations themselves, compensating for the typically limited harmonic range of classical variations.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: only a short extension, hardly worth calling a coda
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: 40 measures (m. 148-87); includes enharmonic conversion and Neapolitan region episode
- Beethoven, Eroica Symphony-I: 135 measures, almost one-fifth of the entire movement
- Beethoven, Op. 57-I (m. 239): segment lengths decrease 6, 4, 3, 3, 1, 1 through a dramatic crescendo, then six measures diminishing to ppp
- Mozart, String Quartet K.V. 575-IV (m. 200): all six segments overlap (dovetailing technique)
- Mozart, String Quartet K.V. 421-IV (m. 113): coda begins with unvaried quotation of the first eight measures
- Beethoven, Twelve Variations in A: coda comprises about a third of the whole work, very rich in remote modulations

# Common Errors
- Adding a coda solely to establish tonality when the preceding sections have failed to do so
- Making the coda a mechanical repetition of tonic harmony without expressive content
- Failing to derive motive material from previous themes

# Common Confusions
- The coda does not exist primarily to establish tonality; it is an addition beyond formal necessity
- The great diversity of coda types makes inclusive generalization impossible
- A coda is not the same as a codetta; codettas are the smaller closing segments that may appear within or at the end of a coda
- Many codas grow out of a final repetition of the main theme, blurring the boundary between recapitulation and coda
