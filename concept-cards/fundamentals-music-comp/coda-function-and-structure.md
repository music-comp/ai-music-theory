# Quick Definition
An extrinsic addition at the end of a movement, not strictly necessary for formal completeness, in which the composer "wants to say something more" -- typically featuring richly elaborated cadences that progressively reduce in length and complexity.

# Formal Definition
Since many movements have no codas, it is evident that the coda must be considered as an extrinsic addition. The assumption that it serves to establish the tonality is hardly justified; it could scarcely compensate for failure to establish the tonality in the previous sections. In fact, it would be difficult to give any other reason for the addition of a coda than that the composer wants to say something more. This accounts for the observed diversity of size and shape.

# Construction/Recognition
Typical features include:
- Start with richly elaborated cadences, containing deviations leading even into rather remote regions
- As the length of segments decreases, so also the complexity of cadences decreases
- The last codettas may omit even the cadential subdominant
- Interchange of V and I often gives way to mere repetitions of the tonic
- Motive material is derived from previous themes, reformulated to conform to cadential harmony and effectively liquidated
- Many codas grow out of a final repetition of the main theme

# Musical Context
Codas appear in sonata-allegro movements, rondos, and variation sets. Their length varies enormously -- from a short extension barely worth calling a coda (Op. 2/1-I) to the 135-measure coda of the Eroica Symphony first movement, nearly a fifth of the entire movement.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: only a short extension
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: coda of 40 measures (m. 148-87)
- Beethoven, Op. 2/3-IV: coda of 54 measures (m. 259-312)
- Eroica Symphony-I: coda of 135 measures, almost one-fifth of the entire movement
- Beethoven, Op. 57-I: coda (m. 239) with segment lengths decreasing 6, 4, 3, 3, 1, 1 through a dramatic crescendo, followed by six measures diminishing to ppp

# Common Confusions
- The coda does not exist primarily to establish tonality; it is an addition beyond formal necessity
- The great diversity of coda types makes inclusive generalization impossible; the progressive-reduction pattern is common but not invariable
- A coda is not the same as a codetta; codettas are the smaller closing segments that may appear within or at the end of a coda
