# Quick Definition
A fundamental principle: contrasting sections must maintain connection to the whole through shared motivic and structural processes; incoherent contrast, though tolerated in descriptive music, is intolerable in well-organized form.

# Core Definition
Contrast presupposes coherence. Incoherent contrast, though tolerated in "descriptive" music, is intolerable in a well-organized form. Contrasting sections, therefore, must utilize the same processes by which motive-forms are connected in simpler formulations. The contrast must arise from within the material itself, not from arbitrary juxtaposition of unrelated ideas.

# Key Properties
- Contrast presupposes coherence (not the reverse)
- Incoherent contrast is tolerated in descriptive music but not in well-organized form
- Contrasting sections must use the same connective processes as simpler formulations
- Contrast should arise from within the material, not from arbitrary juxtaposition
- The B-section connects to the A-section through: metre, motive-forms not too remote, shared rhythmic characteristics

# Construction / Recognition
When creating contrasts: (1) use forms of the basic motive not too remote from the A-section, (2) maintain coherence through shared metre, (3) derive new variations from the basic motive rather than introducing entirely new material, (4) ensure harmonic contrast uses closely related regions. The contrast should feel like a different perspective on the same material.

# Context & Application
This is one of Schoenberg's most fundamental principles, applicable at every level of musical form from the small ternary to the symphony. Without coherence, contrast becomes mere juxtaposition; with coherence, contrast becomes dialectical development.

# Examples
- Beethoven Op. 2/1-II: the B-section uses dotted rhythm of the upbeat, frequent suspensions, and increasing sixteenth notes as connecting elements to the A-section
- Beethoven Op. 27/1-III: syncopations in m. 13-16 connected to those in m. 6-7 and m. 9, maintaining coherence despite seeming contrast
- All contrasting middle sections in the Beethoven sonata examples maintain motivic connection to their A-sections

# Common Errors
- Introducing entirely new material in the B-section with no motivic connection
- Assuming contrast means disconnection
- Tolerating incoherent contrast in abstract forms as though they were descriptive music

# Common Confusions
- Contrast does not mean disconnection; the strongest contrasts maintain deep structural connection
- "Incoherent contrast" is tolerated in descriptive music but not in abstract forms -- this is contextual, not absolute
- Coherence is achieved through motivic processes, not through surface similarity
