# Quick Definition
The practice of removing excessive variation, embellishment, figuration, remote motive-variations, and abrupt register changes that contribute to imbalance in a composition.

# Formal Definition
Too much variation, too much embellishment and figuration, excessively remote motive-variations, and too abrupt a change of register may contribute to imbalance. The composer must critically evaluate each element and eliminate those that do not serve the essential musical content. This parallels the principle that a melody achieves its power through limitation and condensation.

# Construction/Recognition
Evaluate a composition for: (1) excessive variation that obscures the basic motive, (2) over-elaborate embellishment or figuration, (3) motive-variations so remote they lose connection to the original, (4) register changes too abrupt for the context. Remove elements that contribute to imbalance while preserving those that serve the essential content.

# Musical Context
This principle embodies the economy of means that Schoenberg considers essential to good composition. The discipline of elimination is especially important for students who may be tempted to display every technique they know. Restraint in variation and embellishment allows the essential ideas to be heard clearly.

# Examples
- Ex. 105c: the chromatic return to V is too crowded with rapid harmonic change to balance the preceding sparse harmonization
- Ex. 105g: so filled with remote and rapidly changing harmonies that it fails to balance the surrounding sections

# Common Confusions
- Elimination does not mean simplification to the point of banality; it means removing what does not serve the musical idea
- The goal is balance, not minimalism; some passages require rich figuration, others demand restraint
