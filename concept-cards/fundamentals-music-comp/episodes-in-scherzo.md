# Quick Definition
Passages that interrupt the normal flow of a section by dwelling upon non-modulatory, non-cadential progressions, often settling in a harmonically remote region and introducing small phrases foreign to the previously used motive-forms.

# Formal Definition
Episodes interrupt the normal flow of a section. They dwell upon such progressions as neither modulate nor produce a cadence. They often settle down in a more or less remote contrasting region, especially if within a modulatory section. They often introduce small phrases, strangely foreign to the previously used motive-forms. In some cases, the modulation of the B-section is interrupted by an episode which settles down at a harmonically remote point, after which a remodulation follows, using residues or other derivatives.

# Construction/Recognition
- Interrupts the sequential or cadential flow of the section
- Harmony neither modulates nor cadences -- it "settles down"
- Often in a remote harmonic region
- May introduce motivally foreign phrases
- Followed by a remodulation to return to the structural plan
- May appear within the B-section (interrupting modulation) or within the A'-section
- Distinguished from extensions by their digressive, non-progressive character

# Musical Context
Episodes provide moments of contrast and harmonic color within the larger formal framework. They function as structural surprises, departing from the expected sequential or cadential progression. The remoteness of the region often necessitates a relatively long and rapid remodulation to return to the structural plan.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2, Scherzo, m. 19 ff.: Episode both motivally and harmonically remote, connected to basic material merely by its upbeat (related to tone repetitions of m. 3); remoteness requires a long, rapid remodulation
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 2: Modulation interrupted by an episode that settles at a harmonically remote point
- Beethoven, Op. 74 (Harp Quartet), Presto, m. 17: Long episode in the Neapolitan region; partial repetition remodulates
- Beethoven, Op. 59/2, Allegretto, m. 21-28: Segment possessing the independence of an episode but functioning as a remodulation (Neapolitan triad, followed by V and I)

# Common Confusions
- Episodes are not mere digressions -- they serve a structural purpose within the larger form
- An episode is not the same as an extension: episodes interrupt the flow; extensions prolong it
- A passage may have the independence of an episode but function as something else (e.g., a remodulation)
