# Quick Definition
A transition built upon a special, new thematic idea that follows the main theme and is customarily different in structure from both the preceding and following themes.

# Core Definition
When a special transitional theme follows the main theme, it is customarily different in structure from the preceding and following themes. The theme consists of a short segment within which the constituent motive-forms are only slightly varied. Short rhythmic figures lend themselves readily and flexibly to the processes of modulation and liquidation. After the initial statement, at least a partial repetition establishes the idea, after which modulation begins. Fluid change of region and prompt elimination of characteristic features distinguish the transition from the stable themes which normally precede and follow it.

# Key Properties
- Uses a special theme distinct from both principal and subordinate themes
- Short segments with only slightly varied motive-forms
- Short rhythmic figures preferred for flexibility in modulation and liquidation
- Initial statement followed by at least partial repetition to establish the idea
- Fluid regional change and prompt elimination of characteristic features
- Ideally, the transitional theme is still a derivative of the basic motive, even if the connection is not readily visible

# Construction / Recognition
1. Compose a short rhythmic figure that can readily undergo modulation and liquidation
2. State the transitional idea and confirm it through at least partial repetition
3. Begin modulation after establishment, moving fluidly through regions
4. Promptly eliminate characteristic features (liquidation)
5. Arrive at a suitable upbeat chord for the subordinate theme

# Context & Application
Independent transitions appear in sonata-allegro movements, rondos, and other large forms when the composer chooses to introduce genuinely new material between the principal and subordinate themes. The choice between independent and derived transitions depends partly on the amount of internal repetition in the principal theme.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I: transition starts m. 32 with a segment built from an inversion of m. 9-10 of the main theme; partial repetition a third higher modulates to V of the dominant
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: initial segment built on a cadence (m. 17-20) with a sixteenth-note figure providing motival content
- Beethoven, Op. 7-IV: transition (m. 17) uses a motive from the contrasting middle section of the main theme (m. 9); settles to interchange of I and V of dominant region with usual liquidation; ends (m. 36) with an enriched cadence to I of the dominant region
- Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I: four-measure initial segment (m. 13-16) with repetition modified to end on V (m. 21)
- Beethoven, Op. 13-III: transition (m. 18-21) consists of little more than a modulatory progression
- Additional examples: Op. 10/1-I (m. 32), Op. 22-II (m. 13-18), Op. 90-I (m. 25)

# Common Errors
- Creating a transitional theme that is too memorable or self-sufficient, competing with the principal and subordinate themes
- Failing to liquidate the transitional theme's characteristics before the subordinate theme enters
- Making the transitional theme too harmonically stable, undermining its modulatory function

# Common Confusions
- An "independent" transition theme is not entirely unrelated to the basic motive; ideally it derives from it, even if the connection is not readily visible
- The independence refers to its distinct structural profile and thematic identity, not to complete motivic isolation from the rest of the movement
- Some transitions use material from the middle section of the main theme (e.g., Op. 7-IV), blurring the line between independent and derived
