# Quick Definition
The optional closing section of a sonata-allegro movement, applying the same principles as codas in other forms -- repeated tonic cadences, citation of previous themes, progressive reduction -- with possible modulatory segments as transient contrasts.

# Formal Definition
The function of the coda and techniques employed in it were discussed in Chapter XVIII. Its application to the sonata-allegro form does not differ from its use in the cases described there. The presence or absence of a coda, its length and complexity, its character and thematic material, are subject to endless variety. Common features are: repeated cadences to the tonic; citation of previous themes; reduction in length and content of segments in approaching the end. In the more elaborate codas, modulatory segments often appear as transient contrasts, returning to the tonic.

# Construction/Recognition
Common features:
- Repeated cadences to the tonic
- Citation (often partial) of previous themes
- Progressive reduction in length and content of segments
- Modulatory segments as transient contrasts in more elaborate codas
- May grow out of a deceptive progression interrupting the closing section
- May include references to the leading motive, subordinate themes, or codetta figures
- Pedal points on V may serve as penultimate emphasis before final tonic

# Musical Context
The coda provides a final summation and confirmation of the movement's tonic. In sonata-allegro form, it follows the recapitulation and may range from a brief codetta extension to a massive section rivaling the exposition in length.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: only a short codetta added (m. 148-52)
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I: no additions
- Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I: closing theme interrupted (m. 218) by deceptive progression to flat VI; through modulatory broken chords and a cadenza, tonic reintroduced in m. 233 with leading motive quotation
- Beethoven, Op. 7-I: closing codettas enriched and extended (m. 313 ff.); pedal point on V reached in m. 339; tonic prevails from m. 351 to end
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-I: retransition from exposition introduces the coda (m. 299); emphasis of subdominant region, then dominant of flat II (m. 317); tonic reapproached in m. 327
- Mozart, K.V. 550-I: coda includes a modulatory passage (m. 280-4) and imitative, liquidated quotations of the leading motive

# Common Confusions
- The coda is not a necessary part of the sonata-allegro form; its presence, length, and character are entirely at the composer's discretion
- The coda in sonata-allegro form uses the same techniques as codas in rondos and other forms; no special principles apply
- Modulatory passages within a coda are transient contrasts, not structural modulations; they always return to the tonic
