# Quick Definition
The optional concluding section of a sonata-allegro movement, applying the general coda principles of Chapter 18 -- with common features including repeated tonic cadences, citation of previous themes, progressive reduction, and occasional modulatory segments as transient contrasts.

# Core Definition
The function of the coda, and techniques employed in it, were discussed in Chapter XVIII. Its application to the sonata-allegro form does not differ from its use in the cases described there. The presence or absence of a coda, its length and complexity, its character and thematic material, are subject to endless variety. Common features are: repeated cadences to the tonic; citation of previous themes; reduction in length and content of segments in approaching the end. In the more elaborate codas, modulatory segments often appear as transient contrasts, returning to the tonic.

# Key Properties
- Optional -- many movements have no coda
- Applies Ch 18 coda principles to sonata-allegro context
- Common features: repeated tonic cadences, citation of previous themes, progressive reduction
- Elaborate codas include modulatory segments as transient contrasts
- Presence, length, complexity, character, and material are endlessly varied
- May range from a short codetta (Op. 2/1-I) to no additions at all (Op. 2/2-I)
- Some codas are inaugurated by deceptive progressions (Op. 2/3-I)

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify where the recapitulation's material is complete
2. Look for new elaborative activity beyond the exposition's closing material
3. Note repeated tonic cadences and citation of previous themes
4. Identify any modulatory segments providing transient contrast
5. Track the progressive reduction toward the end

# Context & Application
The sonata-allegro coda is not structurally necessary but provides an opportunity for the composer to add further expressive content. Its treatment does not differ from codas in other forms, drawing on the same principles of progressive reduction, thematic citation, and cadential emphasis.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: short codetta (m. 148-52)
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I: no additions
- Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I: closing theme interrupted (m. 218) by deceptive progression to flat VI; broken chords and cadenza reintroduce tonic (m. 233); imitative syncopations lead through extended cadence; closing octaves from exposition close the movement
- Beethoven, Op. 7-I: closing codettas enriched and extended (m. 313); second subordinate theme referenced (m. 324-39); pedal point on V (m. 339); codetta figure like end of retransition; tonic from m. 351 to end
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-I: retransition from exposition introduces coda (m. 299); subdominant emphasis; dominant of flat II (m. 317); leading theme references and neutral passage work with tonic ostinato
- Mozart, Symphony K.V. 550-I: modulatory passage (m. 280-4) and imitative, liquidated leading motive quotations (m. 286-92) between codetta figures

# Common Errors
- Adding a coda that merely repeats what the recapitulation has already accomplished
- Failing to derive motive material from previous themes
- Making the coda too long relative to the movement

# Common Confusions
- The coda does not differ in principle from codas in other forms; Ch 18 principles apply directly
- The wide variety of coda treatment makes generalization impossible
- A deceptive progression (Op. 2/3-I) is one common way to inaugurate a coda, creating a moment of surprise before the final tonic affirmation
