# Quick Definition
The smallest musical idea -- a rhythmicized succession of notes with characteristic intervals and rhythms that generally appears in a striking manner at the beginning of a piece and recurs throughout as the basis for unity and development.

# Formal Definition
The motive generally appears in a characteristic and impressive manner at the beginning of a piece. The features of a motive are intervals and rhythms, combined to produce a memorable shape or contour which usually implies an inherent harmony. Every element or feature of a motive or phrase must be considered to be a motive if it is treated as such -- that is, if it is repeated with or without variation.

# Construction/Recognition
Any rhythmicized succession of notes can be used as a basic motive, but there should not be too many different features. Recognizable characteristics include:
- **Rhythmic features**: may be very simple, even for the main theme of a sonata
- **Interval features**: a motive need not contain a great many; a succession of thirds can suffice
- **Contour or shape**: may be significant even when rhythm and intervals change (e.g., an upward leap, stepwise ascent, or sweep-and-return pattern)

# Musical Context
Even the writing of simple phrases involves the invention and use of motives, though perhaps unconsciously. Consciously used, the motive should produce unity, relationship, coherence, logic, comprehensibility, and fluency. The basic motive is often considered the "germ" of the idea from which the entire piece grows.

# Examples
- Beethoven Piano Sonata Op. 2/3-IV: upward sweep followed by return pervades the movement
- Beethoven's Fifth Symphony (Exs. 12b, c, 13): primarily note repetitions contributing distinctive characteristics
- Brahms's Fourth Symphony (Ex. 13): constructed on a succession of thirds, though also containing sixths and octaves
- Ex. 12a: simple rhythmic features sufficient for the main theme of a sonata

# Common Confusions
- A motive is not the same as a theme; it is the smallest identifiable musical idea from which themes are built
- The primary form of the motive does not determine the final impression -- "everything depends on its treatment and development"
- Whether a motive is simple or complex matters less than how it is used
- Any element treated as a motive (repeated with or without variation) becomes a motive, regardless of its origin
