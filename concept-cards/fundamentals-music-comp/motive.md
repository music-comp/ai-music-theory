# Quick Definition

The smallest musical idea -- a rhythmicized succession of notes with characteristic intervals, rhythms, and contour that generally appears in a striking manner at the beginning of a piece and recurs throughout as the basis for unity and development.

# Core Definition

"The motive generally appears in a characteristic and impressive manner at the beginning of a piece. The features of a motive are intervals and rhythms, combined to produce a memorable shape or contour which usually implies an inherent harmony" (Ch. 3, p. 8). "Every element or feature of a motive or phrase must be considered to be a motive if it is treated as such, i.e. if it is repeated with or without variation" (Ch. 3, p. 8). "Any rhythmicized succession of notes can be used as a basic motive, but there should not be too many different features" (Ch. 3, p. 8).

# Prerequisites

- **phrase** -- The motive operates within the context of phrases and is the smallest element from which phrases are built

# Key Properties

1. Features consist of intervals, rhythms, and contour
2. Produces a memorable shape implying an inherent harmony
3. Appears in a "characteristic and impressive manner" at the beginning
4. Should not contain too many different features
5. Rhythmic features may be very simple, even for a sonata main theme
6. A motive need not contain many interval features
7. Contour can be significant even when rhythm and intervals change
8. Any element treated as a motive (repeated with/without variation) becomes a motive
9. The final impression depends not on the primary form but on its treatment and development

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct a Motive

1. Choose a rhythmic pattern (may be very simple)
2. Combine it with an intervallic pattern (a succession of thirds, a leap, stepwise motion)
3. Ensure the combination produces a memorable contour
4. Limit the number of distinct features to maintain clarity
5. Imply a clear harmony through the pitch content

## To Recognize a Motive

1. Look for the characteristic figure at the opening of a piece
2. Identify its rhythmic profile, intervallic content, and contour
3. Trace its recurrence throughout the piece in varied forms
4. Note that any recurring element treated motivically becomes a motive

# Context & Application

"Even the writing of simple phrases involves the invention and use of motives, though perhaps unconsciously. Consciously used, the motive should produce unity, relationship, coherence, logic, comprehensibility and fluency" (Ch. 3, p. 8). The motive is the seed from which the entire composition grows. Schoenberg emphasizes that "everything depends on its treatment and development" -- the primary form matters less than how it is used.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 8, Ex. 12a): Simple rhythmic features sufficient for the main theme of a sonata, with an upward leap as characteristic contour.

**Example 2** (p. 8, Exs. 12b, c, 13): Beethoven's Fifth Symphony -- motives consisting primarily of note repetitions contributing distinctive characteristics.

**Example 3** (p. 8, Ex. 13): Brahms's Fourth Symphony -- constructed on a succession of thirds, though also containing sixths and octaves.

**Example 4** (p. 8): Beethoven Op. 2/3-IV -- "the upward sweep followed by a return within it which pervades" the entire movement, illustrating contour as the primary motive feature.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Making the motive too complex with too many different features
  **Correction**: "There should not be too many different features" -- simplicity allows for development

- **Error**: Treating the primary form of the motive as determining the piece's character
  **Correction**: "Everything depends on its treatment and development" -- the primary form is just the starting point

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Confusing the motive with the theme
  **Clarification**: The motive is the smallest identifiable musical idea; themes are larger structures built from motive-forms

- **Confusion**: Believing a motive must be complex to be effective
  **Clarification**: "Rhythmic features may be very simple, even for the main theme of a sonata" (Ch. 3, p. 8)

- **Confusion**: Thinking only the explicitly designated opening figure is a motive
  **Clarification**: "Every element or feature...must be considered to be a motive if it is treated as such"
