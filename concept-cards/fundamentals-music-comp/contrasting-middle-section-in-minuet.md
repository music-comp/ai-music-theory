# Quick Definition

The B-section of the minuet, which provides contrast through sequential modulation, remote motive derivation, and modulatory movement, often resembling the elaboration (Durchfuhrung) technique found in scherzos and sonata forms.

# Core Definition

The contrasting middle section of the minuet serves as the B-section of the ternary form. Its chief characteristic is sequential modulation -- moving through related keys by transposing a pattern to successive pitch levels. The end of the section is often marked by dwelling on the dominant over a pedal point. This pedal point is a retarding device, employed where the harmony must be prevented from developing too rapidly or too distantly. Usually the sustained note is in the bass, and the upper voices carry out a progression from V to V. Motive-forms used in the B-section are typically remote derivations from the A-section material.

# Key Properties

- Built from motive-forms remotely derived from the A-section
- Sequential modulation is the chief characteristic
- The section often ends by dwelling on the dominant over a pedal point
- May include episodes and co-ordinate ideas (sometimes rudimentary, sometimes independent)
- Modulation often follows the circle of fifths
- The pedal point retards harmonic movement, creating repose and suspense before the recapitulation
- Many minuet B-sections resemble the elaboration (Durchfuhrung) discussed under the scherzo

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify the motive-forms and trace their derivation from the A-section
2. Look for sequential modulation as the primary modulatory device
3. Check whether the section ends on a dominant pedal point
4. Note whether episodes or co-ordinate ideas appear
5. Assess the degree of modulatory complexity (simple contrast vs. genuine elaboration)

# Context & Application

Schoenberg observes that the contrasting middle sections of many minuets resemble the scherzo elaboration. The distinction between the two is often blurred in practice -- some minuets have genuine Durchfuhrung-like B-sections, while some scherzos have simple contrasts more typical of the minuet. This continuity between the two forms supports Schoenberg's view that compositional technique transcends formal labels.

# Examples

- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-III: Sequential modulation leads to the subdominant; motive reduced (liquidated) to three notes in m. 23-24, then reassembled in eighth-note chains moving to the dominant
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-III: Two-measure phrase makes four appearances moving in a circle of fifths (iii-vi-II-V)
- Beethoven, Op. 22-III: Trill-like segment derived from the A-section's left-hand eighth-notes under influence of sixteenth-notes
- Beethoven, Op. 7-III and Op. 27/2-II: Movements not called Minuets that feature the same sequential modulation technique

# Common Errors

- Writing a B-section that merely contrasts in character without genuine modulatory content
- Failing to derive B-section motive-forms from A-section material
- Omitting the dominant pedal point preparation before the recapitulation

# Common Confusions

- **Minuet B-section vs. scherzo elaboration**: The distinction is often blurred in practice; many minuets have genuine modulatory elaboration.
- **Pedal point function**: The pedal point at the end is retarding (preventing further development), not cadential (closing the section).
