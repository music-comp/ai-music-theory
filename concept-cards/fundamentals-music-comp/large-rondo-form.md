# Quick Definition
A rondo form with the pattern aba-c-aba, where c is a trio section creating a complex ternary structure -- typically used as a finale with dance-song character, the trio being longer and more elaborate than the B-section.

# Core Definition
The large rondo forms generally express the character of the dance-song. The rate of movement is moderate or rapid, and the mood cheerful, playful, or brilliant. The whole form becomes a complex ternary structure: the c-section is usually longer and more elaborate than the B-section, resembling the trio of a scherzo or the elaboration of a sonata-allegro. The trio section is often itself ternary and expresses a definite contrasting key, usually somewhat more remote than that of the B-section. The character of the c-section contrasts with both the A- and B-sections. Quite often it is etude-like; not infrequently it is contrapuntal.

# Key Properties
- Pattern: aba-c-aba (complex ternary structure)
- Dance-song character: moderate or rapid tempo, cheerful/playful/brilliant mood
- C-section (trio) usually longer and more elaborate than B-section
- Trio key usually more remote than B-section's key
- Trio often etude-like or contrapuntal in character
- Trio often itself ternary with internal repetitions
- Final A repetition often serves as beginning of a coda
- The trio may take over the transition function at its beginning

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the A-section (typically a small ternary form)
2. Note the transition to B-section in a contrasting region
3. Identify the C-section (trio) in a more remote key
4. Note the trio's character: etude-like, contrapuntal, or both
5. Look for the return of aba after the trio
6. Check whether the final A-section is varied to introduce a coda

# Context & Application
The classical composers frequently used the large rondo as the final movement in sonatas, string quartets, and symphonies. Occasionally the C-section is comparable in size to the B-section, producing an undifferentiated abacaba. Usually, however, the trio provides a deeper level of contrast.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: A-section small ternary (m. 1-16); transition (m. 17-26) to B in dominant; trio (m. 57-99) etude-like in tonic minor; trio theme recurs in coda (m. 161)
- Beethoven, Op. 7-IV: structurally similar; trio etude-like with striking rhythmic figure; final A modified through unexpected shift to flat submediant, introducing coda
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-IV: all parts relatively short and compact; trio (m. 35-45) in flat submediant, etude-like in both hands
- Beethoven, Op. 13-III: trio (m. 79) consists of six versions of first phrase with contrapuntal interplay; principal notes filled in with eighth-notes to produce descending scale lines (m. 99-106)
- Beethoven, Op. 28-IV: trio (m. 79-101) pseudo-contrapuntal with double counterpoint treatment; upper voice appears successively in upper, middle, and lower voices

# Common Errors
- Making the trio section too similar to the B-section in key remoteness and character
- Failing to provide retransition from the trio back to the principal theme
- Omitting the coda or its preparation through the final A-section variation

# Common Confusions
- Occasionally the C-section is comparable in size to the B-section (as in Op. 18/4-IV), producing an undifferentiated abacaba; usually the c-section is longer and more elaborate
- The "large" in large rondo form refers to the structural complexity (trio section), not necessarily to the length
- The trio is often etude-like or contrapuntal, providing textural contrast rather than just harmonic contrast
