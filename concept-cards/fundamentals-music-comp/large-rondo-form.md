# Quick Definition
A rondo form with the pattern aba-c-aba, where c is a trio section that creates a complex ternary structure, typically used as a finale with a dance-song character.

# Formal Definition
The large rondo forms generally express the character of the dance-song. The rate of movement is moderate or rapid, and the mood cheerful, playful, or brilliant. The whole form becomes a complex ternary structure: the c-section is usually longer and more elaborate than the B-section, resembling the trio of a scherzo or the elaboration of a sonata-allegro. The trio section is often itself ternary and expresses a definite contrasting key, usually somewhat more remote than that of the B-section.

# Construction/Recognition
- The A-section is typically a small ternary form
- A transition leads to the B-section in a contrasting region (usually dominant or relative major)
- A bridge or retransition connects back to the A-section
- The C-section (trio) provides a deeper contrast, often etude-like or contrapuntal in character
- The trio's key is usually more remote than the B-section's key
- The final repetition of the A-section often serves as the beginning of a coda
- The trio may include its own retransition, modifying its ending to lead back to the dominant of the main key

# Musical Context
The classical composers frequently used this form as the final movement in sonatas, string quartets, and symphonies. The trio section provides a substantial contrasting middle that gives the form its "large" character.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: A-section is a small ternary form (m. 1-16); transition (m. 17-26) leads to B in dominant; trio (m. 57-99) is etude-like in tonic minor; trio theme recurs in the coda (m. 161)
- Beethoven, Op. 7-IV: structurally similar; trio is etude-like with a striking rhythmic figure; final A modified to introduce coda through unexpected shift to flat submediant
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-IV: all parts relatively short and compact; trio (m. 35-45) in flat submediant is etude-like in both hands
- Beethoven, Op. 13-III: trio (m. 79) consists of six versions of first phrase with contrapuntal interplay; principal notes filled in with eighth-notes to produce descending scale lines (m. 99-106)
- Beethoven, Op. 28-IV: trio contains pseudo-contrapuntal treatment in the manner of double counterpoint

# Common Confusions
- Occasionally the C-section is comparable in size to the B-section (as in Op. 18/4-IV), producing an undifferentiated abacaba; usually, however, the c-section is longer and more elaborate
- The "large" in large rondo form refers to the structural complexity (trio section), not necessarily to the length of the movement
- The trio is often etude-like or contrapuntal, providing textural contrast rather than just harmonic contrast
