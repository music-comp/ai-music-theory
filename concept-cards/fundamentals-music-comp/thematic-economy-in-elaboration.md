# Quick Definition
The principle that the elaboration section uses previously exposed thematic material rather than introducing new ideas, deriving maximum variety from the wealth of motives already presented in the exposition.

# Formal Definition
Economy dictates the use of previously "exposed" thematic material (a wealth of differing ideas is presented in the first division). Variety requires roving, modulating harmony as a contrast to the generally stable nature of the exposition. The elaboration normally makes use of variants of previously exposed themes, seldom evolving new musical ideas. Often a small number of features, formerly inconspicuous, dominate the entire division.

# Construction/Recognition
- Material is drawn from themes of the exposition in any order
- Formerly inconspicuous features may become dominant elements
- Some elaborations are essentially monothematic (e.g., Op. 13-I, Op. 28-I)
- Others draw on multiple themes but transform them through motival techniques
- Occasionally an idea appears that, though deriving from the basic material, never appeared before as formulated -- an exception demonstrating "masterly imagination"
- The principle of economy ensures that the exposition's motivic investment yields maximum return

# Musical Context
The principle of economy is both aesthetic and practical: the exposition presents such a wealth of thematic material that introducing entirely new ideas in the elaboration would be wasteful and would undermine the motivic unity of the movement.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: principal theme appears only in introductory measures and retransition; the main body uses subordinate theme and residues
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I: introductory quotation of principal theme; etude-like passage; then a lyrical section derived from m. 9-12
- Beethoven, Op. 10/1-I: built upon a theme not found in the exposition, though related to the transition and subordinate themes
- Beethoven, Op. 10/2-I: chief material derives from the incidental octaves at the end of the exposition (m. 65-66) and ornamental triplet sixteenths -- formerly inconspicuous features
- Mozart, K.V. 465-I: built entirely from the initial motive-form (m. 23-24), demonstrating the fullest exploitation of a single idea

# Common Confusions
- Economy does not mean restriction; the elaboration achieves great variety through transformation, recontextualization, and modulation of the existing material
- "Formerly inconspicuous" features becoming dominant is not a violation of economy; it reveals hidden potential in the exposition's material
- The rare appearance of genuinely new formulations (Op. 10/1-I) demonstrates that economy is a guiding principle, not an absolute rule
