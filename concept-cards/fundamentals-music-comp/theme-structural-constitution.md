# Quick Definition
The structural requirements for a good variation theme: simple harmony, clear phrasing, definite subdivision, closely related motive-forms, and a unifying accompaniment -- because a theme with too many interesting features leaves little scope for variation.

# Formal Definition
There are themes which facilitate, and others which impede, the production of variations. Variations are primarily repetitions, which would become intolerable without constant restimulation of the listener's interest. If the theme contains too many and too interesting features, there remains little scope for the additions which a simple theme readily permits. A simple theme will consist of closely related motive-forms, in preference to distant ones. Structurally, the theme should show definite subdivision and clear phrasing, typically in binary or ternary form.

# Construction/Recognition
- Simple harmony, not changing too frequently or irregularly
- Mere interchange of I and V contributes to simplicity
- Closely related motive-forms (not distant ones)
- Clear phrasing and definite subdivision
- Binary or ternary form (passacaglia-like sentence is exceptional)
- A unifying accompaniment facilitates variation; one that changes too much is a handicap
- Shorter and simpler themes allow more variations; longer and more complex themes limit the number

# Musical Context
Many classical variations are based on popular or folk melodies, which tend to be simple. Beethoven, Bach, and Brahms also composed original themes for variation. Even a master like Beethoven did not make many variations when the theme was long or complicated. It may be useful for students to select a theme whose suitability has already been proved in variation sets by master composers.

# Examples
- Beethoven, 12 Variations in A, 24 Variations in D, Op. 111-II: Binary theme structure
- Beethoven, Op. 14/2-II, Op. 26-I: Ternary theme structure
- Beethoven, 32 Variations in c minor: Exceptional passacaglia-like theme, a sentence of eight measures
- Beethoven, 15 Variations in E-flat, Diabelli Variations Op. 120: Simple harmony with mere interchange of I and V
- Beethoven, 6 Variations in F, Op. 34: Long or complicated theme, resulting in only six variations
- Beethoven, 9 Variations on a March of Dressler: First variation changes only the accompaniment to a unifying figure

# Common Confusions
- Simplicity is a virtue in variation themes, not a defect -- it provides room for the variations to add interest
- A binary theme for variations differs from the small ternary form: it may lack a real motival contrast or identifiable repetition
- The passacaglia theme (a short sentence, typically eight measures) is an exceptional case, not the norm
