# Quick Definition
A second dance movement (minuet, scherzo, march, etc.) that follows the first and provides contrast in tonality, character, or both, after which the original dance is repeated, creating a large-scale A-B-A structure.

# Formal Definition
The trio is nothing else than a second minuet (march, waltz, scherzo; or, in the suites of Bach, a second courante, bourree, or gavotte). Most dance forms are followed by a trio, and it is usual after the trio to repeat the original dance. The trio must constitute a contrast, though there should also be some thematic connection. The form of the trio may exhibit as many deviations as the dance itself: reductions, extensions, additional ideas, codettas, etc.

# Construction/Recognition
- Functions as a contrasting second dance within the larger structure
- Tonality: formerly the same key, or tonic major/minor (maggiore-minore); later, relative major/minor or other related keys
- Character contrast examples: lyrical vs. rhythmical, melodious vs. contrapuntal, melodious vs. etude-like, grazioso vs. energico, dolce vs. vivace, melancholy vs. gay
- Internally structured as its own ternary or binary form
- The original dance is repeated after the trio (da capo)
- May include the same formal deviations as the principal dance

# Musical Context
The trio provides large-scale contrast within a dance movement. The convention of repeating the principal dance after the trio creates an overall A-B-A structure at the movement level (where A = the dance, B = the trio). The relationship between the dance and its trio is one of the primary means of achieving variety and balance in middle movements of cyclic works.

# Examples
- Bach, French Suite No. 5: Second courante, bourree, or gavotte functions as a trio
- Beethoven, various sonatas and symphonies: Trios in tonic major/minor or related keys
- Classical minuet-trio-minuet structure found throughout Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven

# Common Confusions
- The trio is not a section for three instruments (the name is historical, originating from scoring practices)
- The trio is a complete, self-contained form, not merely a contrasting passage
- Thematic connection between the trio and the principal dance is expected, not just tonal contrast
