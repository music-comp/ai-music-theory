# Quick Definition

A second dance movement (minuet, scherzo, march, etc.) that follows the first and provides contrast in tonality, character, or both, after which the original dance is repeated, creating a large-scale A-B-A structure.

# Core Definition

The trio is nothing else than a second minuet (march, waltz, scherzo; or, in the suites of Bach, a second courante, bourree, or gavotte). Most dance forms are followed by a trio, and it is usual after the trio to repeat the original dance. The trio must constitute a contrast, though one assumes that there should also be some thematic connexion. The form of the trio may exhibit as many deviations as the dance itself: reductions, extensions, additional ideas, codettas, etc.

# Prerequisites

- **Minuet form**: Understanding the dance form that the trio accompanies

# Key Properties

- Functions as a contrasting second dance within the larger structure
- Tonality contrast: formerly the same key, or tonic major/minor (maggiore-minore); later, relative major/minor or other related keys
- Character contrast: lyrical vs. rhythmical, melodious vs. contrapuntal, melodious vs. etude-like, grazioso vs. energico, dolce vs. vivace, melancholy vs. gay
- Internally structured as its own ternary or binary form
- The original dance is repeated after the trio (da capo)
- May include the same formal deviations as the principal dance

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify the trio as a complete, self-contained form following the main dance
2. Assess the tonal relationship to the main dance (same key, maggiore/minore, relative key, etc.)
3. Evaluate the character contrast (what type of contrast is achieved)
4. Note the thematic connexion to the main dance (if any)
5. Verify that the original dance returns after the trio (da capo)

# Context & Application

The trio provides large-scale contrast within a dance movement. The convention of repeating the principal dance after the trio creates an overall A-B-A structure at the movement level (A = dance, B = trio). The name "trio" is historical, originating from scoring practices (the middle section was often scored for three instruments), not from any current structural requirement. The relationship between the dance and its trio is a primary means of achieving variety and balance in middle movements of cyclic works.

# Examples

- Bach, French Suite No. 5: Second courante, bourree, or gavotte functions as a trio
- Beethoven, various sonatas and symphonies: Trios in tonic major/minor or related keys
- Classical minuet-trio-minuet structure throughout Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven

# Common Errors

- Failing to provide sufficient contrast between the trio and the main dance
- Omitting thematic connexion (contrast without connection)
- Treating the trio as a mere passage rather than a complete, self-contained form

# Common Confusions

- **Trio vs. "three instruments"**: The name is historical; the trio is not a section for three instruments.
- **Trio as form**: The trio is a complete, self-contained form, not merely a contrasting passage within the main dance.
- **Thematic connexion**: Some thematic relationship between trio and main dance is expected, not just tonal contrast.
