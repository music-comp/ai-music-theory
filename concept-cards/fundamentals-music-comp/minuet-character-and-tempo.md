# Quick Definition

The minuet is characterized by moderate tempo (dotted half-note = 60-70), frequent harmonic change, restrained rhythmic accentuation, and a character ranging from singable to insistent -- distinguishing it from the faster, more rhythmically driven scherzo.

# Core Definition

The character and tempo of the minuet are generally moderate. Most minuets by Mozart and Haydn average dotted half-note = 60-70, though some are as slow as dotted half-note = 40-50. Beethoven's extreme markings of dotted half-note = 108 (First and Eighth Symphonies) are exceptional. Because of the moderate tempo, more small note values (eighths and sixteenths) appear, and the harmony changes more frequently than in the scherzo. In the minuet, harmony seldom lasts longer than one or two measures, and often there are two or more harmonies within a single measure. As a courtly dance, the minuet did not require the rhythmic emphasis of popular dances.

# Key Properties

- Tempo: typically dotted half-note = 60-70 (moderate); Beethoven's extremes reach dotted half-note = 108
- Harmonic rhythm: changes every one to two measures, often multiple harmonies per measure
- Note values: frequent use of eighths and sixteenths due to slower tempo
- Rhythmic accentuation: restrained compared to scherzo and popular dances
- Accompaniment patterns: stylized rather than conventional dance patterns
- Character range: unpretentiously singable to stubbornly insistent, but generally moderate

# Construction / Recognition

1. Check tempo marking -- moderate tempo distinguishes from scherzo
2. Examine harmonic rhythm -- frequent changes (every 1-2 measures) are characteristic
3. Note the prevalence of smaller note values (eighths, sixteenths)
4. Observe restrained rhythmic accentuation vs. more vigorous scherzo-type rhythm
5. Assess overall character -- moderate, not extreme

# Context & Application

The distinction between minuet and scherzo character has important compositional implications. The moderate tempo of the minuet permits more rapid harmonic change and more detailed melodic figuration, while the scherzo's fast tempo constrains both. This reciprocal relationship between tempo, harmonic rhythm, and melodic detail is a key principle in Schoenberg's teaching.

# Examples

- Beethoven, Op. 31/3-III: Unpretentiously singable character
- Mozart, Symphony No. 40 in g minor: Stubbornly insistent character
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 1, Minuet: Dotted half-note = 108 (extreme, already scherzo-like)
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 8, Minuet: Dotted half-note = 108 (extreme for a minuet)

# Common Errors

- Setting a minuet at scherzo tempo without adjusting harmonic rhythm accordingly
- Using infrequent harmonic changes (appropriate for scherzo but not minuet)
- Over-accenting the rhythm in a courtly dance form

# Common Confusions

- **Beethoven's extreme tempi**: His markings of dotted half-note = 108 for the First and Eighth Symphony minuets are exceptional, not representative of the genre.
- **"Moderate" is relative**: It encompasses a range from dotted half-note = 40-50 to 60-70, allowing considerable variety.
