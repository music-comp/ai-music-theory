# Quick Definition

The use of logical, well-organized harmonic progressions to reinforce relationships between connected motive-forms, with every piece of music resembling a cadence in a general way.

# Core Definition

"Coherent harmony reinforces relationship" and is the third of three principal factors contributing to logic in connecting motive-forms (Ch. 4, p. 11). "In a general way every piece of music resembles a cadence, of which each phrase will be a more or less elaborate part. In simple cases a mere interchange of I-V-I, if not contradicted by controversial harmonies, can express a tonality" (Ch. 4, p. 11). "The concept of coherent harmony used here is deduced from the practice of the period from Bach to Wagner" (Ch. 4, footnote, p. 12).

# Prerequisites

- **motive** -- Must understand the motive before discussing its harmonic context

# Key Properties

1. Coherent harmony reinforces the relationships between motive-forms
2. Every piece of music resembles a cadence in general
3. Simple I-V-I interchange can express tonality
4. Traditional music concludes interchanges with a more elaborate cadence
5. Harmony generally moves more slowly than melody
6. The accompanying harmony should reveal a certain regularity
7. Harmonic regularity through motive-like repetitions contributes to unity and comprehensibility
8. The concept is deduced from practice (Bach to Wagner), not abstract rules

# Construction / Recognition

1. Support melodic motive-forms with logical harmonic progressions
2. Ensure harmonic progressions express tonality clearly (especially I-V-I)
3. Avoid "controversial harmonies" that contradict the established tonality
4. Maintain harmonic rhythm slower than melodic rhythm (generally)
5. Use regular, motive-like repetitions in the harmonic pattern
6. Plan the overall harmonic direction cadentially

# Context & Application

"Ordinarily the harmony moves more slowly than the melody; in other words, a number of melodic notes usually refers to a single harmony" (Ch. 4, p. 11). The contrary occasionally occurs "when the harmony moves quasi-contrapuntally against a melody in long notes" (Ch. 4, p. 11). The regularity of the accompanying harmony, "through motive-like repetitions, contributes to unity and comprehensibility" (Ch. 4, p. 11). For detailed treatment of root progressions, Schoenberg refers to his Theory of Harmony and Structural Functions of Harmony.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using harmonies that contradict the established tonality ("controversial harmonies")
  **Correction**: The harmonic framework should express tonality clearly, especially in opening phrases

- **Error**: Moving harmony faster than melody, creating confusion
  **Correction**: Harmony generally moves more slowly than melody; the contrary requires careful handling

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Equating "coherent" with "simple"
  **Clarification**: Coherent means logically organized according to tonal principles; complexity is permitted within a coherent framework

- **Confusion**: Treating the cadential analogy as requiring formal cadences at every phrase end
  **Clarification**: The overall harmonic direction is cadential; individual phrases need not end with formal cadences

- **Confusion**: Believing harmonic coherence derives from abstract rules
  **Clarification**: "The concept of coherent harmony used here is deduced from the practice of the period from Bach to Wagner"
