# Quick Definition

The two fundamental structures for articulating a complete musical idea or theme, similar in that both center around a tonic and have definite endings, but distinguished by the treatment of the second phrase and the continuation after it.

# Core Definition

"A complete musical idea or theme is customarily articulated as a period or a sentence. These structures usually appear in classical music as parts of larger forms (e.g. as a in the aba form), but occasionally are independent (e.g. in strophic songs). There are many different types which are similar in two respects: they centre around a tonic, and they have a definite ending" (Ch. 5, p. 20). "The distinction between the sentence and the period lies in the treatment of the second phrase, and in the continuation after it" (Ch. 5, p. 21).

# Prerequisites

- **phrase** -- Both structures are built from phrases
- **motive** -- Both structures present and develop motivic material

# Key Properties

1. Both period and sentence center around a tonic
2. Both have a definite ending
3. Both usually consist of an even number of measures (usually 8 or multiples)
4. They usually appear as parts of larger forms (e.g., a in aba)
5. They occasionally appear independently (e.g., strophic songs)
6. The distinction lies in the treatment of the second phrase
7. In the sentence, the second phrase is an immediate repetition of the first
8. In the period, the second phrase introduces more contrasting material
9. In very rapid tempos, 16 or 32 measures may function as 8

# Construction / Recognition

## Sentence

1. First phrase (mm. 1-2) presents the basic motive (tonic form)
2. Second phrase (mm. 3-4) immediately repeats it (dominant form or variant)
3. Continuation (mm. 5-8) develops and liquidates toward a cadence

## Period

1. First phrase (antecedent, mm. 1-4) presents material without immediate repetition
2. Second phrase (consequent, mm. 5-8) repeats the antecedent in modified form
3. Antecedent typically ends on V; consequent ends on I

## Key Distinction

The sentence uses immediate repetition within the first half, then develops; the period postpones repetition to the second half (consequent).

# Context & Application

Period and sentence are the two basic templates for theme construction in classical music. Understanding their distinction is essential for both analysis and composition. Schoenberg treats the sentence beginning first (Ch. 5) because immediate repetition is "the simplest solution" for continuation. The period is treated in Chapters 6-7.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using "period" and "sentence" interchangeably
  **Correction**: They are fundamentally different structures with different internal organization

- **Error**: Assuming both must be exactly eight measures
  **Correction**: Eight measures is the norm "in the simplest cases," but lengths vary, especially in rapid tempos

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the distinction is about length
  **Clarification**: Both can be eight measures; the distinction is about internal organization -- immediate vs. postponed repetition

- **Confusion**: Using "period" in its everyday English sense
  **Clarification**: Schoenberg uses "period" as a specific technical term for a particular theme structure

- **Confusion**: Assuming all themes are either periods or sentences
  **Clarification**: These are the two basic types, but many themes in practice are hybrids or deviations
