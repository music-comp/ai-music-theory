# Quick Definition

The smallest structural unit of music -- a kind of musical molecule consisting of integrated musical events, possessing a certain completeness, and well adapted to combination with other similar units.

# Core Definition

"The smallest structural unit is the phrase, a kind of musical molecule consisting of a number of integrated musical events, possessing a certain completeness, and well adapted to combination with other similar units" (Ch. 2, p. 3). Structurally, a phrase approximates "what one could sing in a single breath." Its ending "suggests a form of punctuation such as a comma." In homophonic-harmonic music, the essential content is concentrated in one voice, the principal voice, which implies an inherent harmony.

# Key Properties

1. Smallest structural unit in music
2. Consists of integrated (not random) musical events
3. Possesses a certain completeness as a unit
4. Adapted to combination with other similar units
5. Approximates what one could sing in a single breath
6. Ending suggests punctuation (like a comma)
7. Often contains motivic features that recur within it
8. Seldom an exact multiple of the measure length
9. Nearly always crosses metrical subdivisions rather than filling measures completely

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct a Phrase

1. Choose a harmonic basis (I and V are especially useful for opening phrases)
2. Create a melodic contour outlining the chosen harmony
3. Add rhythmic interest and variety to establish character
4. Include non-chordal notes for fluency (passing notes, auxiliary notes, etc.)
5. Differentiate the ending rhythmically to provide punctuation
6. Ensure the phrase functions as a complete musical unit

## To Recognize a Phrase

1. Identify the sense of completeness and the punctuation at its ending
2. Look for motivic features that appear more than once within the unit
3. Note that the phrase typically crosses barlines rather than aligning with them

# Context & Application

The phrase is the fundamental unit from which all larger structures are built. Schoenberg recommends making "a great many sketches of phrases based on a predetermined harmony" to stimulate the inventive faculties and acquire technical facility. He warns that early attempts "may be stiff and awkward, but, with patience, the co-ordination of the various elements will rapidly become smoother, until real fluency and even expressiveness is attained" (Ch. 2, p. 4).

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Making the melody too elaborate too early, obscuring the harmony
  **Correction**: Begin with simple chord-tone melodies, then add non-chordal notes progressively

- **Error**: Inventing melody without being conscious of its harmony
  **Correction**: "The composer should never invent a melody without being conscious of its harmony" (Ch. 2, p. 3)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Assuming a phrase is a fixed number of measures (e.g., always 4 measures)
  **Clarification**: Phrase length varies with metre and tempo; there is no fixed length

- **Confusion**: Confusing phrases with measures or motives
  **Clarification**: A phrase is a complete structural unit that may contain multiple motives and typically spans several measures

- **Confusion**: Expecting phrases to align with barlines
  **Clarification**: "The phrase is seldom an exact multiple of the measure length; it usually varies by a beat or more" (Ch. 2, p. 4)
