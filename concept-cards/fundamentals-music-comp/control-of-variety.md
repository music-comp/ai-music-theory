# Quick Definition

The principle that variety must be limited and controlled to maintain comprehensibility, using delimitation, subdivision, and simple repetition as the primary means of preventing too-rapid development.

# Core Definition

"Variety must never endanger comprehensibility or logic. Comprehensibility requires limitation of variety, especially if notes, harmonies, motive-forms or contrasts follow each other in rapid succession. Rapidity obstructs one's grasp of an idea. Thus pieces in rapid tempo exhibit a lesser degree of variety" (Ch. 5, p. 20). "There are means by which the tendency toward too rapid development, which is often the consequence of disproportionate variety, can be controlled. Delimitation, subdivision and simple repetition are the most useful" (Ch. 5, p. 20).

# Key Properties

1. Variety must never endanger comprehensibility or logic
2. Rapid succession of contrasts obstructs comprehension
3. Pieces in rapid tempo require less variety
4. Three primary control mechanisms: delimitation, subdivision, simple repetition
5. Only as much variation as character, length, and tempo require should be admitted
6. The coherence of motive-forms should be emphasized
7. The principle applies equally to popular and classical music

# Construction / Recognition

1. **Delimitation**: Set boundaries on how far variation extends
2. **Subdivision**: Break complex passages into graspable units
3. **Simple repetition**: Restate material to anchor the listener's comprehension
4. **Tempo awareness**: Reduce variety in fast tempos
5. **Emphasis on coherence**: Maintain motive-form coherence even when introducing variety

# Context & Application

Schoenberg presents this principle in the context of beginning theme construction. He cites the classical masters as seeking a "popular touch" in their themes: "Discretion is especially necessary when the goal is an immediate intelligibility, as in popular music. However, such discretion is not restricted to popular music alone. On the contrary, it is most characteristic of the manner in which the classical masters constructed their forms" (Ch. 5, p. 20). He quotes Mattheson (1739): "A theme should have a certain something which the whole world already knows."

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 20): The eighteenth-century "ars nova" detaching itself from contrapuntal complexity in favor of more immediately intelligible structures.

**Example 2** (p. 20): Romain Rolland's Musical Journey citing Mattheson's Vollkommener Kappelmeister (1739) on the "new style" -- composers should "hide the fact that they write great music."

**Example 3** (p. 20): Fast movements in classical sonatas exhibiting less motivic variety than slow movements.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Introducing excessive variety in fast tempos
  **Correction**: "Rapidity obstructs one's grasp of an idea" -- reduce variety in fast passages

- **Error**: Suppressing variety entirely to maintain control
  **Correction**: Control means purposeful management, not elimination; variety is still necessary

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing the "popular touch" is simplistic or inferior
  **Clarification**: The classical masters themselves sought immediate intelligibility -- it is a hallmark of great composition, not a compromise

- **Confusion**: Thinking control of variety applies only to popular music
  **Clarification**: It is "most characteristic of the manner in which the classical masters constructed their forms"
