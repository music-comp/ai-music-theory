# Quick Definition
The principle that variety must be limited and controlled to maintain comprehensibility, using delimitation, subdivision, and simple repetition as the primary means of preventing too-rapid development.

# Formal Definition
Variety must never endanger comprehensibility or logic. Comprehensibility requires limitation of variety, especially if notes, harmonies, motive-forms, or contrasts follow each other in rapid succession. Rapidity obstructs one's grasp of an idea, so pieces in rapid tempo exhibit a lesser degree of variety. There are means by which the tendency toward too rapid development, which is often the consequence of disproportionate variety, can be controlled: delimitation, subdivision, and simple repetition are the most useful.

# Construction/Recognition
Control of variety is exercised through:
- **Delimitation**: setting boundaries on how far variation extends
- **Subdivision**: breaking complex passages into graspable units
- **Simple repetition**: restating material to anchor the listener's comprehension
- **Tempo awareness**: reducing variety in fast tempos, where the listener has less time to process new information
- **Emphasis on coherence**: maintaining the coherence of motive-forms even when introducing variety

# Musical Context
Schoenberg presents this as a principle equally applicable to popular and classical music. He cites the classical masters as seeking a "popular touch" in their themes, and quotes Mattheson (1739) from the Vollkommener Kappelmeister as saying that in the "new style" composers should "hide the fact that they write great music" and that "a theme should have a certain something which the whole world already knows." This ideal of immediate intelligibility governs the control of variety.

# Examples
- Classical masters constructing forms with a "popular touch"
- The eighteenth-century "ars nova" detaching itself from contrapuntal complexity
- Romain Rolland's Musical Journey citing Mattheson on the "new style"
- Fast movements in Beethoven sonatas exhibiting less motivic variety than slow movements

# Common Confusions
- Control of variety does not mean suppression of variety; it means purposeful management
- The "popular touch" sought by classical masters is not simplicity for its own sake but immediate intelligibility
- The need for control applies equally to popular and art music, though the balance point differs
- Rapid tempo is not an excuse for monotony; it is a context that requires a different balance of variety and repetition
