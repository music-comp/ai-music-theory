# Quick Definition
Centripetal harmony remains centered about the tonic region, reinforcing tonal stability, while centrifugal harmony tends to move away from the tonic, potentially producing modulations that must be balanced compositionally.

# Formal Definition
Schoenberg introduces these terms to describe the directional tendency of harmonic progressions relative to the tonic. Centripetal harmony is centered about the tonic region and expresses the tonality clearly, as in diatonic sequences. Centrifugal harmony tends to move away from the tonic, potentially through the use of substitute chords (altered or chromatic chords), and may produce modulations. The balancing of centrifugal harmony becomes a compositional problem -- a challenge that Romantic composers addressed by simplifying motivic and rhythmic construction to compensate for harmonic complexity.

# Construction/Recognition
- Centripetal: diatonic chords, clear expression of tonality, tonic-centered progressions
- Centrifugal: chromatic or substitute chords, tendency toward foreign regions, modulatory implications
- Balance centrifugal harmony with simpler motivic and rhythmic construction
- Balance remote motival variation with centripetal (simpler) harmony
- Substitutes should reinforce the logic of the progression, not simply add complexity

# Musical Context
The centripetal/centrifugal distinction is central to Schoenberg's understanding of tonal balance. It applies not only to sequences but to all harmonic writing. The principle of compensating complexity in one dimension with simplicity in another -- complex harmony balanced by simple motives, or complex motives balanced by stable harmony -- governs large-scale structural planning. This principle explains historical trends in Romantic composition and provides practical guidance for the student.

# Examples
- Diatonic sequences: harmony remains centripetal, expressing tonality clearly
- Sequences with substitute chords: harmony becomes centrifugal, producing modulations
- Romantic composers (Wagner, Bruckner, Debussy, Franck, Tchaikovsky, Sibelius): compensated centrifugal harmony with unvaried repetitions and frequent sequences

# Common Confusions
- Centrifugal harmony is not inherently bad -- it provides richness and expressiveness -- but it must be balanced by compensating simplification elsewhere.
- The terms describe tendencies, not absolute categories; a passage may shift between centripetal and centrifugal orientations.
- Centripetal harmony is not "boring" harmony; it is structurally stable harmony that serves a compositional purpose.
