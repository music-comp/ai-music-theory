# Quick Definition

Centripetal harmony remains centered about the tonic region, reinforcing tonal stability, while centrifugal harmony tends to move away from the tonic, potentially producing modulations that must be balanced compositionally.

# Core Definition

Centripetal harmony is centered about the tonic region and expresses the tonality clearly, as in diatonic sequences. Centrifugal harmony tends to move away from the tonic through the use of substitute chords (altered or chromatic chords), potentially producing modulations. The balancing of centrifugal harmony becomes a compositional problem (Schoenberg, p. 40).

Schoenberg identifies a broader compositional principle: complexity in one dimension should be compensated by simplicity in another. Centrifugal harmony should be balanced with simpler motival and rhythmic construction. Conversely, remote motival variation should be balanced with centripetal (simpler) harmony. This principle explains historical trends: Romantic composers who used complex harmony (Wagner, Bruckner, Debussy, Franck, Tchaikovsky, Sibelius) counteracted the tension with unvaried repetitions and frequent sequences (pp. 25-26).

# Key Properties

1. Centripetal: diatonic chords, clear tonality, tonic-centered progressions
2. Centrifugal: chromatic or substitute chords, tendency toward foreign regions, modulatory
3. Balance centrifugal harmony with simpler motival/rhythmic construction
4. Balance remote motival variation with centripetal harmony
5. The terms describe tendencies, not absolute categories
6. Substitutes should reinforce the logic of the progression

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify whether a passage maintains clear tonal orientation (centripetal) or tends away from the tonic (centrifugal)
2. When using centrifugal harmony, simplify motival and rhythmic construction to compensate
3. When using remote motival variation, stabilize the harmony (centripetal approach)
4. Use substitute chords to reinforce progression logic, not merely to add complexity

# Context & Application

The centripetal/centrifugal distinction is central to Schoenberg's understanding of tonal balance. It applies not only to sequences but to all harmonic writing. The compensating complexity principle governs large-scale structural planning and explains both Classical and Romantic compositional practice. By the time of Mahler, Reger, Strauss, and Ravel, far-reaching harmony no longer seriously endangered comprehensibility.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using centrifugal harmony without compensating simplification elsewhere
  **Correction**: Balance centrifugal harmony with simpler motival and rhythmic construction

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking centrifugal harmony is inherently bad
  **Clarification**: It provides richness and expressiveness but must be balanced

- **Confusion**: Treating centripetal/centrifugal as absolute categories
  **Clarification**: They describe tendencies; a passage may shift between orientations

- **Confusion**: Equating centripetal with "boring"
  **Clarification**: Centripetal harmony is structurally stable and serves a clear compositional purpose
