# Quick Definition

The principle that a well-balanced melody progresses in waves -- each upward elevation countered by a depression -- approaching a climax through intermediate lesser high points interrupted by recessions, with upward movements balanced by downward movements.

# Core Definition

"A well-balanced melody progresses in waves, i.e. each elevation is countered by a depression. It approaches a high point or climax through a series of intermediate lesser high points, interrupted by recessions. Upward movements are balanced by downward movements; large intervals are compensated for by conjunct movement in the opposite direction" (Ch. 4, p. 12).

# Prerequisites

- **phrase** -- Wave-like motion operates at the phrase and multi-phrase level
- **motive** -- The motive's contour contributes to the wave pattern

# Key Properties

1. Each elevation is countered by a depression (wave pattern)
2. The climax is approached through intermediate lesser high points
3. Recessions interrupt the approach to the climax
4. Upward movements are balanced by downward movements
5. Large intervals are compensated by conjunct movement in the opposite direction
6. The melody stays within a reasonable compass

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct a Wave-Like Melody

1. Begin within a central range
2. Allow the melody to rise to an intermediate high point
3. Follow with a recession (descent or settling)
4. Rise again to a slightly higher point
5. Continue the pattern of ascent-recession toward the climax
6. After the climax, allow the melody to descend and settle
7. Compensate large intervals with stepwise contrary motion

## To Recognize Wave-Like Motion

1. Graph the pitch contour over time
2. Look for alternation of ascending and descending motion
3. Identify intermediate high points before the climax
4. Note whether large intervals are compensated

# Context & Application

This principle applies when connecting motive-forms into phrases. The composer must ensure that successive transformations do not push the melody relentlessly in one direction. Wave-like motion is a natural consequence of good phrase construction: it creates a self-regulating quality that makes melody balanced and graspable. The principle connects to compass and register: a melody that progresses in waves naturally stays within a reasonable range. The principle is treated more extensively in Chapter 11: Melody and Theme, with contour analysis of melodies by Bach, Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Moving too long in one direction without compensating motion
  **Correction**: Balance upward movements with downward movements; compensate large intervals with conjunct contrary motion

- **Error**: Reaching the climax too early without intermediate high points
  **Correction**: Approach the climax through a series of intermediate peaks interrupted by recessions

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking wave motion means mechanical alternation
  **Clarification**: The waves are irregular and varied in amplitude; the pattern is organic, not mechanical

- **Confusion**: Believing wave-like motion prohibits large intervals
  **Clarification**: Large intervals are permitted but must be "compensated for by conjunct movement in the opposite direction"

- **Confusion**: Assuming the climax must be the highest note
  **Clarification**: The climax is the point of greatest intensity, which often but not always coincides with the highest pitch
