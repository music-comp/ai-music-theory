# Quick Definition
The practice of playing or reading harmony and melody separately, multiple times, to prevent self-deception -- since the virtues of one element may hide the shortcomings of the other.

# Core Definition
Play or read harmony and melody separately several times. This sometimes prevents self-deception. Perhaps only one of these factors is poor, its shortcomings hidden behind the virtues of the other. If the harmonic progression is satisfactory, the beginner may easily overlook shortcomings in the melody. A good melody should be smooth, fluent, and balanced when it is played without accompaniment.

# Prerequisites
- self-criticism: This is the first step in the self-criticism framework

# Key Properties
- Play or read harmony and melody separately multiple times
- Prevents self-deception caused by one element masking another's flaws
- A satisfactory harmonic progression can hide a poor melody
- A good melody should sound smooth, fluent, and balanced without accompaniment
- The best tool of a musician is the ear
- Every good musician must possess the "inner ear" (auditory imagery)

# Construction / Recognition
To apply this principle: (1) play only the melody without accompaniment and evaluate its contour, balance, and fluency, (2) play only the harmonic progression and evaluate its logic and flow, (3) listen for weaknesses in each that might be masked by the other, (4) ensure the melody stands on its own as a smooth and balanced line.

# Context & Application
This is Schoenberg's first and most fundamental advice for self-criticism. The ear is the musician's primary tool. Separating the elements forces honest evaluation of each component. A melody that only sounds good with its accompaniment may not be a good melody at all.

# Examples
- A beginner who writes a satisfactory harmonic progression may easily overlook a poor melody because the harmony masks its shortcomings
- A good melody should sound smooth, fluent, and balanced when played without any accompaniment

# Common Errors
- Evaluating melody and harmony only together, never separately
- Assuming a good harmonic progression guarantees a good melody
- Neglecting to play through the work multiple times

# Common Confusions
- "Listening separately" is not about orchestral parts or voices; it means separating the melodic line from its harmonic support
- A melody that only sounds good with its accompaniment may not be a good melody at all
