# Quick Definition

The technique of altering the harmony of the theme in variations through methods such as major-minor interchange, substitution of passing harmonies, reinterpretation of harmonic functions, and chromatic bass movement, while maintaining the overall harmonic framework.

# Core Definition

Harmonic variation encompasses all changes to the theme's harmonic framework within a variation. These include: interchange of major and minor (never carried out mechanically by merely changing the key signature); substitution of different chord progressions in sequential passages; reinterpretation of harmonic functions (e.g., converting a I chord to the dominant of the subdominant); addition or omission of passing harmonies; chromatic movement in melody and bass; and changes in the harmonic identity of structural points. The interchange of major and minor is a striking source of harmonic variety, but specific chords must be adapted.

# Key Properties

- Major-minor interchange: requires adaptation, not merely changing the key signature (e.g., IV may be replaced by VI-II)
- Melodic bass progression can be transplanted to create root progressions (e.g., B-flat to D becomes I-III)
- Passing harmonies may be added or omitted to alter harmonic density
- Sequential passages are often harmonically varied by substituting different progressions
- Structural cadence points may be approached through different harmonic paths
- The approach to the cadence is often modified along with the mode change
- New harmonic skeletons may be produced by changing the viewpoint on what is essential

# Construction / Recognition

1. Compare the variation's harmony to the theme's harmony measure by measure
2. Identify major-minor interchange and check for specific chord adaptations
3. Note substituted progressions in sequential passages
4. Check for added or omitted passing harmonies
5. Trace changes at structural cadence points
6. Identify chromatic bass movement

# Context & Application

Harmonic variation is one of the most powerful tools available to the variation composer. It allows fundamental character change while preserving structural proportions and melodic outline. Brahms frequently produces new skeletons by changing the viewpoint on essential features, enabling him to reinterpret harmonic functions creatively.

# Examples

- Beethoven, 32 Variations in c minor: Major-minor interchange in Variations 12, 13, 15, 16; IV of m. 3-4 replaced by VI-II; approach to cadence modified
- Beethoven, Op. 35, Variation 6: Theme harmonized almost entirely in c minor, with only last two measures re-establishing E-flat
- Beethoven, Op. 35, Variation 14: In e-flat minor, deviating only through transformations (especially Neapolitan sixth)
- Brahms, Handel Variations: Melodic progression B-flat to D transplanted to bass as root progression I-III; mediant at third measure in Variations 7, 9, 11, 14, 19
- Brahms, Handel Variations, Variation 9: B-section on dominant (F); written-out repetition on F-sharp (as G-flat), establishing flat submediant major
- Brahms, Handel Variations, Variation 20: Characterized by chromatic movement in melody and bass

# Common Errors

- Mechanically changing the key signature for major-minor interchange (specific chords must be adapted)
- Abandoning the harmonic framework entirely (some connection must be maintained)
- Failing to adapt the approach to the cadence when changing mode

# Common Confusions

- **Mechanical vs. genuine interchange**: Major-minor interchange never involves merely changing the key signature; specific chords (e.g., IV becoming VI-II) must be adapted.
- **New skeleton vs. arbitrary change**: Brahms's technique of producing new skeletons by changing the viewpoint on essential features is systematic, not arbitrary.
