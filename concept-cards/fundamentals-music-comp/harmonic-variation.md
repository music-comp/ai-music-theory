# Quick Definition
The technique of altering the harmony of the theme in variations through methods such as major-minor interchange, substitution of passing harmonies, reinterpretation of harmonic functions, and chromatic bass movement, while maintaining the overall harmonic framework.

# Formal Definition
Harmonic variation encompasses all changes to the theme's harmonic framework within a variation. These include: interchange of major and minor (never carried out mechanically by merely changing the key signature); substitution of different chord progressions in sequential passages; reinterpretation of harmonic functions (e.g., converting a I chord to the dominant of the subdominant); addition or omission of passing harmonies; chromatic movement in melody and bass; and changes in the harmonic identity of structural points (e.g., replacing I or III at the third measure with VI or IV).

# Construction/Recognition
- Major-minor interchange: requires adaptation, not merely changing the key signature (e.g., IV may be replaced by VI-II)
- Melodic bass progression (e.g., B-flat to D in the Handel Variations) can be transplanted to create a root progression (I-III)
- Passing harmonies may be added or omitted to alter the harmonic density
- Sequential passages are often harmonically varied by substituting different progressions
- Structural cadence points may be approached through different harmonic paths
- The approach to the cadence is often modified along with the mode change

# Musical Context
Harmonic variation is one of the most powerful tools available to the variation composer. It allows the character of a variation to be fundamentally changed while preserving the theme's structural proportions and melodic outline. The most sophisticated harmonic variations can make the theme nearly unrecognizable harmonically while maintaining structural intelligibility.

# Examples
- Beethoven, 32 Variations in c minor: Interchange of major and minor in Variations 12, 13, 15, 16; IV of m. 3-4 replaced by VI-II; approach to cadence modified
- Beethoven, 15 Variations in E-flat, Op. 35, Variation 6: Theme harmonized almost entirely in c minor, with only the last two measures re-establishing E-flat
- Beethoven, 15 Variations in E-flat, Op. 35, Variation 14: In e-flat minor, deviating from original harmony only through transformations (especially Neapolitan sixth)
- Brahms, Handel Variations: Melodic progression B-flat to D transplanted to the bass as root progression I-III; mediant at the third measure used in Variations 7, 9, 11, 14, 19 to lead to various cadences
- Brahms, Handel Variations, Variation 9: B-section begins on the dominant (F), written-out repetition begins on F-sharp (as G-flat), establishing flat submediant major
- Brahms, Handel Variations, Variation 20: Characterized by chromatic movement in melody and bass

# Common Confusions
- Major-minor interchange is never a mechanical process of changing the key signature; specific chords must be adapted
- Harmonic variation does not mean abandoning the theme's harmonic framework; it means reinterpreting it
- Even the most far-reaching harmonic variations preserve the overall structural proportions
