# Quick Definition
A group of subsidiary themes in a contrasting key that together form the second main section of a sonata-allegro exposition, characterized by looser construction and greater reliance on repetition and juxtaposition than the principal theme.

# Formal Definition
Subordinate themes may have originated as condensations and stabilizations within a contrasting modulatory movement. At first little more than episodes, they later developed into definite subsidiary sections, establishing and ending in a related key (e.g., dominant or relative major). Ideally, subordinate themes are derivatives of the basic motive, even though the connexion may not be readily visible. Contrast in mood, character, dynamics, rhythm, harmony, motive-forms, and construction should distinguish main themes from subordinate, and subordinate themes from each other.

# Construction/Recognition
- Repetitions within the main theme enhance memorability and prepare for development; in subordinate themes, mere repetition and juxtaposition often replace development and elaboration
- Frequently consists of a number of distinct formulations, each established and then abandoned to make way for the next
- Evidence of subordination lies in the looser construction: immediate repetition of relatively short segments, joined to others by mere juxtaposition, with a lesser degree of internal development
- Great diversity of length and complexity may occur, even within a single composer's style

# Musical Context
The subordinate theme group provides the primary thematic contrast to the principal theme in the exposition of sonata-allegro and rondo forms. The aesthetically most important type of contrast is that of construction, since it is evidence of subordination.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: a phrase appears three times (m. 21-25), followed by juxtaposed segments with partial repetitions; codettas begin in m. 41
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I: similarly organized with sequential repetitions (m. 59-62), two-measure segments sequenced (m. 70), derivatives of the principal theme juxtaposed without connective (m. 76)
- Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I: first subordinate theme (m. 27) in minor v; a six-measure segment repeated quasi-sequentially; a more lyrical theme (m. 47-61) with imitations; additional formulations in m. 61, 69, 73
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-IV: subordinate theme of deliberate insignificance (little more than a chromatic scale line), emphasizing its subordinate function

# Common Confusions
- The subordinate theme group is not a single theme but typically a succession of distinct formulations; the term "group" is essential
- Subordinate themes are ideally derived from the basic motive, even when the connection is hidden; they are not arbitrary contrasts
- The "looseness" of construction is a deliberate compositional strategy, not a weakness
