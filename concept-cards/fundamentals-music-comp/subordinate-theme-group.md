# Quick Definition
A group of distinct thematic formulations in a contrasting key, each established and then abandoned to make way for the next, characterized by loose construction with repetition and juxtaposition replacing development and elaboration.

# Core Definition
Subordinate themes may have originated as condensations and stabilizations within a contrasting modulatory movement. At first little more than episodes, they later developed into definite subsidiary sections, establishing and ending in a related key (e.g., dominant or relative major). Ideally, subordinate themes are derivatives of the basic motive, even though the connexion may not be readily visible. Contrast in mood, character, dynamics, rhythm, harmony, motive-forms, and construction should distinguish main themes from subordinate, and subordinate themes from each other. The most important type of contrast is that of construction, since it is evidence of subordination: in subordinate themes, mere repetition and juxtaposition often replace development and elaboration.

# Key Properties
- Typically a group of several distinct formulations, not a single theme
- Each formulation is established and then abandoned for the next
- Loose construction: repetition and juxtaposition replace development
- Contrast with the principal theme in mood, character, dynamics, rhythm, harmony, and construction
- Ideally derivative of the basic motive, even when the connection is hidden
- Great diversity of length and complexity possible even within one composer's works

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for a succession of distinct short segments, each repeated and then dropped
- Identify juxtaposition (segments joined without connective development)
- Note the "looser" construction compared to the principal theme
- The harmony should establish and end in a related key (dominant or relative major)
- Segments may be linked by overlap, or simply placed side by side
- Codettas typically follow to close the group

# Context & Application
The subordinate theme group provides the primary thematic contrast in sonata-allegro and rondo forms. Its loose construction is aesthetically significant as evidence of subordination -- the principal theme commands attention through development and variation, while subordinate themes achieve richness through multiplication and juxtaposition.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: a phrase appears three times (m. 21-25), followed by juxtaposed segments with partial repetitions; codettas begin m. 41
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I: similarly organized with sequential repetitions (m. 59-62), two-measure derivatives (m. 70), juxtaposed principal theme derivatives (m. 76), and a cadential segment with repetition (m. 84)
- Beethoven, Op. 2/3-I: multiple distinct formulations including a lyrical theme with imitations (m. 47-61), plus additional formulations in m. 61, 69, 73, followed by codettas
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-IV: subordinate theme is "little more than a chromatic scale line up and down" -- its insignificance of content emphasizes subordinateness

# Common Errors
- Making subordinate themes as tightly organized as principal themes, destroying the essential contrast of construction
- Creating subordinate themes that are entirely unrelated to the basic motive
- Failing to provide sufficient variety among the distinct formulations within the group

# Common Confusions
- "Subordinate" does not mean inferior in quality; it refers to structural function and type of construction
- The great diversity of length and complexity (from Op. 14/1-III's eight measures to Op. 2/3-I's extensive group) makes generalization difficult
- The number of distinct formulations within the group is highly variable and determined by the needs of the individual composition
