# Quick Definition
Changes of character within a single movement -- even within its smaller sections -- are essential to good music; all good music consists of many contrasting ideas.

# Core Definition
The changes of character within a single movement are even more important than the contrasts between movements. An idea achieves distinctness and validity in contrast with others. Heraclitus called contrast "the principle of development." Musical thinking is subject to the same dialectic as all other thinking. All good music consists of many contrasting ideas that achieve their distinctness through juxtaposition.

# Prerequisites
- character-and-mood: Understanding what character is before understanding how contrasts in character operate

# Key Properties
- Internal contrast is more important than contrast between movements
- An idea achieves distinctness and validity in contrast with others
- Contrast is "the principle of development" (Heraclitus)
- Musical thinking is dialectical, like all other thinking
- Contrast operates at every structural level, from within phrases to between large sections

# Construction / Recognition
Look for contrasts in: (1) texture changes, (2) articulation shifts (e.g., staccato vs. legato), (3) dynamic shifts, (4) rhythmic character changes, (5) changes in thematic material, and (6) dramatic pauses or sudden shifts. Even small sections should contain internal contrasts.

# Context & Application
Internal contrast is the engine of musical development. Without it, even well-crafted thematic material becomes monotonous. The principle applies at every structural level, from within a phrase to between large sections. This concept is fundamental to understanding how larger forms generate interest and momentum.

# Examples
- Beethoven Op. 57-I: sudden change of texture in m. 24, intensification in m. 28 ff., gradual liquidation m. 31 ff.
- Op. 57-I: dramatic change when "dolce legato" (m. 35) replaces the previous hard staccato of the left hand
- Op. 57-I: the movement suddenly stops in m. 41, followed by a new and even stronger contrast in texture (m. 51)
- Strong contrast between principal and subordinate themes in Op. 57-I

# Common Errors
- Writing sections without internal contrast, producing monotony
- Providing contrast without maintaining coherence
- Limiting contrast to large-scale sectional differences while neglecting moment-to-moment variety

# Common Confusions
- Contrast does not mean disconnection; contrasting ideas must still be related to maintain coherence
- Contrast operates at multiple scales simultaneously -- large-scale sectional contrast and moment-to-moment textural contrast coexist
