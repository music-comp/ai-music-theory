# Quick Definition

The technique of repeating a short musical segment immediately after its first statement, often with slight variation, as a primary means of establishing thematic material in subordinate theme groups before it is abandoned.

# Core Definition

In subordinate themes, "mere repetition and juxtaposition often replace development and elaboration" (Ch. 18, p. 103). The direct and immediate repetition of relatively short segments -- typically two to four measures -- is a defining characteristic of the loose construction that distinguishes subordinate from principal themes. Such repetition establishes each formulation before it is abandoned to make way for the next. The evidence suggests "a 'looser' construction, depending on immediate repetition of relatively short segments, joined to others by mere juxtaposition; and a lesser degree of internal development" (Ch. 18, p. 104).

# Prerequisites

- **subordinate-theme-group** -- Direct repetition is the primary establishment technique within subordinate theme groups
- **loose-construction** -- Direct repetition is characteristic of loose construction

# Key Properties

1. A short segment (often two to four measures) is stated and immediately repeated
2. The repetition may be exact, sequential (transposed), or slightly varied
3. After establishment through repetition, the idea typically gives way to another distinct formulation
4. Multiple such repetition-and-abandonment units are strung together to form the subordinate group
5. The technique is fundamentally different from developmental repetitions in principal themes, which involve progressive variation
6. Unified by a persistent rhythm, the intervals may change freely
7. The simple construction is deliberate evidence of subordination

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct Direct and Immediate Repetition

1. Create a short thematic segment (typically 2-4 measures)
2. Repeat it immediately -- exactly, sequentially, or with slight variation
3. Optionally add a third repetition with further variation or liquidation
4. Abandon the idea and move to a new formulation (via juxtaposition)
5. Repeat the establish-and-abandon pattern for subsequent ideas in the subordinate group

## To Recognize Direct and Immediate Repetition

1. Identify a short segment that is restated immediately after its first appearance
2. Confirm the repetition is exact, sequential, or only slightly varied (not developmental)
3. Note that after establishment, the material is abandoned rather than developed further
4. Observe the pattern occurring within a subordinate thematic context

# Context & Application

Direct and immediate repetition is fundamental to the construction of subordinate theme groups in sonata-allegro and rondo forms. It provides a simpler, more accessible type of thematic presentation that contrasts with the developmental procedures of principal themes. The "popular touch" of many such themes derives from this reliance on immediate repetition: "Unified by a persistent rhythm, the intervals change freely" (Ch. 18, p. 104). Even in Beethoven's Fifth Symphony, the subordinate theme features continuous quarter-note movement where "the first phrase (four measures) is repeated twice without structural change" (Ch. 18, p. 104).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 103): Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I. A phrase appears three times in m. 21-25, establishing the subordinate theme through immediate repetition before juxtaposed segments follow.

**Example 2** (p. 103): Beethoven, Op. 2/2-I. A small segment (m. 59-62) is twice sequentially repeated; a two-measure segment (m. 70) is also sequenced twice, demonstrating sequential immediate repetition.

**Example 3** (p. 103): Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV. A two-measure phrase (m. 27-28) is repeated and, after a partial second repetition, liquidated through sequential and other repetitions of a differing segment (m. 32-39).

**Example 4** (p. 104): Beethoven, Op. 14/1-III. The entire subordinate theme comprises eight measures (m. 22-29) -- a four-measure segment and its repetition. The simplest possible case.

**Example 5** (p. 104): Beethoven, Fifth Symphony-I (m. 63-93). Continuous quarter-note movement; the first four-measure phrase is repeated twice without structural change, followed by two more four-measure phrases plus liquidation.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Developing material after establishing it through immediate repetition in a subordinate theme
  **Correction**: After establishment through repetition, the material should be abandoned in favor of a new formulation

- **Error**: Using developmental variation where simple repetition is needed
  **Correction**: Subordinate themes use simpler repetition (exact, sequential, or slightly varied) rather than progressive developmental variation

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Direct repetition in subordinate themes is the same as repetition in principal themes
  **Clarification**: In principal themes, repetition serves development and variation; in subordinate themes, it serves establishment before the material is abandoned

- **Confusion**: "Immediate" repetition means any repetition
  **Clarification**: "Immediate" means the segment is repeated right away, not after intervening material

- **Confusion**: Simple repetition implies weak composition
  **Clarification**: It is a deliberate constructional technique -- "evidence of subordination" -- and contributes to the necessary contrast between principal and subordinate themes
