# Quick Definition
The principles governing the ordering and grouping of variations within a set, including the contrast of character between variations, the building toward climaxes, the grouping of related variations, and the traditional maggiore-minore key contrast.

# Formal Definition
The most important principle of organization is variety. This does not preclude grouping two or three variations without considerable change of character, especially if an idea is elaborated in several steps. Since Beethoven, the general tendency has been to build up toward a climax (in very long sets, a series of climaxes), which may be emotional, rhythmic, dynamic, velocitative, or any combination of these. Contrasts of character are especially necessary if the piece offers no greater key contrast than tonic major-tonic minor (maggiore-minore). The set is often concluded by a coda, finale, or fugue.

# Construction/Recognition
- Variety is the primary organizational principle
- Related variations may be grouped (2-3 variations elaborating one idea)
- Build toward climax(es): emotional, rhythmic, dynamic, velocitative
- Character contrasts: lyric vs. rhythmic, cantabile vs. energico, etc.
- Traditional key contrast: maggiore-minore (tonic major to tonic minor)
- Exceptional key schemes are possible (e.g., Beethoven Op. 34: variations in F, D, B-flat, G, E-flat, c)
- Metre and tempo may change between variations for additional contrast
- Transitions or bridges may be inserted between variations
- A coda or finale is generally added, sometimes as an outgrowth of the last variation
- Interpretive indications (legato, staccato, risoluto, dolce, energico, grazioso, legiero) contribute to character differentiation

# Musical Context
The organization of a variation set is one of the most creative aspects of the form, requiring the composer to balance unity and variety across the entire piece. The traditional restriction to one tonic has been outgrown, just as the sonata and symphony moved beyond single-key restriction. The intermingling of contrasting characters (lyric with rhythmic, cantabile with energetic) prevents monotony and maintains the listener's interest.

# Examples
- Beethoven, 6 Variations Op. 34: Unique key scheme (F, D, B-flat, G, E-flat, c minor) with variations in different tempi and metres; contrast enhanced by altering the metre across variations
- Brahms, Handel Variations: Lyric or cantabile (5, 6, 11); impassioned (9, 20); rhythmic (7, 8); Hungarian (13); musette-like (22); with interpretive indications including legato, staccato, risoluto, dolce, energico, grazioso, legiero
- Beethoven, 32 Variations in c minor: Groups of related variations (1-3, 10-11, 15-16, 20-21-22) with major-minor interchange
- Beethoven, 15 Variations in E-flat, Op. 35: Begins with bass alone, progressively adding voices; concludes with Finale alla Fuga

# Common Confusions
- "Formal variations" and "character variations" are not mutually exclusive -- every variation has character, and formal variations also possess it
- Grouping related variations is not the same as lacking variety -- it is a deliberate organizational choice
- The build toward climax does not mean a simple crescendo; it may be emotional, rhythmic, or structural
