# Quick Definition

The principles governing the ordering and grouping of variations within a set: variety as the primary principle, the building toward climaxes, the grouping of related variations, the intermingling of contrasting characters, and the traditional maggiore-minore key contrast.

# Core Definition

The most important principle of organization is variety. This does not preclude grouping two or three variations without considerable change of character, especially if an idea is elaborated in several steps. Since Beethoven, the general tendency has been to build up toward a climax (in very long sets, a series of climaxes), which may be emotional, rhythmic, dynamic, velocitative, or any combination of these. Contrasts of character are especially necessary if the piece offers no greater key contrast than tonic major-tonic minor (maggiore-minore). The set is often concluded by a coda, finale, or fugue.

# Key Properties

- Variety is the primary organizational principle
- Related variations may be grouped (2-3 variations elaborating one idea)
- Build toward climax(es): emotional, rhythmic, dynamic, velocitative
- Character contrasts: lyric vs. rhythmic, cantabile vs. energico, etc.
- Traditional key contrast: maggiore-minore (tonic major to tonic minor)
- Exceptional key schemes are possible (Beethoven Op. 34: F, D, B-flat, G, E-flat, c)
- Metre and tempo may change between variations for additional contrast
- Transitions or bridges may be inserted between variations
- A coda or finale is generally added, sometimes as an outgrowth of the last variation
- Interpretive indications (legato, staccato, risoluto, dolce, energico, grazioso, legiero) differentiate character

# Construction / Recognition

1. Assess the variety across the set (character, rhythm, dynamics, tempo, key)
2. Identify groups of related variations (elaborating the same idea)
3. Trace the climactic build (or series of climaxes)
4. Note key relationships (maggiore-minore, exceptional schemes)
5. Check for transitions or bridges between variations
6. Identify the concluding section (coda, finale, fugue)
7. Note interpretive indications that differentiate character

# Context & Application

Schoenberg observes that the traditional restriction to one tonic has been outgrown, just as the sonata and symphony moved beyond single-key restriction. The organization of a variation set is one of the most creative aspects of the form, requiring balance of unity and variety across the entire piece. Brahms's technique of intermingling lyric and rhythmic variations is particularly effective.

# Examples

- Beethoven, 6 Variations Op. 34: Unique key scheme (F, D, B-flat, G, E-flat, c minor); different tempi (adagio to allegro) and metres; concluded by a coda returning to adagio
- Brahms, Handel Variations: Lyric (5, 6, 11); impassioned (9, 20); rhythmic (7, 8); Hungarian (13); musette-like (22); with markings legato, staccato, risoluto, dolce, energico, grazioso, legiero; concluded by fugue
- Beethoven, 32 Variations: Groups of related variations (1-3, 10-11, 15-16, 20-21-22); major-minor interchange
- Beethoven, Op. 35: Begins with bass alone, progressively adding voices; concluded by Finale alla Fuga

# Common Errors

- Placing too many similar variations in succession without character contrast
- Failing to build toward a climax
- Omitting a concluding section (coda, finale, fugue)

# Common Confusions

- **Grouping vs. monotony**: Grouping 2-3 related variations is a deliberate organizational choice, not a failure of variety.
- **Climax types**: A climax need not be loud; it may be emotional, rhythmic, or velocitative (increasing speed).
- **Key restriction**: Schoenberg notes that the restriction to one tonic is outdated, though maggiore-minore remains the most common contrast.
