# Quick Definition

A pair of related phrase formulations analogous to dux and comes in fugue, where the first phrase is based on tonic harmony (tonic form) and the second repeats it with dominant harmony (dominant form), providing variety within unity.

# Core Definition

"In many classical examples one finds a relationship between first and second phrase similar to that of dux (tonic form) and comes (dominant form) in the fugue" (Ch. 5, p. 21). "This kind of repetition, through its slightly contrasting formulation, provides variety in unity. In the repetition, the rhythm and contour of the melody are preserved. An element of contrast enters through the changed harmony and the necessary adaptation of the melody" (Ch. 5, pp. 21-22). The terms "tonic version" and "dominant version" would be more precise, "but Schoenberg preferred to use the more familiar" terminology (editor's footnote, Ch. 5, p. 21).

# Prerequisites

- **motive** -- The motive provides the material that is presented in tonic and dominant forms

# Key Properties

1. Tonic form uses tonic-area harmony (I, I-V, I-V-I, I-IV, I-II)
2. Dominant form answers with dominant-area harmony
3. Rhythm and contour are preserved; harmony and melody adapt
4. V-I is preferable to simple harmonic reversal because it "expresses the tonality more clearly"
5. The ending on I preceded by V is "so useful that it is often applied" regardless of the tonic form's harmonies
6. Terminology borrowed from fugal practice (dux/comes)

# Construction / Recognition

## Harmonic Correspondence Table

| Tonic Form | Dominant Form |
|------------|---------------|
| I          | V             |
| I-V        | V-I           |
| I-V-I      | V-I-V         |
| I-IV       | V-I           |
| I-II       | V-I           |

## Key Guidelines

1. "In the last two cases, simple reversal of the harmonies is not impossible; but the V-I progression is preferable because it expresses the tonality more clearly" (Ch. 5, p. 22)
2. "The ending on I preceded by V is so useful that it is often applied when, for example, tonic form is I-III, I-VI or even I-III" (Ch. 5, p. 22)
3. When the tonic form has too many harmonies, only the main harmonies need be answered

# Context & Application

The tonic form / dominant form relationship is one of the most important techniques for constructing the opening of a sentence. It allows repetition with enough harmonic contrast to avoid monotony while maintaining tonal coherence. The dominant form does not imply modulation to the dominant key; it means using dominant-area harmony within the tonic key.

**Example 3** (p. 21, Ex. 36): I-V-I answered by V-I-V.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Mechanically reversing all harmonies from tonic to dominant form
  **Correction**: V-I is preferable to simple reversal for the last harmony because it "expresses the tonality more clearly"

- **Error**: Attempting to answer a harmonically complex tonic form literally
  **Correction**: "To answer literally a tonic form with too many harmonies is impracticable" -- answer only the main harmonies

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking "dominant form" means modulation to the dominant key
  **Clarification**: Dominant form uses dominant-area harmony within the tonic key, not a modulation

- **Confusion**: Equating tonic/dominant form with literal transposition up a fifth
  **Clarification**: The process involves harmonic reinterpretation with melodic adaptation, not simple transposition

- **Confusion**: Assuming the fugal terminology implies contrapuntal procedure
  **Clarification**: The dux/comes analogy is structural; sentence construction is homophonic
