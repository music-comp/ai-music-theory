# Quick Definition
A pair of related phrase formulations analogous to dux and comes in fugue, where the first phrase is based on tonic harmony and the second repeats it with dominant harmony, providing variety within unity.

# Formal Definition
In many classical examples, one finds a relationship between the first and second phrase similar to that of dux (tonic form) and comes (dominant form) in the fugue. This kind of repetition, through its slightly contrasting formulation, provides variety in unity. In the repetition, the rhythm and contour of the melody are preserved. An element of contrast enters through the changed harmony and the necessary adaptation of the melody.

# Construction/Recognition
The harmonic correspondence between tonic and dominant forms follows these patterns:

| Tonic Form | Dominant Form |
|------------|---------------|
| I | V |
| I-V | V-I |
| I-V-I | V-I-V |
| I-IV | V-I |
| I-II | V-I |

In the last two cases, simple reversal of harmonies is possible, but V-I is preferable because it expresses the tonality more clearly. The ending on I preceded by V is so useful that it is applied even when the tonic form uses I-III, I-VI, or I-III.

# Musical Context
The tonic form/dominant form relationship is one of the most important techniques for constructing the opening of a sentence. It allows the composer to repeat the basic motive with enough harmonic contrast to avoid monotony while maintaining clear tonal coherence. The terms "tonic version" and "dominant version" would be more precise, but Schoenberg preferred the more familiar terminology.

# Common Confusions
- The tonic form/dominant form relationship is not literal transposition; it involves harmonic adaptation, not just shifting everything up a fifth
- Answering a tonic form with too many harmonies literally is "impracticable" -- only the main harmonies need to be answered
- The terminology comes from fugal practice (dux/comes) but the application is to homophonic sentence construction
- "Dominant form" does not mean the phrase is in the dominant key; it means the phrase uses dominant-area harmony
