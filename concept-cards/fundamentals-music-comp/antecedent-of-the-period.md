# Quick Definition
The antecedent is the first half of a period, typically four measures long, which presents the initial thematic material and ends with a caesura, most commonly on the dominant.

# Formal Definition
The antecedent (Schoenberg's "fore-sentence") constitutes the first half of a period structure. Its first phrase (m. 1-2) presents the basic motive, while m. 3-4 introduce more remote, contrasting motive-forms rather than an immediate repetition. This contrasting continuation differentiates the antecedent from the opening of a sentence, where m. 3-4 are a modified repetition. The antecedent concludes with a caesura that creates the structural punctuation necessary before the consequent's modified repetition.

# Construction/Recognition
- M. 1-2: Present the basic motive and its immediate context
- M. 3-4: Introduce contrasting yet coherent motive-forms (not a simple repetition)
- The ending typically arrives on V, approached through half cadence, full cadence, or interchange of I and V
- Antecedents ending on I also exist but are less common
- A connective may bridge the gap between basic motive-forms and more remote derivatives (Exs. 44l, 45c, d, g, 46c, 47d)
- Abrupt juxtaposition of contrasting forms does not necessarily produce imbalance (Ex. 45a)

# Musical Context
The antecedent sets up the expectation for the consequent's repetition. Since the consequent will function as a kind of repetition, the antecedent must be completed with sufficiently remote motive-forms in m. 3-4 to justify that upcoming repetition. The harmonic basis should use progressions with regular harmonic change, which supports unity as a primitive kind of "motive of the accompaniment."

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-II: Antecedent harmony is mere interchange of I and V; the upbeat is twice varied; feminine endings in m. 2, 4, and 8 are unifying characteristics
- Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV: Caesura on V reinforced by half cadence in m. 4; contrast between first and second phrases
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3, Rondo: Chain-like construction in m. 3-4 where the end of each motive-form is the beginning of the next (overlapping like links of a chain)

# Common Confusions
- The antecedent is not a simple statement followed by a simple answer; m. 3-4 must introduce genuinely contrasting material, not merely repeat m. 1-2 (that would make it a sentence opening).
- Ending on V is most common but not mandatory -- antecedents can end on I.
- The contrast in m. 3-4 must still maintain coherence with the basic motive; contrast without connection risks incoherence.
