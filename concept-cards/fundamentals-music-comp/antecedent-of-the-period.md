# Quick Definition

The antecedent is the first half of a period, typically four measures long, presenting the basic motive and then introducing contrasting yet coherent motive-forms, ending with a caesura most commonly on V.

# Core Definition

The construction of the beginning determines the construction of the continuation. The period differs from the sentence in the postponement of repetition: the first phrase is not repeated immediately but is united with more remote (contrasting) motive-forms to constitute the first half of the period, the antecedent. After this contrast, repetition cannot be longer postponed without endangering comprehensibility, so the second half (the consequent) is constructed as a kind of repetition of the antecedent (Schoenberg, p. 22).

In a practice form, the antecedent consists of four measures. M. 1-2 present the basic motive; m. 3-4 introduce more remote motive-forms rather than immediate repetition. In a great majority of cases, the antecedent ends on V, usually approached through a half or full cadence, but sometimes through mere interchange of I and V. Antecedents ending on I also exist.

# Prerequisites

- **Motive** -- The antecedent begins with the basic motive in m. 1-2
- **Phrase** -- The antecedent is built from phrases that must cohere
- **Connecting motive-forms** -- Understanding how motive-forms relate and derive from each other is essential for building the contrasting continuation in m. 3-4

# Key Properties

1. First half of a period, typically four measures in an eight-measure practice form
2. M. 1-2 present the basic motive; m. 3-4 introduce contrasting yet coherent motive-forms
3. Ends with a caesura, most commonly on V
4. Does NOT repeat the first phrase immediately (unlike the sentence opening)
5. The contrast in m. 3-4 must maintain coherence with the basic motive
6. Harmonic basis should use progressions with regular harmonic change

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct an Antecedent

1. Compose m. 1-2 presenting the basic motive and its immediate context
2. For m. 3-4, derive more remote motive-forms that contrast with yet cohere to m. 1-2
3. Follow the "tendency of the smallest notes" -- expect an increase in small notes in m. 3-4, though a decrease (reduction) also works
4. If the gap between basic and remote motive-forms is not obvious, use a connective to bridge them (Exs. 44l, 45c, d, g, 46c, 47d)
5. Mark the caesura melodically by decreasing note values or descending below the opening register
6. End on V through half cadence, full cadence, or interchange of I and V
7. Build motive-forms from broken chords to assure a sound relationship between melody and harmony

## To Recognize an Antecedent

1. Identify the four-measure first half of a period
2. Confirm that m. 3-4 introduce contrasting material rather than repeating m. 1-2
3. Locate the caesura at the end of m. 4

# Context & Application

Only a small percentage of classical themes are periods, and Romantic composers use them still less. However, writing periods is a convenient way to become acquainted with many technical problems. The antecedent sets up the expectation for the consequent's modified repetition. Since the consequent functions as a kind of repetition, the antecedent must be completed with sufficiently remote motive-forms in m. 3-4 to justify that upcoming repetition.

Schoenberg insists that envisioning a definite character helps stimulate inventiveness and that the accompaniment should be incorporated from the very beginning, not added as an afterthought.

# Examples

- **Beethoven, Op. 2/1-II, Adagio** (p. 22): Antecedent ends on V (m. 4) through mere interchange of I and V. The upbeat is twice varied; feminine endings in m. 2, 4, and 8 are unifying characteristics. The melodic approach to a climax in m. 6 is supported by more frequent changes of harmony and increasing use of small notes.

- **Beethoven, Op. 2/2-IV** (p. 22): Caesura on V reinforced by half cadence in m. 4. Contrast between first and second phrases. M. 7-8 produce necessary intensification through remote variation and richer movement.

- **Beethoven, Op. 10/3, Rondo** (p. 22): Chain-like construction in m. 3-4 where the end of each motive-form is the beginning of the next (Ex. 42). The consequent introduces two sequences of the motive in m. 5-6 in place of the single repetition in m. 1-2.

- **Beethoven, Op. 10/3, Menuetto** (p. 22): Antecedent and consequent of eight measures each. First four measures of the consequent transpose the antecedent's melody and harmony a note higher without other variation.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Making m. 3-4 a simple repetition of m. 1-2
  **Correction**: The antecedent requires contrasting motive-forms in m. 3-4; simple repetition would create a sentence opening, not an antecedent

- **Error**: Adding the accompaniment only after composing the melody
  **Correction**: The accompaniment should be conceived together with the theme from the start

- **Error**: Overcrowding the texture with too many small notes following the "tendency of the smallest notes"
  **Correction**: Control the tendency; too many small notes produce a crowded effect

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing the antecedent must always end on V
  **Clarification**: V is the most common ending, but antecedents ending on I also exist

- **Confusion**: Thinking contrast in m. 3-4 means lack of connection to the basic motive
  **Clarification**: The contrast must be coherent -- even remote motive-forms derive from and relate to the basic motive

- **Confusion**: Equating the antecedent with the first phrase of any binary structure
  **Clarification**: The antecedent specifically requires contrasting continuation (not repetition) and a caesura before the consequent's modified repetition
