# Quick Definition

Any harmonically self-sufficient melodic segment may remain unaccompanied, supplying contrast and transparency; however, accompaniment of harmonically ambiguous passages is mandatory.

# Core Definition

Unaccompanied music exists in folk music, ancient church music, solo sonatas, and as segments within otherwise accompanied music. Any harmonically self-sufficient melodic segment may remain unaccompanied, supplying contrast and transparency which may contribute to the character of the piece. Transparency is a merit in itself, and "pauses never sound bad." However, the omission of accompaniment should be confined to self-explanatory segments. Accompaniment of harmonically ambiguous passages is mandatory (Schoenberg, pp. 51-52).

# Key Properties

1. Harmonically self-sufficient segments may remain unaccompanied
2. Transparency is a merit in itself
3. Pauses never sound bad
4. Accompaniment is mandatory for harmonically ambiguous passages
5. Omission confined to self-explanatory segments
6. Common locations: upbeats, theme beginnings, contrapuntal openings, figures within themes, ends of elaboration sections

# Construction / Recognition

## When to Omit Accompaniment

1. Upbeats where harmonic meaning is clear
2. Beginning segments of themes (especially in unison)
3. Contrapuntal or semi-contrapuntal openings (self-explanatory)
4. Brief unaccompanied figures within a theme
5. Ends of elaboration sections
6. Remodulation passages (sometimes in simple unison)

## When Accompaniment Is Mandatory

1. Harmonically ambiguous passages require harmonic clarification
2. Complex rhythmic passages need harmonic support

# Context & Application

Strategic omission of accompaniment is a compositional technique, not a default. Unaccompanied passages create contrast with accompanied ones, provide textural transparency, and can contribute powerfully to character and drama.

# Examples

- **Unaccompanied upbeats** (p. 51): Beethoven, Op. 2/1-II and III; Op. 2/2-III; Op. 7-IV

- **Unaccompanied beginnings** (p. 51): Op. 2/1-I; Op. 10/1-III; Op. 10/2-II; Op. 10/3-I (m. 1-4 and 17-22); Op. 26-IV; Op. 28-III; Op. 57-I

- **Op. 28-III** (p. 51): Piquant ambiguity -- the f-sharp could suggest F-sharp or B major/minor; that it implies D is only unmasked in m. 5-8

- **Op. 53, Rondo, m. 98 ff.** (p. 52): Remodulation from relative minor to tonic carried out in simple unison

- **Unaccompanied elaboration endings** (p. 52): Op. 2/2, Rondo, m. 95 ff.; Op. 2/3, Scherzo, m. 37 ff.; Op. 13-I, m. 187 ff.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Leaving harmonically ambiguous passages unaccompanied
  **Correction**: Accompaniment is mandatory when harmonic meaning is unclear

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking omitting accompaniment is laziness
  **Clarification**: It is a deliberate compositional choice serving contrast and transparency

- **Confusion**: Interpreting "pauses never sound bad" as license for excessive silence
  **Clarification**: It means well-placed rests and gaps are always effective, not that silence should be overused
