# Quick Definition
Any harmonically self-sufficient melodic segment may remain unaccompanied, supplying contrast and transparency; however, accompaniment of harmonically ambiguous passages is mandatory.

# Formal Definition
Unaccompanied music exists in folk music, ancient church music, solo sonatas, and as segments within otherwise accompanied music. Any harmonically self-sufficient melodic segment may remain unaccompanied, providing contrast and transparency that contribute to the character of the piece. Transparency is a merit in itself, and pauses never sound bad. However, accompaniment is mandatory for harmonically ambiguous passages. The principle is that omission of accompaniment should be confined to self-explanatory segments.

# Construction/Recognition
- Unaccompanied upbeats: where harmonic meaning is not in question (Op. 2/1-II, III; Op. 2/2-III; Op. 7-IV)
- Unaccompanied beginning segments: theme opens in unison or without accompaniment (Op. 2/1-I; Op. 10/1-III; Op. 10/2-II; Op. 10/3-I, m. 1-4 and 17-22; Op. 26-IV; Op. 28-III; Op. 57-I)
- Unaccompanied contrapuntal beginnings: self-explanatory due to the contrapuntal texture (Op. 2/3-III; Op. 10/2-III)
- Unaccompanied figures within a theme: brief unaccompanied passages mid-theme (Op. 2/1-I; Op. 10/2-I; Op. 10/3-I, m. 56 ff., 105 ff.)
- Unaccompanied ends of elaboration sections (Op. 2/2, Rondo, m. 95 ff.; Op. 13-I, m. 187 ff.)
- Special case: remodulation carried out in simple unison (Op. 53, Rondo, m. 98 ff.)

# Musical Context
The strategic omission of accompaniment is itself a compositional technique, not merely a default. Unaccompanied passages create contrast with accompanied ones, provide textural transparency, and can contribute powerfully to the character and drama of the piece. The decision of when to accompany and when to leave the melody bare is an essential compositional judgment.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: Theme begins unaccompanied
- Beethoven, Op. 10/3-I: M. 1-4 and m. 17-22 unaccompanied
- Beethoven, Op. 57-I: Unaccompanied beginning
- Beethoven, Op. 28-III: Piquant ambiguity -- the f-sharp could suggest F-sharp or B (major or minor), but D is only revealed in m. 5-8
- Beethoven, Op. 53, Rondo, m. 98 ff.: Remodulation from relative minor to tonic carried out in simple unison

# Common Confusions
- Omitting the accompaniment is not laziness; it is a deliberate compositional choice that serves contrast and transparency.
- Not every passage can be left unaccompanied; harmonically ambiguous passages require accompaniment to clarify the harmony.
- "Pauses never sound bad" does not mean one should use excessive silence; it means that well-placed rests and gaps are always effective.
