# Quick Definition
The process of adapting the motive of variation to every harmonic movement in the theme, including the further variation required by structural features such as cadences, contrasts, and subdivisions, producing "variation within the variation."

# Formal Definition
The motive of variation must accommodate itself to every movement of the harmony (which itself is subject to variation) and must contain all the principal tones of the theme. Variety within the variation requires appropriate elaboration of the motive. Additional elaborations are the consequences of structural features such as cadences, contrasts, and subdivisions. This results in variation within the variation. The contrast between static and sequential harmonic passages in the theme requires great flexibility in the motive, which may need to be very short to accommodate rapid harmonic change.

# Construction/Recognition
- The motive must be adapted where harmonic change is more rapid (e.g., sequential passages)
- Static harmonic passages and sequential passages require different treatments of the same motive
- Cadential passages often require modification of the motive
- The motive may be further varied at points of structural contrast
- Sequences in the theme are varied in almost all variations by substituting different progressions
- The piano style (or instrumental idiom) must remain consistent throughout each variation

# Musical Context
This concept explains why no variation is a mere mechanical repetition of a single figure. The structural demands of the theme -- its internal contrasts, cadences, and harmonic rhythm -- force the motive of variation to be adapted and elaborated. The most sophisticated variations show a development of the motive within a single variation that parallels the development of themes in other forms.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Diabelli Variations: Contrast between static harmony (m. 1-8, 17-24) and sequences (m. 9-12, 25-28) requires great flexibility; many motives are very short as a consequence
- Beethoven, Diabelli Variations, Variation 5: Sequences varied with very far-reaching substitute progressions
- Beethoven, Diabelli Variations, Variation 20: "Harmonically fantastic; even in the post-Wagnerian epoch it would have been called 'modernistic'"
- Beethoven, 15 Variations in E-flat, Op. 35, Variation 3: Motive compounded of two elements whose shifting deranges the phrasing
- Beethoven, 15 Variations in E-flat, Op. 35, Variation 4: Sixteenth-notes begin as scale lines but change to broken chords (m. 9) and then to a different arpeggiation system (m. 13)
- Ex. 126g: Complete variation showing motive-forms shifted and further varied in the cadence region

# Common Confusions
- "No matter how far-reaching the modifications within a variation, the piano style remains consistent" -- consistency of idiom is maintained even when the motive is extensively modified
- Variation within the variation is not a defect; it is a structural necessity demanded by the theme's internal organization
- The motive of variation is not applied mechanically -- it is a living principle that adapts to structural context
