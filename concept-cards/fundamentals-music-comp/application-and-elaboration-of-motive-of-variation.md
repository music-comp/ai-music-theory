# Quick Definition

The process of adapting the motive of variation to every harmonic movement in the theme, including the further variation required by structural features such as cadences, contrasts, and subdivisions, producing "variation within the variation."

# Core Definition

The motive of variation must accommodate itself to every movement of the harmony (which itself is subject to variation) and must contain all the principal tones of the theme. Variety within the variation requires appropriate elaboration of the motive. Additional elaborations are the consequences of structural features such as cadences, contrasts, and subdivisions. This results in variation within the variation. The contrast between static and sequential harmonic passages in the theme requires great flexibility in the motive, which may need to be very short to accommodate rapid harmonic change. No matter how far-reaching the modifications within a variation, the piano style (or instrumental idiom) remains consistent.

# Prerequisites

- **Motive of variation**: Understanding the figure being applied
- **Circumscription of principal tones**: Understanding the basic technique

# Key Properties

- The motive must adapt where harmonic change is more rapid (e.g., sequential passages)
- Static and sequential passages require different treatments of the same motive
- Cadential passages often require modification of the motive
- The motive may be further varied at points of structural contrast
- Sequences in the theme are varied in almost all variations by substituting different progressions
- The instrumental idiom must remain consistent throughout each variation
- "Variation within the variation" is not a defect but a structural necessity

# Construction / Recognition

1. Trace the motive of variation through the entire variation
2. Note where it is adapted to rapid harmonic change
3. Identify modifications at cadential passages
4. Check for "variation within the variation" at structural contrasts
5. Verify consistency of instrumental idiom throughout
6. Compare treatment of static vs. sequential passages

# Context & Application

This concept explains why no variation is a mere mechanical repetition of a single figure. The structural demands of the theme force the motive to be adapted and elaborated. The most sophisticated variations show development of the motive that parallels thematic development in other forms. Schoenberg emphasizes that consistency of idiom (the "piano style") is maintained even when the motive is extensively modified.

# Examples

- Beethoven, Diabelli Variations: Contrast between static harmony (m. 1-8, 17-24) and sequences (m. 9-12, 25-28) requires great flexibility; many motives are very short
- Beethoven, Diabelli Variations, Variation 5: Sequences varied with far-reaching substitute progressions
- Beethoven, Diabelli Variations, Variation 20: "Harmonically fantastic; even in the post-Wagnerian epoch it would have been called 'modernistic'"
- Beethoven, 15 Variations, Op. 35, Variation 3: Motive compounded of two elements whose shifting deranges the phrasing
- Beethoven, Op. 35, Variation 4: Sixteenth-notes begin as scale lines, change to broken chords (m. 9), then to different arpeggiation (m. 13)
- Ex. 126g: Complete variation with motive-forms shifted and further varied in the cadence region

# Common Errors

- Applying the motive mechanically without adapting to harmonic and structural demands
- Abandoning the motive of variation at points of structural difficulty
- Losing consistency of instrumental idiom despite motive modifications

# Common Confusions

- **Variation within the variation**: This is not a defect; it is a structural necessity demanded by the theme's internal organization.
- **Consistency of idiom**: "No matter how far-reaching the modifications within a variation, the piano style remains consistent" -- Schoenberg emphasizes this principle strongly.
