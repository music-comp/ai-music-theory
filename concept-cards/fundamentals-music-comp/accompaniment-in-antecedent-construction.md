# Quick Definition
The accompaniment contributes importantly to the character of the antecedent and should be incorporated from the very beginning of composition, using features such as detached/legato differentiation, rests, unharmonized upbeats, semi-contrapuntal middle voices, afterbeat harmony, and special rhythmic figures.

# Formal Definition
Schoenberg insists that accompaniment features be employed from the very beginning of theme construction, not added as an afterthought. These features include: differentiation between detached and legato notes; rests where harmonization is not required; unharmonized upbeats; semi-contrapuntal treatment of middle voices; afterbeat harmony; and special rhythmic figures. A complex accompaniment must be reconciled with good piano style, keeping everything within easy reach of the fingers and minimizing pedal dependence.

# Musical Context
Envisioning a definite character helps stimulate inventiveness. The accompaniment makes important contributions to the expression of character and should not be treated as a separate problem from the melody. However, the composer must beware of imbalance through overcrowding, destruction of character, and obscuration of harmonic progression. Fluent part-writing does not endanger harmonic clarity, but control of root progressions is essential.

# Common Confusions
- The accompaniment is not a separate problem to be solved after composing the melody; it should be conceived together with the theme from the start.
- Good piano style should ignore the existence of the pedal as much as possible -- everything should be within easy reach of the fingers.
- Rich accompaniment is a merit only when it does not obscure the harmonic progression or overcrowd the texture.
