# Quick Definition
A transition that evolves from elements of the main theme rather than introducing new thematic material, applying the processes of modulation and liquidation to motives already heard.

# Core Definition
Technically, it makes no essential difference whether the process of modulation and liquidation is applied to a special theme or to elements of the main theme. Sometimes the modulation starts promptly and the transitional character is pronounced. In other cases almost the entire main theme is repeated and transitional features are postponed to the last measures. These instances make the impression of varied repetitions, the transitional techniques at the end growing out of the variation.

# Prerequisites
- Transition function and purpose
- Four-element transition structure

# Key Properties
- Uses elements of the main theme rather than introducing new material
- Two main source points: beginning of the main theme or ending ("tail")
- Modulation may start promptly or be postponed to the last measures
- When postponed, the passage resembles a varied repetition of the main theme
- Some transitions repeat the entire main theme adapted to modulatory harmony
- The transitional function is determined by the harmonic outcome, not by the amount of new material

# Construction / Recognition
Two main approaches:
1. **From the beginning**: Transposition or quotation of the opening initiates modulation, with liquidation beginning immediately
2. **From the ending ("tail")**: The closing figure of the theme is picked up and used sequentially to lead to the new region, often through chain-like linkage of residues

In some cases, the principal theme is repeated substantially in its entirety but adapted to modulatory harmony.

# Context & Application
This type of transition is equally useful as the independent transition theme. The choice depends in part on the amount of internal repetition already incorporated in the principal theme. When the main theme contains little internal repetition, recycling its material for the transition provides additional exposure.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: transposition of the main theme beginning (m. 9) initiates modulation; liquidation begins immediately
- Beethoven, Op. 13-I: the "tail" quarter-note figure from the theme's end is picked up quasi-sequentially (m. 39) to lead to V of the relative major
- Beethoven, Op. 31/1-III: the twice-repeated figure at the theme's end (m. 33) supplies all material for the transition, reduced to its upbeat in chain linkage (m. 36)
- Beethoven, Op. 53-I: slightly varied repetition (m. 14) with continuation transposed up a third; every element of the main theme appears in the transitional section
- Beethoven, Op. 57-I: varied repetition (m. 17) with long notes subdivided into alternating chord pattern, stretching four measures to six

# Common Errors
- Simply repeating the main theme without introducing modulatory or liquidating elements
- Beginning the transition too similarly to the main theme's opening, confusing the listener about formal position
- Failing to create a clear sense of departure from the main theme's tonal region

# Common Confusions
- When nearly the entire main theme is repeated with transitional features only at the end, the passage may seem like a varied repetition rather than a transition; the transitional function is determined by the harmonic outcome, not by the amount of new material
- This type of transition does not require new thematic invention, but it does require artful transformation of existing material
- The "tail" approach (using the theme's ending) and the "head" approach (using the beginning) are both legitimate strategies
