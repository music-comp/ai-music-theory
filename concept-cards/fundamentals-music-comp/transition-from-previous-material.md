# Quick Definition
A transition that evolves from elements of the main theme rather than introducing new thematic material, applying the processes of modulation and liquidation to motives already heard.

# Formal Definition
Technically, it makes no essential difference whether the process of modulation and liquidation is applied to a special theme or to elements of the main theme. Sometimes the modulation starts promptly and the transitional character is pronounced. In other cases almost the entire main theme is repeated and transitional features are postponed to the last measures. These instances make the impression of varied repetitions, the transitional techniques at the end growing out of the variation.

# Construction/Recognition
Two main approaches:
1. **From the beginning of the main theme**: A transposition or quotation of the opening initiates the modulation, with liquidation beginning immediately (e.g., Op. 2/1-I, Op. 14/1-I)
2. **From the ending of the main theme**: The "tail" figure at the end of the theme is picked up and used sequentially to lead to the new region (e.g., Op. 13-I, Op. 31/1-III)

In some cases, the principal theme is repeated substantially in its entirety but adapted to modulatory harmony (e.g., Op. 53-I, Op. 57-I).

# Musical Context
This type of transition is equally useful as the independent transition theme, the choice depending in part on the amount of internal repetition incorporated in the principal theme. When the main theme already contains extensive internal repetition, an independent transitional theme may be preferred to avoid redundancy.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1-I: transposition of the main theme beginning (m. 9) initiates modulation; liquidation begins immediately
- Beethoven, Op. 13-I: the "tail" quarter-note figure from the theme's end is picked up quasi-sequentially (m. 39) to lead to V of the relative major
- Beethoven, Op. 31/1-III: the twice-repeated figure at the theme's end (m. 33) supplies all material for the transition, reduced to its upbeat in chain linkage (m. 36)
- Beethoven, Op. 53-I: slightly varied repetition (m. 14) with continuation transposed up a third; every element of the main theme appears in the transitional section
- Beethoven, Op. 57-I: varied repetition (m. 17) with long notes subdivided into alternating chord pattern

# Common Confusions
- When nearly the entire main theme is repeated with transitional features only at the end, the passage may seem like a varied repetition rather than a transition; the transitional function is determined by the harmonic outcome, not by the amount of new material
- This type of transition does not require new thematic invention, but it does require artful transformation of existing material
