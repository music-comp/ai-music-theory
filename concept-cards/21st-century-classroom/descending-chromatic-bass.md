# Quick Definition
The descending chromatic bass line (1-7-b7-6-b6-5) is a common musical pattern spanning four centuries, harmonized with diverse chords including secondary dominants, borrowed chords, and augmented sixth chords -- the "lament bass" of Baroque tradition.

# Core Definition
A common musical pattern is the descending chromatic bass line: 1-7-b7-6-b6-5. Composers harmonize this bass pattern with all manner of harmonies, including secondary chords, borrowed chords, augmented sixth chords, and rarely-used diatonic chords. This pattern has been used across four centuries of music, from Purcell's Baroque lament to contemporary popular songs. The harmonization choices at each bass degree create different emotional colors while maintaining the inexorable chromatic descent.

# Prerequisites
- **Mode mixture**: Source of the b7 and b6 harmonizations
- **Secondary dominants**: Used to harmonize certain bass degrees
- **Augmented sixth chords**: Common at the b6 position

# Key Properties
1. Bass pattern: 1-7-b7-6-b6-5
2. Common harmonizations vary by era and style
3. At b6: Ger+6, Fr+6, iv6, or bVI are common
4. At 7: typically V6
5. At b7: V4/2/iv or i4/2
6. At 6: IV6 or #vio7
7. The pattern can be truncated (not all six notes required)
8. Associated with lament and tragedy in Baroque era

# Construction / Recognition
Harmonization comparison across composers:
- Purcell: i - V6 - V4/2/iv - IV6 - iv6 - V
- Bach (Crucifixus): i-viio4/V - V6 - viio7-viio4/3/iv - IV6 - iv6-Fr+6 - I6/4-V6
- Beethoven (32 Variations): i - V6 - V4/2/iv - IV6 - Ger+6 - I6/4
- Popular music often uses: i - (skip 7) - i4/2 - IV6 - bVI or bVIM7

# Context & Application
The descending chromatic bass creates a sense of inexorable descent. In the Baroque era, it was the "lament bass," associated with grief and tragedy (Purcell's Dido's Lament, Bach's Crucifixus). In popular music, it serves dramatic or emotional purposes without the specific lament association. The harmonization at scale degree 6 can use #vio7, a chord from the melodic minor scale. Comparing harmonizations across centuries reveals how different composers use the same bass pattern to create different expressive effects.

# Examples
- Purcell, Dido and Aeneas, "When I am laid in earth" (Dido's Lament, 1688)
- J.S. Bach, Mass in B minor, Crucifixus (1733)
- Beethoven, 32 Variations in C minor, WoO 80 (1806)
- George Harrison, "While My Guitar Gently Weeps" (1968)
- Led Zeppelin, "Stairway to Heaven" (1971)
- Aerosmith, "Dream On" (1973)
- Stone Temple Pilots, "Interstate Love Song" (1994)
- Christina Perri, "Jar of Hearts" (2010)

# Common Errors
- Assuming all descending chromatic basses must use all six notes
- Expecting identical harmonizations across different composers and eras

# Common Confusions
- **#vio7 at scale degree 6**: This chord comes from melodic minor (not borrowed from parallel minor)
- **Flexibility**: Composers harmonize the same bass note differently -- there is no single "correct" harmonization
- **Truncation**: Many examples use only part of the pattern (e.g., 1-7-b7-6 only)
