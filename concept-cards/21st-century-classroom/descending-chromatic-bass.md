# Quick Definition
The descending chromatic bass line (1-7-flat-7-6-flat-6-5) is a common musical pattern harmonized with various chromatic chords including secondary chords, borrowed chords, and augmented sixth chords.

# Formal Definition
A descending chromatic bass line moves stepwise from scale degree 1 down to scale degree 5 through chromatic passing tones: 1-7-flat-7-6-flat-6-5. Composers harmonize this bass pattern with diverse chord types, including secondary chords, borrowed chords, augmented sixth chords, and rarely-used diatonic chords. This pattern has been used across four centuries of music.

# Construction/Recognition
Bass line: 1 - 7 - flat-7 - 6 - flat-6 - 5

Common harmonizations for each bass note:
- 1: i (tonic)
- 7: V6 (dominant in first inversion)
- flat-7: V4/2 of iv, or i4/2
- 6: IV6, or #vi-half-dim7
- flat-6: iv6, Ger+6, Fr+6, or VI
- 5: V, or i6/4

# Musical Context
- Creates a sense of inexorable descent
- Often associated with lament or tragedy (Baroque "lament bass")
- Also used in rock and popular music for dramatic effect
- The harmonization choices create different emotional colors

# Examples
Historical examples:
- Purcell, Dido and Aeneas, "When I am laid in earth" (Dido's Lament) (1688)
- J.S. Bach, Mass in B minor, Crucifixus (1733)
- Beethoven, 32 Variations in C minor, WoO 80 (1806)

Popular music examples:
- George Harrison, "While My Guitar Gently Weeps" (1968)
- Led Zeppelin, "Stairway to Heaven" (1971)
- Aerosmith, "Dream On" (1973)
- Stone Temple Pilots, "Interstate Love Song" (1994)
- Christina Perri, "Jar of Hearts" (2010)

# Common Confusions
- The harmonization varies greatly between composers and eras
- Scale degree 6 can be harmonized with #vi-half-dim7 (from melodic minor)
- Not all descending bass lines use all six notes
- The pattern can be truncated or extended
