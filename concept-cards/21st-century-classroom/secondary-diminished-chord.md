# Quick Definition
A diminished triad, half-diminished seventh, or fully diminished seventh chord that functions as viio of a chord other than the tonic, with its root a minor second below the target chord's root.

# Core Definition
The viio chord has dominant function in the harmonic function flowchart. Since chords of dominant function typically resolve to chords of tonic function, diminished triads as well as half-diminished and fully diminished seventh chords can occur as secondary diminished chords, tonicizing diatonic chords other than I. The root of a secondary diminished chord is a minor second below the root of the chord being tonicized. Chromaticism is an "essential" part of secondary diminished chords (as opposed to "non-essential" chromaticism from chromatic non-chord tones).

# Prerequisites
- **Tonicization**: The concept of temporarily treating a non-tonic chord as tonic
- **Fully diminished seventh chord**: One of the possible qualities
- **Half-diminished seventh chord**: Another possible quality

# Key Properties
1. Root is a minor second below the target chord's root
2. Quality: diminished triad, half-diminished 7th, or fully diminished 7th
3. Minor triads are tonicized by fully diminished seventh chords
4. Major triads are traditionally tonicized by half-diminished seventh chords (but fully diminished also works)
5. The raised chromatic note acts as leading tone to the target root
6. Labeled viio/x, viio7/x, or viio7/x with appropriate figured bass

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify a chromatic chord with diminished quality
- The root is a m2 below a diatonic chord root
- Stack in thirds to verify root and quality
- Check for the "essential" chromaticism that distinguishes it from diatonic chords
- Common in harmonic sequences with chromatically ascending bass lines

# Context & Application
Secondary diminished chords provide an alternative to secondary dominants for creating tonicization. They are particularly common in sequences and often appear with chromatic bass motion. While secondary dominants are more common overall, secondary diminished chords add their own distinctive color and are especially effective in chromatic sequences (e.g., circle-of-fifths progressions with applied viio chords).

# Examples
- J.S. Bach, "Jesu, Joy of Man's Desiring" (1723) -- harmonic sequence with secondary diminished chords
- Tchaikovsky, The Nutcracker, "Trepak" (1892)
- Billy Joel, "Just the Way You Are" (1977)
- Dewayne Blackwell and Earl Bud Lee, "Friends in Low Places" (1990)
- Freddie Mercury, "We Are the Champions" (1977)

# Common Errors
- Using P5 below to find the target (that is for secondary dominants; use m2 above for secondary diminished)
- Failing to match quality to target: fully-diminished for minor targets, half-diminished for major

# Common Confusions
- **m2 below, not P5 above**: The root relationship differs from secondary dominants
- **Quality matching**: Traditionally, fully diminished tonicizes minor triads; half-diminished tonicizes major triads
- **Essential vs. non-essential chromaticism**: The chromatic notes in secondary diminished chords are chord tones, not non-chord tones
