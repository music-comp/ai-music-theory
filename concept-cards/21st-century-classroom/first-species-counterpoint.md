# Quick Definition
First species is note-against-note counterpoint in whole notes where only consonant intervals are used, beginning and ending on unison or octave, with the final approached by contrary motion.

# Core Definition
In first species counterpoint, one writes note-against-note counterpoint in whole notes against a cantus firmus also in whole notes. All vertical intervals must be consonant -- unisons, 3rds, 5ths, 6ths, and their compound versions (10ths, 12ths, 13ths). Dissonances (2nds, 4ths, 7ths) are not allowed. The rules are derived from observations of Renaissance compositions by Palestrina, as codified by Fux (Hutchinson, Ch. 30, p. 424).

# Prerequisites
- **Cantus firmus** -- First species is written against a given cantus firmus
- **Consonance in counterpoint** -- Must distinguish perfect from imperfect consonances
- **Intervals** -- Must identify interval sizes between the two voices

# Key Properties
1. Note-against-note rhythm (whole note vs. whole note)
2. Only consonant intervals permitted: unisons, 3rds, 5ths, 6ths, 8ves, and compounds
3. Dissonances (2nds, 4ths, 7ths) are strictly forbidden
4. Perfect consonances must be approached by contrary motion only
5. No more than three consecutive 3rds or 6ths
6. Allowed melodic leaps: 3rds, 4ths, 5ths, ascending minor 6ths, and 8ves
7. No melodic tritone outlines at turning points
8. No repeated notes (to maintain melodic flow)

# Construction / Recognition
**Rules for writing first species:**
1. Begin with an octave or unison
2. End on an octave or unison
3. Approach the ending by contrary motion: one voice has 7-8 while the other has 2-1
4. In Mixolydian, Dorian, or Aeolian: raise the 7th to create a leading tone at the final cadence (F-sharp, C-sharp, G-sharp respectively)
5. Write only consonant harmonic intervals in each measure
6. Imperfect consonances (3rds and 6ths) can be approached in any manner, but no more than three consecutively
7. Perfect consonances (unisons, 5ths, 8ves) may only be approached in contrary motion
8. When the c.f. is in the upper part, write consonant intervals below (unison, 3rd, 5th, 6th)
9. Avoid outlining a tritone melodically between two turning points

# Context & Application
First species teaches the fundamental relationship between consonance and dissonance in two-voice texture. It isolates the problem of choosing vertical intervals while maintaining melodic independence. The strict prohibition on dissonance means every note must contribute to a consonant sonority. Mastery of first species provides the foundation for all subsequent species.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Using a perfect 4th as a consonance
  **Correction**: In two-voice counterpoint, the 4th is treated as dissonant
- **Error**: Approaching a perfect consonance by parallel or similar motion
  **Correction**: Perfect consonances (unisons, 5ths, 8ves) require contrary motion
- **Error**: Writing more than three consecutive 3rds or 6ths
  **Correction**: Limit consecutive imperfect consonances to three
- **Error**: Forgetting to raise the leading tone at the final cadence in modal contexts
  **Correction**: Use sharps to create leading tones in Mixolydian, Dorian, and Aeolian modes

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking that any consonant interval can be used freely
  **Clarification**: Perfect consonances have stricter approach rules (contrary motion only) than imperfect consonances
- **Confusion**: Confusing first species rules with free counterpoint
  **Clarification**: First species uses simplified Renaissance-style rules; free counterpoint allows more flexibility
