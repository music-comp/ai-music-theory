# Quick Definition
First species is note-against-note counterpoint in whole notes where only consonant intervals are used, beginning and ending on unison or octave, with the final approached by contrary motion.

# Formal Definition
First species counterpoint writes one whole note against each whole note of the cantus firmus. All vertical intervals must be consonant (unisons, 3rds, 5ths, 6ths, and their compounds). No dissonances are allowed. Strict rules govern the beginning, ending, and approach to perfect intervals.

# Rules/Procedures
1. **Begin** with an octave or unison above/below the cantus firmus
2. **End** on an octave or unison
3. **Approach the ending** by contrary motion: one voice has 7-8 while the other has 2-1
   - In Mixolydian, Dorian, or Aeolian modes: raise the 7th to create leading tone
4. **Use only consonances**: unisons, 3rds, 5ths, 6ths, 8ves, 10ths, 12ths, 13ths
   - Dissonances (2nds, 4ths, 7ths) are NOT allowed
5. **Imperfect consonances** (3rds and 6ths): Can be approached any way
   - Do not use more than three consecutive 3rds or 6ths
6. **Perfect consonances** (unisons, 5ths, 8ves): Must be approached by CONTRARY motion only
7. **Allowed melodic leaps**: 3rds, 4ths, 5ths, ascending minor 6ths, and 8ves
8. **Avoid tritone outline**: Do not melodically outline a tritone at turning points
9. **Avoid repeated notes** to maintain melodic flow

# Musical Context
First species teaches the fundamental relationship between consonance and dissonance in two-voice texture. The rules reflect Renaissance polyphonic practice and create smooth, independent melodic lines.

# Examples
- C.F. on C-D-E-F-E-D-C with counterpoint starting an 8ve above
- Final cadence: C.F. ends D-C while counterpoint ends B-C (contrary motion, both to unison)

# Common Confusions
- Using perfect 4ths (they are dissonant in two-voice counterpoint)
- Approaching perfect consonances by similar or parallel motion (forbidden)
- Using more than three consecutive 3rds or 6ths
- Outlining tritones melodically at turning points
- Forgetting to raise the leading tone in modal contexts
