# Quick Definition
The Picardy third is the use of a major I chord instead of the expected minor i chord at the end of a piece in a minor key, providing an unexpectedly bright conclusion.

# Formal Definition
The Picardy third (or Picardy 3rd) is the rare instance of borrowing from the major mode during a passage in a minor key. It occurs specifically at the end of a piece in minor, where the major I chord replaces the expected minor i chord. The "third" refers to the raised third of the tonic triad.

# Construction/Recognition
- Appears only at the final cadence of a piece in minor
- The tonic chord has a major third instead of minor third
- Example: In D minor, final chord is D-F#-A instead of D-F-A
- Notated simply as I (major) in a minor key context

# Musical Context
- Most commonly encountered in Baroque era music
- Used from the Baroque era to the present, though less frequently over time
- Creates a sense of resolution, hope, or transcendence at the end
- The major third is sometimes called the "Picardy third"

# Examples
- J.S. Bach, French Suite No. 1 in D Minor, BWV 812, Sarabande - ends with major I chord

# Related Concepts

# Common Confusions
- It is rare to borrow from major mode while in minor key EXCEPT for the Picardy third
- If a major I chord appears mid-piece in minor, it typically functions as V/iv, not as mode mixture
- Raised 6 and 7 in minor are part of melodic minor, not mode mixture
- The Picardy third is only at the END of a piece
