# Quick Definition
The Picardy third is the use of a major I chord at the end of a piece in a minor key -- the rare exception to the rule that mode mixture borrows from minor into major, most common in Baroque music.

# Core Definition
The Picardy third (tierce de Picardie) is the one significant instance of borrowing from the major mode during a passage in a minor key. It occurs specifically at the end of a piece in minor, where the major I chord replaces the expected minor i chord. It is rare to borrow from major mode while in minor for two reasons: a major tonic in a minor-key context usually functions as V/iv (not as mode mixture), and raised 6 and 7 already occur naturally as part of the melodic minor scale.

# Prerequisites
- **Mode mixture**: The broader concept (usually minor into major)
- **Parallel major/minor**: The relationship being exploited

# Key Properties
1. Major I chord at the END of a piece in minor
2. The "third" refers to the raised third of the tonic triad
3. Rare exception to one-directional mode mixture
4. Most common in Baroque era
5. Used from Baroque to present, with decreasing frequency
6. If major I appears mid-piece in minor, it typically functions as V/iv, not mode mixture

# Construction / Recognition
- Only at the final cadence of a piece in minor key
- The tonic chord has a major third instead of minor third
- Example: In D minor, the final chord is D-F#-A instead of D-F-A
- Labeled simply as I (major) in a minor-key context
- The raised third is the only difference from the expected minor tonic

# Context & Application
The Picardy third was standard practice in Baroque music, where many pieces in minor keys ended with a major tonic chord. It creates a sense of resolution, brightness, or transcendence at the conclusion. While less common after the Baroque era, it continues to appear occasionally through the present day. The effect depends on the listener expecting a minor ending and receiving the brighter major sound.

# Examples
- J.S. Bach, French Suite No. 1 in D Minor, BWV 812, Sarabande -- ends with major I chord

# Common Errors
- Calling any major tonic in a minor context a Picardy third (it must be at the END)
- Confusing V/iv with a Picardy third when major I appears mid-piece

# Common Confusions
- **End only**: The Picardy third occurs only at the final cadence, not mid-piece
- **V/iv confusion**: A major I chord in the middle of a minor piece typically functions as V/iv
- **Melodic minor is not borrowing**: Raised 6 and 7 in minor are part of the melodic minor scale, not mode mixture
