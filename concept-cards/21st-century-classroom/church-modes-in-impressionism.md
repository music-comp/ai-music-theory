# Quick Definition
Impressionist composers used medieval church modes (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian) to create harmonic color distinct from major/minor tonality and chromatic Romanticism.

# Formal Definition
The church modes (also called ecclesiastical or medieval modes) provided Impressionist composers with alternatives to major and minor scales without resorting to chromaticism. Each mode has a characteristic interval that distinguishes it from major or minor, creating unique harmonic possibilities. In Phrygian, for example, the seventh chord on scale degree 5 is half-diminished rather than dominant.

# Construction/Calculation
The seven modes built on white keys:
- Ionian (C): major scale
- Dorian (D): minor with raised 6th
- Phrygian (E): minor with lowered 2nd
- Lydian (F): major with raised 4th
- Mixolydian (G): major with lowered 7th
- Aeolian (A): natural minor
- Locrian (B): diminished tonic (rarely used as primary mode)

Key characteristic intervals distinguish each mode from major/minor.

# Musical Context
Debussy and Ravel used modes as a deliberate alternative to Wagner's chromatic style. Modal writing allows diatonic harmony while avoiding the dominant-tonic relationships of common-practice tonality. The modes create distinctive colors: Lydian sounds bright and floating, Phrygian sounds dark and Spanish-influenced, Dorian has a folksong quality.

# Examples
- Debussy, String Quartet, I: Phrygian mode (v chord is half-diminished)
- Debussy, "Danse profane": Lydian mode (raised 4th creates floating quality)
- Ravel, Piano Trio, I: Opens in Dorian, ends in Lydian
- Note: Composers often mix modes or include chromatic passing chords

# Common Confusions
- Modal music is not the same as atonal music - it has a clear tonal center
- Composers often briefly depart from the mode for expressive purposes
- Modal harmony produces different chord qualities than major/minor (e.g., vii is not diminished in Mixolydian)
- The characteristic tone of each mode is its defining feature
