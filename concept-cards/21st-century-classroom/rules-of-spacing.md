# Quick Definition
Rules of spacing require that the distance between adjacent upper voices (soprano-alto and alto-tenor) not exceed an octave, while the bass can be more than an octave from the tenor.

# Formal Definition
Spacing rules govern the vertical distribution of notes in four-part writing. The three upper voices (soprano, alto, tenor) should be kept close together, with no more than an octave between soprano and alto, and no more than an octave between alto and tenor. The bass voice can be more than an octave below the tenor. This reflects the natural spacing of the overtone series, where larger intervals occur in lower registers.

# Rules/Procedures
1. Soprano to Alto: Keep within one octave (spacing error if exceeded)
2. Alto to Tenor: Keep within one octave (spacing error if exceeded)
3. Tenor to Bass: May exceed one octave (no restriction)
4. Consider the overtone series: larger spaces work better in lower registers
5. Chords voiced with wider spacing in the bass and closer spacing in the upper voices resonate better

# Musical Context
These rules ensure chords sound balanced and resonant. The overtone series demonstrates that harmonics are spaced farther apart in lower registers and closer together in upper registers. Following this natural acoustic principle produces better-sounding voicings.

# Examples
- Acceptable: Soprano on G4, Alto on C4 (5th apart)
- Spacing error: Soprano on G5, Alto on E4 (10th apart)
- Acceptable: Tenor on G3, Bass on C2 (12th apart)
- Well-voiced chord: Bass C2, Tenor G3, Alto E4, Soprano C5

# Common Confusions
- Applying the octave restriction to the bass-tenor interval (bass can be farther away)
- Forgetting to check spacing when voices move (spacing must be correct on every chord)
- Not understanding why this rule exists - it mimics natural acoustic spacing
- Confusing spacing with range (spacing is between voices; range is the limits of individual voices)
