# Quick Definition
The lowered sixth scale degree (flat-6) from the parallel minor can be harmonized as the root, third, fifth, or seventh of various borrowed chords, each creating distinct harmonic colors.

# Formal Definition
Flat-6 is the most common borrowed scale degree in mode mixture. It can function as different chord members, generating multiple borrowed chord possibilities. When flat-6 is the root, it produces flat-VI; as the third, it produces iv; as the fifth, it produces ii diminished chords; as the seventh, it appears in various seventh chords.

# Construction/Recognition
Flat-6 as different chord members:
- Root: flat-VI (major triad on lowered 6th degree)
- 3rd: iv (minor subdominant)
- 5th: ii diminished, ii half-diminished 7
- 7th: Various seventh chord constructions

To analyze: stack the chord in thirds, identify the root and quality, then determine the Roman numeral based on the lowered scale degree.

# Musical Context
- The 1-5-6-flat-6 bass line is extremely common in popular music
- flat-VI often used in deceptive cadences
- iv provides darker color than diatonic IV
- ii diminished/half-diminished intensifies pre-dominant function

# Examples
- Linda Perry, "Beautiful" (2002) - 1-5-6-flat-6 bass line
- Hoyt Axton, "Joy to the World" (1970) - same bass pattern
- "Part of Your World" from The Little Mermaid (1989) - same bass pattern
- Chopin, Nocturne in E-flat major, Op. 9, No. 2 (analysis exercise)

# Common Confusions
- The quality of the borrowed chord changes based on which chord member flat-6 represents
- Do not confuse flat-VI (major triad) with vi (minor triad)
- flat-6 in bass does not always mean flat-VI chord - check the full chord structure
