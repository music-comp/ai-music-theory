# Quick Definition
The lowered sixth scale degree (b6) can be harmonized as root (bVI), third (iv), fifth (iio), or seventh of various borrowed chords, with b3 and b7 generating additional borrowed chords -- the 1-5-6-b6 bass line is especially common in popular music.

# Core Definition
Flat-6 is the most common borrowed scale degree in mode mixture. It can function as different chord members: as root, it produces bVI (major triad); as 3rd, it produces iv (minor subdominant); as 5th, it produces iio or iio7; as 7th, it appears in various seventh chords. Similarly, b3 and b7 can each be harmonized as root, 3rd, or 5th of borrowed chords, with some overlap between categories. Rather than memorizing all possible chords, one should recognize that including any minor-mode scale degree in a chord alters its quality.

- **Mode mixture**: The concept of borrowing from parallel minor

# Key Properties
1. b6 as root: bVI (major triad on lowered 6th)
2. b6 as 3rd: iv (minor subdominant)
3. b6 as 5th: iio, iio7 (half-diminished supertonic)
4. b6 as 7th: various seventh chord configurations
5. b3 as root: bIII; b3 as 3rd or 5th creates other altered chords
6. b7 as root: bVII; b7 as 3rd or 5th creates other altered chords
7. The 1-5-6-b6 bass line is extremely common in popular music

# Construction / Recognition
- The 1-5-6-b6 bass line: I - V - vi - iv (or bVI) progression with chromatic descent in bass
- Identify which chord member b6 occupies to determine the chord type
- b6 in bass does not always mean bVI -- check the full chord structure
- b3 and b7 generate chords that may overlap with b6 harmonizations

# Context & Application
The 1-5-6-b6 bass line pattern appears in countless popular songs across decades. The chromatic descent from natural 6 to b6 creates a distinctive emotional darkening. Understanding how b6 functions as different chord members reveals why so many different borrowed chords share this expressive quality. The b3 and b7 harmonizations extend the palette further.

# Examples
- Linda Perry, "Beautiful" (2002) -- 1-5-6-b6 bass line
- Hoyt Axton, "Joy to the World" (1970) -- same bass pattern
- Howard Ashman and Alan Menken, "Part of Your World" from The Little Mermaid (1989) -- same bass pattern
- Chopin, Nocturne in E-flat major, Op. 9, No. 2 (1832) -- analysis exercise with borrowed chords

# Common Errors
- Assuming b6 in the bass always means bVI chord
- Not recognizing the same b6 coloring across different chord positions

# Common Confusions
- **Multiple chord functions**: b6 can be root, 3rd, 5th, or 7th of different chords
- **Bass line pattern**: The 1-5-6-b6 pattern is about the bass motion, not a fixed chord progression
- **Overlap**: Some chords contain multiple borrowed scale degrees (e.g., viio7 contains b6 and b3)
