# Quick Definition
Mode mixture (synonymous with "borrowed chords") typically consists of borrowing chords from the parallel minor during a passage in a major key, creating expressive chromatic color through lowered scale degrees -- most commonly b6, but also b3 and b7.

# Core Definition
Mode mixture typically consists of borrowing chords from the parallel minor during a passage in a major key. "Borrowed chords" is synonymous with mode mixture. The most common lowered chromaticisms are b6, b3, and b7. Unlike secondary chords, borrowed chords are analyzed simply by noting their root, quality, and inversion in Roman numeral analysis. The emotional effect of switching to the minor mode creates darker, more expressive color.

# Prerequisites
- **Parallel major/minor**: Understanding that C major and C minor share the same tonic
- **Diatonic chords in major**: Knowing the expected chord qualities
- **Diatonic chords in minor**: The source of borrowed chords

# Key Properties
1. Borrowing from parallel minor while in a major key
2. b6 is the most common borrowed scale degree
3. b3 and b7 also appear in borrowed chords
4. Roman numeral analysis notes root, quality, and inversion (no slash notation)
5. Flats before Roman numerals mean "lower the root by m2" (not literally add a flat)
6. Creates darker, more expressive harmonic color

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for lowered accidentals (b6, b3, b7) in a major-key context
- The chord quality will differ from the expected diatonic chord
- Common borrowed chords: iv, bVI, bVII, iio, iio7, viio7
- Analyze simply as root + quality + inversion (unlike secondary chords)
- Be careful: b before a Roman numeral means lower by m2 in the key, not literally add a flat

# Context & Application
Mode mixture is found extensively in popular music, Romantic-era classical music, and film scores. Common patterns include the 1-5-6-b6 bass line (found in "Beautiful," "Joy to the World," "Part of Your World"), IV to iv progressions ("Creep"), I alternating with borrowed chords (Brahms Symphony No. 3, III), and the dramatic bVI-bVII-I ending. It is rare to borrow from major while in a minor key, except for the Picardy third.

# Common Errors
- Interpreting flats before Roman numerals as literal flat accidentals (bVI in A major is built on F-natural, not Ab)
- Using secondary chord analysis (slash notation) for borrowed chords

# Common Confusions
- **Flat notation**: bVI means "lower the sixth scale degree by m2," not "add a flat to the note"
- **One-way borrowing**: Mode mixture primarily borrows from minor into major, not the reverse
- **Simple analysis**: Unlike secondary chords, borrowed chords just show root, quality, and inversion
