# Quick Definition
Mode mixture is borrowing chords from the parallel minor mode while in a major key, creating expressive chromatic color through lowered scale degrees.

# Formal Definition
Mode mixture typically consists of borrowing chords from the parallel minor during a passage in a major key. The term "borrowed chords" is synonymous with mode mixture and refers specifically to borrowing chords from minor. The most common lowered chromaticisms are flat-6, flat-3, and flat-7.

# Construction/Recognition
- Identify lowered scale degrees (flat-6 most common, also flat-3 and flat-7)
- Look for chord qualities that differ from diatonic major-key chords
- The root, quality, and inversion are noted in Roman numeral analysis
- Flats before Roman numerals indicate lowering the root by a minor second in the key, not a literal flat

# Musical Context
- Creates emotional effect of switching to minor mode
- Common bass line pattern: 1-5-6-flat-6 (as in "Beautiful," "Joy to the World," "Part of Your World")
- Often used for expressive or "epic" moments
- Found extensively in popular music, Romantic-era classical music, and film scores

# Examples
- Beatles, "Blackbird" (1968) - mode mixture for emotional effect
- Richard Strauss, Also Sprach Zarathustra (1896) - I to i progression
- Radiohead, "Creep" (1992) - IV to iv progression
- Kurt Cobain, "Lithium" (1992)
- Brahms, Symphony No. 3, III - alternation between I and borrowed chord
- Tchaikovsky, Nutcracker, "Waltz of the Flowers" (1892)

# Common Confusions
- Flats before Roman numerals lower the root by m2 in the key signature, not literally adding a flat to the note name (e.g., in A major, flat-VI is built on F-natural, not A-flat)
- Unlike secondary chords, borrowed chords are analyzed simply by root, quality, and inversion
- Mode mixture is primarily borrowing from minor while in major, not the reverse
