# Quick Definition
In two-voice counterpoint, consonances (unisons, 3rds, 5ths, 6ths, 8ves) form the structural framework, while dissonances (2nds, 4ths, 7ths) require special treatment such as passing tones or suspensions.

# Formal Definition
Species counterpoint classifies intervals as consonant or dissonant based on their acoustic stability and treatment:

**Consonances**:
- Perfect consonances: unison (1), perfect fifth (5), octave (8), and their compounds (12, 15)
- Imperfect consonances: thirds (3), sixths (6), and their compounds (10, 13)

**Dissonances**: seconds (2), fourths (4), sevenths (7), and tritones

In two-voice counterpoint, the perfect fourth is treated as a dissonance because there is no third voice to provide a bass support. Dissonances may only appear under specific controlled conditions (passing tones, suspensions).

# Rules/Procedures
1. **Perfect consonances**: Require approach by contrary motion
   - Create strong structural points (beginnings, endings, strong beats)
   - Avoid parallel motion to perfect consonances
2. **Imperfect consonances**: May be approached any way
   - Provide melodic freedom between structural points
   - Limit consecutive 3rds or 6ths to three in a row
3. **Dissonances (2, 4, 7, tritone)**:
   - First species: Not allowed
   - Second species: Passing tones only (weak beat, approached/left by step)
   - Third species: Passing tones (not on beat 1), cambiata
   - Fourth species: Suspensions (prepared, tied, resolved down)

# Musical Context
The consonance/dissonance classification in species counterpoint reflects Renaissance practice and acoustic principles. The careful control of dissonance creates the characteristic smooth flow of Palestrina-style polyphony.

# Examples
- First species: Only 1, 3, 5, 6, 8 allowed (all consonant)
- Second species dissonance: Half note passing tone on beat 3
- Fourth species: 7-6 suspension is dissonant on beat 1, resolves to consonance

# Common Confusions
- Using the fourth as a consonance (dissonant in two-voice counterpoint)
- Allowing dissonances in first species (only consonances allowed)
- Not approaching perfect consonances by contrary motion
- Confusing "imperfect" consonance with "dissonance" (imperfect consonances ARE consonant)
