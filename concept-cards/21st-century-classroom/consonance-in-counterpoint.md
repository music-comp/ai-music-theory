# Quick Definition
In two-voice counterpoint, consonances (unisons, 3rds, 5ths, 6ths, 8ves) form the structural framework, while dissonances (2nds, 4ths, 7ths) require special treatment.

# Core Definition
Species counterpoint classifies intervals as consonant or dissonant. Perfect consonances include the unison, perfect fifth, octave, and their compounds (12th, 15th). Imperfect consonances include thirds, sixths, and their compounds (10th, 13th). Dissonances include seconds, fourths, sevenths, and tritones. In two-voice counterpoint, the perfect fourth is treated as a dissonance because no third voice provides bass support. Perfect and imperfect consonances have different approach rules: perfect consonances require contrary motion, while imperfect consonances may be approached freely (Hutchinson, Ch. 30, pp. 424-425).

# Key Properties
1. **Perfect consonances**: unison (1), perfect fifth (5), octave (8), and compounds (12, 15)
2. **Imperfect consonances**: thirds (3), sixths (6), and compounds (10, 13)
3. **Dissonances**: seconds (2), fourths (4), sevenths (7), tritones
4. Perfect consonances: approach by contrary motion only
5. Imperfect consonances: may be approached any way, but limit consecutive use to three
6. Dissonances: forbidden in first species; controlled in later species (passing tones, suspensions)
7. The fourth is dissonant in two-voice counterpoint

# Construction / Recognition
**Identifying consonance/dissonance in counterpoint:**
1. Measure the interval between the two voices
2. Classify as perfect consonance, imperfect consonance, or dissonance
3. Check that the approach to perfect consonances uses contrary motion
4. Verify that any dissonance is properly treated per the relevant species rules

**Dissonance treatment by species:**
- First species: no dissonances allowed
- Second species: passing tones only (weak beat, step-step)
- Third species: passing tones (not on beat 1) and cambiata
- Fourth species: suspensions (prepared, tied, resolved down by step)

# Context & Application
The consonance/dissonance classification in species counterpoint reflects Renaissance practice and acoustic principles. The careful control of dissonance creates the characteristic smooth flow of Palestrina-style polyphony. The distinction between perfect and imperfect consonances, with their different approach rules, is fundamental to maintaining voice independence.

# Examples
- First species: only intervals 1, 3, 5, 6, 8 and compounds allowed (p. 424)
- Second species: half-note passing tone on weak beat creates brief dissonance (p. 426)
- Fourth species: 7-6 suspension -- dissonant 7th on beat 1 resolves to consonant 6th (p. 429)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Using the fourth as a consonance in two-voice counterpoint
  **Correction**: The fourth is treated as dissonant in two-voice texture
- **Error**: Approaching a perfect consonance by parallel or similar motion
  **Correction**: Perfect consonances require contrary motion

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing "imperfect" consonance with "dissonance"
  **Clarification**: Imperfect consonances (3rds, 6ths) ARE consonant; "imperfect" distinguishes them from "perfect" consonances, not from consonance itself
- **Confusion**: Thinking the fourth is always dissonant
  **Clarification**: The fourth is dissonant specifically in two-voice counterpoint; in three or more voices with bass support, it can be consonant
