# Quick Definition
Six-four chords (second inversion triads) are restricted to three specific contexts: pedal 6/4, passing 6/4, and cadential 6/4, each defined by the bass line's behavior.

# Formal Definition
Second inversion triads (6/4 chords) are considered unstable due to the dissonant 4th above the bass. They are only used in three specific situations:
- **Pedal 6/4**: The bass stays on the same note before and after (e.g., I-IV6/4-I or V-I6/4-V)
- **Passing 6/4**: The bass moves by step through the 6/4 chord (e.g., I-V6/4-I6 or IV-I6/4-IV6)
- **Cadential 6/4**: The 6/4 functions as a dominant embellishment resolving to V or V7 at cadences

# Rules/Procedures
1. **Pedal 6/4**: Bass note = same as surrounding chords
   - Common patterns: I-IV6/4-I, V-I6/4-V
   - The 6/4 chord embellishes a static bass
2. **Passing 6/4**: Bass moves stepwise
   - Common patterns: I-V6/4-I6, IV-I6/4-IV6 (and retrogrades)
   - The bass passes through the 6/4 connecting root position to first inversion
3. **Cadential 6/4**: Resolves to V
   - Bass is scale degree 5
   - The 6-5 and 4-3 suspensions resolve to V
4. Always double the bass (5th of chord) in all 6/4 chords

# Musical Context
Six-four chords are the most restricted inversion in tonal harmony. Random use of 6/4 chords sounds stylistically incorrect in common practice style. The cadential 6/4 is particularly important at phrase endings where it creates a strong dominant preparation.

# Examples
- Pedal 6/4: C-F/C-C progression (bass stays on C throughout)
- Passing 6/4: C-G/D-C/E (bass passes C-D-E by step)
- Cadential 6/4: C/G-G-C (I6/4 resolves to V at a cadence)

# Common Confusions
- Using 6/4 chords freely without regard to context
- Confusing the cadential 6/4 with a I chord (it functions as dominant embellishment)
- Forgetting to double the bass (5th) in 6/4 chords
- Not recognizing which type of 6/4 a given chord represents based on bass behavior
