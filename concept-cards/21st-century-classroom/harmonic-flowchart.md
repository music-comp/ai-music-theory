# Quick Definition
A visual diagram showing the typical paths that chords follow based on their harmonic function, illustrating how tonic progresses to tonic prolongation, then to pre-dominant, then to dominant, and back to tonic.

# Formal Definition
The harmonic flowchart maps chord progressions according to harmonic function: Tonic (I) can progress to any function; Tonic Prolongation (vi, iii) progresses to Pre-Dominant; Pre-Dominant (ii, IV) progresses to Dominant; Dominant (V, vii°) progresses to Tonic. The basic flow is T -> TP -> PD -> D -> T.

# Analysis/Application
Use the flowchart to:
1. Predict likely chord progressions
2. Identify when progressions follow or deviate from common practice
3. Understand why certain progressions feel natural
4. Compose chord progressions that follow tonal conventions

# Musical Context
The flowchart represents common-practice harmony from the Baroque through Romantic eras and remains applicable to much popular music. Exceptions to the flowchart often occur through harmonic sequences or stylistic conventions.

# Examples
Major key flowchart:
- Tonic: I
- Tonic Prolongation: vi, iii (dashed lines show movement within function)
- Pre-Dominant: ii, IV
- Dominant: V, vii°

Minor key flowchart adds:
- Subtonic VII (progresses to III only)

# Common Confusions
- The plagal cadence (IV to I) and deceptive cadence (V to vi) are exceptions to the flowchart
- In minor, VII has a special function - it only progresses to III
- The I chord can progress directly to any other function, not just tonic prolongation
