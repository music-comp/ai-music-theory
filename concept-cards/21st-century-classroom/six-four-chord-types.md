# Quick Definition
Second-inversion chords (6/4 chords) appear in only four approved contexts in classical music: cadential (before V at cadences), passing (stepwise bass), pedal (repeated bass), and melodic bass (bass has the melody).

# Core Definition
Second-inversion chords with the fifth of the chord in the bass are special chords found only in four situations in classical music: (1) Cadential six-four, where I6/4 precedes V at cadences; (2) Passing six-four, where the bass line has three stepwise ascending or descending notes and the middle chord is a 6/4 chord; (3) Pedal six-four, where the bass consists of three repeated notes and the middle chord is a 6/4 chord; (4) Melodic bass six-four, where the bass voice has the melody and the fifth of the chord appears in this melodic bass line.

# Prerequisites
- **Figured bass inversion symbols**: Understanding 6/4 as second inversion
- **Cadential six-four**: The most common 6/4 usage

# Key Properties
1. Cadential 6/4: I6/4 before V at cadences; dominant function
2. Passing 6/4: bass moves stepwise (e.g., do-re-mi), middle chord is 6/4
3. Pedal 6/4: bass repeats (e.g., do-do-do), middle chord is 6/4
4. Melodic bass 6/4: bass carries the melody, 6/4 arises when fifth occurs melodically
5. All 6/4 chords are considered less stable than root position or first inversion
6. Each type is identified by the bass line behavior

# Construction / Recognition
- **Cadential**: Look for I6/4 immediately before V at a cadence point
- **Passing**: Look for three stepwise bass notes; the middle one supports a 6/4 chord
- **Pedal**: Look for three repeated bass notes; the middle one supports a 6/4 chord
- **Melodic bass**: Look for the bass voice carrying thematic material; 6/4 occurs when the fifth appears
- Always check the bass line pattern to determine the type

# Context & Application
Six-four chords are the most restricted inversion in tonal harmony. Random or free use of 6/4 chords sounds stylistically incorrect in common-practice writing. The restriction exists because the perfect fourth above the bass (between bass and a chord member) was considered dissonant in classical voice-leading practice. Each approved type provides a specific voice-leading justification for the fourth above the bass.

# Examples
- Cadential: I6/4-V at phrase endings (Mozart, K. 331)
- Passing: I-V6/4-I6 (bass passes stepwise: do-re-mi)
- Pedal: I-IV6/4-I (bass stays on do throughout)
- Melodic bass: occurs when bass voice carries thematic material

# Common Errors
- Using 6/4 chords freely without an approved bass-line context
- Failing to identify which type of 6/4 a given chord represents

# Common Confusions
- **Why restricted?**: The perfect fourth above the bass is treated as dissonant in classical style
- **Identification method**: Always check the BASS LINE to determine the type
- **Cadential vs. others**: The cadential 6/4 has dominant function; passing and pedal 6/4 chords are embellishing
