# Quick Definition
Second-inversion chords (6/4 chords) occur in only four approved contexts in classical music: cadential, passing, pedal, and melodic bass situations.

# Formal Definition
Second-inversion chords with the fifth of the chord in the bass are special chords found only in four situations in classical music: (1) Cadential six-four: I6/4 preceding V at cadences; (2) Passing six-four: bass moves stepwise with middle chord as 6/4; (3) Pedal six-four: bass repeats with middle chord as 6/4; (4) Melodic bass six-four: bass has the melody and the chord fifth appears melodically.

# Analysis/Application
Identifying 6/4 chord types:
1. Cadential: I6/4 before V at a cadence
2. Passing: Three stepwise bass notes, middle chord is 6/4
3. Pedal: Three repeated bass notes, middle chord is 6/4
4. Melodic bass: Bass voice has melody, 6/4 occurs when fifth is melodic note

# Musical Context
Second-inversion chords are considered less stable than root position or first inversion. Classical composers used them in these specific contexts to create voice-leading smoothness while managing their instability.

# Examples
- Cadential: I6/4-V at phrase endings
- Passing: I-V6/4-I6 (bass: do-re-mi ascending)
- Pedal: I-IV6/4-I (bass stays on do)
- Melodic bass: occurs when bass voice has thematic material

# Common Confusions
- 6/4 chords are restricted in classical style - they don't occur freely
- Each type has a characteristic bass line pattern
- The passing and pedal types use the 6/4 chord as a neighbor or passing chord
