# Quick Definition
Serialism is a compositional technique that organizes music according to a fixed series of elements, most commonly the twelve pitch classes of the chromatic scale.

# Formal Definition
Serialism encompasses twelve-tone technique and related approaches that use a predetermined series (row) of elements to structure a composition. The technique was developed by Schoenberg, Webern, and Berg (the Second Viennese School). While twelve-tone serialism uses all twelve pitch classes, serialism can also apply to series of fewer than twelve pitches, and later composers serialized rhythm, dynamics, and other parameters.

# Construction/Calculation
Basic premises of twelve-tone serialism:
1. All twelve notes of the chromatic scale must occur
2. No note repeats until all other 11 notes have occurred
3. Exceptions: direct repetition, trills, tremolos are permitted
4. The series can be transformed: inverted, retrograded, retrograde inverted
5. The order of notes remains fixed (no reordering within the row)
6. Any row form can be transposed to any of 12 pitch levels

# Musical Context
Serialism arose from expressionist atonality as composers sought systematic organization for non-tonal music. Every note in a twelve-tone composition can be traced to the original series or its transformations, providing structural unity. The row serves as both a melodic generator and a repository of intervals characteristic of the piece.

# Examples
- Schoenberg, Suite for Piano, Op. 25
- Webern, Symphony, Op. 21
- Berg, Violin Concerto
- Stravinsky adopted serial techniques later in his career (Septet, 1953)

# Common Confusions
- Serialism includes but is broader than twelve-tone technique
- Serial music is not necessarily atonal (some serial works have tonal elements)
- The row is not a theme - it's a structural principle
- Total serialism (serializing rhythm, dynamics, etc.) is a later development
