# Quick Definition
A tuplet is a grouping of notes that divides the beat differently than normal: triplets (3 in the space of 2) in simple meter, duplets (2 in the space of 3) in compound meter.

# Core Definition
Tuplet is a generic term that describes a grouping of notes that would not normally occur within a beat. A quarter note naturally divides into two eighth notes or four sixteenth notes. A triplet is a grouping of three eighth notes that occurs within the span of the quarter note. A quintuplet would be a grouping of five sixteenth notes to occur within the span of one quarter note. In compound meter, a duplet is a grouping of two eighth notes to occur within the span of a dotted quarter note, and a quadruplet is a grouping of four eighth notes within a dotted quarter.

# Prerequisites
- **Simple meter**: Where triplets and quintuplets occur
- **Compound meter**: Where duplets and quadruplets occur

# Key Properties
1. Triplet: 3 notes in the time of 2 (common in simple meter)
2. Duplet: 2 notes in the time of 3 (common in compound meter)
3. Quintuplet: 5 notes in the time of 4
4. Sextuplet: 6 notes in the time of 4
5. Quadruplet: 4 notes in the time of 3 (in compound meter)
6. Indicated by a number (and often a bracket) above or below the notes

# Construction / Recognition
- Number above/below a group of notes indicates the tuplet type
- Triplet: 3 with bracket over three notes in simple meter
- Duplet: 2 with bracket over two notes in compound meter
- If triplet divisions are constant, use compound meter instead

# Context & Application
Tuplets add rhythmic variety and complexity. Triplets are extremely common for creating a flowing, rolling feel in simple meter. Duplets in compound meter create a straighter, more march-like feel. If a piece naturally has triplet division throughout, use compound meter (6/8, 9/8, 12/8) rather than writing constant triplets.

# Examples
- Three eighth-note triplet = one quarter note duration in 4/4
- Duplet eighth notes in 6/8 = 2 eighths filling one dotted-quarter beat
- Quintuplet sixteenths = 5 sixteenths in the space of one quarter note
- Two measures that sound the same: triplets in 4/4 vs. normal eighths in 12/8

# Common Errors
- Writing constant triplets in simple meter when compound meter would be more appropriate
- Confusing the number of notes with the number of beats they occupy

# Common Confusions
- **Use compound meter for constant triplets**: If music naturally has triplet divisions, use 6/8, 9/8, or 12/8 instead of writing triplet brackets everywhere
- **Tuplets are exceptions**: They are departures from the normal beat division, not the rule
- **Notation**: The number indicates how many notes fit in the grouping
