# Quick Definition
A turnaround is a chord progression (typically iii-vi-ii-V or I-vi-ii-V) used in the final measures of a tune to create harmonic motion leading back to the beginning.

# Formal Definition
The turnaround replaces the static harmony of the I chord in the last two measures of a tune with a progression that creates forward momentum back to the top of the form. The standard turnaround is iii-vi-ii-V (or I-vi-ii-V), which can be modified so all four chords become dominant sevenths: III7-VI7-II7-V7 (secondary dominants in a chain of fifths). This dominant-chord version can then employ tritone substitutions.

# Construction/Calculation
Basic iii-vi-ii-V (in C):
- Em7 - Am7 - Dm7 - G7

Dominant version (III7-VI7-II7-V7):
- E7 - A7 - D7 - G7 (all as secondary dominants)

With tritone substitutions:
- E7 - Eb7 - D7 - Db7 (subs on vi and V)
- Creates chromatic bass motion

# Musical Context
Turnarounds appear at the end of the form in countless jazz standards, providing harmonic interest during what would otherwise be static tonic harmony. They are particularly common in 32-bar AABA forms and 12-bar blues. The chromatic voice leading created by tritone-substituted turnarounds is a hallmark of bebop harmony.

# Examples
- Standard: Em7 - Am7 - Dm7 - G7 (to C)
- Dominant: E7 - A7 - D7 - G7
- With tritone subs: E7 - Eb7 - D7 - Db7
- Blues turnaround: C7 - A7 - Dm7 - G7

# Common Confusions
- "Turnaround" refers to the function (turning around to the top), not a specific chord sequence
- Both iii-vi-ii-V and I-vi-ii-V are called turnarounds
- Tritone substitutions require converting chords to dominants first
- The turnaround creates motion that makes repeating back to I feel natural
