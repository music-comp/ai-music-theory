# Quick Definition
Syncopation occurs when notes on weak beats and on weak parts of beats are emphasized and nearby strong beats are de-emphasized.

# Core Definition
Syncopation occurs when notes on weak beats and on weak parts of beats are emphasized and nearby strong beats are deemphasized. Strong beats are the first beat of each measure (in 2/4 and 3/4) and beats 1 and 3 in 4/4. Syncopation at the beat level involves ties across those strong beats. Syncopation can also occur at the division of the beat level. Common syncopation patterns are acceptable notation even when they obscure the beat.

# Prerequisites
- **Meter**: Understanding of strong and weak beats
- **Beat and pulse**: The underlying pulse that syncopation disrupts
- **Dots and ties**: The notation tools used to create syncopation

# Key Properties
1. Accents on normally weak beats or weak parts of beats
2. Strong beats obscured by ties, rests, or held notes
3. Creates a "pull" against the regular pulse
4. Occurs at beat level (ties across strong beats)
5. Also occurs at division level (eighth-note syncopation)
6. Common syncopation figures are acceptable even when they hide beats

# Construction / Recognition
- Ties across strong beats (beats 1 or 3 in 4/4)
- Accents on "and" of beats
- Held notes that begin on weak beats and sustain through strong beats
- Numbers in parentheses indicate obscured beats

# Context & Application
Syncopation is essential to popular music styles and creates rhythmic interest and groove. It gives music forward momentum and energy by disrupting expected metric patterns. The rhythm in "Eleanor Rigby" (Beatles) features syncopation at the eighth-note level (the division of the beat), where syncopating notes gives them emphasis and creates a "pull" against the rhythm of the accompaniment.

# Examples
- A note tied from beat 4 to beat 1 of the next measure (beat-level syncopation)
- Accenting the "and" of beat 2 instead of beat 2 itself (division-level syncopation)
- "Eleanor Rigby" features division-level syncopation in the melody
- Common syncopated patterns: dotted-quarter + eighth in 4/4 across the beat boundary

# Common Errors
- Writing syncopation with overly complex notation when common patterns exist
- Hiding all beats in rhythmic notation (standard practice shows downbeats except in recognized syncopation patterns)

# Common Confusions
- **Acceptable notation**: Common syncopation figures do not need to be "properly" notated with ties showing every beat -- simplified notation is preferred
- **Two levels**: Syncopation occurs at the beat level (ties across downbeats) and at the division level (emphasis on offbeats)
- **Strong beats in 4/4**: Beats 1 and 3 are strong; beats 2 and 4 are weak
