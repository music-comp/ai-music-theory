# Quick Definition
Harmonic function describes the tendency of chords to progress to other chords or remain at rest, categorizing chords into four functional roles: tonic, dominant, pre-dominant, and tonic prolongation.

# Core Definition
Harmonic function refers to the tendency of certain chords to progress to other chords, or to remain at rest. This text discusses four harmonic functions: (1) Tonic function (abbreviated "ton."): The I chord has tonic function, a state of stability and rest; tonic chords do not demand progression. (2) Dominant function (abbreviated "dom."): V and viio (chords containing the leading tone and supertonic) tend to progress to tonic. (3) Pre-dominant function (abbreviated "pre-dom."): IV and ii (chords containing the subdominant and submediant scale degrees) tend to progress to dominant. (4) Tonic prolongation function (abbreviated "ton. prol."): vi and iii (chords sharing two common tones with I) tend to occur after tonic and progress to pre-dominant.

# Key Properties
1. Tonic (ton.): I -- stability and rest
2. Dominant (dom.): V, viio -- tendency toward tonic (contain scale degrees 7 and 2)
3. Pre-dominant (pre-dom.): IV, ii -- tendency toward dominant (contain scale degrees 4 and 6)
4. Tonic prolongation (ton. prol.): vi, iii -- share two common tones with I; follow tonic, proceed to pre-dominant
5. Two exceptions: IV has tonic prolongation function when it progresses to I (plagal cadence); I/5th has dominant function when it resolves to V
6. The I chord can progress directly to a chord of any other function

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the chord by Roman numeral
2. Assign the function based on the category (T, TP, PD, D)
3. Use abbreviations: "ton.", "dom.", "pre-dom.", "ton. prol."
4. Check for exceptions (plagal cadence, deceptive cadence, I/5th)

# Context & Application
Harmonic function underlies most tonal progressions from the Baroque era through contemporary popular music. The fundamental pattern T-PD-D-T can be realized in many ways (I-ii-V-I, I-IV-V-I, I-IV-viio-I, I-ii-viio-I). Understanding function explains why certain progressions feel natural and others feel unusual. Rock and roll is often said to be made up of three chords: I, IV, and V -- one for each primary function.

# Examples
- T-D-T: I-V-I (Mozart K. 283, Beethoven Op. 2 No. 3, "Hey Jude," "La donna e mobile")
- T-PD-D-T: I-ii-V-I (Bach WTC Prelude 1, "Theme from New York, New York"), I-IV-V-I (Chopin Waltz Op. 34 No. 1, "I'll Be There For You")
- T-TP-PD-D: I-vi-IV-V ("Heart and Soul," "Earth Angel")
- Star-Spangled Banner: I/5th has dominant function when resolving to V

# Common Errors
- Labeling IV as pre-dominant when it progresses to I (it has tonic prolongation function in the plagal cadence)
- Assuming "dominant" always means the V chord (viio also has dominant function)

# Common Confusions
- **"Dominant" has two senses**: Scale degree 5 and also a functional category (chords that progress to tonic)
- **IV to I exception**: IV has tonic prolongation function when it progresses to I in a plagal cadence
- **viio has dominant function**: Even though it is not the V chord, it contains the leading tone and resolves to I
