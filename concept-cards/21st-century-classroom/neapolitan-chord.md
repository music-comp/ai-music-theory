# Quick Definition
The Neapolitan chord is a major triad built on the lowered second scale degree (bII), most commonly in first inversion (N6), functioning as a dramatic chromatic pre-dominant -- labeled "N" in classical analysis rather than bII.

# Core Definition
The label "Neapolitan" is used in classical music for the bII chord and is labeled as "N" instead of bII. Being a chromatically altered ii chord, the Neapolitan has pre-dominant harmonic function. When it occurs in first inversion (its most common inversion), it is labeled N6. The chord's most distinctive quality is its dramatic, colorful sound, and composers often alter texture, register, and dynamics when the Neapolitan appears.

# Prerequisites
- **Chromatic pre-dominants**: The functional category the Neapolitan belongs to
- **Triads**: The Neapolitan is always a major triad

# Key Properties
1. Major triad built on b2 (lowered second scale degree)
2. Labeled "N" (not bII) in classical analysis
3. Most common inversion: first inversion (N6) with scale degree 4 in bass
4. Pre-dominant harmonic function (replaces ii or IV)
5. Quality is always major
6. Often accompanied by changes in texture, register, dynamics
7. Common in both major and minor keys

# Construction / Recognition
- In C major/minor: Db-F-Ab (root position) or F-Ab-Db (first inversion, N6)
- Scale degree 4 in the bass when in first inversion
- Always major quality on b2
- Use "N" label instead of bII in Roman numeral analysis
- Compare with other pre-dominants: try iv, iio6 in its place to hear the difference

# Context & Application
The Neapolitan chord creates a distinctive, dramatic color before the dominant. Its special character often prompts composers to highlight it with textural changes. It is particularly prominent in Classical and Romantic era music and has found new life in film scores, where its dramatic quality suits climactic moments. The chord can also serve as a pivot chord in modulation to distant keys.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 7, Op. 92, I (1812) -- with notable texture/dynamic changes
- Beethoven, Moonlight Sonata, Op. 27, No. 2, I (1802) -- compare with iv and iio6
- John Williams, Raiders of the Lost Ark, "Raiders March" (1981)
- Michael Giacchino, Star Trek, "End Credits" (2009)
- Hans Zimmer, Batman v Superman, "The Red Capes are Coming" (2016)

# Common Errors
- Labeling as bII instead of N in classical analysis
- Forgetting that the Neapolitan is almost always in first inversion

# Common Confusions
- **N vs. bII**: Use "N" in classical analysis; bII is sometimes used in popular music contexts
- **Almost always first inversion**: Root position Neapolitan is rare; N6 is standard
- **Pre-dominant, not dominant**: Despite its dramatic quality, the Neapolitan leads to V, not to I
