# Quick Definition
Borrowed chords are chords taken from the parallel minor mode and used in a major key context, featuring lowered scale degrees that alter the quality of diatonic chords.

# Formal Definition
Borrowed chords refers to borrowing chords from the parallel minor mode and is synonymous with mode mixture. When a scale degree from the minor mode is included in a chord, the quality of that diatonic chord will be altered. The most commonly borrowed scale degrees are flat-6, flat-3, and flat-7.

# Construction/Recognition
Harmonization of flat-6 as root, 3rd, 5th, or 7th:
- As root: flat-VI (major triad)
- As 3rd: iv (minor triad)
- As 5th: ii diminished or ii half-diminished 7
- As 7th: various seventh chords

Harmonization of flat-3 and flat-7:
- flat-3 and flat-7 can each serve as root, 3rd, or 5th of borrowed chords
- Some chords overlap between flat-6, flat-3, and flat-7 harmonizations

# Musical Context
- Pre-dominant function is common (ii diminished, iv)
- Tonic prolongation (flat-VI, flat-VII)
- Creates darker, more expressive color in major keys
- Particularly common in Romantic-era music and popular music

# Examples
- ii half-diminished (contains flat-6 as the 5th)
- iv (contains flat-6 as the 3rd)
- flat-VI (contains flat-6 as the root)
- flat-VII (contains flat-7 as the root)
- Frank Loesser, "Inchworm" (1952) - I alternating with flat-VII7

# Related Concepts
- Mode mixture
- Parallel minor
- Chromatic pre-dominants
- Deceptive cadence with flat-VI

# Common Confusions
- Do not memorize all possible borrowed chords; instead, recognize that including a minor-mode scale degree alters chord quality
- Writing borrowed chords requires attention to quality indicated by the Roman numeral
- A flat before a Roman numeral means lower by m2, not add a flat accidental
