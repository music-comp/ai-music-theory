# Quick Definition
Borrowed chords are specific chords taken from the parallel minor and used in a major-key context, with b6 harmonizable as root (bVI), 3rd (iv), 5th (iio), or 7th of various chords -- analyzed simply by root, quality, and inversion.

# Core Definition
When a scale degree from the minor mode is included in a chord, the quality of that diatonic chord is altered. The most commonly borrowed scale degree is b6, which can be harmonized as the root (producing bVI), the third (producing iv), the fifth (producing iio or iio7), or the seventh of various chords. Similarly, b3 and b7 can each serve as root, 3rd, or 5th of borrowed chords, with some overlap. Unlike secondary chords, borrowed chords are analyzed simply by root, quality, and inversion.

- **Mode mixture**: The underlying concept of borrowing from parallel minor

# Key Properties
1. b6 as root: bVI (major triad)
2. b6 as 3rd: iv (minor subdominant)
3. b6 as 5th: iio, iio7 (diminished/half-diminished supertonic)
4. b6 as 7th: various seventh chords
5. b3 and b7 generate additional borrowed chords
6. Some chords appear in multiple categories (overlap between b6, b3, b7 harmonizations)
7. No need to memorize all possibilities; recognize that minor-mode scale degrees alter chord quality

# Construction / Recognition
To analyze a borrowed chord:
- Stack in thirds, determine root and quality
- Identify the Roman numeral based on the root's scale degree
- Note the altered quality (e.g., ii becomes iio, IV becomes iv)
- Include inversion via figured bass

To write from a Roman numeral (e.g., bVI in A major):
- b6 in A major = F-natural (lower A's 6th degree, F#, by m2)
- Build a major triad on F: F-A-C
- Flats before Roman numerals mean "lower by m2 in the key," not literally add a flat

# Context & Application
Borrowed chords serve multiple harmonic functions. Pre-dominant borrowed chords (iv, iio, iio7) intensify the drive to the dominant. Tonic-prolongation borrowed chords (bVI, bVII) extend the tonic area with unexpected color. The emotional darkening from major to minor mode is one of the most powerful expressive tools in tonal harmony.

# Examples
- iv (contains b6 as 3rd) -- darker subdominant
- bVI (contains b6 as root) -- dramatic major chord on lowered 6th degree
- bVII (contains b7 as root) -- subtonic chord
- iio7 (contains b6 as 5th) -- intensified pre-dominant
- Frank Loesser, "Inchworm" (1952) -- I alternating with bVII7

# Common Errors
- Interpreting bVI as "A-flat major chord" when in the key of A major (it is F major)
- Memorizing all possible borrowed chords instead of understanding the principle

# Common Confusions
- **Flat notation**: bVI in A major = F major (F# lowered by m2 = F-natural)
- **No slash notation**: Borrowed chords are NOT labeled like secondary chords (no V/x or viio/x)
- **Principle over list**: Understand that any minor-mode scale degree alters chord quality; do not merely memorize a list
