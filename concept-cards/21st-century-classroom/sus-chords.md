# Quick Definition
Sus chords (suspended chords) replace the third of a triad with either a perfect fourth (sus4) or major second (sus2).

# Formal Definition
"Sus" is short for "suspended," referring to a non-chord tone technique. In sus4 chords, a perfect fourth replaces the third. In sus2 chords, a major second replaces the third. Both maintain the perfect fifth from root to fifth. These chords have an open, unresolved quality because they lack the defining major or minor third.

# Construction/Recognition
- Sus4: Root + P4 + P5 (e.g., C-F-G)
- Sus2: Root + M2 + P5 (e.g., C-D-G)
- No third present (neither major nor minor quality)
- Sus4 often resolves the 4th down to the 3rd
- Lead-sheet: Csus4 (or Csus), Csus2

# Musical Context
Sus chords create tension that often resolves to a regular major or minor triad. They are common in pop, rock, and folk music for their open, floating quality. Sus4 chords are more common than sus2.

# Examples
- Csus4: C-F-G (P4 replaces the 3rd)
- Csus2: C-D-G (M2 replaces the 3rd)
- Gsus4: G-C-D
- Asus2: A-B-E
- "Hard to Say I'm Sorry" opens with a sus4 chord

# Common Confusions
- Sus chords are neither major nor minor (no third)
- "Sus" alone usually means sus4
- Sus2 is sometimes called "sus9" but this can cause confusion
