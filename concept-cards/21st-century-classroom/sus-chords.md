# Quick Definition
The IV/5-hat chord (e.g., F/G in C major) is a four-note chord that sounds and functions similarly to a V7 chord, commonly used as a dominant substitute in popular music. More generally, sus chords replace the third of a triad with a fourth (sus4) or second (sus2).

# Core Definition
The IV/5-hat (or IV over scale-degree-5) chord places the subdominant triad over the dominant bass note. In C major, this is F/G, which contains the notes G, F, A, C -- the same pitch classes as a G7sus4 or G11 chord. It functions as a dominant substitute because it shares the dominant bass note while creating a softer, more open resolution to tonic. Separately, "sus" chords in general replace the third with either a perfect fourth (sus4) or major second (sus2), creating an open, unresolved quality.

# Prerequisites
- **Triad**: Basic three-note chord construction

# Key Properties
1. IV/5-hat: subdominant triad over dominant bass note
2. Contains scale degrees 5, 4, 6, and 1
3. Equivalent to V7sus4 or V11
4. Functions as dominant substitute without the leading tone
5. Sus4: root + P4 + P5 (e.g., C-F-G)
6. Sus2: root + M2 + P5 (e.g., C-D-G)
7. Neither major nor minor (no third present)

# Construction / Recognition
1. For IV/5-hat: take the IV chord and place scale degree 5 in the bass
2. Lead-sheet notation: F/G in C major, Ab/Bb in Eb major
3. For sus4: replace the third with a perfect fourth
4. For sus2: replace the third with a major second
5. "Sus" alone usually means sus4

# Context & Application
The IV/5-hat chord softens the dominant function by removing the leading tone, creating a more open, less urgent resolution to tonic. It is widely used in pop, soul, and R&B music. General sus chords create tension that often resolves to a regular major or minor triad and are common in pop, rock, and folk music.

# Examples
- Stan Vincent, "O-o-h Child" -- IV/5-hat chord in A major (D/E)
- Beatles, "The Long and Winding Road" -- Ab/Bb in Eb major
- Csus4: C-F-G; Gsus4: G-C-D
- Csus2: C-D-G

# Common Errors
- Confusing IV/5-hat with a simple IV chord (the bass note changes the function)
- Treating sus chords as major or minor (they have no third)

# Common Confusions
- **Not a classical suspension**: Although called "sus," the pop-music sus chord does not follow the classical preparation-suspension-resolution pattern
- **Same notes as V11 or V7sus4**: The IV/5-hat chord can be analyzed multiple ways depending on context
- **"Sus" alone usually means sus4**: Sus2 must be specified explicitly
