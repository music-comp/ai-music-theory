# Quick Definition
AABA form is a 32-bar song form associated with Broadway musicals and the Great American Songbook, where three A sections contain the main melody and the B section (bridge or "middle eight") provides contrast.

# Formal Definition
The AABA form was dominant in popular music from the 1930s to the 1950s. Each section (A or B) is typically 8 measures long. The A sections contain the primary melody while the B section provides contrast and is often called the "bridge" or "middle eight." The entire 32-bar form is sometimes called a "refrain" or "chorus."

# Analysis/Application
When analyzing AABA form:
1. Each section is typically 8 bars
2. A sections contain the main melodic material
3. B section (bridge/middle eight) provides contrast
4. One or more A sections may be instrumental
5. The form may repeat (AABA AABA) with variations

# Musical Context
AABA was the dominant form for Broadway show tunes and popular songs from the 1930s through the 1950s. It remained common through the 1960s (Beatles) and occasionally appears in contemporary music.

# Examples
- George Gershwin, "I Got Rhythm" (1930) - classic AABA
- Cole Porter: "Anything Goes," "Love for Sale"
- Irving Berlin: "Blue Skies," "Puttin' on the Ritz"
- The Beatles: "Yesterday," "Norwegian Wood," "Hey Jude"
- The Cure, "Friday, I'm in Love" (1992)
- Norah Jones, "Don't Know Why" (1999)

# Common Confusions
- Some AABA songs have a "verse" before the 32-bar refrain (different from verse-chorus verse)
- The entire 32-bar AABA may be called a "refrain" or "chorus"
- Terms like "prelude" or "introduction" are sometimes used for the pre-AABA verse
