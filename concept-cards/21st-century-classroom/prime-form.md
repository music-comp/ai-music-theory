# Quick Definition
Prime form is the most reduced representation of a pitch-class set, transposed to start on 0 and inverted if necessary to achieve the most compact arrangement.

# Formal Definition
Prime form represents a pitch-class set in its most essential, abstract form - transposed to begin on C (pitch integer 0) and inverted if the inversion produces a more compact form. It categorizes sets into equivalence classes called "set classes," similar to how we categorize C major, E major, and Ab major triads all as "major triads." Prime form allows comparison across transpositions and inversions.

# Construction/Calculation
Steps to determine prime form (Rahn method):
1. Determine normal form
2. Transpose normal form to start on 0
3. Invert the transposed form (intervals go opposite direction)
4. Write inverted form in ascending order
5. If there were ties in normal form, recheck for most compact inverted ordering
6. Compare: the most compact form (original vs inverted) is prime form
7. Write in parentheses without commas: (014)
8. Use T for 10, E for 11 if needed

# Musical Context
Prime form reveals deep relationships between pitch-class sets that may not be obvious from the music's surface. Two passages using different notes may share the same prime form, indicating structural similarity. The 208 possible prime forms are catalogued with Forte numbers for reference.

# Examples
- Eb-B-D {3, 11, 2}: normal form [11, 2, 3] -> transposed [0, 3, 4] -> inverted [0, 8, 9] -> reorder [0, 1, 4] = (014)
- G#-C-A {8, 0, 9}: normal form [8, 9, 0] -> prime form (014) [same as above!]
- (037) is the prime form of all major and minor triads
- (0148) requires comparing transposed and inverted versions

# Common Confusions
- Prime form always starts on 0 and uses the most compact arrangement
- Written in parentheses WITHOUT commas: (014) not (0, 1, 4)
- Use T for 10, E for 11: (013568T)
- Forte method differs slightly from Rahn for 6 of 208 sets
- (037) represents ALL major AND minor triads (inversionally equivalent)
