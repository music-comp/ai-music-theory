# Quick Definition
A major seventh chord consists of a major triad with a major seventh above the root, producing a warm, lush, relatively stable sonority.

# Core Definition
The major seventh chord is built from a major triad plus a major seventh interval from root to seventh (11 half steps). Its intervallic structure is M3 + m3 + M3. In major keys it naturally occurs on scale degrees 1 and 4 (IM7 and IVM7). In Roman numeral notation, "M7" designates the major seventh quality. The "maj" in the lead-sheet symbol refers to the seventh interval, not the triad (which is already major).

# Prerequisites
- **Seventh chord**: General concept of four-note chords

# Key Properties
1. Major triad + major seventh from root to seventh (11 half steps)
2. Third-stacking pattern: M3 + m3 + M3
3. Occurs naturally on I and IV in major keys
4. Roman numeral notation: IM7, IVM7
5. Lead-sheet symbols: Cmaj7, CM7, C-triangle-7
6. Relatively stable and consonant compared to dominant seventh

# Construction / Recognition
1. Build a major triad on the root
2. Add a note a major third above the fifth (or a major seventh above the root)
3. The top note is a half step below the octave of the root
4. Distinguish from dominant seventh by checking the seventh interval quality

# Context & Application
The major seventh chord has a lush, sophisticated, jazzy quality. In classical music it appears as IM7 and IVM7. In jazz, bossa nova, and pop ballads it is extremely common as a tonic chord, often with added extensions. It is less dissonant and more stable than the dominant seventh.

# Examples
- Cmaj7: C-E-G-B (IM7 in C major)
- Fmaj7: F-A-C-E (IVM7 in C major)

# Common Errors
- Confusing Cmaj7 (major seventh chord, with B natural) with C7 (dominant seventh chord, with Bb)

# Common Confusions
- **Cmaj7 vs. C7**: Cmaj7 has a major seventh (B natural); C7 has a minor seventh (Bb)
- **"maj" refers to the seventh**: The triad is already major; "maj" specifies the seventh interval
