# Quick Definition
A 6/9 chord combines a triad with both the 6th and 9th scale degrees, creating a bright, colorful sonority without the defining 7th of extended chords.

# Formal Definition
A 6/9 chord consists of a triad (major or minor) with both the major 6th and major 9th added. Unlike ninth chords, there is no 7th present. The notation places the 6 above the 9 (written as a fraction-like symbol) to indicate both additions. The 6th always comes from the major scale unless otherwise specified.

# Construction/Calculation
1. Build a major or minor triad
2. Add the major 6th from the major scale
3. Add the major 9th from the major scale
4. Major 6/9: root - M3 - P5 - M6 - M9 (C-E-G-A-D)
5. Minor 6/9: root - m3 - P5 - M6 - M9 (C-Eb-G-A-D)

# Musical Context
The 6/9 chord is a common voicing for tonic chords in jazz, providing color without the pull of the 7th. It appears as a resolution chord in ii-V-I progressions, particularly in the minor ii-V-i where the minor 6/9 serves as the tonic. The absence of the 7th gives it a more stable, "finished" quality.

# Examples
- C6/9 = C-E-G-A-D (major 6/9)
- Cm6/9 = C-Eb-G-A-D (minor 6/9)
- Used as tonic resolution in minor: Dm7(b5)-G7alt-Cm6/9

# Common Confusions
- 6/9 chords do NOT contain a 7th
- This is different from a 13th chord which does contain the 7th
- The notation 6/9 is not a fraction; both notes are present
- Voice the 6th like you would voice the 7th in standard jazz voicings
