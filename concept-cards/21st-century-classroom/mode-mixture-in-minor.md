# Quick Definition
Borrowing from major while in a minor key is rare because a major I chord in minor typically functions as V/iv (not mode mixture), and raised 6 and 7 in minor are already part of the melodic minor scale -- the Picardy third (major I at the very end) is the sole significant exception.

# Core Definition
It is rare to borrow from the major mode during a passage in a minor key for two reasons: (1) The major tonic usually functions as V/iv if it is not the last chord of a piece -- a major I chord in a minor context sounds like it wants to resolve to iv. (2) Raised 6 and 7 occur naturally as part of the melodic minor scale and are therefore not considered borrowed from the major mode. The one place to borrow from major while in minor is at the end of a piece, using the Picardy third (major I instead of minor i).

# Prerequisites
- **Mode mixture**: Understanding the primary direction (minor into major)
- **Picardy third**: The sole exception to one-directional borrowing

# Key Properties
1. Borrowing from major into minor is rare
2. Major I in minor = V/iv (secondary dominant function), not mode mixture
3. Raised 6 and 7 = melodic minor, not borrowing
4. Exception: Picardy third (major I at the END of a piece)
5. Mode mixture is primarily a one-directional process (minor into major)

# Construction / Recognition
- If major I chord appears mid-piece in minor: analyze as V/iv
- If major I chord appears at the final cadence in minor: Picardy third
- Raised 6 and 7 in minor are NOT mode mixture -- they belong to melodic minor
- True "reverse" mode mixture (borrowing from major into minor) is extremely limited

# Context & Application
This asymmetry between the two directions of mode mixture is an important theoretical point. Mode mixture is overwhelmingly a major-key phenomenon, enriching major-key harmony with minor-mode colors. The minor mode already has considerable chromatic flexibility through its three scale forms (natural, harmonic, melodic), so borrowing from major adds relatively little that is not already available. The Picardy third, as the sole significant exception, has a special expressive quality precisely because it is exceptional.

# Examples
- Major I chord in measure 5 of a C minor piece: analyze as V/iv (resolves to Fm), NOT as mode mixture
- Major I chord as the final chord of a C minor piece: Picardy third
- C#-D# in A minor: raised 6 and 7 of melodic minor, NOT borrowed from A major

# Common Errors
- Labeling any major chord in a minor key as mode mixture
- Considering raised 6 and 7 as borrowed from major

# Common Confusions
- **V/iv vs. Picardy third**: Major I mid-piece = V/iv; major I at the end = Picardy third
- **Melodic minor is not borrowing**: Raised 6 and 7 are indigenous to minor through melodic minor
- **One-way process**: Mode mixture is overwhelmingly minor-into-major, not the reverse
