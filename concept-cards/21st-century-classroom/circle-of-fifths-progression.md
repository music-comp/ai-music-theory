# Quick Definition
A chord progression where roots descend by perfect fifths through all diatonic scale degrees, creating a sense of harmonic inevitability through sequential motion.

# Formal Definition
The circle of fifths progression (I-IV-vii°-iii-vi-ii-V-I in major, or i-iv-VII-III-VI-ii°-V-i in minor) moves through all diatonic chords with roots descending by fifths. This differs from the circle of fifths for key signatures, which contains all 12 chromatic notes; the harmonic circle contains only diatonic notes.

# Analysis/Application
When analyzing:
1. Look for root motion descending by fifths
2. The progression may use only a portion of the full circle
3. Some chords may be inverted while the roots still follow the pattern
4. Listen for the sequential "feeling of inevitability"

# Musical Context
The circle of fifths progression was a stalwart of the Baroque era (Bach, Handel, Vivaldi) and has remained popular through jazz standards, 1950s pop, and contemporary music. The sequential nature creates predictable, satisfying harmonic motion.

# Examples
- J.S. Bach, Brandenburg Concerto No. 2 in F Major, BWV 1047, I
- Handel, Suite in G minor, Passacaglia
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, I
- "Fly Me to the Moon" (Bart Howard, 1950s)
- "I Will Survive" (1970s)
- "Love You Like A Love Song" (2010s)

# Common Confusions
- The circle for harmonic progression is different from the circle of fifths for key signatures
- Sometimes called "circle of descending fifths"
- Inverted chords still follow the pattern if the roots descend by fifths
