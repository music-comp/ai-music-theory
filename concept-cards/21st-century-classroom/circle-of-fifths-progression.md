# Quick Definition
A chord progression where roots descend by perfect fifths through all diatonic scale degrees (I-IV-viio-iii-vi-ii-V-I), creating a sense of harmonic inevitability through sequential motion.

# Core Definition
The circle of fifths progression (I-IV-viio-iii-vi-ii-V-I in major, or i-iv-VII-III-VI-iio-V-i in minor with subtonic VII) moves through all diatonic chords with roots descending by fifths. This differs from the circle of fifths for key signatures, which contains all 12 chromatic notes; the harmonic circle contains only diatonic notes. The progression has a feeling of inevitability because it consists of harmonic sequences -- the bass line follows a sequential descending-fifth pattern.

# Prerequisites
- **Roman numeral analysis**: Ability to identify chords by Roman numeral
- **Diatonic chords in major**: Knowledge of chord qualities on each scale degree

# Key Properties
1. Full progression: I-IV-viio-iii-vi-ii-V-I
2. In minor: i-iv-VII-III-VI-iio-V-i (with subtonic VII)
3. Roots descend by diatonic fifths (or ascend by fourths)
4. Contains only diatonic notes (unlike chromatic circle of fifths)
5. Sequential bass line creates feeling of inevitability
6. Commonly used in segments rather than in its entirety

# Construction / Recognition
1. Listen for root motion descending by fifths
2. The progression may use only a portion of the full circle
3. Some chords may be inverted while the roots still follow the pattern
4. Look for the sequential pattern in the bass line

# Context & Application
The circle of fifths progression was a stalwart of the Baroque era (Bach, Handel, Vivaldi), especially in minor with subtonic VII. Shorter segments (ii-V-I, vi-ii-V-I, iii-vi-ii-V) remain foundational across all styles from classical to jazz to contemporary pop.

# Examples
- J.S. Bach, Brandenburg Concerto No. 2 in F Major, BWV 1047, I
- Handel, Suite in G minor, Passacaglia
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, I
- Bart Howard, "Fly Me to the Moon" (1950s)
- "I Will Survive" (1970s)
- "Love You Like A Love Song" (2010s) -- bass line as two-note descending sequence

# Common Errors
- Confusing the diatonic circle (harmonic progression) with the chromatic circle (key signatures)
- Missing the sequential pattern when chords are inverted

# Common Confusions
- **Two different circles**: The circle for harmonic progression is diatonic; the circle for key signatures is chromatic
- **Also called "circle of descending fifths"**: The roots descend by fifth (or equivalently ascend by fourth)
- **Inverted chords still follow the pattern**: The roots follow the circle even when bass notes differ due to inversions
