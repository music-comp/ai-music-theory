# Quick Definition
Closely related keys share key signatures within one accidental of the home key; foreign (distantly related) keys have key signatures further removed.

# Formal Definition
"Closely related" keys have key signatures one degree "sharper" or "flatter" than the starting key. For any major or minor key, there are five closely related keys, including the relative major or minor of the home key. Key signatures that are not closely related are said to be "foreign" or "distantly related."

# Construction/Recognition
For any key, the five closely related keys are:
1. Relative major/minor
2. Dominant key
3. Subdominant key
4. Relative of dominant
5. Relative of subdominant

Example - A major closely related keys:
- f# minor (relative minor)
- E major (dominant)
- D major (subdominant)
- c# minor (relative of dominant)
- b minor (relative of subdominant)

Foreign keys: all others (more than one accidental difference)

# Musical Context
- Baroque/Classical: primarily modulate to closely related keys
- Romantic era: exploration of foreign key relationships
- Common-tone modulations exploit foreign key relationships for dramatic effect
- The "tonal distance" affects the perceived drama of modulation

# Examples
Analysis:
- From C major: closely related = G, F, a, e, d
- From a minor: closely related = C, G, d, e, F
- C major to Db major = foreign (distant) relationship
- C major to Ab major = foreign relationship

# Common Confusions
- Closely related keys differ by only ONE accidental
- The relative key is always closely related
- Parallel major/minor are NOT closely related (different key signatures)
- Foreign keys require special modulatory techniques (chromatic pivots, common-tone)
