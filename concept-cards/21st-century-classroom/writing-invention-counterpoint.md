# Quick Definition
Counterpoint accompanying theme statements in inventions must emphasize the underlying harmony, which may require altering the theme itself or creating "microharmonies" within measures.

# Core Definition
When writing counterpoint for invention expositions, the accompanying line must support the harmonic function of each measure (I or V). This differs from species counterpoint because tonal considerations take precedence. Themes may need alteration when transposed to fit the harmonic context. Additionally, individual measures may contain "microharmonies" -- brief subsidiary harmonic motions like I-IV-I or I-vii6-I -- even when the overriding harmony is stable (Hutchinson, Ch. 30, pp. 431-433).

# Prerequisites
- **Invention exposition** -- Must understand the four-measure I-I-V-V-I structure
- **Species counterpoint** -- Counterpoint skills applied in a tonal context

# Key Properties
1. Counterpoint must emphasize the underlying harmony (I or V) in each measure
2. Themes may need alteration when transposed to different harmonic contexts
3. In minor, scale degrees 2 and 3 become raised-6 and raised-7 when transposed to V area
4. Microharmonies: brief harmonic motions within a measure under a stable overriding harmony
5. Alter as few notes as possible when modifying themes

# Construction / Recognition
**Theme alteration scenarios:**
1. Theme that naturally leads I to V may need contour changes in mm. 3-4 to emphasize V-to-I motion
2. Theme landing on scale degree 3 (not 5): alter end of m. 2 to reach scale degree 5 for m. 3 bass entry
3. Minor themes with scale degrees 2 and 3: become raised-6 and raised-7 when transposed up a fifth (mm. 3-4); use melodic minor scale

**Writing the counterpoint:**
1. After copying and transposing the theme, add counterpoint in the free voice for mm. 2-4
2. Counterpoint must emphasize the harmony of each measure
3. Create microharmonies where needed (I-IV-I, I-vii6-I) within measures of stable harmony

# Context & Application
These techniques bridge strict species counterpoint with the harmonic demands of tonal music. Bach's C major invention (BWV 772) demonstrates microharmonies in mm. 1-2 where brief IV and vii6 chords appear within the predominant I harmony. Understanding microharmonies helps explain how Baroque composers maintained harmonic clarity while writing melodically active counterpoint.

# Examples
- **Theme alteration for scale degree 3** (p. 431): Alter last note of m. 2 to lead to scale degree 5 for m. 3
- **Minor key raised degrees** (p. 432): Theme with D-E transposed to V area becomes A-B (raised-6 and raised-7)
- **Microharmonies** (p. 433, Figure 30.7.4): Bach C major invention, mm. 1-2 show I-IV-I and I-vii6-I within overall I harmony

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Writing species-style counterpoint that ignores the underlying harmony
  **Correction**: Counterpoint must clearly support I or V in each measure
- **Error**: Not raising scale degrees 6 and 7 in minor when the theme moves to the V area
  **Correction**: Use melodic minor (raised 6 and 7) for mm. 3-4 in minor keys

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Mistaking microharmonies for actual harmonic changes
  **Clarification**: Microharmonies are subsidiary motions within a measure whose overriding harmony remains stable (I or V)
- **Confusion**: Thinking themes must always be transposed exactly
  **Clarification**: Themes may and should be altered to fit the harmonic context, changing as few notes as possible
