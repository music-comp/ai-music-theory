# Quick Definition
The ii-V-I progression is the most fundamental chord progression in jazz, consisting of a minor seventh chord on the second scale degree moving to a dominant seventh on the fifth degree, resolving to the tonic.

# Formal Definition
The ii-V-I progression is a three-chord sequence built on the 2nd, 5th, and 1st scale degrees. In major keys, the typical voicing is: ii = minor 9th, V = dominant 9th or 13th (often with alterations), I = major 9th or 6/9. In minor keys: ii = half-diminished 7th (m7b5), V = altered dominant 7th, i = minor 6/9 or minor-major 7th. This progression combines subdominant, dominant, and tonic functions.

# Construction/Calculation
Major key ii-V-I:
- ii: Dm9 (or Dm7)
- V: G9(13) or G7 with alterations
- I: CΔ9 (or CΔ7 or C6/9)

Minor key ii-V-i:
- ii: Dm7(b5) (half-diminished)
- V: G7alt (altered dominant)
- i: Cm6/9 (or CmΔ7)

# Musical Context
The ii-V-I is ubiquitous in jazz standards. It appears in classics like "Autumn Leaves," "What Is This Thing Called Love," "Tune-Up," "Pent Up House," "Lady Bird," and "Firm Roots." Many jazz compositions are essentially chains of ii-V-I progressions in various keys. Mastering this progression is essential for jazz improvisation and comping.

# Examples
- C major: Dm9 - G9(13) - CΔ9
- C minor: Dm7(b5) - G7alt - Cm6/9
- F major: Gm9 - C9(13) - FΔ9
- Key recognition: Dm7-G7-C identifies C major; Dm7(b5)-G7alt-Cm identifies C minor

# Common Confusions
- The ii chord is minor (not major II) in both major and minor keys
- In minor, the ii chord is half-diminished (not minor 7th)
- The V chord in minor is typically altered; in major it may be unaltered or with extensions
- ii-V-I can occur in any key and is used to identify key centers in analysis
