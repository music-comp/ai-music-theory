# Quick Definition
A motive (or motif) is the smallest identifiable melodic idea in music, typically consisting of two to seven notes with a distinctive rhythmic and/or intervallic profile, serving as a building block for larger structures.

# Core Definition
A motive (or motif) is the smallest identifiable melodic idea in music. However, "compound" motives can be broken into smaller fragments (called "germs" by some authors). Motives serve as building blocks for larger musical structures and are often extracted and developed through various techniques including sequence, inversion, augmentation, diminution, and other melodic alterations.

# Prerequisites
- None (fundamental concept)

# Key Properties
1. Smallest identifiable melodic idea
2. Typically 2-7 notes
3. Has distinctive rhythmic and/or intervallic profile
4. Can be "compound" (divisible into fragments)
5. Developed through sequence, inversion, augmentation, etc.
6. Labeled with numbers (1, 2, 3, etc.) in analysis
7. Transposition (sequence) is so common it is not labeled as an alteration

# Construction / Recognition
1. Label motives with numbers (1, 2, 3, etc.)
2. Look for motives to be sequenced, inverted, augmented, or otherwise altered
3. Note when motives are extracted and developed separately
4. Consider the relationship between motives within a phrase

# Context & Application
Motivic development is central to Western classical music, especially in the works of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven. Motives provide unity and coherence while allowing for variety through development techniques. In development sections, composers extract motives and develop them through sequences and alterations.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 1, Op. 21, I -- four motives within the first four measures, each later developed through sequences
- J.S. Bach, Invention No. 1 in C Major -- 7-note compound motive divided into two overlapping fragments

# Common Errors
- Confusing motives with subphrases (motives are shorter: 2-7 notes)
- Labeling simple transposition as a motivic alteration (it is too common to label)

# Common Confusions
- **Motive vs. subphrase**: Motives are short (2-7 notes); subphrases are longer (6+ notes, usually 2 measures)
- **Compound motives**: Can be divided into smaller fragments for independent development
- **Transposition not labeled**: Sequence (transposition) is so common it is not considered a motivic alteration worth labeling
