# Quick Definition
A motive (or motif) is the smallest identifiable melodic idea in music, typically consisting of two to seven notes with a distinctive rhythmic and/or intervallic profile.

# Formal Definition
A motive (or motif) is the smallest identifiable melodic idea in music. Motives can be "compound" and broken into smaller fragments (sometimes called "germs"). Motives serve as building blocks for larger musical structures and are often extracted and developed through various techniques.

# Analysis/Application
When analyzing motives:
1. Label motives with numbers (1, 2, 3, etc.)
2. Look for motives to be sequenced, inverted, augmented, or otherwise altered
3. Note when motives are extracted and developed separately
4. Consider the relationship between motives within a phrase

# Musical Context
Motivic development is central to Western classical music, especially in the works of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven. Motives provide unity and coherence while allowing for variety through development techniques.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 1, Op. 21, I - four motives within the first four measures of the primary theme, later extracted and developed through sequences
- J.S. Bach, Invention No. 1 in C Major - 7-note compound motive divided into two overlapping fragments

# Common Confusions
- Motives are short (2-7 notes); subphrases are longer (usually 2 measures)
- A compound motive can be divided into smaller fragments
- Transposition of a motive (sequence) is so common it's not labeled as an alteration
