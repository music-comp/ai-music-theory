# Quick Definition
The dominant seventh chord and German augmented sixth are enharmonically equivalent -- V7 can be reinterpreted as Ger+6 (or vice versa) to modulate between distant keys, with the most common respelling affecting the top note (7th of V7 = #4 of Ger+6).

# Core Definition
In this type of enharmonic modulation, V7 is enharmonically reinterpreted as Ger+6, or Ger+6 is reinterpreted as V7. Secondary dominants (V7/IV, V7/V, etc.) can also be reinterpreted as Ger+6 chords (and vice versa). The two chords have the same sound but different spellings: V7 resolves down a perfect fifth to its tonic, while Ger+6 resolves with its root moving down a half step to a chord of dominant function (typically I6/4). Most commonly, the top note is respelled: the 7th of V7 becomes the #4 of Ger+6 (or vice versa).

# Key Properties
1. V7 and Ger+6 are enharmonically equivalent (same sound)
2. V7 resolves root down P5; Ger+6 resolves root down m2
3. Most common respelling: top note (7th of V7 = #4 of Ger+6)
4. Less commonly: bottom three notes respelled, top note retained
5. Secondary dominants (V7/IV, V7/V) can also be reinterpreted
6. The respelling is never written -- analyst must visualize it
7. Each V7 implies a key (root = scale degree 5); each Ger+6 implies a key (root = b6)

# Construction / Recognition
Common respelling pattern:
- G7 = G-B-D-F (V7 in C): F respelled as E# = Ger+6 in B minor
- D7 = D-F#-A-C: C respelled as B# = Ger+6 in F# minor
- Ab7 = Ab-C-Eb-Gb: Gb respelled as F# = Ger+6 in... wait, check: Ab as b6, F# as #4 = key where 5 = G = C major/minor

Less common: respell bottom three notes, keep top note:
- G7 = G-B-D-F: G respelled as Ab, B as Cb, D as Ebb; F retained = Ger+6 in Db

# Context & Application
This enharmonic modulation type is the most common and appears throughout Classical and Romantic literature. The V7/Ger+6 duality also applies to secondary dominants, greatly expanding the modulatory possibilities. Composers exploit this to reach keys that would be impossible through diatonic pivot techniques. The analyst must develop the ability to mentally respell chords to recognize these modulations.

# Examples
- Tchaikovsky, Nutcracker, "Waltz of the Flowers" (1892): D7 spelled D-F#-A-B# (sounds like V7/IV in D major), resolves as Ger+6 in F# minor
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 5, II (1808): V7/IV in Ab major reinterpreted as Ger+6 in C major
- Les Miserables, "On My Own" (1980): Ger+6 in E minor spelled as V7 in F major, resolves to F major

# Common Errors
- Not recognizing that secondary dominants (V7/IV, V7/V) can also be reinterpreted
- Looking for the respelling to be written in the score

# Common Confusions
- **Same sound, different resolution**: V7 resolves down P5; Ger+6 resolves root down m2
- **Secondary dominants too**: V7/IV, V7/V, etc. are all subject to this reinterpretation
- **Visualize, don't expect to see**: The respelling is never written; you must envision it
