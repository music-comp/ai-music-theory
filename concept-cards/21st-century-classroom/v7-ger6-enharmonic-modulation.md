# Quick Definition
The dominant seventh chord and German augmented sixth chord are enharmonically equivalent, allowing V7 to be reinterpreted as Ger+6 (or vice versa) to modulate between distant keys.

# Formal Definition
In this type of enharmonic modulation, V7 is enharmonically reinterpreted as Ger+6, or Ger+6 is reinterpreted as V7. Secondary dominants (V7/IV, V7/V, etc.) can also be reinterpreted as Ger+6 chords. The two chords have the same sound but different spellings, reflecting different resolutions: V7 resolves down a fifth to its tonic, while Ger+6 resolves to a chord with its root a half step lower.

# Construction/Recognition
Enharmonic equivalence:
- V7: root-3rd-5th-7th (resolves down P5)
- Ger+6: flat-6-1-flat-3-sharp-4 (resolves down m2 to dominant function)

Most common respelling: the 7th of V7 = sharp-4 of Ger+6
- G7 = G-B-D-F (V7 in C)
- Respelled: G-B-D-E# = Ger+6 in B minor

Less common: respell bottom three notes, keep top note as common tone

# Musical Context
- The V7 normally wants to resolve down a fifth
- The Ger+6 wants to resolve down a half step (to i6/4 or V)
- The dual meaning creates the "harmonic pun"
- Enables modulation to keys a tritone or other distant intervals away

# Examples
- Tchaikovsky, Nutcracker, "Waltz of the Flowers" (1892):
  - D7 spelled D-F#-A-B# (not C)
  - Sounds like V7/IV in D major
  - Spelled and resolves as Ger+6 in F# minor

- Beethoven, Symphony No. 5, II (1808):
  - V7/IV in Ab major = Ger+6 in C major

- Les Miserables, "On My Own" (1980):
  - Ger+6 in E minor spelled as V7 in F major, resolves to F major

# Common Confusions
- The chord SOUNDS identical but RESOLVES differently
- The spelling on the page shows the intended resolution
- You must visualize the alternative spelling for analysis
- V7/IV and V7/V are also subject to this reinterpretation
