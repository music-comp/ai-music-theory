# Quick Definition
Tonicization means treating a chord other than I like a temporary tonic by approaching it with its own dominant, creating a momentary sense of a new key through chromaticism without actually modulating.

# Core Definition
Tonicization means treating a chord other than the I chord like a tonic by approaching it with its dominant. A secondary dominant is a major triad or dominant seventh chord that resolves to (or tonicizes) a chord other than the I chord. The chromaticism introduced by the secondary chord intensifies the drive to the target chord. Unlike modulation, tonicization is temporary -- the music returns to the original key without establishing a cadence in the "new" key. Diminished chords (vii° and ii° in minor) cannot be tonicized because a diminished triad cannot function as a convincing tonic.

# Prerequisites
- **Harmonic function**: Understanding tonic, dominant, pre-dominant roles
- **Authentic cadence**: The V-I resolution being replicated
- **Leading tone**: The chromatic note that drives resolution

# Key Properties
1. Temporarily treats a non-tonic chord as if it were tonic
2. Uses secondary dominants (V/x) or secondary diminished (viio/x) chords
3. The chromatic note acts as leading tone to the target chord's root
4. Brief duration -- can be as short as two chords
5. Does not require cadential confirmation in the "new" key
6. Diminished chords (viio, iio) cannot be tonicized

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify a chord with chromaticism that precedes a diatonic chord
- The chromatic chord should be major or dominant-seventh quality (for secondary dominant)
- The target chord receives its own "V" or "viio"
- The Roman numeral after the slash is the chord being tonicized
- Example: V/V means "the V of V" -- the dominant of the dominant

# Context & Application
Tonicization adds harmonic color and creates stronger motion to important chords without the commitment of a full modulation. It is common in both classical and popular music. Tonicizing V (using V/V) is the most frequent application. Tonicization can span from two chords to several measures, creating ambiguity with short modulations in fast harmonic rhythm (as in Bach chorales).

# Examples
- V/V to V -- tonicizing the dominant (most common)
- V7/ii to ii -- strengthening motion to the supertonic
- "Yesterday" (McCartney, 1965) -- V/V and other secondary dominants
- "Don't Know Why" (Harris, 2002) -- secondary dominants in pop context
- Verdi, Rigoletto, "La donna e mobile" (1851) -- classical tonicization

# Common Errors
- Attempting to tonicize diminished chords (viio, iio)
- Labeling a secondary dominant as a diatonic chord (e.g., calling V/V "II" instead)

# Common Confusions
- **Tonicization vs. modulation**: Tonicization is temporary; modulation requires cadential confirmation
- **Why not viio?**: Diminished triads cannot serve as convincing tonics
- **Duration ambiguity**: Extended tonicizations resemble short modulations
