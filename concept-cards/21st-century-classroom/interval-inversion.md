# Quick Definition
Interval inversion occurs when the lower note is moved up an octave (or the upper note down), making the two notes switch positions.

# Formal Definition
To invert an interval, move the lower note up an octave or the higher note down an octave so their positions reverse. The original and inverted intervals always sum to 9 for the numeric size and opposite in quality (major inverts to minor, augmented to diminished, perfect to perfect).

# Construction/Recognition
- Numeric sizes sum to 9: unison+8ve, 2nd+7th, 3rd+6th, 4th+5th
- Quality inversions: P↔P, M↔m, +↔o
- Major 3rd (M3) inverts to minor 6th (m6)
- Perfect 4th (P4) inverts to Perfect 5th (P5)
- Augmented 4th (+4) inverts to diminished 5th (o5)

# Musical Context
Interval inversion is useful for writing intervals below a given note. It also explains why certain interval pairs (like M3 and m6) share similar harmonic properties. Compound intervals can be reduced to simple intervals through inversion logic.

# Examples
- M6 (C to A) inverts to m3 (A to C): 6+3=9, M becomes m
- P5 (C to G) inverts to P4 (G to C): 5+4=9, P stays P
- +4 (C to F#) inverts to o5 (F# to C): 4+5=9, + becomes o
- m2 inverts to M7; M2 inverts to m7

# Common Confusions
- Numbers add to 9, not 8 (count the shared note twice)
- Perfect stays perfect when inverted
- Tritone (+4/o5) inverts to itself in size (6 half steps)
