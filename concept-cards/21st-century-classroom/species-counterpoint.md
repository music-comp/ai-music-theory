# Quick Definition
Species counterpoint is a pedagogical method from Fux's "Gradus ad Parnassum" (1725) that teaches contrapuntal composition by progressively adding rhythmic complexity against a given melody (cantus firmus).

# Formal Definition
Species counterpoint is a systematic approach to teaching counterpoint developed by Johann Joseph Fux in his treatise "Gradus ad Parnassum" (1725). The method uses simple rhythmic values and teaches composers to write one, two, three, or four notes against a longer note value (usually a whole note). Each "species" represents a different rhythmic relationship and introduces specific contrapuntal challenges. The method is based on the style of Palestrina (16th-century Renaissance polyphony).

# Rules/Procedures
1. **First Species**: Note against note (whole note vs. whole note)
2. **Second Species**: Two notes against one (half notes vs. whole note)
3. **Third Species**: Four notes against one (quarter notes vs. whole note)
4. **Fourth Species**: Syncopation and suspensions (tied half notes vs. whole note)
5. **Fifth Species (Florid)**: Free mixture of all species
6. Typically written in two voices initially, expanding to four voices in advanced study
7. The given melody is called the "cantus firmus" (c.f.)

# Musical Context
Species counterpoint prepares students for free composition by isolating specific contrapuntal problems. While the style is based on Renaissance practice, the skills transfer to Baroque, Classical, and later styles. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier demonstrates advanced contrapuntal mastery.

# Examples
- Bach, Well-Tempered Clavier, Book I, Fugue 3 in C-sharp major demonstrates advanced counterpoint
- Orlande de Lassus, "Expectatio Justorum" shows florid (fifth species) counterpoint
- Palestrina's masses represent the stylistic model

# Related Concepts
- Cantus Firmus

# Common Confusions
- Thinking species counterpoint rules are arbitrary (they derive from Renaissance practice)
- Confusing species counterpoint with Baroque counterpoint (different stylistic conventions)
- Expecting species counterpoint to sound like Bach (it's more Palestrina-like)
- Not understanding that each species isolates specific problems
