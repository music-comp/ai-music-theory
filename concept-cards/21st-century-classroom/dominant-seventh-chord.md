# Quick Definition
A dominant seventh chord consists of a major triad with a minor seventh above the root, creating the characteristic tension that resolves to tonic.

# Formal Definition
A dominant seventh chord (also called major-minor seventh) is built with a major triad (M3 + m3) plus a minor seventh interval from root to seventh. The intervals stacked are: M3 (root to 3rd), m3 (3rd to 5th), m3 (5th to 7th). It contains a tritone between the third and seventh, creating its characteristic tension.

# Construction/Recognition
- Major triad + minor 7th from root
- Intervals: M3 + m3 + m3
- Total: 10 half steps from root to 7th
- Contains a tritone (between 3rd and 7th)
- Lead-sheet: C7, G7, D7

# Musical Context
The dominant seventh chord is the most important seventh chord in tonal music. It occurs naturally on ^5 (V7) and creates strong pull toward resolution to I. The tritone between ^7 and ^4 resolves to ^1 and ^3 of the tonic chord.

# Examples
- G7: G-B-D-F (in C major: V7)
- C7: C-E-G-Bb
- D7: D-F#-A-C (in G major: V7)
- The tritone (B-F in G7) resolves to C-E in C major

# Common Confusions
- "Dominant seventh" refers to quality, not necessarily V chord position
- C7 is NOT the same as Cmaj7 (C7 has Bb; Cmaj7 has B)
- Any chord with major triad + minor 7th is a "dominant-type" seventh chord
