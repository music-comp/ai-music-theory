# Quick Definition
When adding non-chord tones to voice leading, care must be taken to avoid creating parallel fifths and octaves, which can occur when embellishments move by step into notes that form perfect intervals.

# Formal Definition
Non-chord tones (passing tones, neighbor tones, suspensions, etc.) are common in chorale-style writing and add melodic interest to individual voices. However, adding non-chord tones to an otherwise correct voice leading can inadvertently create objectionable parallels. The embellishments themselves can form parallel 5ths or 8ves with other voices if not carefully placed.

# Rules/Procedures
1. First complete the basic voice leading without non-chord tones
2. Identify all intervals of 5ths and 8ves between voices
3. Avoid adding non-chord tones that move by step INTO notes separated by a 5th or 8ve
4. When adding non-chord tones in two voices simultaneously, use parallel 3rds or 6ths
5. If simultaneous non-chord tones cannot form consonant intervals, stagger them rhythmically
6. Neighbor tones, passing tones, and suspensions all can create parallels if placed carelessly

# Musical Context
J.S. Bach's 371 Chorales frequently use non-chord tones while maintaining proper voice leading. The embellished textures add melodic interest without compromising contrapuntal integrity.

# Examples
- J.S. Bach, Chorale 323, "Wie schon leuchtet der Morgenstern": Rich with non-chord tones
- J.S. Bach, Chorale 326, "Allein Gott in der Hoh' sei Ehr": Demonstrates NCT usage
- Parallel 5ths created by NCTs: If soprano and alto form a 5th on beat 2, a passing tone in the alto stepping to that note from a 4th creates parallel 5ths

# Common Confusions
- Thinking that because the framework is correct, any embellishments are safe
- Not checking for parallels created between the NCT and other voices
- Adding simultaneous NCTs in parallel 5ths or 8ves
- Forgetting that the NCT itself participates in the voice leading
