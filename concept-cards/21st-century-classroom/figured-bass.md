# Quick Definition
A Baroque-era shorthand notation consisting of a bass line on a staff with numbers below it representing intervals to be played above the bass note within the key signature -- the historical predecessor to modern lead-sheet symbols.

# Core Definition
Figured bass (also known as thoroughbass) was developed in the Baroque era (roughly 1600--1750 C.E.) as a shorthand for writing chords. It consists of a bass line notated on a staff accompanied by numbers representing intervals to be played above the bass note within the key signature. These figured-bass numbers are traditionally notated below the bass line. Like lead-sheet symbols, figured bass allowed a keyboardist or guitarist freedom in choosing chord voicings. In the present day, figured bass is taught in music theory courses primarily as a shorthand for chord inversion symbols, placed after Roman numerals.

# Prerequisites
- **Intervals**: Understanding numeric intervals above a bass note
- **Triads**: The chord structures being represented
- **Key signatures**: Figured bass assumes notes within the key signature unless marked otherwise

# Key Properties
1. Numbers represent intervals above the bass note
2. All notes are assumed to be within the key signature unless chromatically altered
3. Numbers are traditionally placed below the bass line
4. In modern theory, figured bass symbols are placed after Roman numerals
5. Performers had freedom to choose specific voicings
6. Original manuscripts required "realization" (writing out the full chords)

# Construction / Recognition
- Read the bass note on the staff
- Interpret each number as an interval above that bass note
- Assume all pitches fall within the prevailing key signature
- Look for chromatic alteration symbols (sharps, flats, slashes) for notes outside the key
- In modern Roman numeral analysis, figures appear after the Roman numeral (e.g., V6/5)

# Context & Application
Figured bass was standard practice in Baroque music by composers like J.S. Bach and Handel. Original scores show just the bass line with figures, requiring the keyboardist to "realize" the accompaniment in real time. Modern editions of Baroque compositions typically write out full realizations. Today, figured bass notation survives primarily as the shorthand for chord inversions used in Roman numeral analysis throughout music theory courses.

# Examples
- Handel, Recorder Sonata in A minor, HWV 362, I. Larghetto -- shows figured bass in original manuscript and in realized version
- J.S. Bach, Chorale 175, "Jesus, meine Zuversicht" -- analysis using figured bass inversion symbols

# Common Errors
- Interpreting figured bass numbers as chord tones rather than intervals above the bass
- Forgetting that notes are assumed within the key signature unless marked otherwise

# Common Confusions
- **Figured bass vs. Roman numerals**: Figured bass is intervals above bass; Roman numerals identify root and function
- **Historical vs. modern usage**: In Baroque practice, figures appeared below the bass line; in modern theory, they appear after Roman numerals
- **Realization**: Original figured bass required the performer to improvise voicings; modern editions write everything out
