# Quick Definition
The I-vi-IV-V progression, also called the "'50s progression" or "'50s doo-wop progression," follows the Tonic-Tonic Prolongation-PreDominant-Dominant functional pattern with a characteristic descending-thirds bass line.

# Formal Definition
The I-vi-IV-V progression realizes the functional sequence Tonic-Tonic Prolongation-PreDominant-Dominant. It can also be rotated to appear as I-vi-ii-V (with ii/3rd creating the same bass line) or as IV-V-I-vi. The bass line characteristically descends in thirds (in C: C-A-F-G).

# Analysis/Application
When analyzing, look for:
1. The characteristic descending-thirds bass line followed by ascending step
2. The progression may appear in any rotation
3. Half-note or whole-note harmonic rhythm is common
4. Often loops continuously

# Musical Context
Extremely prevalent in 1950s rock 'n' roll and doo-wop, though the progression was also widely used in the 1930s and '40s. It remains common in contemporary pop music.

# Examples
- Hoagy Carmichael and Frank Loesser, "Heart and Soul" (in C major)
- "Earth Angel" (1954, in A-flat major)
- Dolly Parton, "I Will Always Love You" (1974, in A major)
- The Ramones, "Rock 'n' Roll High School" (1978, in C major)
- John Legend, "All of Me" (2013, in A-flat major) - whole-note harmonic rhythm
- MAGIC!, "Rude" (2014) - rotated to IV-V-I-vi

# Common Confusions
- Different rotations are the same progression starting on different chords
- Compare I-vi-IV-V (50s) with I-V-vi-IV (best-seller) - different orders
- I-vi-ii/3rd-V has the same bass line but uses ii instead of IV
