# Quick Definition
A sequence is a musical idea repeated at a different pitch level; a harmonic sequence involves both the bass line and the chords above following a repeated pattern at successive pitch levels.

# Core Definition
A sequence is a musical idea repeated at a different pitch level (Definition 9.1.11). Sequences can be short or long. In a harmonic sequence, the bass line acts as a "melody" that is sequenced, and the harmonies follow the same pattern at the new pitch level. The circle of fifths progression is a sequence. Harmonic sequences create a sense of inevitability and can explain chord progressions that would otherwise seem to violate the harmonic flowchart.

# Prerequisites
- **Roman numeral analysis**: Ability to identify chords and root motion

# Key Properties
1. A musical idea repeated at a different pitch level
2. Bass line follows a sequential pattern
3. Harmonies above follow the same pattern transposed
4. Can be short (two-note units) or long (four-bar phrases)
5. Creates predictability and inevitability
6. Explains exceptions to the harmonic flowchart

# Construction / Recognition
1. Look for repeated patterns in the bass line at different pitch levels
2. Check if the harmonic pattern repeats at each new transposition level
3. Sequences often involve descending step or descending fifth motion
4. Unusual progressions that seem to violate normal function may be explained by sequential patterns

# Context & Application
Harmonic sequences appear throughout Western music from the Baroque era to contemporary pop. They can create dramatic builds, provide structural predictability, and justify otherwise unusual chord progressions. When you encounter unusual harmonic progressions, look for sequential patterns as an explanation.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 5, Op. 67, I -- short sequential motive
- Mozart, Symphony No. 40, I -- four-bar ideas sequenced down a step
- "Love You Like A Love Song" -- bass line as a two-note descending sequence
- "Flashdance... What A Feeling" (1983) -- sequence between first two and last two bars explains exceptions
- "Hotel California" (1977) -- unusual progressions explained by harmonic sequences

# Common Errors
- Failing to recognize sequential patterns when analyzing unusual progressions
- Confusing exact transposition with diatonic sequence (most tonal sequences are diatonic)

# Common Confusions
- **Sequences explain exceptions**: When progressions seem to violate the harmonic flowchart, check for sequential patterns
- **Roots may be sequential even when chords are inverted**: The underlying root pattern follows the sequence
- **Melodic vs. harmonic sequence**: A melodic sequence involves only the melody; a harmonic sequence involves both melody/bass and the harmonies
