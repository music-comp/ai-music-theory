# Quick Definition
Spread voicings are five-note jazz chord voicings with root, 3rd, and 7th as the lowest notes, with upper extensions voiced above, often in fourths or fifths.

# Core Definition
Spread voicings have 5-note chords with the root, 3rd, and 7th as the lowest notes, with other chord members voiced above, often in intervals of a fourth or fifth. The root is always the lowest note, while the 3rd can be voiced above or below the 7th. For sus chords (no 3rd), voice the sus4 like the 3rd; for 6th chords (no 7th), voice the 6th like the 7th. The 5th may be omitted to maintain five parts (Hutchinson, Ch. 31, p. 454).

# Prerequisites
- **Guide tones** -- Spread voicings are built around the 3rd and 7th
- **Jazz ninth chords** -- Upper extensions fill out the voicing

# Key Properties
1. Five-note chords
2. Root always lowest
3. 3rd and 7th next (R-3-7 or R-7-3)
4. Upper extensions voiced above in 4ths or 5ths
5. 5th may be omitted
6. Sus chords: R-4-7; 6th chords: R-3-6

# Construction / Recognition
1. Place root as bass note
2. Voice 3rd and 7th next (either order)
3. Add two upper extension notes above
4. Upper voices often span 4ths or 5ths

# Context & Application
Spread voicings create the characteristic open jazz piano sound, suitable for big band and orchestral settings. The wide spacing allows each voice to be heard clearly.

# Examples
- Dm9 spread: D-F-C-E-A (p. 454)
- G9(13) spread: G-F-B-D-E
- CΔ9 spread: C-E-B-D-G
- Blues progression in spread voicings (pp. 456)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Not keeping the root as the lowest note
  **Correction**: Root is always the bass in spread voicings

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing spread voicings with classical "open position"
  **Clarification**: Spread voicings have a specific R-3-7 or R-7-3 lowest-voice structure
