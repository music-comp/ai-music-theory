# Quick Definition
Augmented sixth chords are a special class of chromatic pre-dominant chords containing b6 and #4, which approach the dominant (scale degree 5) from a half step above and below simultaneously -- identified by the presence of a diminished third when stacked in thirds.

# Core Definition
Augmented sixth chords are a special class of pre-dominant chords with notes that approach the dominant (5) from a half-step below (#4) and from a half-step above (b6) simultaneously. The interval between b6 and #4 is an augmented sixth, which resolves outward to an octave on scale degree 5. In minor keys, b6 does not require an accidental because 6 is already lowered in the minor key signature. There are three general types: Italian (It+6), French (Fr+6), and German (Ger+6), plus the Enharmonic German (EnGer+6). The geographic labels have no known historical reasoning.

- **Chromatic pre-dominants**: The functional category

# Key Properties
1. All types contain b6 and #4
2. The augmented sixth interval (b6 to #4) resolves outward to an octave on 5
3. Pre-dominant function: intensify the drive to V
4. When stacked in thirds, all types contain a diminished third
5. The diminished third is the key identifier
6. Three main types plus the Enharmonic German variant
7. Geographic labels (Italian, French, German) are historically arbitrary

# Construction / Recognition
- **Identification principle**: When you encounter a chord with a diminished third (stacked in thirds), it is an augmented sixth chord
- All types stacked in thirds contain the diminished third interval
- Determine the type by checking which additional scale degrees are present:
  - It+6: b6, 1, #4 (three notes)
  - Fr+6: b6, 1, 2, #4 (four notes)
  - Ger+6: b6, 1, b3, #4 (four notes)
  - EnGer+6: b6, 1, #2, #4 (four notes, major keys only)

# Context & Application
Augmented sixth chords are among the most powerful chromatic pre-dominants. The outward resolution of the augmented sixth to an octave on scale degree 5 creates an intensely directed approach to the dominant. They appear frequently in Classical, Romantic, and film music, and are also common in jazz and popular music (where they are typically spelled as dominant seventh chords). Understanding both classical and lead-sheet analysis of these chords is essential for cross-genre work.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 5, Op. 67, I (1808) -- It+6
- Schubert, Winterreise, "Der Wegweiser" (1823) -- Fr+6
- Rossini, William Tell Overture (1829) -- Ger+6
- John Coltrane, "Mr. P.C." (1959) -- Ger+6 as VI7

# Common Errors
- Failing to check for the diminished third when stacking in thirds
- Confusing Ger+6 with a dominant seventh chord

# Common Confusions
- **Diminished third = augmented sixth chord**: This is the most reliable identification method
- **Sound vs. function**: Ger+6 sounds like a dominant seventh but functions as pre-dominant
- **Geographic labels**: Italian, French, German have no historical reasoning -- just tradition
