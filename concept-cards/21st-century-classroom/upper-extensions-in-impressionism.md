# Quick Definition
Impressionist composers used chords with 9ths, 11ths, and 13ths as coloristic sonorities, often with chromatic alterations, anticipating jazz harmony.

# Formal Definition
Upper extensions in Impressionist music refer to the addition of 9ths, 11ths, and 13ths to triads and seventh chords, sometimes with chromatic alterations (b9, #9, #11, b13). Unlike functional harmony where these tones might resolve, Impressionist composers often treated extended chords as stable, coloristic sonorities valued for their sound quality rather than their harmonic function.

# Construction/Calculation
1. Begin with a triad or seventh chord
2. Add the 9th (major or chromatically altered)
3. Add the 11th (perfect or raised to #11)
4. Add the 13th (major or chromatically altered)
5. Alterations create additional color: b9, #9, #11
6. The chord may move without traditional resolution

# Musical Context
Debussy and Ravel pioneered the use of extended chords as primary harmonic materials, not just passing sonorities. This approach directly influenced jazz harmony. The rich sonorities of 9th, 11th, and 13th chords became signatures of the Impressionist sound, providing harmonic density without chromatic complexity.

# Examples
- Debussy, "Clair de lune" bar 15: Ebm9 chord with 9th (F) in the melody
- Debussy, "La Puerta del Vino" bar 8: Db7 with alternating #9 and b9
- Debussy, "La Puerta del Vino" bar 70: Includes #11 along with #9 and b9
- Extended chords used for color, not necessarily for function

# Common Confusions
- Upper extensions in Impressionism are often static/coloristic, not functional
- Alterations (b9, #9, #11) add color without implying resolution
- This practice directly influenced and anticipated jazz harmony
- Multiple alterations can occur simultaneously (e.g., #9 and b9 together)
