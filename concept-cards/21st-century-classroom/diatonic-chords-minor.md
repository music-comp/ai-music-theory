# Quick Definition
Minor keys have more diatonic chord possibilities due to three scale forms (13 possible triads), but the most commonly used are i-iio-III-iv-V-VI-viio (or VII).

# Core Definition
Because there are three versions of the minor scale, there are more than seven diatonic chords in minor. The sixth and seventh scale degrees affect all of the triads except the tonic, making 13 possible diatonic triads in minor. However, when one analyzes a large amount of tonal music, the most commonly used Roman numerals in minor are: i (minor), iio (diminished), III (major), iv (minor), V (major), VI (major), and either viio (diminished, from raised ^7) or VII (major, from natural ^7). Both VII (the "subtonic triad") and viio (the "leading-tone triad") are included.

# Prerequisites
- **Roman numeral analysis**: The notation system
- **Natural minor scale**: Source of some diatonic chords

# Key Properties
1. i = minor (^1); iio = diminished (^2); III = major (^3)
2. iv = minor (^4); V = major (^5, requires raised ^7)
3. VI = major (^6); viio = diminished (raised ^7) OR VII = major (natural ^7)
4. 13 possible triads due to variable ^6 and ^7
5. V chord requires raised ^7 from harmonic/melodic minor
6. Both VII (subtonic) and viio (leading-tone) commonly used
7. The subtonic triad (VII) is built on lowered ^7 (natural minor)

# Construction / Recognition
- i, iio, iv, VI come from natural minor
- V and viio require raised ^7 (harmonic minor)
- III uses natural ^7; III+ would use raised ^7
- The subtonic triad (VII) requires no accidentals in minor keys

# Context & Application
The V chord in minor requires raising ^7 (from harmonic/melodic minor) to create a major dominant with leading tone. The VII (subtonic) chord is common in rock and pop progressions. Compositions freely mix chords from all three minor scale forms. The subtonic triad regularly occurs in circle-of-fifths progressions in minor and in rock and pop music. The leading-tone triad (viio) is usually either a passing harmony or has dominant function.

# Examples
- A minor: Am-Bo-C-Dm-E-F-G#o (or G major = VII)
- E minor: Em-F#o-G-Am-B-C-D#o (or D major = VII)
- VII-i in rock: G-Am in A minor
- V-i (authentic cadence in minor): E-Am in A minor

# Common Errors
- Forgetting that V is MAJOR in minor keys (requires raised ^7)
- Using only natural minor chords when harmonic minor chords are needed for cadences

# Common Confusions
- **Both VII and viio exist**: VII = major triad on natural ^7 (subtonic); viio = diminished triad on raised ^7 (leading-tone)
- **V is MAJOR in minor**: The dominant chord in minor requires raised ^7 from harmonic minor
- **More options than major**: Minor keys have 13 possible diatonic triads vs. major's fixed 7
