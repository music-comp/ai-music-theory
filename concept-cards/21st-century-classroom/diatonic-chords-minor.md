# Quick Definition
Minor keys have more diatonic chord possibilities due to three minor scale forms, with the most common being i-iio-III-iv-V-VI-viio (or VII).

# Formal Definition
Because minor keys use three scale forms (natural, harmonic, melodic), there are more than seven possible diatonic triads. The variable ^6 and ^7 scale degrees affect triads built on ^3, ^5, ^6, and ^7. The most commonly used chords in minor are: i (minor), iio (diminished), III (major), iv (minor), V (major), VI (major), and either viio (diminished) or VII (major).

# Construction/Recognition
- i = minor (on ^1)
- iio = diminished (on ^2)
- III = major (on ^3, natural minor)
- iv = minor (on ^4)
- V = major (on ^5, requires raised ^7)
- VI = major (on ^6, natural minor)
- viio = diminished (on raised ^7) OR VII = major (on lowered ^7)

# Musical Context
The V chord in minor requires raising ^7 (from harmonic/melodic minor) to create a major dominant with leading tone. The VII (subtonic) chord is common in rock and pop. Compositions freely mix chords from all three minor scales.

# Examples
- A minor: Am-Bo-C-Dm-E-F-G#o (or G)
- E minor: Em-F#o-G-Am-B-C-D#o (or D)
- The raised ^7 is needed for V and viio
- VII-i is common in rock (G-Am in A minor)

# Related Concepts
- Diatonic chords in major
- Three minor scales
- Subtonic triad (VII)
- Leading-tone triad (viio)
- Raised ^7
- Mode mixture

# Common Confusions
- Both viio AND VII exist in minor (leading-tone vs. subtonic)
- V is MAJOR in minor keys (raised ^7)
- More chord options exist than in major keys
