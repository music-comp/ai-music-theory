# Quick Definition
Scale degree names are specific terms for each note of a scale: tonic, supertonic, mediant, subdominant, dominant, submediant, leading tone (or subtonic).

# Formal Definition
Scale degrees are numbered ^1 through ^7 and each has a specific name that describes its function or position within the scale. These names apply to both major and minor keys, with ^7 being called "leading tone" when a half step below tonic or "subtonic" when a whole step below.

# Construction/Recognition
- ^1: Tonic - the home note, tonal center
- ^2: Supertonic - one step above tonic
- ^3: Mediant - midway between tonic and dominant
- ^4: Subdominant - a fifth below tonic (or fourth above)
- ^5: Dominant - the most important note after tonic
- ^6: Submediant - midway between subdominant and tonic (below)
- ^7: Leading tone (half step below tonic) or Subtonic (whole step below)

# Musical Context
Scale degree names are used throughout music theory to refer to scale degrees, chords built on those degrees, and keys related to those degrees. Understanding these names is essential for harmonic analysis.

# Examples
- In C major: C=tonic, D=supertonic, E=mediant, F=subdominant, G=dominant, A=submediant, B=leading tone
- In C natural minor: Bb is the subtonic (whole step below C)
- In C harmonic minor: B is the leading tone (half step below C)

# Common Confusions
- Subtonic vs. leading tone: subtonic is a whole step below tonic; leading tone is a half step below
- "Sub-" prefix means "below" (subdominant = fifth below, submediant = third below mediant)
- Mediant and submediant are named for their position relative to tonic and dominant
