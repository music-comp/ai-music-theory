# Quick Definition
A deceptive cadence is a phrase ending where V resolves to vi (or another non-tonic chord) instead of the expected I.

# Formal Definition
A deceptive cadence (DC) occurs when the dominant chord (V) resolves to a chord other than the expected tonic, most commonly the submediant (vi in major, VI in minor). The term "deceptive" reflects that the listener expects V to resolve to I but is "deceived" by the unexpected resolution. More broadly, V to "not-I" constitutes a deceptive cadence.

# Construction/Recognition
- V - vi (most common form)
- V resolves to something other than I
- Creates surprise and prolongs the phrase
- The leading tone often still resolves to ^1
- May also go to IV/3rd, IV, or other chords

# Musical Context
Deceptive cadences create surprise and extend phrases by delaying the expected tonic arrival. They are used for dramatic effect and to keep music moving. The V-vi progression in the Tristan Prelude is one of the most famous deceptive cadences in classical music.

# Examples
- G-Am in C major = V-vi (deceptive cadence)
- Mozart's "Voi che sapete" features a deceptive cadence
- Wagner's Tristan Prelude has a famous deceptive resolution
- Mozart's Ave Verum: V to IV/3rd (deceptive, non-standard)

# Common Confusions
- V-vi is the most common but V to any "not-I" is deceptive
- The deception is harmonic, not melodic (^7 can still go to ^1)
- A deceptive cadence delays, not avoids, eventual tonic arrival
