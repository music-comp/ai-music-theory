# Quick Definition
Secondary dominants do not always resolve to their expected target chord; they sometimes resolve "deceptively" to other chords, particularly in popular music.

# Formal Definition
The roots of secondary dominants do not always resolve down a perfect fifth to the tonicized chord. Secondary dominants can resolve deceptively, with V/V to IV and V/vi to IV being particularly common irregular resolutions in popular music.

# Analysis/Application
Common irregular resolutions:
- V/V to IV (IV substitutes for V in popular music)
- V/V to bVII (bVII substitutes for V in rock)
- V/vi to IV (can be seen as V/vi to VI/vi, deceptive in vi area)
- V7/IV to V7/ii (chromatic bass line continues)

# Musical Context
Irregular resolutions are especially common in popular music, where IV can substitute for V. Despite not resolving as expected, secondary dominants still add chromatic color and intensified motion.

# Examples
- "Yesterday" (McCartney) - V/V resolves to IV
- "Forget You" (Cee Lo Green) - V7/V to IV
- "I Want You to Want Me" (Cheap Trick) - V/V to bVII
- "Baby Love" (Supremes) - V4/2/IV to V7/ii
- "(Sittin' On) The Dock of the Bay" - V7/vi to IV
- "Imagine" (Lennon) - V7/vi to IV
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata Op. 53, I - V/vi to IV

# Common Confusions
- Irregular resolution doesn't mean the analysis is wrong
- The secondary dominant still tonicizes the expected chord, it just doesn't arrive there
- V/vi to IV can be understood as a deceptive motion within the vi area
