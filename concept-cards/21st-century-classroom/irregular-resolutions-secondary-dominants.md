# Quick Definition
Secondary dominants do not always resolve to their expected target chord; common deceptive resolutions include V/V to IV, V/V to bVII, and V/vi to IV, particularly in popular music where IV and bVII can substitute for V.

# Core Definition
The roots of secondary dominants do not always resolve down a perfect fifth to the tonicized chord. In popular music especially, secondary dominants frequently resolve deceptively. The most common irregular resolutions include V/V resolving to IV (where IV substitutes for V), V/V resolving to bVII (where bVII substitutes for V in rock), and V7/vi resolving to IV (interpretable as a deceptive motion within the submediant area: V/vi to VI/vi).

# Prerequisites
- **Secondary dominant**: The chords that are resolving irregularly
- **Deceptive cadence**: The concept of V resolving somewhere other than I

# Key Properties
1. V/V to IV: IV substitutes for V in popular music
2. V/V to bVII: bVII substitutes for V in rock music
3. V7/vi to IV: can be understood as V/vi to VI/vi (deceptive within vi area)
4. V4/2/IV to V7/ii: chromatic bass line continues (lowered chromatic resolves down)
5. Irregular resolution does not invalidate the secondary dominant analysis
6. The secondary dominant still creates chromatic intensification

# Construction / Recognition
- A secondary dominant followed by a chord other than its expected target
- Look for functional substitutes: IV for V, bVII for V
- V7/vi to IV: iii to IV is a normal diatonic progression; V/vi to IV is its chromatic variant
- The lowered chromatic in V7/IV tends to resolve downward regardless of the target chord

# Context & Application
Irregular resolutions are especially common in popular music, where IV frequently substitutes for V as a harmonic goal. In classical music, deceptive resolutions of secondary dominants also occur but are less systematic. Understanding irregular resolutions helps analysts avoid forcing regular resolution labels onto progressions that clearly move elsewhere. Secondary chords still add chromatic color and intensified motion even when they resolve irregularly.

# Examples
- "Yesterday" (McCartney, 1965) -- V/V resolves to IV
- "Forget You" (CeeLo Green/Mars, 2010) -- V7/V to IV
- "I Want You to Want Me" (Cheap Trick, 1977) -- V/V to bVII
- "Baby Love" (Supremes, 1964) -- V4/2/IV to V7/ii (chromatic bass continues)
- "(Sittin' On) The Dock of the Bay" (Redding/Cropper, 1967) -- V7/vi to IV
- "Imagine" (Lennon, 1971) -- V7/vi to IV
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata Op. 53, I (1804) -- V/vi to IV

# Common Errors
- Assuming all secondary dominants must resolve to their target
- Failing to label a chord as a secondary dominant because it resolves irregularly

# Common Confusions
- **Still a secondary dominant**: Irregular resolution does not change the analysis of the chromatic chord itself
- **V/vi to IV**: Can be heard as iii to IV (diatonic) chromaticized, or as V/vi to VI/vi (deceptive within vi area)
- **Popular music context**: IV and bVII regularly substitute for V, making these resolutions idiomatic, not exceptional
