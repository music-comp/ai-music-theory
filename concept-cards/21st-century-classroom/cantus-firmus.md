# Quick Definition
The cantus firmus (c.f.) is a pre-existing melody in whole notes against which counterpoint is written in species counterpoint exercises.

# Formal Definition
The cantus firmus (Latin for "fixed song") is a given melody used as the foundation for species counterpoint exercises. It consists of whole notes, typically 8-12 notes long, and remains unchanged while the student writes counterpoint against it. The cantus firmus may be placed in either the upper or lower voice. Historically, cantus firmi were drawn from liturgical chant, but in pedagogical contexts, they are often newly composed melodies following specific guidelines.

# Rules/Procedures
1. The cantus firmus is provided; the student writes counterpoint against it
2. C.F. is always in whole notes (breves in older notation)
3. May be placed above or below the counterpoint being written
4. C.F. typically:
   - Moves primarily by step
   - Begins and ends on the final (tonic) of the mode
   - Has a clear high point and low point
   - Avoids large leaps and awkward intervals
5. The c.f. determines the mode (Dorian, Phrygian, etc.)
6. Rules for counterpoint depend on whether c.f. is above or below

# Musical Context
Species counterpoint derived from Renaissance practice where composers added voices to existing chant melodies. The term "cantus firmus" originally referred to these chant melodies. In Fux's pedagogical method, simple melodies serve the same structural purpose.

# Examples
- A typical c.f. in D Dorian: D-E-F-G-A-G-F-E-D
- The c.f. remains unchanged throughout the exercise
- Different species create different rhythmic relationships to the c.f.

# Common Confusions
- Trying to alter the cantus firmus (it is fixed)
- Forgetting that rules change depending on whether c.f. is above or below
- Not identifying the mode of the c.f. (affects leading tone treatment)
- Confusing cantus firmus with fugue subject (different contexts)
