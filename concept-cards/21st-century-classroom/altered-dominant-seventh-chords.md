# Quick Definition
Altered dominant seventh chords have either or both the 5th and 9th chromatically raised or lowered, creating tension that intensifies the resolution to the tonic.

# Formal Definition
A dominant seventh chord is considered "altered" when the 5th or 9th (or both) are chromatically modified. The root, 3rd, and 7th remain unchanged. Possible alterations include: #5, b5, #9, b9, or combinations thereof. The chord symbol "G7alt" indicates an altered dominant but is vague about which specific alterations are used.

# Construction/Calculation
1. Start with a dominant 7th chord (root, major 3rd, perfect 5th, minor 7th)
2. Raise or lower the 5th: #5 or b5 (also written +5 or -5)
3. Raise or lower the 9th: #9 or b9 (also written +9 or -9)
4. Both 5th and 9th may be altered simultaneously
5. Enharmonic respelling is common for readability (e.g., #9 spelled as b10)
6. The #9 often appears as both major and minor 3rd in the written chord (e.g., C-E-G-Bb-Eb)

# Musical Context
Altered dominants are typically used when the chord has dominant function (root resolving down by fifth). They create maximum tension before resolution. Unaltered dominants are often used when they have tonic function, such as the first chord in a blues. The alterations provide chromatic voice leading into the resolution chord.

# Examples
- C7(#5) = C-E-G#-Bb
- C7(b9) = C-E-G-Bb-Db
- C7(#9) = C-E-G-Bb-D# (often spelled C-E-G-Bb-Eb for ease of reading)
- C7(#9/#5) = C-E-G#-Bb-D# (both alterations)
- C7alt = unspecified alterations to 5th and/or 9th

# Common Confusions
- The #9 spelled as b10 looks like the chord has both major and minor 3rds, but it's actually the #9
- b5 and #11 can refer to the same pitch, but #11 implies the natural 5th is also present
- b13 is enharmonically the same as #5, but b13 implies the unaltered 5th is present
- "Alt" is vague - in analysis, specify the exact alterations when possible
