# Quick Definition
Major and minor intervals are 2nds, 3rds, 6ths, and 7ths -- intervals that can be major or minor (but never perfect); minor intervals are one half step smaller than their major counterparts.

# Core Definition
Intervals of the second, third, sixth, and seventh can be major or minor but never perfect. All the notes above the tonic in a major scale are either perfect or major. If the upper note is not in the major scale but is a half step smaller than the major interval, it is a minor interval. Minor intervals are one half step smaller than major intervals. These intervals can also be augmented or diminished.

# Key Properties
1. m2 = 1 half step; M2 = 2 half steps
2. m3 = 3 half steps; M3 = 4 half steps
3. m6 = 8 half steps; M6 = 9 half steps
4. m7 = 10 half steps; M7 = 11 half steps
5. Minor = one half step smaller than major (same numeric size)
6. All notes in major scale above tonic produce major (or perfect) intervals
7. Can never be perfect in quality

# Construction / Recognition
- Method 1: Think of lower note as tonic of a major scale; if upper note is in that scale, the interval is major; if one half step lower, it is minor
- Method 2: Count half steps and compare to the table
- If the lower note has a sharp/flat, cover it up, determine the interval, then factor it back in

# Context & Application
Major and minor thirds are essential for determining chord quality (major vs. minor triad). Major and minor seconds define scale step types. Sixths and sevenths are important for chord extensions, inversions, and voice leading.

# Examples
- C to D = M2 (2 half steps); C to Db = m2 (1 half step)
- C to E = M3 (4 half steps); C to Eb = m3 (3 half steps)
- C to A = M6 (9 half steps); C to Ab = m6 (8 half steps)
- C to B = M7 (11 half steps); C to Bb = m7 (10 half steps)

# Common Errors
- Calling a 3rd or 7th "perfect" (wrong quality family)
- Confusing M3 (4 half steps) with m3 (3 half steps) when building chords

# Common Confusions
- **Never perfect**: Major/minor intervals cannot be perfect in quality
- **Minor = one half step smaller**: Minor is always exactly one half step smaller than the corresponding major interval
- **"Major" is not "bigger"**: Major describes quality, not relative size compared to other interval numbers
