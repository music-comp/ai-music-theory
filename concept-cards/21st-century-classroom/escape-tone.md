# Quick Definition
An escape tone is a non-chord tone approached by step and left by leap in the opposite direction, creating surprise by leaping away from the expected stepwise resolution.

# Core Definition
An escape tone is approached by step and left by leap in the opposite direction. It "escapes" from the expected stepwise resolution by leaping away. The step and leap must be in opposite directions. Escape tones are less common than passing tones and neighbor tones.

# Prerequisites
- **Non-chord tones overview**: Understanding the classification system

# Key Properties
1. Approached by step, left by leap in the opposite direction
2. Step and leap are in opposite directions
3. Creates a sense of surprise by leaping away
4. Less common than passing tones and neighbor tones
5. Often found at phrase endings or in sequential passages

# Construction / Recognition
1. Look for a non-chord tone approached by step
2. Confirm it leaves by leap in the opposite direction
3. The escape tone moves away from the expected resolution
4. Label as ET or E.T.

# Context & Application
Escape tones create surprise by leaping away from an expected stepwise resolution. They often occur at phrase endings or in sequential passages. A memorable example is the end of the "Star-Spangled Banner."

# Examples
- "Star-Spangled Banner" -- the word "of" in "home of the brave" is a classic escape tone
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C Major, Hob. XVI:35, III (1780) -- escape tones in melodic sequence
- Paul McCartney, "Silly Love Songs" (1976) -- escape tone in popular music

# Common Errors
- Confusing with appoggiatura (escape = step then leap; appoggiatura = leap then step)
- Forgetting that the step and leap must be in opposite directions

# Common Confusions
- **Inverse of appoggiatura**: Escape tone = step then leap; appoggiatura = leap then step
- **Opposite directions required**: The step and leap must go in opposite directions
- **Star-Spangled Banner mnemonic**: "Home OF the brave" -- "of" is the escape tone
