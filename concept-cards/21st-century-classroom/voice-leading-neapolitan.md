# Quick Definition
The Neapolitan chord (N6 or flat-II6) has two voice leading rules: double the bass (the 3rd of the chord), and resolve flat-2 down to the nearest note in the following chord.

# Formal Definition
The Neapolitan chord is a major triad built on the lowered second scale degree, almost always found in first inversion (N6). Its unique characteristics require specific voice leading:
1. **Double the bass**: Unlike typical first-inversion chords where you do NOT double the bass, the Neapolitan doubles the bass (which is the 3rd of the chord, scale degree 4)
2. **Resolve flat-2 down**: The root of the chord (flat-2) is a chromatic tendency tone that must resolve downward to the nearest note in the next chord

# Rules/Procedures
1. **Doubling**: Always double the bass (scale degree 4, the 3rd of the N6 chord)
2. **Flat-2 resolution**: Resolve flat-2 (the root of the chord) DOWN by step
   - To V or V7: flat-2 typically moves to the leading tone
   - To i-6/4 (cadential): flat-2 moves to scale degree 1
3. Voice lead remaining notes smoothly to the next chord
4. Avoid creating augmented second (flat-2 to leading tone) in same voice if possible; cross-voice resolution is preferred

# Musical Context
The Neapolitan chord typically functions as a chromatic pre-dominant, usually resolving to V or to a cadential 6/4 that then resolves to V. It creates a distinctive dark, dramatic color common in minor-key music.

# Examples
- In C minor: N6 = Db/F chord (Db-F-Ab with F in bass)
- N6 to V: Db resolves down to B (leading tone); F doubled
- N6 to i-6/4 to V: Db resolves to C; then C to B

# Common Confusions
- Applying the normal first-inversion rule (don't double the bass) - Neapolitan is an exception
- Resolving flat-2 upward instead of downward
- Forgetting that N6 is almost always in first inversion
- Creating an augmented second melodically from flat-2 to leading tone
