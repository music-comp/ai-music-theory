# Quick Definition
A real answer transposes the subject exactly to the dominant key; a tonal answer alters certain intervals (typically 1-5 becomes 5-1) to smooth the tonic-dominant relationship.

# Formal Definition
When the second voice enters in a fugue with the answer, the composer must decide between two types:

**Real Answer**: The subject is transposed exactly to the dominant key. All intervals are preserved. This creates a true transposition where each note moves up a perfect fifth (or down a perfect fourth).

**Tonal Answer**: Certain intervals are altered, typically at the beginning of the subject. When the subject prominently features scale degrees 1-5, the answer responds with 5-1 (and vice versa for 5-1 becoming 1-5). This adjustment prevents jarring tonic-supertonic relationships and maintains better tonal balance.

# Rules/Procedures
1. **Use real answer when**:
   - Subject does not begin with prominent 1-5 or 5-1 motion
   - Exact transposition creates no tonal problems
2. **Use tonal answer when**:
   - Subject begins with motion from 1 to 5: Answer with 5 to 1
   - Subject begins with motion from 5 to 1: Answer with 1 to 5
   - This is sometimes called "mutation" of the subject
3. The interval change is typically limited to the first few notes
4. After the tonal adjustment, the answer usually continues with exact intervals
5. Identify real vs. tonal by comparing intervals in subject and answer

# Musical Context
The tonal answer evolved as a convention to handle the tonal implications of subject entries in tonic and dominant. If a subject leaps 1-5 (C to G in C major), an exact transposition would create G to D (5 to 2), which emphasizes the supertonic awkwardly. The tonal answer (G to C) corrects this.

# Examples
- Bach, Fugue 2 in C minor: Subject note 3 to note 4 descends a P4; answer descends a P5 (TONAL)
- Real answer example: Subject C-D-E-F-G becomes G-A-B-C-D (all intervals exact)
- Tonal answer: Subject C-G... becomes G-C... (P5 becomes P4)

# Common Confusions
- Thinking tonal answers are "wrong" or mistakes
- Not recognizing the subtle interval changes in a tonal answer
- Expecting the entire answer to be altered (usually only first few notes)
- Confusing the melodic adjustment with rhythmic changes
