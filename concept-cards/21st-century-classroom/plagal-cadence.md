# Quick Definition
A plagal cadence (PC) is a phrase ending with IV resolving to I, commonly called the "Amen" cadence for its traditional use at the end of hymns.

# Core Definition
A plagal cadence (PC) occurs when a phrase ends with the chords IV-I. It creates a sense of arrival but is generally less conclusive than an authentic cadence. The plagal cadence is often called the "Amen" cadence due to its traditional use at the end of hymns. In minor: iv-i is plagal (minor subdominant to minor tonic).

# Prerequisites
- **Cadence**: The general concept of harmonic arrival

# Key Properties
1. IV-I at a phrase ending
2. Less conclusive than authentic cadence (V-I)
3. Commonly called the "Amen" cadence
4. Common in hymns, gospel, rock, and pop styles
5. Often used as a "tag" or extension after an authentic cadence
6. In minor: iv-i

# Construction / Recognition
- Last two chords of phrase: IV followed by I
- Subdominant to tonic motion
- No leading tone involved (unlike authentic cadence)

# Context & Application
Plagal cadences create a softer, more settled sense of arrival than authentic cadences. They are common in gospel, rock, pop, and hymn styles. "Take Me to Church" (Hozier-Byrne) features plagal cadences. "Just Give Me a Reason" (Pink, Bhasker, and Ruess) uses IV-I motion.

# Examples
- "Amen" sung at the end of hymns: F-C in C major (IV-I)
- "Take Me to Church" (Hozier-Byrne) features plagal cadences
- "Just Give Me a Reason" (Pink, Bhasker, Ruess) uses IV-I motion
- F-C in C major = IV-I = plagal cadence
- In minor: Dm-Am in A minor = iv-i

# Common Errors
- Confusing IV-I (plagal) with V-I (authentic)
- Assuming plagal cadences cannot end pieces (they can, just less commonly than AC)

# Common Confusions
- **IV-I is plagal; V-I is authentic**: Do not confuse the two
- **Less common as a final cadence**: Plagal cadences end pieces less often than authentic cadences
- **In minor**: iv-i is plagal (minor subdominant to minor tonic)
