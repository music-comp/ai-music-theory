# Quick Definition
A plagal cadence is a phrase ending with IV resolving to I, commonly known as the "Amen" cadence.

# Formal Definition
A plagal cadence (PC) occurs when a phrase ends with the subdominant chord (IV) resolving to the tonic chord (I). It creates a sense of arrival but is generally less conclusive than an authentic cadence. The plagal cadence is often called the "Amen" cadence due to its traditional use at the end of hymns.

# Construction/Recognition
- IV - I at phrase end
- Also called "Amen" cadence
- Less conclusive than authentic cadence
- Common in hymns and popular music
- Often follows an authentic cadence as a "tag"

# Musical Context
Plagal cadences create a softer, more settled sense of arrival than authentic cadences. They are common in gospel, rock, pop, and hymn styles. Often used as a "tag" or extension after an authentic cadence has established the tonic.

# Examples
- "Amen" sung at the end of hymns: F-C in C major (IV-I)
- "Take Me to Church" (Hozier) features plagal cadences
- "Just Give Me a Reason" (Pink) uses IV-I motion
- F-C in C major = plagal cadence

# Related Concepts
- Authentic cadence
- Half cadence
- Deceptive cadence
- Subdominant chord
- Tonic chord
- "Amen" cadence

# Common Confusions
- IV-I is plagal; V-I is authentic (don't confuse)
- Plagal can end pieces but is less common than authentic
- In minor: iv-i is plagal (minor subdominant to minor tonic)
