# Quick Definition
A suspension is an accented non-chord tone prepared by the same note (held from the previous chord), occurring on a downbeat, and resolving downward by step. It consists of three parts: preparation, suspension, and resolution.

# Core Definition
Suspensions are accented non-chord tones occurring on downbeats. A suspension is approached by the same note (preparation) and resolves down by step (resolution). A suspension consists of three parts: preparation, suspension, and resolution. The preparation may be tied to the suspension. Suspensions are classified by numbers (9-8, 7-6, 4-3, 2-3, and sometimes 6-5) that specify the interval distance of the suspended note and its resolution to the bass note.

# Key Properties
1. Approached by same note (preparation), resolves down by step
2. Accented: occurs on the downbeat
3. Three parts: preparation, suspension, resolution
4. Preparation may be tied to the suspension
5. Classified by interval numbers: 9-8, 7-6, 4-3, 2-3, 6-5
6. The 2-3 is a "bass suspension" measured against an upper voice
7. Reduce compound intervals: 11-10 becomes 4-3
8. Inverted chords may create non-standard numbers (5-4, 3-2)
9. Decorations may occur before the resolution

# Construction / Recognition
1. Look for a note held from the previous beat that creates dissonance on the downbeat
2. Confirm it resolves downward by step
3. Identify the three parts: preparation, suspension, resolution
4. Classify by the interval numbers (suspension interval - resolution interval to bass)
5. Reduce compound intervals to standard numbers

# Context & Application
Suspensions create expressive tension on strong beats, resolving to consonance. They are especially common at cadences in classical music and create the characteristic sound of Baroque counterpoint. Classical composers like Mozart often used retardations and suspensions at cadences in slow movements.

# Examples
- Samuel Barber, Adagio for Strings (1936) -- 4-3 suspension
- Handel, Suite No. 2 in D Major, HWV 349: II. Alla Hornpipe (1717) -- 7-6 and 9-8 suspensions
- J.S. Bach, Chorale 238, "Liebster Jesu, wir sind hier" -- 2-3 (bass) suspension
- J.S. Bach, French Suite No. 1 in D Minor, BWV 812, Sarabande (1722) -- decorated suspension

# Common Errors
- Forgetting to reduce compound intervals (11-10 becomes 4-3)
- Confusing the 2-3 bass suspension with an upper-voice suspension

# Common Confusions
- **2-3 is a "bass suspension"**: Measured against an upper voice, not the bass
- **Compound intervals**: 11-10 becomes 4-3; always reduce to standard numbers
- **Non-standard numbers**: Inverted chords may produce 5-4 or 3-2 suspensions
- **Decorated suspensions**: Other notes may intervene before the resolution
