# Quick Definition
A suspension is an accented non-chord tone that is prepared by the same note, held over the bar line or beat, and resolves downward by step.

# Formal Definition
Suspensions are accented non-chord tones occurring on downbeats. A suspension is approached by the same note (preparation) and resolves down by step (resolution). Suspensions consist of three parts: preparation, suspension, and resolution. The preparation may be tied to the suspension.

# Analysis/Application
Suspensions are classified by numbers indicating the interval from the suspension to its resolution measured against the bass:
- 9-8 suspension: ninth resolving to octave
- 7-6 suspension: seventh resolving to sixth
- 4-3 suspension: fourth resolving to third
- 2-3 suspension: bass suspension (measured against upper voice)
- 6-5 suspension: sixth resolving to fifth (less common)

# Musical Context
Suspensions create expressive tension on strong beats, resolving to consonance. They are especially common at cadences in classical music and create the characteristic sound of Baroque counterpoint.

# Examples
- Samuel Barber, Adagio for Strings (1936) - 4-3 suspension
- Handel, Suite No. 2 in D Major, HWV 349: II. Alla Hornpipe (1717) - 7-6 and 9-8 suspensions
- J.S. Bach, Chorale 238, "Liebster Jesu, wir sind hier" - 2-3 (bass) suspension
- J.S. Bach, French Suite No. 1 in D Minor, BWV 812, Sarabande (1722) - decorated suspension

# Common Confusions
- Reduce compound intervals: 11-10 becomes 4-3, etc.
- The 2-3 is a "bass suspension" measured against an upper voice
- Inverted chords may create non-standard numbers like 5-4 or 3-2
- Decorations can occur before the resolution
