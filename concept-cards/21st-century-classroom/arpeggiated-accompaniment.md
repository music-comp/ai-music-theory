# Quick Definition
Arpeggiated accompaniment breaks chords into individual notes played in succession rather than simultaneously, creating continuous rhythmic motion while expressing the underlying harmony.

# Formal Definition
Arpeggiated accompaniments express chords rhythmically through arpeggios - chord tones played in succession rather than simultaneously. Arpeggios can ascend, descend, or alternate direction. A bass line often accompanies the arpeggios, frequently forming a tenth (octave plus third) with the top note.

# Analysis/Application
Common arpeggio patterns:
1. Ascending arpeggios
2. Descending arpeggios
3. Ascending then descending (or vice versa)
4. Alberti bass pattern (low-high-middle-high)

# Musical Context
Arpeggiated accompaniment is common across classical and popular music, from Beethoven's Moonlight Sonata to contemporary pop ballads. It creates flowing, continuous motion while maintaining harmonic clarity.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Moonlight Sonata, Op. 27, No. 2, I (1802) - triplet arpeggios with bass octaves
- Alicia Keys, "If I Ain't Got You" (2004) - descending arpeggios
- "Colour My World" (Chicago, 1970) - arpeggios ascending and descending
- "Someone Like You" (Adele, 2011) - arpeggiated piano accompaniment
- "No Scrubs" (TLC, 1999) - sixteenth-note arpeggios

# Common Confusions
- Arpeggios can include non-chord tones as passing tones
- The bass line is often separate from the arpeggios
- Alberti bass is a specific type of arpeggiated pattern
