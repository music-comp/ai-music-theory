# Quick Definition
Sonata principle states that material first presented in a secondary key must be restated in the tonic key to achieve tonal resolution, treating the secondary key as a "large-scale dissonance."

# Formal Definition
As discussed by Charles Rosen in Sonata Forms, sonata principle treats the dominant key as a "dissonant tonality" in the exposition. The material played outside the tonic is dissonant with respect to the center of stability. This polarization creates a "dissonant section" that requires resolution: a simple reintroduction of the tonic key will not suffice; the entire section outside the tonic needs to be resolved as a whole by being restated in tonic.

# Construction/Recognition
The principle in action:
- Exposition: Secondary theme in V (or III) = "dissonant section"
- Recapitulation: Secondary theme in I = resolution of dissonance

This applies beyond sonata form:
- In rondo: B section first in V, later in I
- In any form with secondary key material

The key insight: Tonal design is as important as thematic design.

# Musical Context
- Fundamental to understanding Classical form
- Explains why recapitulation must alter the transition
- The secondary key creates tension; tonic resolution provides release
- Haydn's monothematic sonatas show tonal design's primacy

# Examples
- Beethoven, Pathetique Sonata, Op. 13, III (rondo):
  - B section first in Eb major (III)
  - B section later restated in C (major tonic)
  - Demonstrates sonata principle in rondo form

# Common Confusions
- Sonata principle applies to TONAL relationships, not just themes
- The "dissonance" is large-scale (not chord-level)
- A simple return to tonic is NOT sufficient resolution
- The entire secondary section must return in tonic
