# Quick Definition
Balanced binary form features a first section ending in a new key, with the second section ending with essentially the same cadential material, now transposed to the original key.

# Formal Definition
According to Douglass Green in Form in Tonal Music, balanced binary form features a binary form with a first section (A) ending in a new key, and the second section ending with essentially the same cadence, now transposed to the original key. Because the first section ends in a new key, balanced binary is inherently a continuous binary form.

# Construction/Recognition
Characteristics:
- First section: ends in NEW key (usually V or III)
- Second section: ends with SAME cadential material, transposed to tonic
- The similar endings create "balance" between sections
- Always continuous (A section modulates)

Diagram:
| First Section | Second Section |
| Begins in I, ends in V (or III) | Returns to I, ends with similar material in I |

# Musical Context
- Common in Baroque era dance movements
- Found in suites by Bach, Handel, and others
- The balanced endings provide satisfying symmetry
- Often two-reprise (both sections repeat)

# Examples
- J.S. Bach, Invention No. 8 in F major, BWV 779
- J.S. Bach, French Suite No. 6 in E major, BWV 817, Courante:
  - Bars 15-16: ending in B major (dominant)
  - Bars 31-32: similar ending, now in E major (tonic)

# Common Confusions
- Balanced binary is always CONTINUOUS (first section modulates)
- The "balance" refers to similar ENDINGS, not similar beginnings
- Different from rounded binary (which has similar BEGINNINGS in sections)
- The transposition of cadential material is the key feature
