# Quick Definition
The melodic minor scale raises both ^6 and ^7 when ascending to avoid the augmented second, and descends using the natural minor scale.

# Formal Definition
The melodic minor scale has two forms: ascending and descending. The ascending form raises both ^6 and ^7 from natural minor, eliminating the augmented second of harmonic minor while retaining the leading tone. The descending form is identical to natural minor, with lowered ^6 and ^7.

# Construction/Recognition
- Ascending: W-H-W-W-W-W-H (raised ^6 and ^7)
- Descending: same as natural minor (lowered ^7 and ^6)
- Ascending upper tetrachord matches major scale
- Provides smooth melodic motion to and from the tonic
- Both raised notes require accidentals in notation

# Musical Context
The melodic minor scale was developed to create smoother melodic lines when approaching the tonic from below, avoiding the awkward augmented second of harmonic minor. In jazz, the ascending form is often used in both directions.

# Examples
- A melodic minor ascending: A-B-C-D-E-F#-G#-A
- A melodic minor descending: A-G-F-E-D-C-B-A (natural minor)
- C melodic minor ascending: C-D-Eb-F-G-A-B-C
- Jazz melodic minor uses the ascending form in both directions

# Common Confusions
- Classical melodic minor differs ascending and descending; jazz uses ascending both ways
- The raised ^6 and ^7 require accidentals (not in key signature)
- Compositions freely mix all three minor scale forms
