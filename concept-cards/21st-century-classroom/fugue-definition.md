# Quick Definition
A fugue is a contrapuntal composition featuring sections called expositions (containing subject statements) and episodes (developing material through fragmentation and sequence).

# Core Definition
A fugue is a contrapuntal composition whose form features sections called expositions and episodes. In the first exposition, each voice enters in succession with the fugue subject or its answer. Later expositions contain at least one full statement of the subject in a different key. Episodes do not contain complete subject statements but instead develop the subject or other prominent ideas through fragmentation and sequencing. Some theorists reserve "exposition" for the first exposition only and use "middle entry" for later subject groups (Hutchinson, Ch. 30, p. 433).

# Prerequisites
- **Species counterpoint** -- Fugue requires mastery of contrapuntal technique
- **Harmonic function** -- Fugue involves tonic-dominant key relationships and modulation

# Key Properties
1. Form consists of alternating expositions and episodes
2. First exposition: all voices state the subject in turn
3. Later expositions: at least one complete subject statement, possibly in different keys
4. Episodes: develop material through fragmentation and sequence, no complete subject
5. Voices alternate between subject, answer, and countersubject roles
6. Typically three or four voices, though any number is possible

# Construction / Recognition
**Analyzing a fugue:**
1. Identify the subject (first melodic idea, stated by the first voice)
2. Find the answer (subject in dominant key, second voice)
3. Determine if the answer is real or tonal
4. Identify the countersubject (if consistent accompanying material exists)
5. Map expositions (sections with complete subject statements) and episodes
6. Note key areas for each exposition
7. Identify motivic fragmentation in episodes (subject head, tail, countersubject fragments)

# Context & Application
The fugue represents the culmination of Baroque contrapuntal technique. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier contains 48 fugues (two in each major and minor key) that serve as primary models. Fugue technique appears throughout Western art music from the Baroque through the 20th century, including works by Mozart, Beethoven, Brahms, and Shostakovich.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Confusing the first exposition with later expositions in analysis
  **Correction**: The first exposition has all voices entering with subject/answer; later expositions may have fewer entries

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking fugue is a strict form with fixed sections
  **Clarification**: Fugue is flexible; the number of expositions, episodes, and their arrangement varies
- **Confusion**: Thinking all imitative counterpoint is fugue
  **Clarification**: Fugue has specific structural requirements (expositions with subjects, episodes with development)
