# Quick Definition
A fugue is a contrapuntal composition featuring sections called expositions (containing subject statements) and episodes (developing material through fragmentation and sequence).

# Formal Definition
A fugue is a highly organized contrapuntal composition whose form consists of alternating expositions and episodes. In the first exposition, each voice enters in turn with the fugue subject (or its answer). Later expositions feature statements of the subject in different keys. Episodes develop material through motivic fragmentation and sequential treatment. Fugues may be written for any number of voices, though three and four voices are most common.

# Rules/Procedures
1. **First exposition**: All voices enter with the subject (or answer), one at a time
2. **Later expositions**: Contain at least one full subject statement
3. **Episodes**: Develop subject material without complete subject statements
4. **Alternation**: Expositions and episodes typically alternate throughout
5. **Key scheme**: First exposition in tonic; later expositions in related keys
6. **Voices**: Each voice should have clear independence

# Musical Context
The fugue represents the culmination of Baroque contrapuntal technique. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier contains 48 fugues (two in each major and minor key) that serve as the primary models. Fugue technique appears throughout Western art music from the Baroque through the 20th century.

# Examples
- Bach, Fugue 2 in C minor, WTC Book I: Three-voice fugue with multiple expositions and episodes
- Bach, Fugue 3 in C-sharp major, WTC Book I: Advanced three-voice fugue
- Bach, Mass in B minor, Kyrie eleison II: Four-voice fugue

# Common Confusions
- Thinking fugue is a strict form with fixed sections (it's more flexible)
- Confusing the first exposition with later expositions
- Not recognizing episodes as developmental sections
- Thinking all imitative counterpoint is fugue
