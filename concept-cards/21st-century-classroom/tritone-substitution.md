# Quick Definition
Tritone substitution replaces a dominant seventh chord with another dominant seventh chord whose root is a tritone away, exploiting their shared guide tones.

# Formal Definition
A tritone substitution (also called "tritone sub" or "flat-II substitution") replaces a dominant seventh chord with the dominant seventh chord built a tritone (augmented 4th/diminished 5th) away. This works because the two chords share the same guide tones (3rd and 7th), which are simply exchanged. The bII7 chord can substitute for V7 because they have identical guide tones and both resolve effectively to I.

# Construction/Calculation
1. Identify a dominant 7th chord (e.g., G7)
2. Find the root a tritone away (G to Db = tritone)
3. Build a dominant 7th on that root (Db7)
4. The guide tones swap positions: G7 (B-F) and Db7 (F-Cb/B)
5. Both resolve to C: G7-C and Db7-C

Result: bII7 can substitute for V7

# Musical Context
Tritone substitution is fundamental to bebop and modern jazz harmony. It creates chromatic bass motion (e.g., Dm7-Db7-C instead of Dm7-G7-C), adding sophistication to standard progressions. The substitution is particularly effective with altered dominants, as Db9(13) shares voicings with G7(#9/#5). It appears throughout jazz standards and is essential for reharmonization.

# Examples
- G7 can be replaced by Db7 (both resolve to C)
- V7 replaced by bII7
- ii-V-I becomes ii-bII-I: Dm7 - Db7 - CΔ7
- Turnaround with subs: E7 - Eb7 - D7 - Db7
- The voicings for G7(#9/#5) and Db9(13) are identical (not counting roots)

# Common Confusions
- Only works for dominant 7th chords (or chords functioning as dominants)
- The sub chord must also be dominant 7th quality
- bII7 and V7 share guide tones - they don't share all notes
- Creates chromatic bass motion which is a key stylistic feature
