# Quick Definition
A secondary dominant is a major triad or dominant seventh chord that resolves to (tonicizes) a chord other than the I chord, temporarily treating that chord as a tonic.

# Formal Definition
A secondary dominant is a major triad or dominant seventh chord that resolves to (or tonicizes) a chord other than the I chord. In the Roman numeral label V/x or V7/x, the numeral after the slash is the chord being tonicized by the V chord before the slash. Secondary dominants introduce chromatic notes that intensify the drive to the target chord.

# Analysis/Application
To identify secondary dominants:
1. Stack the chord in thirds to find root and quality
2. If major or major-minor seventh, check if root is P5 above a diatonic chord
3. The chord a P5 below is the chord being tonicized
4. Label as V/x or V7/x where x is the target chord

To write secondary dominants:
1. Identify the target chord (after the slash)
2. Build V7 in the key of that chord
3. Apply the specified inversion

# Musical Context
Secondary dominants are common in both classical and popular music. They add chromatic color and intensify harmonic motion without leaving the key permanently.

# Examples
- "Yesterday" (Paul McCartney, 1965) - V/V
- "Don't Know Why" (2002) - secondary dominants
- "Saturday in the Park" (Chicago, 1972) - multiple secondary dominants
- Verdi, Rigoletto, "La donna e mobile" - V/V in classical context
- Tchaikovsky, The Nutcracker, "Miniature Overture"

# Common Confusions
- V/x is a chromatic chord, not a diatonic chord with a different name
- D7 in C major is V7/V, not II7 (which would be minor)
- Diminished chords (vii° and ii° in minor) cannot be tonicized
