# Quick Definition
A major triad or dominant seventh chord that resolves to (tonicizes) a chord other than I, labeled V/x or V7/x where x is the chord being tonicized -- the most common type of chromatic chord in tonal music.

# Core Definition
A secondary dominant is a major triad or dominant seventh chord that resolves to (or tonicizes) a chord other than the I chord. In the Roman numeral label V/x or V7/x, the numeral after the slash is the chord being tonicized by the V chord before the slash. The chromaticism in secondary dominants intensifies the drive to the target chord. While one might label D7 in C major as "II7," this fails to communicate the chord's function: to progress to a G major chord (V). The proper label V7/V shows both the chord's quality and its functional direction.

# Prerequisites
- **Tonicization**: The concept of temporarily treating a non-tonic chord as tonic

# Key Properties
1. Quality is always major triad or major-minor seventh (dominant seventh)
2. Roman numeral label: V/x or V7/x
3. The numeral after the slash identifies the target chord
4. Raised chromatic notes generally act as leading tones to target root
5. The only secondary dominant with a lowered chromatic is V7/IV
6. Common in both classical and popular music

# Construction / Recognition
- A chromatic major triad or Mm7 chord that is not diatonic
- Its root is a P5 above a diatonic chord root
- The chromatic notes function as leading tones to the target
- In V7/IV, the lowered note acts as scale degree 4 of the target key
- Diminished chords cannot be the target (viio, iio in minor)

# Context & Application
Secondary dominants are the most common chromatic chords in tonal music, found extensively in classical, popular, jazz, and film music. They add chromatic color and intensify harmonic motion without leaving the key. V/V is the most frequently encountered secondary dominant. The roots of secondary dominants do not always resolve as expected; irregular (deceptive) resolutions are common, especially in popular music.

# Examples
- Paul McCartney, "Yesterday" (1965) -- V/V resolving deceptively to IV
- Jesse Harris, "Don't Know Why" (2002) -- secondary dominants in pop
- Rick Nielson, "I Want You to Want Me" (1977) -- V/V to bVII
- Robert William Lamm, "Saturday in the Park" (1972)
- Verdi, Rigoletto, "La donna e mobile" (1851) -- classical V/V
- Tchaikovsky, The Nutcracker, "Miniature Overture" (1892)

# Common Errors
- Labeling D7 in C major as "II7" instead of V7/V
- Attempting to tonicize viio or iio with a secondary dominant

# Common Confusions
- **V/x is chromatic**: D7 in C major is not diatonic II7; the uppercase Roman numeral signals chromaticism
- **Function over root**: The label V/V communicates function (dominant of dominant), not just root identity
- **Diminished targets**: viio and iio cannot be tonicized because diminished triads are unstable as "tonics"
