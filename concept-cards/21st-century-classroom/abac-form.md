# Quick Definition
ABAC form is a 32-bar song structure where the A section returns after the B section but is followed by a new C section instead of another A, creating a different ending.

# Core Definition
Many popular songs from the 1930s-1950s were in 32-bar ABAC form. Like AABA, each section is typically 8 bars. The A section appears twice, but instead of ending with another A as in AABA, the form concludes with a C section that provides a different ending. The form may also occur as ABAB', where B' begins like B but ends differently. Songs in ABAC form may also be preceded by a verse.

# Prerequisites
- **AABA form**: Understanding of the related standard form

# Key Properties
1. Typically 32 bars: 8+8+8+8
2. A section returns after B
3. C section provides a different ending than B
4. May also appear as ABAB' (B' begins like B, ends differently)
5. Each section typically 8 bars
6. May be preceded by a verse

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the returning A section after B
2. Note that the final section (C) differs from B
3. Check if C is truly new or a variant of B (ABAB')
4. Listen for a verse preceding the refrain

# Context & Application
ABAC was common in the golden age of American popular song (1930s-1950s). It offers slightly more variety than AABA while maintaining the memorable return of the A melody.

# Examples
- "White Christmas" (Irving Berlin)
- "Someday My Prince Will Come"
- "When I Fall In Love"
- Hoagy Carmichael, "Star Dust" (1927) -- verse precedes the ABAC refrain

# Common Errors
- Confusing ABAC with AABA (check whether the final section is A or C)

# Common Confusions
- **ABAC vs. ABAB'**: ABAB' begins the last section like B but ends differently; truly separate C material makes it ABAC
- **Verse before refrain**: "Star Dust" has a 16-bar verse before the 32-bar ABAC refrain
