# Quick Definition
The cadential formula -- pre-dominant followed by dominant followed by tonic (e.g., ii-V7-I) -- is the sequence that establishes a new key in modulation, stronger than a simple V-I authentic cadence, termed a "full cadence" by Douglass Green.

# Core Definition
A modulation to a new key requires an eventual cadence to confirm that new key. This cadence often has a cadential formula: pre-dominant to dominant to tonic. In his book Form in Tonal Music, Douglass Green defines a V-I authentic cadence with a pre-dominant prefix as a "full cadence." The cadential formula establishes a key more strongly than a simple V-I authentic cadence because the pre-dominant function chord (ii, IV, N6, +6, etc.) provides additional confirmation of the new tonal center before the dominant resolves.

# Prerequisites
- **Cadences**: Understanding of authentic cadences
- **Harmonic function**: Pre-dominant, dominant, tonic categories

# Key Properties
1. Pattern: pre-dominant - dominant - tonic (in new key)
2. Example: ii - V7 - I or ii6 - I6/4 - V7 - I
3. Stronger than simple V-I for establishing a key
4. Douglass Green's term: "full cadence"
5. Pre-dominant can be diatonic (ii, IV) or chromatic (N6, +6, V/V)
6. The pre-dominant prefix is what distinguishes this from a simple authentic cadence

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for a pre-dominant chord (ii, IV, vi, or chromatic pre-dominant) preceding V-I in the new key
- The pattern provides three pieces of evidence for the new key (pre-dominant, dominant, tonic)
- Simple V-I provides only two pieces of evidence
- Ambiguity increases when only V-I is present (could be tonicization)
- The full formula: pre-dominant + cadential I6/4 (optional) + V(7) + I

# Context & Application
The cadential formula is the gold standard for confirming a modulation. When this pattern appears in a new key, the analyst can be confident a modulation has occurred. When only V-I appears, there is greater ambiguity between modulation and tonicization. This is why the distinction between tonicization and short modulation is particularly difficult in Bach chorales, where fast harmonic rhythm often provides only V-I cadences.

# Examples
- J.S. Bach, English Suite No. 4 in F Major, Sarabande -- cadential formula establishing new key
- Standard pattern: ii6 - I6/4 - V7 - I in new key

# Common Errors
- Accepting any V-I as proof of modulation (look for the pre-dominant prefix)
- Not recognizing chromatic pre-dominants (N6, +6) as part of the formula

# Common Confusions
- **Stronger than V-I**: The pre-dominant prefix provides additional tonal confirmation
- **Chromatic pre-dominants count**: N6-V-I or Ger+6-I6/4-V-I also qualify
- **Green's term**: "Full cadence" = pre-dominant + authentic cadence
