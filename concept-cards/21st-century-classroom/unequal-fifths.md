# Quick Definition
Unequal fifths occur when parallel fifths involve one perfect fifth and one diminished fifth; these are considered less objectionable than parallel perfect fifths and may be acceptable in certain progressions.

# Formal Definition
Unequal fifths refer to a situation where two voices move from a perfect fifth to a diminished fifth (or vice versa) in parallel motion. Unlike parallel perfect fifths, which are always forbidden, unequal fifths are sometimes deemed acceptable because the change in quality (perfect to diminished or diminished to perfect) weakens the parallelism. The most common acceptable occurrence is in the I-V4/3-I6 progression.

# Rules/Procedures
1. Parallel perfect 5ths to perfect 5ths: Always forbidden
2. Perfect 5th to diminished 5th (or reverse) in parallel motion: May be acceptable
3. Most commonly acceptable in the I-V4/3-I6 progression
4. The unequal quality provides enough differentiation to preserve voice independence
5. Still should be used cautiously and only in established progressions

# Musical Context
Mozart uses the I-V4/3-I6 progression (which contains unequal fifths) at the end of the theme in Piano Sonata K. 331, I. The 7th of the V4/3 chord resolves upward in this progression, which is an exception to the normal downward resolution of sevenths.

# Examples
- In C major: I-V4/3-I6 creates parallel fifths C-G to B-F# (if in G major context), but the diminished quality of the second 5th makes it acceptable
- Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 331, I: Theme ending demonstrates this progression

# Common Confusions
- Thinking all parallel fifths are equally forbidden (unequal fifths are less objectionable)
- Using unequal fifths as a blanket excuse for poor voice leading (only acceptable in specific contexts)
- Confusing unequal fifths with hidden/direct fifths
- Not recognizing when a fifth is diminished vs. perfect
