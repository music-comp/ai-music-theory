# Quick Definition

Unequal fifths occur when parallel fifths involve one perfect fifth and one diminished fifth; these are considered less objectionable than parallel perfect fifths and may be acceptable in specific progressions like I-V4/3-I6.

# Core Definition

Unequal fifths refer to parallel fifths where one fifth is perfect and the other is diminished. These were deemed less objectionable because the change in quality weakens the parallelism. The most common acceptable occurrence is in the I-V4/3-I6 progression, where the 7th of V4/3 resolves upward (an exception to normal downward resolution), creating unequal fifths (Hutchinson, Ch. 27, p. 404).

# Prerequisites

- **Objectionable parallels** -- Understanding why parallel perfect fifths are forbidden

# Key Properties

1. One perfect fifth + one diminished fifth in parallel motion
2. Less objectionable than parallel perfect fifths
3. Most commonly accepted in I-V4/3-I6 progression
4. The quality change (P5 to d5 or vice versa) weakens the parallelism
5. Should still be used cautiously

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify:
1. Find parallel fifths between two voices
2. Check the quality of both fifths
3. If one is perfect and one is diminished: unequal fifths (may be acceptable)
4. If both are perfect: objectionable parallels (not acceptable)

# Context & Application

- **Typical contexts**: I-V4/3-I6 progression; viio7 resolutions
- **Historical/stylistic notes**: Mozart uses the I-V4/3-I6 progression in Piano Sonata K. 331, I

# Examples

**Example 2**: viio7/V to V -- unequal fifths may be preferred over doubling the leading tone

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using unequal fifths as a blanket excuse for poor voice leading
  **Correction**: Unequal fifths are only acceptable in established progressions, not universally

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: All parallel fifths are equally forbidden
  **Clarification**: Unequal fifths (one P5, one d5) are less objectionable than parallel perfect fifths
