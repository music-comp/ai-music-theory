# Quick Definition

A form is sectional when the first section (A) ends on the tonic chord, and continuous when the first section does not end on tonic -- either modulating to a new key or ending on a half cadence.

# Core Definition

The descriptor "sectional" applies to binary or ternary forms when the first section (the A section) ends on the tonic. The term "continuous" is used when the first section does NOT end on the tonic chord. While this often means the first section ends in a new key, it can also mean the first section ends on the dominant chord in a half cadence (Hutchinson, Ch. 24, p. 344).

# Prerequisites

- **Cadence** -- The type of cadence at the end of the A section determines sectional vs. continuous
- **Modulation** -- Continuous forms often modulate; understanding key change is essential
- **Half cadence** -- One way a continuous form can end its first section without modulating

# Key Properties

1. Sectional: A section ends with an authentic cadence in the tonic key
2. Continuous: A section does NOT end on the tonic chord
3. Continuous can mean modulation to a new key (often V or III)
4. Continuous can also mean ending with a half cadence in the original key
5. The descriptor precedes "binary" or "ternary" in the form name
6. Applies to both binary and ternary forms

# Construction / Recognition

## To Determine Sectional vs. Continuous:
1. Identify the end of the first section (A section)
2. Analyze the final cadence of that section
3. If the cadence is an authentic cadence in the tonic key: SECTIONAL
4. If the cadence ends in a new key or on a half cadence: CONTINUOUS

## Naming Convention:
- Place the descriptor before "binary" or "ternary"
- Examples: "sectional binary," "continuous binary," "sectional ternary," "continuous ternary"

# Context & Application

- **Typical contexts**: Applied to all binary and ternary forms across all style periods
- **Common applications**: Formal analysis, form labeling
- **Historical/stylistic notes**: Baroque binary forms are often continuous (modulate to V or III); sectional forms more common in shorter, simpler pieces

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 344-345): Brahms, "Wiegenlied" (Lullaby), Op. 49, No. 4 (1868) -- sectional form; A section ends on tonic

**Example 2** (p. 345-346): Haydn, Symphony No. 94 in G major, Hob. I:94, II (1791) -- continuous form; A section does not end on tonic

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Placing "sectional" or "continuous" after "binary" or "ternary"
  **Correction**: The descriptor comes BEFORE: "sectional binary" not "binary sectional"

- **Error**: Assuming continuous always means modulation
  **Correction**: A half cadence in the original key also qualifies as continuous

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking "continuous" means "without repeats"
  **Clarification**: "Continuous" refers only to the harmonic ending of the first section, not to repeat signs

- **Confusion**: Believing the distinction applies only to binary forms
  **Clarification**: Both binary AND ternary forms can be sectional or continuous
