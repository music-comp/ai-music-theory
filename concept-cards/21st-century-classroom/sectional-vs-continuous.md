# Quick Definition
A form is sectional when the first section (A) ends on the tonic chord, and continuous when the first section does not end on tonic (ending in a new key or on a half cadence).

# Formal Definition
The descriptor "sectional" applies to binary or ternary forms when the first section (the A section) ends on the tonic. The term "continuous" is used when the first section does NOT end on the tonic chord. While this often means the first section ends in a new key, it can also mean the first section ends on the dominant chord in a half cadence.

# Construction/Recognition
Sectional:
- A section ends with authentic cadence in tonic key
- Creates sense of completion at end of first section
- Example: A section in G major ends with PAC in G major

Continuous:
- A section does NOT end on tonic
- May modulate to new key (often dominant or relative major)
- May end with half cadence in tonic key
- Creates momentum into the second section

Naming convention: use sectional/continuous BEFORE binary/ternary
- "sectional binary"
- "continuous ternary"

# Musical Context
- Sectional forms have more clearly delineated sections
- Continuous forms create forward momentum across sections
- Baroque binary forms are often continuous (modulate to V or III)
- Sectional forms more common in shorter, simpler pieces

# Examples
Sectional:
- Brahms, "Wiegenlied" (Lullaby), Op. 49, No. 4 - A section ends on tonic

Continuous:
- Haydn, Symphony No. 94, II - A section ends in new key or on half cadence

# Common Confusions
- "Continuous" does not mean "without repeats"
- Continuous can mean either modulation OR half cadence
- The distinction applies to both binary AND ternary forms
- This descriptor comes BEFORE "binary" or "ternary" in the full name
