# Quick Definition
A polychord is the simultaneous sounding of two or more triads or seventh chords, creating complex harmonic structures from simpler chord components.

# Formal Definition
A polychord typically consists of two triads sounding simultaneously, though it could also comprise two seventh chords, a seventh chord and triad, or more than two chords (the prefix "poly" means "many"). Polychords are notated using a horizontal line between the chord symbols (distinguishing them from slash chords which use a diagonal slash). Inversions of the component chords need not be specified.

# Construction/Calculation
1. Select two triads or seventh chords
2. Voice them in separate registers (often upper/lower)
3. Notate with horizontal line: upper chord over lower chord
4. Example: E♭ over F♭ = E♭/F♭ (horizontal line, not slash)
5. Component chords can be in any inversion
6. Register placement affects the sound quality

# Musical Context
Polychords became prominent in early 20th-century music, particularly with Stravinsky. The most famous polychord is the "Rite of Spring chord" - an E♭ dominant 7th over an F♭ major triad in "Dance of the Adolescents." Copland also used polychords extensively. The technique creates harmonic complexity while maintaining the recognizable identities of the component chords.

# Examples
- Stravinsky, Rite of Spring, "Dance of the Adolescents": E♭7 over F♭ major (the famous "Rite of Spring chord")
- Copland, Appalachian Spring: E major over A major
- Stravinsky, Petrushka: Chromatically ascending dominant 7ths against repeating G-F-C cycle
- Notation: Horizontal line between chord symbols, not a slash

# Common Confusions
- Polychord notation uses horizontal line; slash chords use diagonal slash
- Polychord ≠ slash chord: polychords have two independent harmonies
- Inversions of component chords are not specified
- The term covers both two-chord and many-chord combinations
