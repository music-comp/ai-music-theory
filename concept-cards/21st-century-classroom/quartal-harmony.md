# Quick Definition
Quartal harmony builds chords by stacking perfect fourths instead of thirds, creating an open, ambiguous sonority characteristic of 20th-century music.

# Formal Definition
Quartal harmony refers to chords constructed entirely (or mostly) from stacked fourths, contrasting with the tertian (third-based) harmony that dominates common-practice music. Quartal chords have an open, hollow, or suspended quality due to the absence of clear major/minor thirds. This approach became more common after 1900 as composers sought to break with traditional harmonic practices.

# Construction/Calculation
1. Select a root note
2. Stack perfect fourths above it: P4 + P4 + P4...
3. Three-note quartal chord: root - P4 - P4 (e.g., C-F-Bb)
4. Four-note quartal chord: root - P4 - P4 - P4 (e.g., C-F-Bb-Eb)
5. Can mix perfect and augmented fourths for variety
6. Notes span: 3-note = m7, 4-note = m10, 5-note = m13

# Musical Context
Quartal harmony became prominent in early 20th-century concert music and later in jazz (particularly modal jazz and fusion). Schoenberg used quartal chords in his Chamber Symphony No. 1. The technique also appears in television theme music, creating a "modern" sound. Quartal voicings are common in jazz piano for modal tunes.

# Examples
- Schoenberg, Chamber Symphony No. 1, Op. 9: Prominent quartal chord structures
- John Parker, CHiPs Theme: Quartal harmony in TV theme
- Merv Griffin/Julann Wright, Jeopardy! Theme: Quartal sonorities
- Jazz: "So What" voicings are quartal stacks

# Common Confusions
- Quartal is stacked fourths, not just any chord containing a fourth
- A sus4 chord is not quartal unless it lacks the fifth and continues stacking 4ths
- Quartal chords are ambiguous regarding key - the same voicing works in multiple contexts
- Quartal and quintal are inversionally related (P4 inverts to P5)
