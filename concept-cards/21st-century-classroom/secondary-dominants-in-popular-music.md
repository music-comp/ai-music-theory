# Quick Definition
Secondary dominants in popular music frequently resolve deceptively (V/V to IV, V/vi to IV) because IV and bVII commonly substitute for V as harmonic goals in pop/rock harmony -- making irregular resolution the norm rather than the exception.

# Core Definition
While secondary dominants in classical music typically resolve to their expected target chords, popular music frequently features irregular (deceptive) resolutions. This is because popular music harmony allows IV and bVII to substitute for V as dominant-function chords. The most common irregular resolutions in popular music include V/V to IV (where IV acts as a dominant substitute), V/V to bVII (where bVII acts as a dominant substitute in rock), and V7/vi to IV (interpretable as a deceptive progression within the submediant area).

# Prerequisites
- **Secondary dominant**: The chord type being used
- **Irregular resolutions**: Understanding why secondary dominants resolve elsewhere

# Key Properties
1. Irregular resolution is more common in pop than classical
2. IV regularly substitutes for V in pop harmonic vocabulary
3. bVII regularly substitutes for V in rock
4. V/V to IV is extremely common
5. V7/vi to IV is a particularly common pattern
6. The chromatic intensification still occurs even without "correct" resolution
7. Pop harmony is less hierarchical about dominant function

# Construction / Recognition
- In pop context: expect secondary dominants may resolve to IV or bVII
- V/V to IV: the V/V intensifies motion, IV provides the resolution
- V7/vi to IV: iii to IV is normal diatonically; chromatic variant creates V/vi to IV
- The analysis labels the secondary dominant for what it IS, not what it resolves TO

# Context & Application
Understanding that secondary dominants resolve differently in popular music prevents analysts from forcing classical resolution expectations onto pop repertoire. The chromatic color and intensification of secondary dominants are valued in pop harmony even when the "expected" resolution does not occur. This reflects the different harmonic vocabulary of popular music, where IV, bVII, and other chords can serve dominant function.

# Examples
- "Yesterday" (McCartney, 1965) -- V/V to IV
- "Forget You" (CeeLo Green/Bruno Mars, 2010) -- V7/V to IV
- "I Want You to Want Me" (Cheap Trick, 1977) -- V/V to bVII
- "(Sittin' On) The Dock of the Bay" (Redding/Cropper, 1967) -- V7/vi to IV
- "Imagine" (Lennon, 1971) -- V7/vi to IV
- "Baby Love" (Supremes, 1964) -- V4/2/IV to V7/ii (chromatic bass chain)

# Common Errors
- Expecting all secondary dominants to resolve "correctly" in pop contexts
- Not labeling chords as secondary dominants because they resolve irregularly

# Common Confusions
- **Still a secondary dominant**: The label reflects the chord's construction, not its resolution
- **Pop vs. classical norms**: Irregular resolution is normal in pop, exceptional in classical
- **IV as dominant substitute**: In pop, IV regularly serves the role that V serves in classical
