# Quick Definition
Beyond b6, the lowered third (b3) and lowered seventh (b7) generate additional borrowed chords -- b3 produces bIII, i, and viio7; b7 produces bVII, v, and bIII -- with some overlap between all three borrowed scale degree families.

# Core Definition
In addition to b6 (the most common borrowed scale degree), b3 and b7 can each be harmonized as root, 3rd, or 5th of borrowed chords. Flat-3 as root produces bIII; as 3rd produces i (minor tonic); as 5th produces various chords. Flat-7 as root produces bVII; as other chord members produces additional borrowings. Some of these chords are duplicates from the b6 harmonization list, since borrowed chords often contain multiple altered scale degrees. The key insight is not to memorize all possible borrowed chords, but to recognize that including any minor-mode scale degree in a chord alters its quality.

# Prerequisites
- **Mode mixture**: The overarching concept
- **Flat-six harmonization**: The most common borrowed scale degree

# Key Properties
1. b3 as root: bIII (major mediant)
2. b3 as 3rd: i (minor tonic -- produces I to i effect)
3. b3 as 5th: contributes to other altered chords
4. b7 as root: bVII (subtonic chord)
5. b7 as 3rd: v (minor dominant -- rare)
6. b7 as 5th: bIII (overlaps with b3 as root)
7. Some borrowed chords contain b3, b6, AND b7 (e.g., viio7)
8. Overlap between categories is normal and expected

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify lowered scale degrees in the chord
- Stack in thirds to find root and quality
- Determine which borrowed scale degrees are present
- The chord quality reveals the borrowing (e.g., minor i in a major-key context = b3)
- Multiple borrowed scale degrees may be present in one chord

# Context & Application
While b6 is the most common, b3 produces distinctive effects like the I-to-i shift (heard dramatically in Also Sprach Zarathustra). The bVII chord (from b7) is extremely common in rock and popular music. Understanding all three borrowed scale degree families gives a complete picture of mode mixture's harmonic palette. The overlap between categories reinforces that mode mixture is a unified concept, not three separate phenomena.

# Examples
- Richard Strauss, Also Sprach Zarathustra (1896) -- I to i (b3 producing minor tonic)
- Kurt Cobain, "Lithium" (1992) -- uses bIII and other borrowed chords
- Frank Loesser, "Inchworm" (1952) -- I alternating with bVII7
- Brahms, Symphony No. 3, III (1883) -- I alternating with bIII6

# Common Errors
- Trying to memorize all possible borrowed chords instead of understanding the principle
- Not recognizing that one chord may contain multiple borrowed scale degrees

# Common Confusions
- **Overlap is normal**: bIII contains both b3 and b7 -- it belongs to both families
- **Principle over list**: Understand that ANY minor-mode scale degree alters chord quality
- **viio7 contains b6, b3, and b7**: It belongs to all three families simultaneously
