# Quick Definition
Each diatonic chord (except vii° in major and ii° and vii° in minor) can be tonicized with a secondary dominant, with specific chromatic alterations required for each.

# Formal Definition
In major keys, secondary dominants can tonicize ii, iii, IV, V, and vi. In minor keys, secondary dominants can tonicize III, iv, V, VI, and VII (subtonic). Diminished triads cannot be tonicized. The raised notes generally act as leading tones to the target chord's root.

# Analysis/Application
Secondary dominants in major (C major examples):
- V/ii = A or A7 (tonicizes Dm)
- V/iii = B or B7 (tonicizes Em)
- V/IV = C or C7 (tonicizes F) - note: has lowered chromatic (Bb)
- V/V = D or D7 (tonicizes G)
- V/vi = E or E7 (tonicizes Am)

In minor, remember:
- V/VII tonicizes the subtonic (bVII), which is major
- V/VII is not the same as vii°, which cannot be tonicized

# Musical Context
The chromaticism in secondary dominants creates color and intensifies harmonic motion. V/V is the most common secondary dominant in both major and minor.

# Common Confusions
- V7/IV has a lowered chromatic (it's the only one with bx)
- In minor, both V/VII (tonicizing subtonic) and the diatonic vii° exist
- The F major chord in C minor can be V/VII or IV depending on function
