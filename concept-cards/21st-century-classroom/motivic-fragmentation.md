# Quick Definition
Motivic fragmentation in fugue involves breaking the subject, answer, and countersubject into smaller portions (head and tail motives) for development in episodes and bridges.

# Formal Definition
Motivic fragmentation is the technique of dividing melodic material (subject, answer, countersubject) into component parts for developmental purposes. The "head motive" is the beginning portion of a theme; the "tail motive" is the ending portion. These fragments are used extensively in episodes, where they are typically treated in sequence. Fragmentation allows the composer to maintain thematic unity while providing variety through varied combinations and transpositions.

# Rules/Procedures
1. **Subject head**: Opening gesture/motive of the subject
2. **Subject tail**: Closing gesture/motive of the subject
3. **Countersubject fragments**: Can also be divided into head and tail
4. In analysis, identify which part of which theme is being used
5. Fragments are typically:
   - Sequenced (most common developmental technique)
   - Combined with other fragments
   - Imitated between voices
6. Fragmentation maintains thematic unity while creating developmental momentum

# Musical Context
Bach's fugues demonstrate masterful fragmentation. In the episodes, recognizing which portion of which theme is being used reveals the compositional craft. The technique shows how a single subject can generate an entire composition.

# Examples
- Bach, Fugue 2 in C minor, Episode 1: Uses subject head in sequence
- Bridge (m.5) uses subject head for modulatory passage
- Episode might combine subject tail with countersubject head

# Common Confusions
- Not recognizing fragments when transposed or inverted
- Thinking fragments must be exact quotes (they can be varied)
- Missing the head/tail distinction (where does the motive split?)
- Not connecting fragments back to their source theme
