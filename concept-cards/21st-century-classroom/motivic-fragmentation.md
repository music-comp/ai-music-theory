# Quick Definition
Motivic fragmentation in fugue involves breaking the subject, answer, and countersubject into smaller portions (head and tail motives) for development in episodes and bridges.

# Core Definition
Motivic fragmentation is the technique of dividing melodic material (subject, answer, countersubject) into component parts for developmental purposes. The "head motive" is the beginning portion of a theme; the "tail motive" is the ending portion. These fragments are used extensively in episodes and bridges, where they are typically treated in sequence. In Bach's Fugue 2 in C minor, there is motivic fragmentation of the subject, countersubject 1, and countersubject 2 (Hutchinson, Ch. 30, p. 435).

# Prerequisites
- **Fugue subject** -- Fragments derive from the subject
- **Countersubject** -- Countersubject material is also fragmented

# Key Properties
1. Subject head: opening gesture/motive of the subject
2. Subject tail: closing gesture/motive of the subject
3. Countersubject fragments: also divisible into head and tail
4. Fragments are sequenced, combined, and imitated in episodes
5. Maintains thematic unity while creating developmental variety
6. Primary technique for building episodes and bridges

# Construction / Recognition
**Identifying fragmentation:**
1. Isolate the beginning of the subject (head motive)
2. Isolate the ending of the subject (tail motive)
3. Do the same for countersubjects
4. In episodes, compare motives to these fragments
5. Note sequential treatment and combination of different fragments

# Context & Application
Bach's fugues demonstrate masterful fragmentation. Recognizing which portion of which theme is being used in episodes reveals the compositional craft. The technique shows how a single subject can generate an entire composition through varied combinations of its components.

# Examples

- **Episode 1** (p. 435, Figure 30.8.2): Contains fragments of subject and countersubject 1

## Related

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Not recognizing fragments when they appear at different pitch levels
  **Correction**: Compare intervallic contour, not specific pitches; fragments are typically transposed

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking fragments must be exact quotes of the original
  **Clarification**: Fragments preserve the essential intervallic and rhythmic profile but may be varied
- **Confusion**: Not connecting fragments back to their source theme
  **Clarification**: Always compare episode material to the subject head, tail, and countersubject(s)
