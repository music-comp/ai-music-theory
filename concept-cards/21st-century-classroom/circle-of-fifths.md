# Quick Definition
The circle of fifths is a diagram showing all major and minor keys arranged by their key signatures, with each adjacent key a perfect fifth apart.

# Formal Definition
The circle of fifths is a visual representation of the relationships among the twelve tones of the chromatic scale, their corresponding key signatures, and associated major and minor keys. Moving clockwise (ascending by fifths) adds one sharp; moving counterclockwise (descending by fifths) adds one flat.

# Construction/Recognition
- C major (no sharps/flats) at the top
- Clockwise motion: G-D-A-E-B-F#-C# (adding sharps)
- Counterclockwise motion: F-Bb-Eb-Ab-Db-Gb-Cb (adding flats)
- Adjacent keys differ by one accidental
- Three enharmonic key pairs at the bottom: B/Cb, F#/Gb, C#/Db

# Musical Context
The circle of fifths is essential for understanding key relationships, modulation, chord progressions, and the structure of Western tonal music. Keys close together on the circle are more closely related and modulation between them is smoother.

# Examples
- C to G is a fifth (G major has one more sharp than C major)
- C to F is a fourth down/fifth up (F major has one flat)
- B major (5 sharps) = Cb major (7 flats) enharmonically
- F# major (6 sharps) = Gb major (6 flats) enharmonically

# Related Concepts
- Key signature
- Perfect fifth
- Order of sharps

- Relative major/minor
- Enharmonic keys

# Common Confusions
- Moving by fifths means the interval, not just counting to five
- Three keys at the bottom are enharmonically equivalent (same sound, different spelling)
- Minor keys can be added to the inside of the circle
