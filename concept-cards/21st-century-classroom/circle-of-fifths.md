# Quick Definition
The circle of fifths is a diagram arranging all major and minor keys by their key signatures, with each adjacent key a perfect fifth apart; clockwise adds sharps, counterclockwise adds flats.

# Core Definition
A helpful learning device to remember the order of keys in relation to the order of sharps and flats is the circle of fifths. As you ascend in fifths (clockwise), key signatures get one degree "sharper." (C to G is a fifth because C=1, D=2, E=3, F=4, and G=5.) As you descend in fifths (counterclockwise), key signatures get one degree "flatter." Three overlapping enharmonic key pairs appear at the bottom: B major = Cb major, F# major = Gb major, and C# major = Db major.

# Prerequisites
- **Key signature**: The sharps/flats that the circle organizes
- **Order of sharps, order of flats**: The sequences displayed on the circle

# Key Properties
1. C major (no sharps/flats) at the top
2. Clockwise (ascending fifths): G-D-A-E-B-F#-C# (adding sharps)
3. Counterclockwise (descending fifths): F-Bb-Eb-Ab-Db-Gb-Cb (adding flats)
4. Adjacent keys differ by one accidental
5. Three enharmonic key pairs at the bottom: B/Cb, F#/Gb, C#/Db
6. Minor keys can be placed on the inside of the circle

# Construction / Recognition
- C at the top, sharps clockwise, flats counterclockwise
- Each step clockwise adds one sharp
- Each step counterclockwise adds one flat
- Bottom three positions have enharmonic equivalents

# Context & Application
The circle of fifths is essential for understanding key relationships, modulation, and chord progressions. Keys close together on the circle are more closely related, and modulation between them is smoother. It visually explains why tetrachords are shared between adjacent keys.

# Examples
- C to G is a fifth; G major has one more sharp than C major
- C to F is a fifth down; F major has one flat
- B major (5 sharps) = Cb major (7 flats) enharmonically
- F# major (6 sharps) = Gb major (6 flats) enharmonically

# Common Errors
- Confusing clockwise (sharps) with counterclockwise (flats)
- Forgetting the enharmonic key pairs at the bottom

# Common Confusions
- **Moving by fifths**: This means the interval of a fifth, not just counting five letters
- **Enharmonic overlap**: Three keys at the bottom are enharmonically equivalent (same sound, different spelling)
- **Minor keys**: Can be added to the inside of the circle, sharing key signatures with their relative majors
