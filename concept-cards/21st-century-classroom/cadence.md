# Quick Definition
A cadence is a harmonic arrival point at the end of a phrase, creating a sense of pause or conclusion like punctuation in language.

# Formal Definition
A cadence is a harmonic progression that creates a point of arrival or rest, functioning like punctuation in music. Cadences mark the ends of phrases and sections, providing the listener with moments to process musical ideas. The four basic cadence types are authentic (V-I), plagal (IV-I), deceptive (V-vi), and half (ending on V).

# Construction/Recognition
- Authentic Cadence (AC): V-I
- Plagal Cadence (PC): IV-I
- Deceptive Cadence (DC): V-vi (or V to "not I")
- Half Cadence (HC): phrase ending on V
- Cadences occur at phrase endings

# Musical Context
Cadences are essential for musical form, marking phrase boundaries and creating the sense of musical "breathing." Strong cadences (authentic) create finality; weak cadences (half) create expectation. Cadences are crucial in both classical and popular music analysis.

# Examples
- "Star-Spangled Banner" ends with an authentic cadence (V-I)
- "Amen" in hymns is a plagal cadence (IV-I)
- Mozart's "Voi che sapete" includes a deceptive cadence
- Half cadences create a "comma" effect, not a "period"

# Common Confusions
- A cadence is at the END of a phrase, not just any chord change
- Deceptive cadence means V goes to something OTHER than I (usually vi)
- Half cadence ends ON V, not going from V somewhere
