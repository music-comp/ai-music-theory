# Quick Definition
The blues scale is a six-note scale identical to the minor pentatonic scale with an added flat 5th (or sharp 4th), creating the characteristic "blue" sound of blues and jazz.

# Formal Definition
The blues scale consists of scale degrees 1-b3-4-b5-5-b7, which is the minor pentatonic scale (1-b3-4-5-b7) with an added b5/sharp4 chromatic passing tone. The b3 and b5 are called "blue notes" because they are not chord tones of the underlying major triad or dominant 7th chord, creating the characteristic tension of blues music.

# Construction/Calculation
1. Start with the minor pentatonic: 1-b3-4-5-b7
2. Add the b5 (chromatic passing tone between 4 and 5)
3. Result: 1-b3-4-b5-5-b7
4. In C: C-Eb-F-Gb-G-Bb
5. Half steps occur: b3 to 4, b5 to 5, and b7 to 1

# Musical Context
The blues scale is foundational to blues, jazz, rock, and popular music. Some players use the blues scale over any chord progression, and listeners often find this acceptable due to the scale's strong character. A well-known example is "Sunshine of Your Love" by Cream, which prominently features the blues scale. The scale works over major, minor, and dominant chord progressions.

# Examples
- C blues scale: C-Eb-F-Gb-G-Bb
- G blues scale: G-Bb-C-Db-D-F
- Blue notes in C: Eb (b3) and Gb (b5) against C major or C7
- "Sunshine of Your Love" by Cream (Jack Bruce and Eric Clapton)

# Common Confusions
- The blues scale is a 6-note scale, not 7 (due to the added b5)
- The b5 is a chromatic passing tone, not a replacement for the 5th
- Blue notes (b3, b5) create tension by being "outside" the chord
- The scale can be used over major AND minor progressions
