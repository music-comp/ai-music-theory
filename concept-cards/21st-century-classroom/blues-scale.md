# Quick Definition
The blues scale is a six-note scale identical to the minor pentatonic with an added b5/sharp4, creating the characteristic "blue" sound.

# Core Definition
The blues scale is identical to the minor pentatonic scale (1-b3-4-5-b7) except with an added b5/#4 (1-b3-4-b5-5-b7). The b5 and b3 are considered "blue notes" because they are not chord tones of a major triad or dominant 7th chord. Blue notes are commonly used in jazz and popular music. Some players use the blues scale over any chord progression (Hutchinson, Ch. 31, p. 457).

- **Intervals** -- Must identify half steps and scale patterns

# Key Properties
1. Six-note scale: 1-b3-4-b5-5-b7
2. Minor pentatonic + added b5/#4
3. Blue notes: b3 and b5 (not chord tones of major/dominant harmony)
4. Can be used over major, minor, and dominant progressions
5. Descending form shown in source: 1-b7-5-b5-4-b3

# Construction / Recognition
1. Start with minor pentatonic: 1-b3-4-5-b7
2. Add b5 as chromatic passing tone between 4 and 5
3. In C: C-Eb-F-Gb-G-Bb

# Context & Application
The blues scale is foundational to blues, jazz, rock, and popular music. "Sunshine of Your Love" by Cream (Jack Bruce and Eric Clapton) is a well-known example (p. 457, Figure 31.9.2).

# Examples
- C blues: C-Eb-F-Gb-G-Bb (p. 457)
- "Sunshine of Your Love" by Cream (Figure 31.9.2, p. 457)
- Blue notes in C: Eb (b3) and Gb (b5) against C major or C7

## Contrasts With
- **Bebop scale** -- Bebop has 8 notes and aligns chord tones with downbeats

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the b5 as a replacement for the natural 5th
  **Correction**: The b5 is a chromatic passing tone; the natural 5th is still in the scale

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the blues scale can only be used over minor chords
  **Clarification**: The blues scale works over major, minor, and dominant progressions
