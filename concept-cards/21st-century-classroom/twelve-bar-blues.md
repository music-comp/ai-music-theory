# Quick Definition
The 12-bar blues is a repeating chord progression lasting 12 measures, built primarily on the I, IV, and V chords, that forms the foundation of blues music and early rock 'n' roll.

# Formal Definition
The 12-bar blues progression is a foundational form in American popular music. The basic progression is: Bars 1-4: I-I-I-I; Bars 5-8: IV-IV-I-I; Bars 9-12: V-IV-I-I. This progression is repeated for each verse or chorus of the song.

# Analysis/Application
Basic 12-bar blues structure:
- Bars 1-4: I chord (4 bars of tonic)
- Bars 5-6: IV chord (2 bars of subdominant)
- Bars 7-8: I chord (return to tonic)
- Bar 9: V chord (dominant)
- Bar 10: IV chord (subdominant)
- Bars 11-12: I chord (tonic)

# Musical Context
The blues originated in the early 1900s and the 12-bar form was popular during the Swing Era (1930s-40s), early Rock 'n' Roll (1950s), and continues to influence music today. The form is foundational to jazz, rock, and R&B.

# Examples
- Chuck Berry, "Johnny B. Goode" (1958)
- "Hound Dog" (Elvis Presley)
- "Rock Around the Clock"
- "In The Mood" (Swing Era)
- "One O'Clock Jump" (Swing Era)

# Common Confusions
- Many variations exist (quick IV in bar 2, turnarounds, etc.)
- The progression repeats for each verse
- Blues progressions often use dominant seventh chords on all three chords (I7, IV7, V7)
