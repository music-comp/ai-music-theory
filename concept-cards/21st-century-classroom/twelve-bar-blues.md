# Quick Definition
The 12-bar blues is a repeating chord progression lasting 12 measures, built on the I, IV, and V chords, forming the foundation of blues music and early rock and roll.

# Core Definition
The 12-bar blues progression is a foundational form in American popular music. The basic progression is: Bars 1-4: I-I-I-I; Bars 5-8: IV-IV-I-I; Bars 9-12: V-IV-I-I. This progression repeats for each verse. Associated with the Blues genre (originating in the early 1900s), it was popular during the Swing Era (1930s-40s) and early Rock and Roll (1950s).

# Prerequisites
- **Roman numeral analysis**: Identifying I, IV, and V chords

# Key Properties
1. 12 measures long, repeating
2. Three sections: I (4 bars), IV-I (4 bars), V-IV-I (4 bars)
3. Uses only three chords: I, IV, V
4. Often uses dominant seventh quality on all three chords
5. The progression repeats for each verse/chorus
6. Many variations exist (quick IV, turnarounds, etc.)

# Construction / Recognition
1. Bars 1-4: Tonic (I) for 4 bars
2. Bars 5-6: Subdominant (IV) for 2 bars
3. Bars 7-8: Tonic (I) for 2 bars
4. Bar 9: Dominant (V)
5. Bar 10: Subdominant (IV)
6. Bars 11-12: Tonic (I) for 2 bars

# Context & Application
The 12-bar blues spans multiple genres and eras: early blues (1900s), Swing Era (1930s-40s), Rock and Roll (1950s), and continues today. It is foundational to jazz, rock, R&B, and country music.

# Examples
- Chuck Berry, "Johnny B. Goode" (1958)
- "Hound Dog" (Elvis Presley)
- "Rock Around the Clock"
- "In The Mood" (Swing Era)
- "One O'Clock Jump" (Swing Era)

# Common Errors
- Assuming the basic form is the only version (many variations exist)
- Forgetting that all three chords often use dominant seventh quality

# Common Confusions
- **Many variations exist**: Quick IV in bar 2, turnarounds in bars 11-12, etc.
- **Dominant 7ths on all chords**: Blues often uses I7, IV7, V7 (all dominant quality)
- **Repeating form**: The 12 bars repeat for each verse
