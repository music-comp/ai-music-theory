# Quick Definition
Chromatic mediants are chords with roots a third apart that share only one common tone and have the same quality (both major or both minor) -- each triad has four chromatic mediants, and the relationship is central to common-tone modulation and Romantic/film-score harmony.

# Core Definition
Chromatic mediants are chords with roots a third apart (mediant relationship) that share only one common tone and have the same quality (both major or both minor). This distinguishes them from diatonic mediants, which may share two common tones or have different qualities. Any major or minor triad has four chromatic mediants. Chromatic mediants can be used progressively (without modulation) or as connecting chords in common-tone modulations.

# Prerequisites
- **Common-tone modulation**: The modulatory context where chromatic mediants operate
- **Triads**: The chord types in the relationship

# Key Properties
1. Roots a third apart (major or minor third, up or down)
2. Same quality: both major OR both minor
3. Share only ONE common tone
4. Every triad has exactly FOUR chromatic mediants
5. Used both progressively and in modulations
6. Maximizes harmonic contrast while maintaining one connection

# Construction / Recognition
Four chromatic mediants of C major: E major (share E), Eb major (share G), A major (share E), Ab major (share C)
Four chromatic mediants of C minor: E minor (share E), Eb minor (share G), A minor (share E), Ab minor (share C)

Three criteria must be met:
1. Root a third higher or lower
2. Same quality as starting chord
3. Only one common tone shared

# Context & Application
Chromatic mediant relationships are central to Romantic-era and film-score harmony. Wagner, John Williams, and many film composers use chromatic mediant progressions for dramatic color without necessarily modulating. In common-tone modulation, the chromatic mediant relationship between connecting chords provides the one-note bridge between keys. The relationship maximizes harmonic contrast (only one common tone) while maintaining a thread of continuity.

# Examples
Chromatic mediant progressions (without modulation):
- Wagner, Das Rheingold, Scene 3 (1854) -- i to bvi (Cm to Abm)
- John Williams, Star Wars: Return of the Jedi, "Main Title" (1983)
- John Williams, The Phantom Menace, "Duel of the Fates" (1999)

# Common Errors
- Accepting chords with different qualities as chromatic mediants (quality must match)
- Confusing diatonic mediants (two common tones) with chromatic mediants (one common tone)

# Common Confusions
- **One, not two**: Chromatic mediants share only ONE common tone; diatonic mediants may share two
- **Same quality required**: Both chords must be the same quality (both major or both minor)
- **Four per triad**: Every major or minor triad has exactly four chromatic mediants
