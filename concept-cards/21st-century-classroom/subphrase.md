# Quick Definition
Subphrases are melodic units smaller than phrases but larger than motives, typically two measures long, representing half of a standard four-measure phrase.

# Formal Definition
Subphrases are smaller than phrases but larger than motives. Most subphrases are two measures long. They are also called "phrase segments" or "phrase members" in other texts. Subphrases are labeled with letters (a, b, etc.) with primes (a', a") indicating alterations.

# Analysis/Application
When analyzing subphrases:
1. Label with lowercase letters (a, b, etc.)
2. Use prime (') to indicate a subphrase that repeats with slight alterations
3. Use double prime (") for a second alteration
4. If merely transposed (sequenced), keep the same letter without prime
5. Subphrases typically contain six or more notes and occupy two measures

# Musical Context
Analyzing subphrases helps understand the construction of melodies. Many compositions have melodic units that are two measures long, and recognizing subphrase structure reveals how phrases are built.

# Examples
- "Don't Stop" (Christine McVie, 1977) - subphrases a and a' share the same first six notes
- "Party Rock Anthem" (2011) - four-measure phrase with subphrases a and b
- "Don't Stop Believin'" (1981) - motive, subphrase, and phrase levels all significant

# Common Confusions
- Motives are typically short (2-7 notes); subphrases are usually longer and occupy 2 measures
- The distinction between motive and subphrase can be ambiguous
- Use primes for alterations, not for simple transposition
