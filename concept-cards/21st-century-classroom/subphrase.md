# Quick Definition
Subphrases are melodic units smaller than phrases but larger than motives, typically two measures long, representing half of a standard four-measure phrase.

# Core Definition
Subphrases are smaller than phrases but larger than motives. Most subphrases are two measures long. They are also called "phrase segments" and "phrase members" in other texts. Subphrases are labeled with letters (a, b, etc.). When a subphrase repeats with slight alterations, use a prime (a'); if merely transposed (sequenced), keep the same letter without prime. Subphrases typically contain six or more notes.

# Prerequisites
- **Phrase**: The larger unit that subphrases divide
- **Motive**: The smaller unit within subphrases

# Key Properties
1. Smaller than phrases, larger than motives
2. Typically two measures long (half of a 4-measure phrase)
3. Usually contain six or more notes
4. Labeled with lowercase letters (a, b, etc.)
5. Prime (') for altered repetitions; double prime (") for further alterations
6. Transposed (sequenced) subphrases keep the same letter without prime
7. Three levels: motive, subphrase, phrase

# Construction / Recognition
1. Divide a four-measure phrase into two-measure units
2. Label with lowercase letters
3. Use prime (') for altered repetitions (different ending)
4. Do not use prime for simple transposition (sequence)
5. Look for repeated melodic patterns at the two-measure level

# Context & Application
Analyzing subphrases helps understand the construction of melodies. Many compositions have melodic units that are two measures long. For some compositions, subphrase analysis is more informative than motive analysis, especially when motives are not extracted and developed.

# Examples
- "Don't Stop" (Christine McVie, 1977) -- subphrases a and a' share same first six notes
- "Party Rock Anthem" (2011) -- subphrases a and b within four-measure phrase
- "Don't Stop Believin'" (1981) -- significant melodic ideas at all three levels (motive, subphrase, phrase)
- "The Very Thought of You" (1934) -- ambiguity between motive and subphrase

# Common Errors
- Using prime for simple transposition (sequences keep the same letter)
- Confusing motives with subphrases (different scale)

# Common Confusions
- **Motive vs. subphrase boundary is ambiguous**: The distinction is not always clear
- **Prime means alteration, not transposition**: a' means the subphrase is altered; a transposed subphrase is still labeled a
- **Three analytical levels**: Motive (2-7 notes), subphrase (2 measures), phrase (4+ measures)
