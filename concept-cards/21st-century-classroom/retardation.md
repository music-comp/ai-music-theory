# Quick Definition
A retardation is an accented non-chord tone approached by the same note and resolving upward by step -- essentially an upward-resolving suspension.

# Core Definition
A retardation is usually an accented non-chord tone, meaning it occurs on a downbeat. A retardation is approached by the same note and resolves up by step. It has the same three-part structure as a suspension (preparation, dissonance, resolution) but resolves in the opposite direction (upward instead of downward).

# Prerequisites
- **Suspension**: Understanding of the downward-resolving counterpart

# Key Properties
1. Approached by same note, resolves upward by step
2. Usually accented (on the downbeat)
3. Same three-part structure as suspension: preparation, retardation, resolution
4. Resolves UP (opposite of suspension)
5. Less common than suspensions
6. Often occurs at cadences in slow movements

# Construction / Recognition
1. Look for a note held from the previous beat that creates dissonance
2. Confirm it resolves upward by step
3. Follows the same prep-dissonance-resolution pattern as suspension
4. Label as Ret. or R

# Context & Application
Retardations are less common than suspensions but appear frequently at cadences, especially in Classical period slow movements. They often occur alongside suspensions for expressive effect. The leading tone held into a cadence and resolving up to tonic is a classic retardation.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C major, K. 545, II (1788) -- retardation at cadence
- J.S. Bach, French Suite No. 1 in D Minor, Sarabande -- "ret." in tenor part alongside suspension

# Common Confusions
- **Retardation = resolves UP; Suspension = resolves DOWN**: This is the only difference
- **Both are approached by same note**: Both are prepared and accented
- **Leading tone retardation**: Holding the leading tone through a cadence and resolving up to tonic is a typical example
