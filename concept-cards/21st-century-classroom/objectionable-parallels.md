# Quick Definition
Objectionable parallels are parallel fifths and parallel octaves (including their octave equivalents) that destroy voice independence and should be avoided in traditional voice leading.

# Formal Definition
Objectionable parallels occur when any two voices move in parallel motion at the interval of a perfect fifth, perfect octave, perfect twelfth (compound fifth), or perfect unison. In Renaissance through Romantic practice, these parallels were considered to undermine the independence of voices because the strong acoustic relationship between these intervals causes the voices to blend into one.

# Rules/Procedures
1. Check every pair of voices between successive chords
2. Identify any intervals of a 5th, 8ve, 12th, or unison
3. If two voices form one of these intervals in consecutive chords AND both voices move in the same direction (parallel motion), this is an objectionable parallel
4. Acceptable parallels: 3rds, 4ths, 6ths
5. Generally not used: parallel 2nds and 7ths

# Musical Context
This rule applies to all traditional voice leading from Renaissance through Romantic periods. Modern and popular music often breaks this rule deliberately. In academic voice leading exercises, avoiding parallel 5ths and 8ves is fundamental.

# Examples
- Parallel 5ths: C-G moving to D-A in the same direction is forbidden
- Parallel 8ves: C-C moving to D-D in the same direction is forbidden
- Parallel 12ths: C3-G4 moving to D3-A4 is equivalent to parallel 5ths
- Acceptable: C-E moving to D-F (parallel 3rds)

# Common Confusions
- Confusing parallel 5ths with direct (hidden) 5ths - parallel requires same interval in both chords
- Forgetting that compound intervals count (12ths = 5ths, unisons = 8ves)
- Thinking all parallel motion is forbidden when only 5ths and 8ves are problematic
- Not checking all voice pairs (S-A, S-T, S-B, A-T, A-B, T-B)
