# Quick Definition
Classical symphonies, sonatas, and quartets typically follow a four-movement structure with standard forms: sonata form (I), slow movement form (II), minuet and trio (III), and rondo or sonata form (IV).

# Formal Definition
The forms commonly encountered in various movements of Classical symphonies, string quartets, and sonatas follow a conventional pattern: first movement in sonata form, second movement in ternary, sonatina, or five-part rondo form, third movement as minuet and trio (compound ternary), and fourth movement in rondo form, sonata form, or sonata rondo form.

# Construction/Recognition
Standard movement structure:

1. FIRST MOVEMENT:
   - Sonata form
   - Usually fast tempo (Allegro)
   - Most substantial movement

2. SECOND MOVEMENT:
   - Ternary form (ABA)
   - OR Sonatina form
   - OR Five-part rondo
   - Usually slow tempo (Adagio, Andante)

3. THIRD MOVEMENT:
   - Minuet and Trio (compound ternary)
   - Moderate tempo
   - Dance-like character

4. FOURTH MOVEMENT:
   - Rondo form
   - OR Sonata form
   - OR Sonata rondo form
   - Usually fast tempo (Allegro, Presto)
   - Often in rondo character

# Musical Context
- This structure was standard in the Classical era
- Provides contrast between movements
- Each movement has distinct character and function
- Three-movement works often omit the minuet

# Examples
- Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven symphonies
- Classical string quartets
- Classical piano sonatas

# Related Concepts
- Sonata form
- Ternary form
- Minuet and trio
- Rondo form
- Sonata rondo

# Common Confusions
- Not all Classical works follow this exact pattern
- Three-movement sonatas are common (omit minuet)
- Beethoven sometimes used scherzo instead of minuet
- Second movements have multiple form options
