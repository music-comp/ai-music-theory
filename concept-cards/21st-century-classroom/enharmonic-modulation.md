# Quick Definition
Enharmonic modulation uses a pivot chord that must be enharmonically respelled to function in the new key -- a "harmonic pun" where one chord has two meanings, exploiting the identical sound of differently spelled chords like V7/Ger+6 or the four spellings of a diminished seventh.

# Core Definition
In an enharmonic modulation, the pivot chord is almost always misspelled in one of the keys and therefore must be reconceptualized enharmonically by the analyst. Like a verbal pun (Shakespeare's "sun/son of York"), the pivot chord has two meanings depending on context. The chord can only be spelled correctly in one of the keys -- the analyst must envision the other spelling. Two sonorities are commonly used: the dominant seventh chord (enharmonically equivalent to Ger+6) and the fully diminished seventh chord (which has four possible spellings/resolutions).

# Prerequisites
- **Modulation**: The broader concept being specialized
- **German augmented sixth**: One of the two sonorities used
- **Fully diminished seventh chord**: The other sonority used

# Key Properties
1. Pivot chord is spelled correctly in only ONE key
2. The analyst must visualize the enharmonic respelling
3. Like a verbal pun: same sound, two meanings
4. Two main types: V7/Ger+6 pivot and diminished seventh pivot
5. Enables dramatic modulations to distant keys
6. Common in Romantic-era music
7. The different spellings reflect different voice-leading implications

# Construction / Recognition
Simple example (C major to B minor):
- G7 in C major = V7 (root wants to cadence down a fifth to C)
- Same chord respelled as Ger+6 in B minor (root wants to resolve down a half step to F#, the dominant)
- The G7 can only be spelled correctly in ONE key: either as V7 (G-B-D-F) or as Ger+6 (G-B-D-E#)

# Context & Application
Enharmonic modulation creates some of the most dramatic modulatory effects in tonal music. The "pun" effect is powerful because the listener hears one meaning then realizes (through resolution) that the chord meant something else entirely. Understanding both spellings is essential -- the respelling is never written out; the analyst must visualize it mentally. This technique is central to the harmonic language of late Classical and Romantic-era music.

# Examples
- C major to B minor: G7 heard as V7, respelled and resolves as Ger+6 in B minor
- Shakespeare's pun analogy: "Now is the winter of our discontent / Made glorious summer by this sun [son] of York"

# Common Errors
- Expecting the enharmonic respelling to be written in the score (it is not -- you must visualize it)
- Not recognizing that the chord sounds the same but means differently

# Common Confusions
- **Not written out**: The respelling is implicit; you must envision it
- **Sound vs. spelling**: The chord sounds identical in both interpretations
- **Different voice leading**: The two spellings imply different resolutions
