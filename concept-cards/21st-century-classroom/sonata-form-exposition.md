# Quick Definition

The exposition presents the main thematic material of a sonata, establishing the primary theme in tonic and the secondary theme in a contrasting key (usually V in major or III in minor).

# Core Definition

The exposition of sonata form presents the thematic material and articulates the movement from tonic to dominant (or mediant in minor). Charles Rosen describes this as a "polarization or opposition" -- a large-scale dissonance where material played outside the tonic is dissonant with respect to the center of stability (Rosen, *Sonata Forms*, cited by Hutchinson, Ch. 25, pp. 368-369).

# Prerequisites

- **Sonata form** -- The exposition is the first of three main sections of sonata form
- **Modulation** -- The exposition modulates from tonic to a secondary key

# Key Properties

1. Primary Theme (PT): establishes tonic key; expository function
2. Transition: modulates to secondary key; transitional function; often ends with HC
3. Secondary Theme (ST): in V (major) or III (minor); may have multiple STs
4. Closing Theme (CT): optional; confirms secondary key; terminative function
5. The exposition typically repeats
6. The tonal opposition creates dramatic tension requiring resolution

# Construction / Recognition

## Tonal Plan:
- **Major**: PT (I) --> Transition --> ST (V) --> CT (V)
- **Minor**: PT (i) --> Transition --> ST (III) --> CT (III)

## Key Recognition Features:
1. Stable, tuneful opening = Primary Theme (expository function)
2. Increased rhythmic activity, modulation = Transition
3. New thematic material in new key = Secondary Theme
4. Emphatic I-V alternation in new key = Closing Theme (if present)

# Context & Application

- **Typical contexts**: Opening section of sonata-form movements
- **Historical/stylistic notes**: Mozart and Haydn often ended transitions with a half cadence followed by a rest to signal the secondary theme's arrival. Cadences are important demarcations within a form.

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 369-370): Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 333, I -- clear secondary theme in dominant; four-phrase parallel double period demonstrating expository function

**Example 2** (pp. 370-373): Haydn, Piano Sonata Hob. XVI:35, I and Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 284, I -- transitions ending with half cadence

# Relationships

## Builds Upon
- **Sonata form** -- The exposition is the first main section

## Enables
- **Sonata form recapitulation** -- The recapitulation resolves the exposition's tonal opposition

## Related
- **Sonata principle** -- The exposition establishes the "dissonant section"
- **Structural functions** -- Each part of the exposition demonstrates a different structural function

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Expecting only one secondary theme
  **Correction**: Multiple STs (ST1, ST2) are fairly common

- **Error**: Missing the closing theme
  **Correction**: Not all sonatas have a closing theme; its absence is normal

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the opposition is purely thematic
  **Clarification**: The opposition is TONAL -- the secondary key itself is "dissonant" relative to tonic

- **Confusion**: Haydn restates the PT in the dominant (monothematic sonata)
  **Clarification**: This reinforces that the tonal design is as important as thematic design
