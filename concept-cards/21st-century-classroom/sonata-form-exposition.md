# Quick Definition
The exposition presents the main thematic material of a sonata, establishing the primary theme in tonic and the secondary theme in a contrasting key (usually V or III).

# Formal Definition
The exposition of sonata form presents the thematic material and articulates the movement from tonic to dominant (or mediant in minor), creating what Charles Rosen calls a "polarization or opposition." This opposition is a large-scale dissonance: material played outside the tonic is dissonant with respect to the center of stability.

# Construction/Recognition
Sections of the exposition:

1. PRIMARY THEME (PT):
   - Establishes tonic key
   - Main melodic material
   - Expository function (stable, clear melody)

2. TRANSITION:
   - Modulates to secondary key
   - Transitional function (unstable, active)
   - Often ends with half cadence (Mozart, Haydn)

3. SECONDARY THEME (ST):
   - In V (major) or III (minor)
   - New thematic material (usually)
   - May have multiple secondary themes (ST1, ST2)

4. CLOSING THEME (CT) - optional:
   - Confirms secondary key
   - Terminative function

Tonal plan:
- Major: I --> V
- Minor: i --> III

# Musical Context
- The exposition typically repeats
- The tonal opposition creates dramatic tension
- The secondary key is "dissonant" in large-scale terms
- Resolution requires recapitulation in tonic

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 333, I - clear secondary theme in dominant
- Haydn, Piano Sonata Hob. XVI:35, I - transition ends with half cadence

# Common Confusions
- Haydn sometimes restates PT where ST would normally appear (monothematic sonata)
- The "opposition" is TONAL, not just thematic
- Multiple STs are common
- The transition is crucial for modulation
