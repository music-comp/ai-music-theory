# Quick Definition
Direct modulation (phrase modulation) moves suddenly to a new key without pivot chords or dominant preparation -- the most common type of modulation in popular music, where it is called a "key change."

# Core Definition
Direct modulation (also known as phrase modulation) is a type of modulation where a composer decides to move suddenly to a new key without using pivot chords or preparing the new tonic with its dominant. There is no attempt to connect two different keys through any pivot chords or common tones. The modulation happens abruptly, often at a phrase boundary, frequently after a cadence (often a half cadence) in the first key. In popular music, this is called a "key change" and typically raises the key by a half step or whole step for the final chorus.

# Prerequisites
- **Modulation**: The broader concept of key change
- **Cadences**: Often precede the direct modulation

# Key Properties
1. No pivot chord connecting the two keys
2. No dominant preparation in the new key
3. Often occurs after a cadence in the first key
4. New key begins immediately, often on its tonic
5. May occur at section boundaries or phrase endings
6. Most common modulation type in popular music
7. Analysis: no pivot bracket needed; indicate end of key 1 and start of key 2

# Construction / Recognition
- A cadence ends the first key (often a half cadence)
- The new key begins immediately, usually on its tonic
- No transitional harmonies or common chords connect the keys
- The abruptness is intentional, not a compositional flaw
- In popular music: often a half-step or whole-step upward shift

# Context & Application
Direct modulation is the primary modulatory device in popular music, where key changes serve emotional intensification (especially for final choruses). In classical music, direct modulation creates abrupt, surprising shifts that can be dramatically effective. Schubert used direct modulation in his song cycles, sometimes juxtaposing distant keys without transition. The technique requires no harmonic preparation -- the new key is simply asserted.

# Examples
Classical:
- Schubert, Schwanegesang, "Liebesbotschaft" (1828) -- direct modulation
- Schubert, Schwanegesang, "Kriegers Ahnung" (1828) -- HC in C minor, then Ab major begins

Popular music:
- James Horner and Will Jennings, "My Heart Will Go On" (1997) -- key change at climax
- Beyonce et al., "Love on Top" (2011) -- multiple direct modulations
- Jon Bon Jovi et al., "Livin' on a Prayer" (1986) -- key change for final chorus

# Common Errors
- Looking for a pivot chord where none exists
- Treating the abruptness as an analytical failure

# Common Confusions
- **No pivot bracket**: Direct modulation requires no pivot bracket in analysis
- **Abruptness is intentional**: The lack of preparation IS the technique
- **"Key change" = "direct modulation"**: Same phenomenon, different terminology in pop vs. classical
