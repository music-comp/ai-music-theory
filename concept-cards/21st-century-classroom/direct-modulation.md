# Quick Definition
Direct modulation (phrase modulation) moves suddenly to a new key without pivot chords or dominant preparation, often occurring between phrases.

# Formal Definition
Direct modulation (also known as phrase modulation) is a type of modulation where a composer moves suddenly to a new key without using pivot chords or preparing the new tonic with its dominant. There is no attempt to connect the two keys smoothly; the modulation happens abruptly, often at a phrase boundary.

# Construction/Recognition
Characteristics:
- No pivot chord connecting the two keys
- Often occurs after a cadence (frequently half cadence) in the first key
- New key begins immediately, often on its tonic
- May occur at section boundaries or phrase endings
- No dominant preparation in the new key

Analysis:
- Indicate the end of the first key
- Mark the beginning of the new key
- No pivot bracket needed

# Musical Context
- Most common type of modulation in popular music ("key change")
- Used for dramatic effect and emotional intensification
- Often raises the key by a half step or whole step for final chorus
- In classical music, can create abrupt, surprising shifts

# Examples
Classical:
- Schubert, Schwanegesang, "Liebesbotschaft" (1828)
- Schubert, Schwanegesang, "Kriegers Ahnung" (1828) - HC in C minor, then begins in Ab major

Popular music:
- "My Heart Will Go On" (1997) - key change at climax
- Beyonce, "Love on Top" (2011) - multiple direct modulations
- Bon Jovi, "Livin' on a Prayer" (1986)

# Common Confusions
- Direct modulation has NO pivot chord
- "Key change" in popular music = direct modulation
- The abruptness is intentional, not a compositional flaw
- Often prepared by a cadence, but the new key has no harmonic connection
