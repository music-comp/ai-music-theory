# Quick Definition
Consonance refers to intervals that sound stable and pleasing; dissonance refers to intervals that sound tense and want to resolve.

# Formal Definition
Consonance and dissonance describe the relative stability or tension of intervals. Perfect consonances (P1, P5, P8) are the most stable. Imperfect consonances (M3, m3, M6, m6) are pleasant but less stable. Dissonances (m2, M2, +4/o5, m7, M7) create tension requiring resolution. These categories have evolved throughout music history.

# Construction/Recognition
- Perfect consonances: P1 (unison), P5, P8
- Imperfect consonances: M3, m3, M6, m6
- Dissonances: m2, M2, P4 (in some contexts), +4/o5, m7, M7
- Perfect fourth is contextually consonant or dissonant
- Dissonances typically resolve to consonances

# Musical Context
The interplay between consonance and dissonance creates tension and release, a fundamental element of musical expression. Dissonances make music interesting; consonances provide resolution. Different style periods treat dissonance differently.

# Examples
- P5 (C-G) sounds open and stable (perfect consonance)
- M3 (C-E) sounds pleasant and full (imperfect consonance)
- m2 (C-Db) sounds harsh and wants to resolve (dissonance)
- Tritone (B-F) is highly dissonant and resolves in cadences

# Common Confusions
- P4 can be consonant (in triads) or dissonant (against bass)
- Dissonance is not "bad"--it creates musical interest
- What counts as dissonant has changed throughout music history
