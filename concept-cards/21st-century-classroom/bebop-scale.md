# Quick Definition
The bebop scale is an eight-note scale that adds a chromatic passing tone to a seven-note scale, allowing chord tones to fall on strong beats when playing continuous eighth notes.

# Formal Definition
The bebop dominant scale is identical to the Mixolydian mode with an added natural 7th (passing tone between b7 and 8). Scale degrees: 1-2-3-4-5-6-b7-natural7. The added chromatic note creates an eight-note scale where chord tones (root, 3rd, 5th, 7th) consistently land on downbeats when playing descending eighth notes. This rhythmic alignment is characteristic of bebop improvisation.

# Construction/Calculation
Bebop Dominant Scale:
1. Start with Mixolydian: 1-2-3-4-5-6-b7
2. Add natural 7 as chromatic passing tone: 1-2-3-4-5-6-b7-natural7
3. In C: C-D-E-F-G-A-Bb-B
4. Used over dominant 7th chords

Bebop Major Scale:
- Adds #5 between 5 and 6: 1-2-3-4-5-#5-6-7
- In C: C-D-E-F-G-G#-A-B

# Musical Context
The bebop scale emerged from the bebop jazz style of the 1940s. When playing descending eighth notes starting on a downbeat from a chord tone, the chord tones (R, 3, 5, 7) fall on beats 1, 2, 3, 4 and passing tones fall on the "ands." This creates strong harmonic alignment in improvisation. Mark Levine's "The Jazz Theory Book" details four-part harmonizations of the bebop scale.

# Examples
- C bebop dominant: C-D-E-F-G-A-Bb-B (used over C7)
- Descending from root: C-B-Bb-A-G-F-E-D (R on 1, 7 on &, b7 on 2, etc.)
- Descending from 3rd: E-D-C-B-Bb-A-G-F
- C bebop major: C-D-E-F-G-G#-A-B (used over CΔ7)

# Common Confusions
- "Bebop scale" often refers to bebop dominant; there are also bebop major and bebop minor scales
- The added note is a chromatic passing tone, making it 8 notes, not 7
- The scale is typically used descending to align chord tones with downbeats
- Different from blues scale (which has 6 notes and different character)
