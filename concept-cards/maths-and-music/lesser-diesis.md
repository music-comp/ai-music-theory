# Quick Definition
The lesser diesis is the interval 128/125, approximately 41.06 cents, representing the amount by which three just major thirds fall short of one octave.

# Formal Definition
The lesser diesis is the rational interval corresponding to the ratio 2 / (5/4)^3 = 2 / (125/64) = 128/125 = 2^7 / 5^3 in Q+. It measures the deficit when three just major thirds are stacked: (5/4)^3 = 125/64 < 128/64 = 2. The interval is a 5-limit quantity with prime factorization 2^7 * 5^(-3). Measured in cents: 1200 * log2(128/125) ~ 41.06.

# Mathematical Context
The lesser diesis is the comma that must be accommodated in the mean-tone scale. Since the mean-tone system is designed so that every four consecutive fifths on the circle yield an exact just major third, and three major thirds do not equal an octave, twelve mean-tone fifths fall short of seven octaves by exactly one lesser diesis. This forces the placement of a wolf fifth that is sharp by ~41 cents -- nearly twice the comma of Pythagoras (~23 cents).

# Musical Context
The lesser diesis is significantly larger than the comma of Pythagoras, making the mean-tone scale's wolf fifth more disruptive than the Pythagorean scale's weak fifth. At ~41 cents (about two-fifths of a semitone), it is clearly audible and unacceptable as a musical interval. This is the primary limitation of mean-tone temperament and explains why the wolf fifth must be carefully placed away from commonly used keys.

# Examples
- (5/4)^3 = 125/64 ~ 1.953125 (short of 2 by the factor 128/125)
- 1200 * log2(128/125) ~ 41.06 cents
- Compare: comma of Pythagoras ~ 23.46 cents (lesser diesis is nearly twice as large)
- In mean-tone: wolf fifth absorbs the entire lesser diesis, producing ~737 cents vs. ~697 for the normal mean-tone fifth

# Common Confusions
- The lesser diesis (128/125) is not the same as the comma of Didymus (81/80) or the comma of Pythagoras (3^12/2^19) -- all three are small intervals but arise from different tuning problems
- The lesser diesis involves only primes 2 and 5, while the comma of Pythagoras involves only primes 2 and 3
- "Lesser" diesis distinguishes it from the "greater diesis" (648/625), which is the difference between four just minor thirds and an octave
