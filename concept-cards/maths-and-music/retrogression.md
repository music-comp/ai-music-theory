# Quick Definition
The reversal of the order of notes in a melodic sequence, analogous to horizontal reflection of a function's graph, creating a mirror image of the original melody.

# Formal Definition
Retrogression is the inversion of the order of notes in a sequence, producing a reflection of the initial melody. It is analogous to the mathematical notion of horizontal reflection, exemplified by replacing $y = f(x)$ with $y = f(-x)$, which reflects the graph around the $y$-axis. (Wright writes $y = -f(x)$ in the text, but the musical operation is a time-reversal, corresponding to horizontal reflection.)

# Mathematical Context
Retrogression is an involution: applying it twice returns the original sequence. If a melody is represented as a sequence of pitches $(p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n)$, the retrograde is $(p_n, p_{n-1}, \ldots, p_1)$. A melody exhibiting symmetry around a central point is its own retrograde (a palindrome). Retrogression preserves the set of intervals but reverses their order and negates their direction.

# Musical Context
Retrogression creates a mirror-image melody. When a melody has approximate palindromic structure, the listener perceives symmetry around a central point. Retrogression is used extensively in serial (twelve-tone) composition as one of the four basic row operations (prime, retrograde, inversion, retrograde inversion). It also appears in freer tonal contexts as a compositional device for creating coherence.

# Examples
- In the excerpt from "Raindrops Keep Falling On My Head," the melody exhibits symmetry around a central point marked with $\wedge$: the pitches on either side form an approximate retrograde of each other
- A simple retrograde: the sequence C-E-G becomes G-E-C
- In serial music, if the tone row is $\{0, 3, 7, 2, 11, \ldots\}$, the retrograde reverses this sequence

# Common Confusions
- Retrogression (time reversal of pitch order) vs. inversion (vertical reflection of intervals) -- both are mirror operations but in different dimensions
- "Retrogression" in Wright's usage means pitch-order reversal; in some other contexts, "retrogression" refers to harmonic motion (a chord progression moving "backward")
- Perfect palindromic melodies are rare; approximate retrogression (as in the Raindrops example) is more common
