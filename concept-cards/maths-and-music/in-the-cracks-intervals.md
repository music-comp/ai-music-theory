# Quick Definition
Integer ratios whose cent values fall nearly halfway between two adjacent semitones, making them poorly approximated by any note on the equally tempered keyboard. The primes 11 and 13 are the most prominent examples.

# Formal Definition
An integer n produces an "in the cracks" interval when the fractional part of 1200 * log2(n) / 100 is close to 0.5, meaning the interval lies approximately a quarter tone (50 cents) from the nearest keyboard notes. The approximation error approaches the theoretical maximum of 50 cents.

# Mathematical Context
- 1200 * log2(11) = 4151.32 cents: falls between 4100 (41 semitones) and 4200 (42 semitones), about 49 cents from the latter
- 1200 * log2(13) = 4440.53 cents: falls between 4400 (44 semitones) and 4500 (45 semitones), about 41 cents from the former
- These errors are close to the theoretical worst case of 50 cents (exactly between two semitones)

# Musical Context
The interval of 11 lies between three octaves plus a perfect fourth and three octaves plus a tritone. The interval of 13 lies near three octaves plus a minor sixth. Both intervals are truly foreign to the 12-tone equal-tempered system. They appear as overtones in natural sounds (the 11th and 13th harmonics) but cannot be meaningfully represented on a standard keyboard.

# Examples
- 11: ~4151.32 cents, closest keyboard note is 42 semitones (4200 cents), error ~49 cents sharp -- "truly in the cracks, lying about a quarter step from the closest tempered scale intervals"
- 13: ~4440.53 cents, closest keyboard note is 44 semitones (4400 cents), error ~41 cents flat
- By contrast, 3 has only ~2 cents error, and even 7 has "only" ~31 cents error

# Common Confusions
"In the cracks" does not mean the interval is musically useless -- these intervals exist naturally in the overtone series. It means only that the 12-tone equal-tempered keyboard cannot represent them. Other tuning systems or continuous-pitch instruments (voice, strings, trombone) can produce them.
