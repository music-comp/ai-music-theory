# Quick Definition
Harmony is the aspect of music in which different pitches are sounded simultaneously, creating vertical sonorities from the combination of tones.

# Formal Definition
Harmony refers to the simultaneous sounding of multiple pitches. It encompasses the study of chords (collections of note classes), their types (determined by modular intervals between constituent notes), their labeling (determined by juxtaposition with the tonic), and the patterns governing their succession in time.

# Mathematical Context
Harmonic structures are analyzed using modular arithmetic in Z_12, where intervals between simultaneously sounding pitches are measured in semitones modulo 12. Chords are defined as collections of note classes with specific sequences of modular intervals. The mathematical properties of these interval sequences -- particularly their cyclic symmetries -- determine whether a chord has a uniquely identifiable root.

# Musical Context
The earliest harmony in Western music consisted of parallel octaves, fourths, and fifths. Over the centuries, a rich array of harmonic patterns evolved. The basic harmonic building block is the chord, which is a collection of notes (usually three or more) sounded simultaneously. Chords have a type determined by their intervals modulo octave and a numerical label determined by their relationship to the tonic note of the key.

# Examples
- A simple harmonization of a melody in F major can use I -> V7 -> I (F -> C7 -> F)
- The same melody can be harmonized with I -> IIm -> IIIm, I -> IV -> V, or I -> bVII -> I, each giving a different character
- Parallel octaves, fourths, and fifths represent the earliest forms of Western harmony

# Common Confusions
- Harmony is not melody: harmony is the vertical (simultaneous) dimension of music, while melody is the horizontal (successive) dimension
- A single note is not harmony; harmony requires multiple pitches sounding together
- Harmony is not the same as consonance -- dissonant combinations are also part of harmony
