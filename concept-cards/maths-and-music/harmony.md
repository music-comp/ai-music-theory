# Quick Definition

Harmony is the aspect of music in which different pitches are sounded simultaneously. Its basic building block is the chord, a collection of notes (usually three or more) sounded at the same time.

# Core Definition

Harmony refers to the simultaneous sounding of multiple pitches. It encompasses the study of chords (collections of note classes), their types (determined by the sequence of modular intervals between constituent notes in Z_12), their labeling (determined by juxtaposition with the tonic note of the key), and the patterns governing their succession in time (Wright, p. 44).

# Key Properties

1. Harmony involves the vertical (simultaneous) dimension of music
2. The basic harmonic unit is the chord -- a collection of notes, usually three or more, sounded simultaneously
3. Chord type is determined by the intervals (modulo octave) between the notes
4. Chord labeling is determined by the chord's relationship to the tonic note of the key
5. Harmonic structures are analyzed using modular arithmetic in Z_12

# Construction / Recognition

## To Recognize Harmony

1. Identify whether multiple pitches are sounding simultaneously
2. Determine the note classes present (reducing pitches modulo octave)
3. Identify the chord type from the interval sequence between note classes
4. Determine the chord label from the root's relationship to the tonic

# Context & Application

The earliest harmony in Western music consisted of parallel octaves, fourths, and fifths. Over the centuries, a rich array of harmonic patterns and cliches evolved. Triads have been fundamental to Western harmony since the seventeenth century. Chapter 11 examines the mathematical basis for why certain chords sound consonant and others dissonant (Wright, p. 44).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 44): The simplest harmonies in early Western music used parallel octaves, fourths, and fifths.

**Example 2** (pp. 54-55): A melody in F major can be harmonized with I -> V7 -> I (F -> C7 -> F), but also with I -> IIm -> IIIm, I -> IV -> V, I -> bVII -> I, or I -> bVII -> V, each giving a different character.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Analyzing a passage's harmony by looking only at individual notes rather than simultaneous combinations
  **Correction**: Identify which notes sound together and reduce them to note classes to determine the chord

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing harmony is the same as consonance
  **Clarification**: Dissonant combinations are also part of harmony; harmony encompasses all simultaneous pitch combinations
- **Confusion**: Thinking a single note constitutes harmony
  **Clarification**: Harmony requires multiple pitches sounding together
