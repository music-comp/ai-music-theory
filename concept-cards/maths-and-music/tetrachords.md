# Quick Definition
A four-note sequence bounded by the interval of a perfect fourth, used as a building block of scales.

# Formal Definition
A tetrachord is a sequence of four consecutive notes spanning the interval of a perfect fourth (5 semitones). Two sequences of pitches are called equivalent if the sequence of respective intervals is the same. The standard (Ionian) scale contains two equivalent tetrachords: C-D-E-F and G-A-B-C, each having the interval pattern $1, 1, \frac{1}{2}$ (whole step, whole step, half step).

# Mathematical Context
Tetrachord equivalence is determined by matching interval sequences, which is an equivalence relation on sequences of notes. The major scale decomposes into two tetrachords separated by a whole step: $(1, 1, \frac{1}{2}) + 1 + (1, 1, \frac{1}{2})$. This decomposition is significant because it reveals the scale's internal symmetry and provides a constructive method for generating scales in different keys.

# Musical Context
The tetrachord concept dates back to ancient Greek music theory. In the standard major scale, the upper tetrachord of one key becomes the lower tetrachord of the key a fifth above. For example, G-A-B-C (upper tetrachord of C major) becomes the lower tetrachord of G major. This relationship underlies the circle of fifths and the systematic generation of key signatures.

# Examples
- C major lower tetrachord: C-D-E-F (intervals: $1, 1, \frac{1}{2}$)
- C major upper tetrachord: G-A-B-C (intervals: $1, 1, \frac{1}{2}$)
- These two tetrachords are equivalent because they share the same interval sequence
- The gap between the two tetrachords is one whole step (F to G)

# Common Confusions
- A tetrachord spans a perfect fourth (5 semitones), not four semitones -- the name refers to four notes, not four semitones
- Different scale types (minor, modal) have tetrachords with different interval patterns; the $1, 1, \frac{1}{2}$ pattern is specific to the major (Ionian) tetrachord
- The two tetrachords of the major scale are separated by a whole step, not directly adjacent
