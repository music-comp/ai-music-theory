# Quick Definition

A four-note sequence bounded by the interval of a perfect fourth, used as a building block of scales.

# Core Definition

A tetrachord is a sequence of four consecutive notes spanning the interval of a perfect fourth (5 semitones). Two sequences of pitches are called *equivalent* if the sequence of respective intervals is the same. The standard (Ionian) scale contains two equivalent tetrachords: C-D-E-F and G-A-B-C, each having the interval pattern $1, 1, \frac{1}{2}$ (Wright, p. 21).

# Prerequisites

- **Musical Intervals** — Tetrachords are defined by their interval pattern
- **Diatonic and Chromatic Scales** — Tetrachords are building blocks of the diatonic scale

# Key Properties

1. A tetrachord spans a perfect fourth (5 semitones) and contains 4 notes
2. The major-scale tetrachord has interval pattern $1, 1, \frac{1}{2}$ (whole, whole, half)
3. The major scale decomposes as: tetrachord + whole step + tetrachord
4. Tetrachord equivalence is determined by matching interval sequences
5. Different scale types (minor, modal) have tetrachords with different interval patterns

# Construction / Recognition

## To identify tetrachords in a scale:

1. Take the first four notes of the scale — this is the lower tetrachord
2. Verify the span is a perfect fourth (5 semitones)
3. Record the interval pattern
4. Take notes 5-8 — this is the upper tetrachord
5. Compare the interval patterns for equivalence

# Context & Application

The tetrachord concept dates back to ancient Greek music theory. In the standard major scale, the upper tetrachord of one key becomes the lower tetrachord of the key a fifth above. For example, G-A-B-C (upper tetrachord of C major) becomes the lower tetrachord of G major. This relationship underlies the circle of fifths and the systematic generation of key signatures.

# Examples

- C major lower tetrachord: C-D-E-F (intervals: $1, 1, \frac{1}{2}$) (p. 21)
- C major upper tetrachord: G-A-B-C (intervals: $1, 1, \frac{1}{2}$) (p. 21)
- These two tetrachords are equivalent because they share the same interval sequence
- The gap between the two tetrachords is one whole step (F to G)

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming a tetrachord spans four semitones
  **Correction**: The name refers to four *notes*, not four semitones; a tetrachord spans a perfect fourth (5 semitones)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking all tetrachords have the same interval pattern
  **Clarification**: Different scale types (minor, modal) have tetrachords with different patterns; the $1, 1, \frac{1}{2}$ pattern is specific to the major tetrachord
- **Confusion**: Believing the two tetrachords of the major scale are adjacent
  **Clarification**: They are separated by a whole step (F to G in C major)
