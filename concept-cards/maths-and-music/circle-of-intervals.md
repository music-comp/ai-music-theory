# Quick Definition
A circular arrangement showing all n-chromatic intervals obtained by successive iteration of a generating interval, generalizing the familiar circle of fifths.

# Formal Definition
For a generating interval $[m] \in \mathbb{Z}_n$ (where $\gcd(m, n) = 1$), the circle of intervals based on $[m]$ is the sequence $[0], [m], [2m], [3m], \ldots, [(n-1)m]$ arranged around a circle. Since $[m]$ is a generator, this sequence visits all $n$ elements of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ before returning to $[0]$.

# Mathematical Context
The circle of intervals is a visual representation of the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_n$ generated by $[m]$. The sequence of successive multiples of $[m]$ produces a permutation of all elements of $\mathbb{Z}_n$. Two generating intervals $[m]$ and $[n - m]$ produce the same circle traversed in opposite directions (clockwise vs. counterclockwise), since $[n - m] = -[m]$.

# Musical Context
The circle of fifths is the most famous example: iterating by 7 semitones (a fifth) in $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ produces C, G, D, A, E, B, F$\sharp$, C$\sharp$, G$\sharp$, D$\sharp$, A$\sharp$, F, then back to C. This circle is foundational for understanding key relationships, chord progressions, and modulation in Western tonal music. The circle of fourths is the same circle traversed in reverse.

# Examples
- In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$: the circle based on [7] (fifths) visits [0], [7], [2], [9], [4], [11], [6], [1], [8], [3], [10], [5]
- In $\mathbb{Z}_{14}$: the circle based on [5] visits [0], [5], [10], [1], [6], [11], [2], [7], [12], [3], [8], [13], [4], [9]
- Generating intervals $[m]$ and $[n-m]$ give the same circle in opposite directions: [5] and [7] in $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ (fourths and fifths)

# Common Confusions
- Only generating intervals produce circles that visit all note classes; non-generating intervals produce smaller cycles that miss some classes
- The circle of fifths and circle of fourths are the same circle traversed in opposite directions, not two different structures
