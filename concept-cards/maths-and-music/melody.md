# Quick Definition

The succession of single pitches with prescribed durations that forms the most prominent and recognizable line in a musical composition, defined equally by its pitch sequence and its rhythm.

# Core Definition

*Melody* is the succession of notes (single pitches with prescribed duration) that are most prominent in a musical composition and serve to define and characterize the piece. A melody is defined and made recognizable not only by its sequence of pitches but by its rhythm (Wright, pp. 37-38).

# Prerequisites

- **Rhythm** — Rhythm is an essential component of melodic identity
- **Pitch and Frequency** — Melody is a sequence of pitches over time

# Key Properties

1. A melody is defined by BOTH its pitch sequence AND its rhythm
2. Melody is the foreground line, distinct from accompaniment
3. Music achieves cohesion through repetition of melodic patterns with variation
4. Melodic transformations correspond to geometric transformations of the pitch-vs-time graph
5. A melody can be modeled as a sequence of ordered pairs $\{(p_i, d_i)\}$ (pitch, duration)

# Construction / Recognition

## To identify a melody:

1. Listen for the most prominent line (what a vocalist would sing)
2. Note both the pitch sequence and the rhythmic pattern
3. Either element alone does not fully define the melody

# Context & Application

In a song, the melody is what the solo vocalist sings while other notes are played in accompaniment. In a symphony, the melody is often (but not always) played by the highest instrument, typically the first violin section. Melodic repetition and transformation create musical cohesion.

# Examples

- A descending C major scale in even eighth notes is not recognizable as any song (p. 37)
- The same scale set to the rhythm of "Joy To The World" is immediately recognized (p. 37)
- In "When The Saints Go Marching In," the melodic sequence F-A-B$\flat$-C appears three times consecutively — melodic translation (p. 38)

## Related

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Defining melody by pitch sequence alone
  **Correction**: Rhythm is equally essential — the same pitches with different rhythms produce different melodies

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking melody includes harmony
  **Clarification**: Melody is the foreground line; harmony and accompaniment are separate
- **Confusion**: Assuming transformed melodies are unrelated to the original
  **Clarification**: Transposed, translated, or retrograded melodies maintain recognizable relationships to the original
