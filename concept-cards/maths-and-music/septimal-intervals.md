# Quick Definition
Septimal intervals are just intervals whose frequency ratios involve the prime number 7, producing a distinctive "blue" or "soulful" quality that lies noticeably away from equal-tempered keyboard notes.

# Formal Definition
Septimal intervals are rational intervals in Q+ whose unique prime factorization includes the prime 7 (i.e., they are 7-limit but not 5-limit intervals). The three principal septimal intervals are: the septimal minor seventh (7/4 ~ 968.83 cents, ~31 cents flat of the tempered minor seventh), the septimal whole tone (8/7 ~ 231.17 cents, ~31 cents sharp of the tempered step), and the septimal minor third (7/6 ~ 266.87 cents, ~33 cents flat of the tempered minor third).

# Mathematical Context
The prime 7 is the fourth prime after 2, 3, and 5, and introduces intervals that are significantly further from 12-tone equal temperament than 5-limit intervals. The septimal minor seventh (7/4) is the interval from the 4th to the 7th harmonic. The septimal whole tone (8/7) is its octave complement's complement: 2 / (7/4) = 8/7. The septimal minor third (7/6) is the interval from the 6th to the 7th harmonic.

# Musical Context
Septimal intervals impart a distinctive texture described as "blue" or "soulful." They are characteristic of blues, jazz, and a cappella vocal styles such as barbershop singing. The septimal minor seventh (7/4) appears in the justly tuned seventh chord 4:5:6:7, which has a "laser-like" consonance despite the seventh sounding "annoyingly flat" compared to its keyboard approximation. This low seventh is commonly heard in blues and barbershop harmony.

# Examples
- Septimal minor seventh: 7/4, 1200 * log2(7/4) ~ 968.83 cents (~31 cents flat of tempered minor seventh at 1000 cents)
- Septimal whole tone: 8/7, 1200 * log2(8/7) ~ 231.17 cents (~31 cents sharp of tempered step at 200 cents)
- Septimal minor third: 7/6, 1200 * log2(7/6) ~ 266.87 cents (~33 cents flat of tempered minor third at 300 cents)
- Just seventh chord 4:5:6:7 uses the septimal seventh for perfect consonance
- The "blue note" in blues music is often close to a septimal interval

# Common Confusions
- The septimal minor seventh (7/4 ~ 969 cents) is very different from the just minor seventh (9/5 ~ 1018 cents) -- they are separated by about 49 cents
- Septimal intervals are not "out of tune" -- they are precisely tuned intervals that simply do not align well with 12-tone equal temperament
- The ~31 cent deviation from keyboard notes is substantial (about a third of a semitone) and clearly audible
