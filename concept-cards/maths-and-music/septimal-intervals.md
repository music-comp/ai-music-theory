# Quick Definition

Septimal intervals are just intervals whose frequency ratios involve the prime number 7, producing a distinctive "blue" or "soulful" quality that lies noticeably away from equal-tempered keyboard notes.

# Core Definition

Septimal intervals are rational intervals in Q+ whose unique prime factorization includes the prime 7. Wright describes: "The prime 7 introduces us to still more just intervals, and these intervals are not so well approximated by 12-tone equal temperament. Three examples are the septimal minor seventh, with ratio 7/4, its complement the septimal whole tone, with ratio 8/7, and the septimal minor third, with ratio 7/6. These intervals are sufficiently far away from keyboard notes as to impart a texture that is sometimes called 'blue,' or 'soulful'" (p. 143).

# Prerequisites

- **Just interval** -- Septimal intervals are a class of just intervals
- **p-limit tuning** -- Septimal intervals are 7-limit but not 5-limit

# Key Properties

1. Involve the prime 7 in their frequency ratios
2. Septimal minor seventh: 7/4 ~ 968.83 cents (~31 cents flat of tempered minor seventh)
3. Septimal whole tone: 8/7 ~ 231.17 cents (~31 cents sharp of tempered whole step)
4. Septimal minor third: 7/6 ~ 266.87 cents (~33 cents flat of tempered minor third)
5. Significantly further from equal temperament than 5-limit intervals
6. Produce "blue" or "soulful" texture in music

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify the interval between harmonics involving the 7th harmonic
2. Septimal minor seventh = interval from 4th to 7th harmonic (7/4)
3. Septimal whole tone = octave complement of septimal seventh: 2 / (7/4) = 8/7
4. Septimal minor third = interval from 6th to 7th harmonic (7/6)
5. Listen for intervals ~30 cents away from keyboard notes with a distinctive "blue" quality

# Context & Application

Septimal intervals are characteristic of blues, jazz, and a cappella vocal styles such as barbershop singing. The septimal minor seventh (7/4) appears in the justly tuned seventh chord 4:5:6:7, which Wright describes as having "laser-like" perfect consonance despite the seventh sounding "annoyingly flat" compared to its keyboard approximation. "This low seventh is often heard in blues, some jazz, and some a cappella vocal styles such as barbershop" (p. 149).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 143): Septimal minor seventh 7/4: 1200 * log2(7/4) ~ 968.83 cents, about 31 cents flat of the keyboard minor seventh.

**Example 2** (p. 143): Septimal whole tone 8/7: 1200 * log2(8/7) ~ 231.17 cents, about 31 cents sharp of the keyboard step.

**Example 3** (p. 143): Septimal minor third 7/6: 1200 * log2(7/6) ~ 266.87 cents, about 33 cents flat of the keyboard minor third.

**Example 4** (p. 148): The just seventh chord 4:5:6:7 uses the septimal seventh for "laser-like" consonance.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating septimal intervals as "out of tune"
  **Correction**: They are precisely tuned intervals that simply do not align well with 12-tone equal temperament

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking the septimal minor seventh (7/4 ~ 969 cents) and the just minor seventh (9/5 ~ 1018 cents) are similar
  **Clarification**: They are separated by about 49 cents -- almost half a semitone

- **Confusion**: Assuming higher primes always produce less consonant chords
  **Clarification**: The septimal seventh chord 4:5:6:7 is more consonant than the 5-limit version 20:25:30:36 because it uses smaller integers overall
