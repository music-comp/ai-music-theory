# Quick Definition

Equal temperament enables harmonic freedom -- the ability to modulate freely between any keys and use any chord root with equal tuning quality -- which was impossible in unequal temperaments where some keys were poorly tuned.

# Core Definition

Wright describes the liberation provided by equal temperament: "In the equally tempered scale, one has to accept sharp thirds and sevenths, but all chords of the same type are tuned precisely the same, regardless of their root relative to the key. While this imperfect tuning was a bitter pill to swallow, it allowed composers and performers to use extensive harmonic variety and freely modulate from one key to another" (p. 158). The mathematical basis is the uniformity of equal temperament: every semitone is exactly 2^(1/12), so transposition preserves all interval relationships.

# Key Properties

1. All chords of the same type are identically tuned regardless of root
2. Transposition is a pure mathematical shift preserving interval relationships
3. Free modulation between any keys within a composition
4. Enharmonic equivalence: G# and Ab are the same pitch
5. Achieved at the cost of uniform imperfection (all non-octave intervals are irrational)
6. Unlocked chromatic harmony, enharmonic modulation, and key-independent progressions

# Construction / Recognition

Harmonic freedom is recognized when:
1. A composition modulates through multiple keys without audible degradation
2. Chord progressions traverse the full circle of fifths smoothly
3. Enharmonic reinterpretation (e.g., G# as Ab) is used for modulation

# Context & Application

The acceptance of equal temperament unlocked compositional techniques defining Western music from the 18th century onward. Wright cites two examples: Bach's "Well-Tempered Clavier" (48 preludes and fugues in all 24 keys) and "Franz Liszt's classic piano piece Liebestraum which extensively traverses the circle of fifths, modulates several times, and uses every root note in the chromatic scale" (p. 159). "A great deal of 19th and 20th century music is deeply entrenched in this liberation" (p. 158).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 159): Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier: 48 pieces in all 24 major and minor keys.

**Example 2** (p. 159): Liszt's Liebestraum: "extensively traverses the circle of fifths, modulates several times, and uses every root note in the chromatic scale of its initial (and final) key, A-flat."

**Example 3**: Enharmonic modulation (treating G# as Ab): only possible when both notes have the same pitch.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Thinking harmonic freedom is a property of the music rather than the tuning system
  **Correction**: Unequal temperaments allow the same notes but with different tuning quality across keys; harmonic freedom is about uniform tuning quality

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking instruments with continuous pitch need equal temperament for harmonic freedom
  **Clarification**: Voice, violin, and other flexible-pitch instruments can adjust intonation in real time, achieving freedom without equal temperament

- **Confusion**: Thinking harmonic freedom has no cost
  **Clarification**: Freedom comes at the cost of uniformly imperfect intervals and the loss of distinctive key characters
