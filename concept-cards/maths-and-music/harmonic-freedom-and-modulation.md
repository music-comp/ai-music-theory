# Quick Definition
Equal temperament enables harmonic freedom -- the ability to modulate freely between any keys and use any chord root -- which was impossible in unequal temperaments where some keys were poorly tuned.

# Formal Definition
Harmonic freedom refers to the ability to use chords built on any of the twelve chromatic roots with equal quality of tuning, and to modulate (change key) freely within a composition. In unequal temperaments, a keyboard tuned in one key produces distractingly out-of-tune results in distant keys. Equal temperament eliminates this constraint by making all chords of the same type identically tuned, regardless of root, at the cost of accepting uniformly imperfect (irrational) intervals.

# Mathematical Context
The mathematical basis for harmonic freedom is the uniformity of equal temperament: every semitone is exactly 2^(1/12), so transposition is a pure mathematical shift that preserves all interval relationships. In unequal temperaments, transposition changes the actual interval ratios (e.g., a C major triad and a D major triad have different tunings in just intonation), making key changes audibly disruptive. Equal temperament's uniformity is achieved precisely because all its intervals are irrational -- no key is privileged by proximity to just ratios.

# Musical Context
The acceptance of equal temperament unlocked compositional techniques that define Western music from the 18th century onward: extensive modulation, enharmonic reinterpretation, chromatic harmony, and key-independent chord progressions. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier demonstrated viability in all 24 keys. Liszt's Liebestraum extensively traverses the circle of fifths and uses every chromatic root note. A "great deal of 19th and 20th century music is deeply entrenched in this liberation."

# Examples
- Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier: 48 pieces in all 24 keys
- Liszt's Liebestraum: modulates several times, traverses the full circle of fifths
- Enharmonic modulation (e.g., treating G# as Ab): only possible when both notes have the same pitch
- Circle-of-fifths progressions through all keys: require uniform fifths
- Chromatic harmony of Wagner, Debussy, and later: built on equal-temperament uniformity

# Common Confusions
- Harmonic freedom is a property of the tuning system, not the music itself -- unequal temperaments allow the same notes but with different tuning quality
- "Freedom" comes at a cost: all intervals are slightly imperfect, and the distinctive key characters of unequal temperaments are lost
- Instruments with continuous pitch (voice, violin) can have harmonic freedom without equal temperament, since they can adjust intonation in real time
