# Quick Definition
Overtone singing is a vocal technique in which the singer manipulates the mouth, tongue, and jaw to isolate specific overtones, making individual harmonics audible as distinct pitches while holding a constant fundamental.

# Formal Definition
In overtone singing, the singer maintains a constant fundamental frequency F while adjusting the resonating cavities of the mouth and throat to selectively amplify one overtone at a time. This effectively creates a narrow, movable formant that isolates the k-th harmonic (frequency kF), making it perceptible as a separate pitch above the drone of the fundamental.

# Mathematical Context
In the Fourier series g(t) = C + sum d_k*sin(2*pi*F*k*t + beta_k), the singer manipulates the amplitudes d_k by creating resonance conditions that dramatically boost one particular d_k while leaving others at normal levels. The boosted harmonic becomes perceptually separated from the integrated timbre of the remaining harmonics.

# Musical Context
While holding a constant fundamental pitch, the overtone singer can "play a tune" by moving through successive overtones. The available pitches are the overtone series: 2F, 3F, 4F, 5F, ..., which provides a natural scale of integer multiples. This technique is practiced in various traditions, notably Tuvan throat singing and Mongolian khoomei.

# Examples
- A singer holding F2 as the fundamental can produce audible overtones at F3, C4, F4, A4, C5, etc.
- The melody follows the overtone series, constrained to integer multiples of the fundamental
- Higher overtones (11th, 13th harmonics) produce "in the cracks" intervals not found on the keyboard

# Common Confusions
Overtone singing does not produce two independent vocal sources. The singer produces one fundamental pitch; the audible overtone is a natural component of that pitch that has been selectively amplified through resonance manipulation. The technique demonstrates that overtones are real frequency components, not abstractions.
