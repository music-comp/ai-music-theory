# Quick Definition
The diminished triad is a three-note chord defined by the interval sequence (3, 3, 6) in semitones, consisting of a root, a minor third, and a diminished fifth (tritone) above the root.

# Formal Definition
The diminished triad is defined by the sequence of modular intervals (3, 3, 6) in Z_12:

root --(3)--> third --(3)--> fifth --(6)--> (root)

The cyclic permutations of (3, 3, 6) are (3, 3, 6), (3, 6, 3), and (6, 3, 3), which are all distinct. Therefore the chord has a discernible root.

# Mathematical Context
The interval sequence (3, 3, 6) sums to 12. Although the first two intervals are equal (both 3), the presence of the distinct interval 6 breaks full cyclic symmetry, allowing root identification. The diminished triad contains two minor thirds stacked, spanning a tritone (6 semitones) from root to fifth.

# Musical Context
The diminished triad has a tense, unstable quality due to the tritone interval between root and fifth. It naturally occurs on scale degree VII in the major mode (e.g., B-D-F in C major). It often functions as a leading-tone chord resolving to the tonic. The chord is labeled with suffix "dim" or the degree symbol (e.g., Bdim or B^0).

# Examples
- B diminished: B-D-F, with intervals (3, 3, 6)
- The chord naturally occurs on scale degree VII in major keys
- Rules of chord spelling tend to be followed less rigorously for diminished triads

# Common Confusions
- The diminished triad (3, 3, 6) should not be confused with the diminished seventh chord (3, 3, 3, 3)
- Despite having two equal intervals, the diminished triad does have a discernible root (unlike the augmented triad)
- The "fifth" of a diminished triad is a diminished fifth (tritone), not a perfect fifth
