# Quick Definition
The justly tuned major triad comprises harmonics 4, 5, and 6 of a fundamental tone, with the ratio 4:5:6 giving a just major third from root to third and a just fifth from root to fifth.

# Formal Definition
A just rendition of the major triad is given by the ratio 4:5:6, meaning the three pitches have frequencies in the proportion 4:5:6. The interval from root to third is 5/4 (just major third), from root to fifth is 6/4 = 3/2 (just fifth), and from third to fifth is 6/5 (just minor third). With the root doubled in "tenth voicing" (third a tenth above the lowest root), the chord is realized as 2:3:4:5. The presence of this chord among the low harmonics explains why it is "so pleasing and so basic in music."

# Mathematical Context
The major triad 4:5:6 appears in the harmonic series as consecutive harmonics starting at the 4th. Each component interval is a just interval involving only the primes 2, 3, and 5. The chord is the foundational structure of 5-limit harmony: in the just intonation scale, the I, IV, and V chords are all tuned as 4:5:6. The minor triad, by contrast, requires higher harmonics (10:12:15), involving larger integers and thus sounding less "primal."

# Musical Context
The major triad is the most fundamental chord in Western tonal music, and its natural occurrence in the harmonic series provides the physical explanation for its consonance. In the just intonation scale, three major triads (I, IV, V) are tuned to 4:5:6, making them perfectly consonant. The equal-tempered major triad approximates this but with a notably sharp third (400 cents vs. 386 cents). Performers with pitch flexibility naturally gravitate toward the 4:5:6 tuning.

# Examples
- C major triad in just intonation: C-E-G with frequencies in ratio 4:5:6
- F2 harmonics 4, 5, 6 give an F major triad
- Doubled root (tenth voicing): harmonics 2, 3, 4, 5 of F2
- Minor triad for comparison: 10:12:15 (larger integers, less consonant)
- The I, IV, and V chords in just intonation are all 4:5:6

# Common Confusions
- The 4:5:6 ratio gives the chord in root position within an octave -- other voicings have different ratios
- The tempered major triad is not 4:5:6 -- its third is about 14 cents sharp, which is audible in sustained chords
- The "presence in the harmonic series" argument explains consonance but does not prescribe that only this tuning is valid
