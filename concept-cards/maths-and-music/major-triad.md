# Quick Definition
The major triad is a three-note chord defined by the interval sequence (4, 3, 5) in semitones, consisting of a root, a major third above the root, and a perfect fifth above the root.

# Formal Definition
The major triad is defined by the sequence of modular intervals (4, 3, 5) in Z_12 between successive note classes:

root --(4)--> third --(3)--> fifth --(5)--> (root)

The three notes are called root, third, and fifth respectively. The sequence (4, 3, 5) has no non-trivial cyclic symmetries, meaning no non-trivial cyclic permutation of the sequence yields the same sequence. Therefore the root, third, and fifth are uniquely determined.

# Mathematical Context
The interval sequence (4, 3, 5) sums to 12 (the octave in semitones), as required for any chord's interval sequence in Z_12. The absence of non-trivial cyclic symmetries in this sequence means the three cyclic permutations -- (4,3,5), (3,5,4), and (5,4,3) -- are all distinct. This is what makes the root uniquely identifiable from the pitch class content alone.

# Musical Context
The major triad has been a fundamental building block of Western harmony since the seventeenth century. It consists of a root with a major third (4 semitones) and a perfect fifth (7 semitones) above it. The chord is labeled by its root with no suffix (e.g., "C" denotes C major). In Roman numeral analysis, the diatonic major triads in a major key occur on scale degrees I, IV, and V.

# Examples
- C major triad: C-E-G (root C, third E, fifth G)
- F major triad: F-A-C (root F, third A, fifth C)
- Regardless of voicing, the root is always identifiable due to the asymmetric interval sequence

# Common Confusions
- The major triad is often called simply "major chord"; when other chords containing it are discussed (like the seventh chord), precision requires "major triad"
- The root is not necessarily the lowest-sounding note in a given voicing
- The interval from root to fifth is 4 + 3 = 7 semitones (a perfect fifth), not 5
