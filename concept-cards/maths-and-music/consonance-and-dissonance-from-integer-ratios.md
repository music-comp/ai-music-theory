# Quick Definition
Musical consonance correlates with simple integer frequency ratios: smaller integers and lower primes tend to produce more consonant intervals, while larger primes produce more dissonant or unfamiliar ones.

# Formal Definition
The consonance of an interval relates to the simplicity of the integer ratio approximating it. The octave (2:1) is the most consonant interval. The fifth (3:2) and fourth (4:3) follow. As the integers in the ratio grow, particularly when large primes are involved (7, 11, 13), the intervals become increasingly dissonant or fall outside familiar musical categories.

# Mathematical Context
Each integer's musical "personality" is determined by its prime factorization. Powers of 2 give octaves (maximally consonant). Products involving only 2 and 3 give intervals within the Pythagorean system. Including 5 extends to just intonation. Primes 7, 11, and 13 give intervals not well represented in the Western 12-tone system.

# Musical Context
Harmony derives from the integers. The sequence of integer ratios from a fundamental generates the overtone series, and the consonance hierarchy of Western music reflects the order of these integers: octave (2), fifth (3), major third (5). The poor keyboard approximation of 7, 11, and 13 suggests these intervals are foreign to the equal-tempered system.

# Examples
- 2:1 (octave): maximally consonant, two notes "blend as one"
- 3:2 (fifth): highly consonant, ~2 cents error on keyboard
- 5:4 (major third): consonant but ~14 cents error -- historically controversial
- 7:4 (natural seventh): ~31 cents from keyboard minor seventh, dissonant in tempered tuning
- 11:8: ~49 cents error, "in the cracks" -- no close keyboard equivalent

# Common Confusions
Consonance from integer ratios should not be confused with consonance in equal temperament. The tempered major third (400 cents) is 14 cents sharper than the pure 5:4 ratio (386 cents). Historical resistance to equal temperament stemmed largely from this discrepancy.
