# Quick Definition

Musical consonance correlates with simple integer frequency ratios: smaller integers and lower primes produce more consonant intervals, while larger primes produce more dissonant or unfamiliar ones. The hierarchy follows the order of prime numbers.

# Core Definition

The consonance of an interval relates to the simplicity of the integer ratio. The octave (2:1) is the most consonant: "when two notes an octave apart are sounded they blend together almost as one" (Wright, Ch. 9, p. 110). The fifth (3:2) follows, then the major third (5:4). As the primes grow, intervals become increasingly foreign to the Western tonal system. Wright observes that "harmony derives from the integers" (p. 112).

# Key Properties

1. The consonance hierarchy follows the sequence of primes: 2 > 3 > 5 > 7 > 11 > 13
2. Powers of 2 give octaves (maximally consonant)
3. Products of 2 and 3 give Pythagorean intervals
4. Including 5 extends to just intonation intervals
5. Primes 7, 11, 13 give intervals poorly represented in the 12-tone system

# Construction / Recognition

## Consonance hierarchy from the chapter:
1. Ratio 2: octave -- maximally consonant, notes "blend as one"
2. Ratio 3: ~octave + fifth -- very consonant, ~2 cents error
3. Ratio 5: ~two octaves + major third -- consonant, ~14 cents error (historically controversial)
4. Ratio 7: ~two octaves + minor seventh -- ~31 cents error, distinctly foreign
5. Ratio 11: ~49 cents error, "truly in the cracks"
6. Ratio 13: ~41 cents error, poorly approximated

# Context & Application

The integers whose intervals are well-approximated by the keyboard (2, 3, 5) correspond to the intervals that form the basis of Western tonal harmony. The historical controversy over equal temperament centered on the 14-cent error for ratio 5 (the major third). The poor approximation of 7, 11, 13 reflects their absence from standard Western harmony.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 110): Ratio 2 -- the octave is "music's most consonant interval."

**Example 2** (p. 112): Ratio 5 -- the tempered major third is "sharp by about 14 cents. [...] This difference is perceptible, upon a careful listening."

**Example 3** (p. 113): Ratio 7 -- "the lowest integer which is poorly approximated by the tempered chromatic scale," about 31 cents sharp.

**Example 4** (p. 114): Ratio 11 -- "truly 'in the cracks', lying about a quarter step from the closest tempered scale intervals."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming all integer ratios are consonant
  **Correction**: Only small-integer ratios (involving small primes) are consonant; ratios involving 7, 11, 13 are foreign to the tempered system

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Conflating consonance from integer ratios with consonance in equal temperament
  **Clarification**: The tempered major third (400 cents) is 14 cents sharper than the pure 5:4 ratio (386 cents); these are related but distinct consonance concepts
