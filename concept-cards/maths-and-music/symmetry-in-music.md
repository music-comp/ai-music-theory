# Quick Definition
The general phenomenon of transformations and repeating sections in music, where a compositional goal is to balance unity and contrast through patterns of repetition, transposition, and reflection.

# Formal Definition
Symmetry in music refers to the presence of transformations (translations, transpositions, retrogressions) and repeating sections. A compositional goal is to create balanced portions of unity and contrast -- enough repetition to give a piece interest and cohesion, but not so much as to make it repetitive or boring. Symmetry operates at multiple levels: within phrases, within sections, and across the entire form.

# Mathematical Context
Musical symmetry corresponds to mathematical symmetry groups acting on melodic and rhythmic structures. The transformations that generate symmetries include:
- Translation (horizontal shift): repeating patterns in time
- Transposition (vertical shift): repeating patterns at different pitch levels
- Retrogression (reflection): creating mirror-image patterns
These compose to form a group of transformations. A piece with high symmetry has many such transformation relationships among its parts.

# Musical Context
Symmetry manifests at multiple scales simultaneously. In "O Tannenbaum," the overall form is AABA, but each section also has internal symmetries: the A section incorporates melodic transposition and rhythmic translation, while the B section features downward diatonic transposition. This multi-level symmetry creates both large-scale coherence (through form) and local interest (through motivic development).

# Examples
- "O Tannenbaum": AABA form (large-scale symmetry) with melodic transposition and rhythmic translation within sections (local symmetry), including diatonic transposition in the B section
- Ragtime: repeating sections (AA BB A CC DD) create formal symmetry
- "Raindrops Keep Falling On My Head": palindromic melody around a central point (reflective symmetry)
- "When The Saints Go Marching In": three-fold melodic translation (translational symmetry)

# Common Confusions
- Musical symmetry is approximate, not exact -- slight variations and embellishments are normal and expected
- "Symmetry" in music is broader than in mathematics: it encompasses any recognizable pattern of repetition or transformation, not just strict mathematical symmetry
- Too much symmetry can make music boring; effective composition balances symmetry with contrast
