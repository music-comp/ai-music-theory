# Quick Definition
Triads are chords containing exactly three distinct note classes. They have been a fundamental element of Western harmony since the seventeenth century.

# Formal Definition
A triad is a chord consisting of exactly three note classes modulo octave. The four standard triad types discussed in Western music theory are defined by their interval sequences in Z_12: major (4, 3, 5), minor (3, 4, 5), diminished (3, 3, 6), and augmented (4, 4, 4). Music that primarily features triads is sometimes called "triadic."

# Mathematical Context
Each triad type corresponds to a partition of 12 into three positive integers, ordered as a cyclic sequence. The four standard triads exhaust the most musically significant such partitions. Two of them (major, minor) have asymmetric interval sequences with unique roots; the diminished triad also has a unique root; the augmented triad's fully symmetric sequence (4, 4, 4) means it has no discernible root.

# Musical Context
Triads are the simplest chords and the foundation of triadic harmony, which dominated Western music from approximately 1600 to 1900. The major and minor triads are by far the most common. The diminished triad often appears as a passing chord, while the augmented triad is used for its ambiguous, floating quality. The term "triadic" describes music built primarily on these three-note chords.

# Examples
- Major triad (4, 3, 5): C-E-G
- Minor triad (3, 4, 5): A-C-E
- Diminished triad (3, 3, 6): B-D-F
- Augmented triad (4, 4, 4): C-E-G#

# Common Confusions
- "Triad" refers to three note *classes*, not three specific notes -- a voicing may use more than three notes through octave doubling
- The augmented triad is the only standard triad without a discernible root
- Triads are defined modulo octave; the specific voicing does not change the triad type
