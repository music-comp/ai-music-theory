# Quick Definition

A just interval is a musical interval whose frequency ratio can be expressed as a ratio n:m of small positive integers, producing a consonant or harmonious sound.

# Core Definition

"Two pitches sounded simultaneously create an effect that we are prone to call 'harmonious' or 'consonant' when the ratio of their frequencies can be expressed as a ratio n:m where m and n are small positive integers. The smaller the integers, the more consonant the interval. We refer to such intervals as just intervals" (Wright, p. 138). Just intervals are a subset of rational intervals, distinguished by the practical criterion that the integers involved are "small" -- though this threshold is not precisely defined.

# Prerequisites

- **Rational interval** -- A just interval is a special case of a rational interval, so the concept of frequency ratios as elements of Q+ must be understood first

# Key Properties

1. Frequency ratio expressible as n:m with small positive integers n and m
2. Smaller integers produce more consonant intervals
3. The boundary between "just" and "merely rational" is not precisely defined
4. Just intervals involving only primes 2, 3, and 5 are the most fundamental (5-limit)
5. Higher primes (7, 11, 13, ...) introduce progressively more exotic intervals
6. Just intervals can be physically constructed using string fretting

# Construction / Recognition

## Consonance Hierarchy

The most consonant just intervals, in rough order:
1. Octave: 2/1 (most consonant)
2. Just fifth: 3/2 (~701.96 cents)
3. Just fourth: 4/3 (~498.04 cents)
4. Just major third: 5/4 (~386.31 cents)
5. Just minor third: 6/5 (~315.64 cents)
6. Just semitone: 16/15 (~111.73 cents, less consonant due to larger integers)

# Context & Application

The perception that small-integer ratios produce consonance has been recognized since antiquity. Just intervals form the basis of tuning systems that predate equal temperament, including Pythagorean tuning (3-limit), just intonation (5-limit), and various systems incorporating septimal intervals (7-limit). While equal temperament approximates these just intervals, the irrationality theorem proves that no equal-tempered interval except multi-octaves is exactly just.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 138): The just fifth (3/2) and just major third (5/4) are paradigmatic just intervals involving the smallest primes.

**Example 2** (p. 143): The just semitone (16/15) has larger numerator and denominator than other basic just intervals, giving "an interval that might be considered 'less just,' and which one might expect to be less consonant."

**Example 3** (p. 143): Septimal intervals like 7/4 involve the prime 7 and produce textures "sometimes called 'blue' or 'soulful'."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating "just" as a synonym for "in tune" or "correct"
  **Correction**: "Just" specifically means the ratio is expressible with small positive integers; equal-tempered intervals are deliberately not just

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing "just" means "exactly equal to the tempered version"
  **Clarification**: Just intervals are defined by small-integer ratios and most differ slightly from their equal-tempered approximations

- **Confusion**: Thinking there is a precise cutoff between "just" and "merely rational"
  **Clarification**: The boundary depends on how small the integers need to be; this is a matter of convention and perception, not strict definition

- **Confusion**: Assuming just intervals can be maintained across all keys simultaneously
  **Clarification**: Just intervals can exist only between specific pitches; they cannot be maintained across all keys on a fixed-pitch instrument, which is why equal temperament was adopted
