# Quick Definition
A just interval is a musical interval whose frequency ratio can be expressed as a ratio of small positive integers, producing a consonant or harmonious sound.

# Formal Definition
An interval is called just when the ratio of the frequencies of its two pitches can be expressed as n:m where m and n are small positive integers. The smaller the integers involved, the more consonant the interval is perceived to be. Just intervals are a subset of rational intervals, distinguished by the practical criterion that the integers involved are "small" (though this threshold is not precisely defined).

# Mathematical Context
Just intervals correspond to elements of Q+ with small prime factorizations. The primes appearing in the factorization and the magnitude of the exponents determine how "just" an interval is considered. Intervals involving only the primes 2, 3, and 5 with small exponents are the most fundamental just intervals (5-limit tuning). Higher primes (7, 11, 13, ...) introduce progressively more exotic intervals.

# Musical Context
The perception that small-integer ratios produce consonance has been recognized since antiquity. Just intervals form the basis of tuning systems that predate equal temperament. The consonance hierarchy roughly follows: octave (2:1) > fifth (3:2) > fourth (4:3) > major third (5:4) > minor third (6:5), with intervals involving larger integers sounding progressively less consonant.

# Examples
- Octave: 2/1 (most consonant)
- Just fifth: 3/2 (~702 cents)
- Just fourth: 4/3 (~498 cents)
- Just major third: 5/4 (~386 cents)
- Just minor third: 6/5 (~316 cents)
- Just semitone: 16/15 (~112 cents, considered "less just" due to larger integers)

# Common Confusions
- "Just" does not mean "exactly equal to the tempered version" -- just intervals are defined by small-integer ratios, and most differ slightly from their equal-tempered approximations
- The boundary between "just" and "merely rational" is not precisely defined -- it depends on how small the integers need to be
- Just intervals can exist only between two specific pitches; they cannot be maintained across all keys simultaneously (which is why equal temperament was adopted)
