# Quick Definition

Each prime number defines a musically irreducible interval with a unique sonic character. Composite integers inherit their musical personality from the combination of their prime factors, with each prime introducing a distinct quality.

# Core Definition

Each positive integer possesses "a unique 'personality' which seems determined by the integer's prime factorization" (Wright, Ch. 9, p. 110). Since the monoid of integral intervals is generated by the primes, every integral interval decomposes uniquely into prime intervals. The cent value $1200 \log_2(n) = 1200(\alpha_1 \log_2 p_1 + \alpha_2 \log_2 p_2 + \cdots)$ shows that errors are additive across prime factors.

# Key Properties

1. The musical character of a composite integer is built from its prime factors
2. The approximation error for a composite is the sum of errors of its prime factors (because keyboard renders powers of 2 exactly)
3. Each prime introduces a distinct quality foreign to all previous primes
4. Higher primes produce intervals increasingly foreign to equal temperament

# Construction / Recognition

## The personality hierarchy of primes:
1. **2**: The octave -- so fundamental it defines pitch class equivalence
2. **3**: Introduces the perfect fifth (3:2), foundation of Pythagorean tuning (~2 cents error)
3. **5**: Introduces the major third (5:4), foundation of just intonation (~14 cents error)
4. **7**: Introduces the "natural seventh" (~31 cents off keyboard), foreign to equal temperament
5. **11**: Profoundly foreign (~49 cents off), truly "in the cracks"
6. **13**: Also poorly approximated (~41 cents off)

# Context & Application

Understanding prime interval personality reveals why certain intervals sound consonant or dissonant and why the Western 12-tone system privileges certain intervals over others. Intervals built from small primes (2, 3, 5) are well-approximated by the keyboard; larger primes produce "exotic" intervals that challenge the tempered system.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 112): $6 = 2 \cdot 3$: combines octave character with fifth character, giving approximately two octaves and a fifth, ~2 cents flat.

**Example 2** (p. 113): $9 = 3^2$: "double fifth" quality -- three octaves plus a major second, ~4 cents flat (double the error of 3).

**Example 3** (p. 114): $10 = 2 \cdot 5$: combines octave with major third character, ~14 cents sharp (same error as 5, since 2 is exact).

**Example 4** (p. 115): $12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$: two octaves plus a fifth, same ~2 cent error as 3.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Thinking each integer has an independent musical character
  **Correction**: Composite integers' characters are built from their prime factors; $6$ sounds like "an octave above a fifth" because $6 = 2 \cdot 3$

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Assuming the error for a composite equals the product of factor errors
  **Clarification**: Errors are additive (in cents), not multiplicative, because cents use a logarithmic scale
