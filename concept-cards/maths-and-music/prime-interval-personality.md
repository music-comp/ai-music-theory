# Quick Definition
Each prime number defines a musically irreducible interval with a unique sonic character. Composite integers inherit their musical personality from the combination of their prime factors.

# Formal Definition
A prime interval is an integral interval whose frequency ratio is a prime number. Since the monoid of integral intervals under multiplication is generated by the primes (by the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic), every integral interval decomposes uniquely into prime intervals. The musical character of each integer ratio is thus determined by its prime factorization.

# Mathematical Context
By unique factorization, every positive integer n = p1^a1 * p2^a2 * ... * pk^ak. The corresponding interval is the composition of a1 copies of the p1-interval, a2 copies of the p2-interval, etc. The cent value is 1200 * log2(n) = 1200 * (a1 * log2(p1) + a2 * log2(p2) + ...), showing errors are additive.

# Musical Context
The first several primes each introduce a distinct musical quality:
- 2: the octave, so fundamental it defines pitch class equivalence
- 3: introduces the perfect fifth (3:2), foundation of Pythagorean tuning
- 5: introduces the major third (5:4), foundation of just intonation
- 7: introduces the "natural seventh," foreign to equal temperament (~31 cents off)
- 11: profoundly foreign to Western music (~49 cents off), "in the cracks"
- 13: also poorly approximated (~41 cents off)

# Examples
- 6 = 2 * 3: combines octave character with fifth character
- 9 = 3^2: "double fifth" -- three octaves + major second, ~4 cents flat
- 10 = 2 * 5: combines octave with major third character, ~14 cents sharp
- 12 = 2^2 * 3: two octaves + fifth, same ~2 cent error as 3

# Common Confusions
Students may think each integer has an independent musical character. In fact, composite integers' characters are built from their prime factors. The integer 6 sounds like "an octave above a fifth" precisely because 6 = 2 * 3.
