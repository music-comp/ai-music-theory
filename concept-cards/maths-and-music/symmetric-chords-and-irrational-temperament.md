# Quick Definition
Augmented triads and diminished seventh chords, which divide the octave into equal parts, can only be rendered using irrational intervals and are therefore unachievable in just intonation.

# Formal Definition
The irrationality theorem proves that equal partitions of the octave in any equally-tempered scale require irrational ratios (except for multi-octaves). Since augmented triads divide the octave into three equal parts (each 2^(4/12) = 2^(1/3)) and fully diminished seventh chords divide it into four equal parts (each 2^(3/12) = 2^(1/4)), these symmetric chords cannot be justly tuned. Completely symmetric chords can only be rendered using irrational temperament.

# Mathematical Context
By the irrationality theorem, if an interval x in Q+ satisfies x^n = 2^k, then x must be a power of 2. For the augmented triad, x^3 = 2 requires x = 2^(1/3), which is irrational. For the diminished seventh, x^4 = 2 requires x = 2^(1/4), also irrational. Any just approximation of these chords necessarily introduces unequal intervals, destroying the symmetry that gives these chords their distinctive harmonic function (no discernible root, maximum ambiguity).

# Musical Context
The functionality of augmented and diminished seventh chords often relies on their property of having equal intervals, hence no discernible root. This equal-interval property is what makes them useful for modulation (any note can serve as root) and gives them their unstable, tension-filled character. One might argue that these chords are a product of equal temperament itself -- they did not exist in pre-tempered tuning systems and their unstable character arises precisely from their irrational tuning.

# Examples
- Augmented triad: requires three equal major thirds of 2^(1/3) each (irrational)
- Three just major thirds: (5/4)^3 = 125/64 < 2 (does not close the octave)
- Diminished seventh: requires four equal minor thirds of 2^(1/4) each (irrational)
- Four just minor thirds: (6/5)^4 = 1296/625 > 2 (overshoots the octave)
- These chords are harmonically ambiguous precisely because of their symmetry

# Common Confusions
- Symmetric chords are not "poorly tuned" in equal temperament -- they are perfectly tuned for their mathematical purpose (equal division)
- The impossibility of justly tuning these chords is a theorem, not an approximation issue
- Just approximations of augmented/diminished chords exist but lose the root-ambiguity that makes these chords musically useful
- The instability of these chords is a feature, not a bug -- it is essential to their harmonic function
