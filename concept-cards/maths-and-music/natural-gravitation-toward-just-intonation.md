# Quick Definition
Musicians playing instruments with continuous pitch control (voice, violin, trombone) naturally gravitate toward just intonation rather than equal temperament, drawn by the superior consonance of small-integer frequency ratios.

# Formal Definition
When performers have access to a continuum of pitch (as opposed to the fixed pitches of a keyboard), the ear of the musician fine-tunes the pitch toward the more natural just tuning, away from the imperfection (and irrationality) of equal temperament. This tendency is particularly pronounced in sustained harmonies where beats between nearly-coincident partials become audible.

# Mathematical Context
The physical basis for this gravitation lies in the harmonic series: when two pitches are in a small-integer ratio, their overtones align precisely, eliminating beating and producing a smooth, stable sonority. Equal-tempered intervals, being irrational, always produce some degree of beating between overtones. The closer a tempered interval is to its just counterpart, the slower and less noticeable the beating.

# Musical Context
This phenomenon is most evident in a cappella choral music, barbershop quartets, string quartets, and other ensembles of continuously-pitched instruments playing sustained harmonies. The septimal seventh chord (4:5:6:7), with its "laser-like" consonance, is commonly heard in blues, jazz, and barbershop singing -- contexts where performers are free to deviate from tempered pitches. Keyboard instruments, by contrast, enforce equal temperament and cannot exhibit this gravitation.

# Examples
- A barbershop quartet naturally tunes to 4:5:6:7 for seventh chords, with the seventh ~31 cents flat of the keyboard note
- String quartets playing sustained chords tend to "lock in" on just intervals
- The low septimal seventh heard in blues singing reflects natural tuning rather than tempered tuning
- A cappella choirs singing major triads settle toward 4:5:6 rather than tempered ratios

# Common Confusions
- This gravitation is not a conscious choice by most performers -- it is an automatic response of the trained ear to the smoothness of just intervals
- Keyboard instruments cannot exhibit this effect because their pitches are fixed
- The gravitation is toward simple ratios, not toward any specific tuning system -- performers may use 5-limit or 7-limit intervals depending on the harmonic context
- This does not mean equal temperament is "wrong" for all music -- some harmonies (augmented, diminished) require equal temperament
