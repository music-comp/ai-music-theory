# Quick Definition

Musicians playing instruments with continuous pitch control (voice, violin, trombone) naturally gravitate toward just intonation rather than equal temperament, drawn by the superior consonance of small-integer frequency ratios.

# Core Definition

Wright observes that with instruments allowing a continuum of pitch, "the ear of the musician fine-tunes the pitch" toward just tuning, away from "the imperfection (and irrationality) of equal temperament" (p. 148). This gravitation is an automatic response to the physical smoothness of small-integer ratios, where overtones align precisely and beating is eliminated.

# Key Properties

1. Occurs only with continuously-pitched instruments (voice, unfretted strings, trombone)
2. The ear automatically fine-tunes toward small-integer ratios
3. Most pronounced in sustained harmonies where beating between partials is audible
4. Keyboard instruments enforce equal temperament and cannot exhibit this tendency
5. Performers may use 5-limit or 7-limit intervals depending on harmonic context
6. Not a conscious choice for most performers -- it is an automatic ear response

# Construction / Recognition

This phenomenon is observed, not constructed. Recognition involves:
1. Listening for sustained chords that "lock in" with unusual smoothness
2. Noticing that a cappella ensembles tune differently from keyboard-accompanied ones
3. Identifying the septimal seventh (~31 cents flat) in blues and barbershop singing
4. Observing that string quartets playing sustained triads produce noticeably smoother sonority than keyboard instruments

# Context & Application

This phenomenon is most evident in a cappella choral music, barbershop quartets, string quartets, and other ensembles of continuously-pitched instruments playing sustained harmonies. The septimal seventh chord (4:5:6:7), with its "laser-like" consonance, is commonly heard in blues, jazz, and barbershop -- contexts where performers are free to deviate from tempered pitches.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 148): "With the human voice and with certain instruments, such as the violin, a continuum of pitch is available so that the ear of the musician fine-tunes the pitch."

**Example 2** (pp. 148-149): The septimal seventh (~31 cents flat of keyboard) "is often heard in blues, some jazz, and some a cappella vocal styles such as barbershop."

**Example 3** (p. 149): The septimal seventh chord 4:5:6:7 has "laser-like" perfect consonance despite the seventh sounding "annoyingly flat" to keyboard-trained ears.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming that performers who deviate from keyboard pitch are "out of tune"
  **Correction**: They may be gravitating toward more consonant just intervals

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking equal temperament is "wrong" for all music
  **Clarification**: Some harmonies (augmented, diminished) require equal temperament; the gravitation toward just intonation applies primarily to sustained consonances

- **Confusion**: Thinking this is a conscious choice by performers
  **Clarification**: For most musicians, the adjustment is automatic -- an unconscious response of the trained ear
