# Quick Definition
The perceived consonance of a musical interval correlates with how small the integers are in its frequency ratio -- the smaller the integers, the more consonant the interval sounds.

# Formal Definition
Two pitches sounded simultaneously create a consonant effect when the ratio of their frequencies can be expressed as n:m where m and n are small positive integers. This is a long-acknowledged principle. The consonance hierarchy roughly follows the size of the integers: 2:1 (octave) > 3:2 (fifth) > 4:3 (fourth) > 5:4 (major third) > 6:5 (minor third) > ... The ratio 16/15 (just semitone), with larger numerator and denominator, is considered "less just" and less consonant.

# Mathematical Context
The physical basis for this correlation lies in the harmonic series: when two tones are in a small-integer ratio, their overtones coincide frequently, producing a smooth sound free of beating. For ratio n:m, the n-th harmonic of the lower tone coincides with the m-th harmonic of the upper tone. The prime factorization of the ratio determines which harmonics align -- simpler factorizations (smaller primes, smaller exponents) produce more coincidences.

# Musical Context
This principle has been recognized since antiquity and forms the foundation of just intonation. It explains why the octave, fifth, and fourth were the earliest accepted consonances, and why thirds and sixths were gradually accepted later (they involve the prime 5, requiring larger integers). Septimal intervals (involving 7) and exotic intervals (involving 11, 13, ...) produce progressively more unusual sonorities.

# Examples
- Octave (2:1): most consonant -- every other harmonic aligns
- Fifth (3:2): very consonant -- every 3rd and 2nd harmonics align
- Major third (5:4): consonant -- every 5th and 4th harmonics align
- Just semitone (16:15): less consonant -- larger integers mean fewer harmonic coincidences
- Septimal seventh (7:4): "blue" quality -- 7th and 4th harmonics align, but 7 is a larger prime

# Common Confusions
- Consonance is not binary (consonant vs. dissonant) but a continuum correlated with integer size
- Larger integers do not make an interval "wrong" -- they make it less consonant, which can be musically desirable
- The correlation is with the integers in the reduced ratio, not the integers in any arbitrary representation
- Consonance also depends on context, register, and timbre, not only on the frequency ratio
