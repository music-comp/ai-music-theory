# Quick Definition

The perceived consonance of a musical interval correlates with how small the integers are in its frequency ratio -- the smaller the integers, the more consonant the interval sounds.

# Core Definition

Wright states at the opening of Chapter 11: "It has long been acknowledged that two pitches sounded simultaneously create an effect that we are prone to call 'harmonious' or 'consonant' when the ratio of their frequencies can be expressed as a ratio n:m where m and n are small positive integers. The smaller the integers, the more consonant the interval" (p. 138). This principle is the foundation for the concept of just intervals and the entire theory of rational tuning systems.

# Key Properties

1. Consonance correlates with the smallness of integers in the frequency ratio
2. The consonance hierarchy roughly follows: 2:1 > 3:2 > 4:3 > 5:4 > 6:5 > ...
3. Larger integers in the ratio produce progressively less consonant intervals
4. Consonance is a continuum, not a binary distinction
5. The boundary between "consonant" and "dissonant" is a matter of context and convention
6. Consonance also depends on context, register, and timbre -- not only on the frequency ratio

# Construction / Recognition

The consonance hierarchy from most to least consonant:
1. Octave (2:1) -- most consonant
2. Fifth (3:2) -- very consonant
3. Fourth (4:3) -- consonant
4. Major third (5:4) -- consonant
5. Minor third (6:5) -- consonant
6. Just semitone (16:15) -- less consonant, with larger integers

# Context & Application

This principle has been recognized since antiquity and forms the foundation of just intonation. It explains why the octave, fifth, and fourth were the earliest accepted consonances in Western music, and why thirds and sixths were gradually accepted later (they involve the prime 5, requiring larger integers). Septimal intervals (involving 7) and exotic intervals (involving 11, 13, ...) produce progressively more unusual sonorities. The principle extends to chords: justly tuned chords with small integer ratios (like 4:5:6 for the major triad) sound more consonant.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 138): The octave (2:1) is the most consonant interval -- every other harmonic aligns.

**Example 2** (p. 143): The just semitone (16:15), with larger integers, "gives an interval that might be considered 'less just,' and which one might expect to be less consonant."

**Example 3** (p. 143): Septimal intervals (involving 7) are "sufficiently far away from keyboard notes as to impart a texture that is sometimes called 'blue,' or 'soulful'."

**Example 4** (p. 147): "The smaller the integers in a chord's ratios, the more 'harmonious,' or consonant, it sounds."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating consonance as binary (consonant vs. dissonant)
  **Correction**: Consonance is a continuum correlated with integer size; there is no sharp boundary

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking larger integers make an interval "wrong"
  **Clarification**: Larger integers make an interval less consonant, which can be musically desirable (e.g., the dissonance of seventh chords creates tension)

- **Confusion**: Assuming the correlation applies only to the ratio as written, not the reduced ratio
  **Clarification**: Consonance correlates with the integers in the fully reduced ratio, not any arbitrary representation
