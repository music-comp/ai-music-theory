# Quick Definition
The minor triad is a three-note chord defined by the interval sequence (3, 4, 5) in semitones, consisting of a root, a minor third above the root, and a perfect fifth above the root.

# Formal Definition
The minor triad is defined by the sequence of modular intervals (3, 4, 5) in Z_12:

root --(3)--> third --(4)--> fifth --(5)--> (root)

Like the major triad, this sequence has no non-trivial cyclic symmetries, so the root, third, and fifth are uniquely determined from the pitch class content.

# Mathematical Context
The interval sequence (3, 4, 5) is related to the major triad's (4, 3, 5) by swapping the first two elements. Both sum to 12. The sequence has three distinct cyclic permutations: (3,4,5), (4,5,3), and (5,3,4), none of which are identical, ensuring a unique root.

# Musical Context
The minor triad differs from the major triad in that it has a minor third (3 semitones) rather than a major third (4 semitones) between root and third, while maintaining the same perfect fifth (7 semitones) between root and fifth. It conveys a darker or more somber quality compared to the major triad. In the major mode, diatonic minor triads occur on scale degrees II, III, and VI.

# Examples
- A minor triad: A-C-E (root A, third C, fifth E)
- D minor triad: D-F-A (root D, third F, fifth A)
- The minor chord is labeled with suffix "m" (e.g., Am, Dm)

# Common Confusions
- The minor triad is sometimes called simply "minor chord," but precision requires "minor triad" to distinguish from the minor seventh chord
- Both major and minor triads have a perfect fifth; only the third differs
- The interval sequence (3, 4, 5) should not be confused with (3, 3, 6) which defines the diminished chord
