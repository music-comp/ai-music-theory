# Quick Definition
A musical interval is properly measured not by the difference between two frequencies, but by their ratio. The octave corresponds to a frequency ratio of 2:1, regardless of the absolute frequencies involved.

# Formal Definition
Given two pitches with frequencies f_1 and f_2, the interval from f_1 to f_2 is determined by the ratio r = f_2/f_1. Two pairs of pitches (f_2, f_1) and (f_2', f_1') represent the same interval if and only if f_2/f_1 = f_2'/f_1'. Each r in R+ gives a unique interval. The ratio r and the corresponding equivalence class f_2 : f_1 are both referred to as the interval ratio.

# Mathematical Context
The function phi: (R+ : R+) -> R+ defined by phi((a:b)) = a/b establishes a bijection between the set of ratio equivalence classes and the positive real numbers. This identification allows intervals to be treated as elements of R+, where the group operation is multiplication. The unison interval corresponds to r = 1, upward intervals to r > 1, and downward intervals to 0 < r < 1.

# Musical Context
The insight that intervals correspond to ratios rather than differences is fundamental. The interval from A3 (220 Hz) to A4 (440 Hz) is one octave, and the interval from A4 (440 Hz) to A5 (880 Hz) is also one octave. The frequency differences are 220 Hz and 440 Hz respectively -- different -- but the ratios are both 2:1. This ratio-based understanding was known to the ancient Greeks and forms the foundation for all mathematical treatment of intervals.

# Examples
- Octave: ratio 2 (e.g., 440 Hz to 880 Hz, or 220 Hz to 440 Hz)
- Various ratios create recognizable intervals: 3/2 (close to a fifth), sqrt(2) (tritone), 3 (octave plus a fifth approximately)
- Even transcendental numbers like pi (~3.14159) and e (~2.71828) define unique intervals

# Common Confusions
- The interval is the ratio, not the difference, of frequencies -- this is the key conceptual shift of Chapter 4
- Equal intervals correspond to equal ratios, not equal frequency differences
- A ratio of 1 is unison (no interval), not "one unit of interval"
