# Quick Definition
Exotic intervals are just intervals involving primes greater than 7, producing unusual sonorities that are often far from any equal-tempered keyboard note.

# Formal Definition
Exotic intervals are rational intervals in Q+ whose unique prime factorization includes primes p >= 11. They are described using the prefixes "sub-" and "super-" before the names of the nearest keyboard intervals. While there is theoretically an infinite list of primes to consider, practical limits arise from the audible range (the human ear can listen to ratios up to about 1000).

# Mathematical Context
Higher primes contribute increasingly remote intervals to the system of just intonation. The intervals 11/8 and 13/8 are significantly displaced from keyboard notes, while 17/16 and 19/16 happen to fall very close to tempered intervals. This illustrates that the closeness of a rational interval to an equal-tempered note depends on the specific arithmetic, not simply on the size of the primes involved.

# Musical Context
Exotic intervals have legitimate uses in music despite their strangeness. The 11th and 13th harmonics contribute to complex jazz voicings -- for example, the final chord of the famous passage in Gershwin's Rhapsody in Blue can be tuned justly as 2:3:5:7:9:11, using all notes as harmonics of a single fundamental. The primes 17 and 19 are noteworthy for giving near-keyboard intervals, better approximated by equal temperament than even the just semitone or just minor third.

# Examples
- Exotic tritone: 11/8, 1200 * log2(11/8) ~ 551.32 cents (almost halfway between tempered fourth and tritone)
- Exotic super-minor sixth: 13/8, 1200 * log2(13/8) ~ 840.53 cents (~41 cents sharp of tempered minor sixth)
- Super-semitone: 17/16, 1200 * log2(17/16) ~ 104.96 cents (~5 cents sharp of tempered semitone)
- Sub-minor third: 19/16, 1200 * log2(19/16) ~ 297.51 cents (~2 cents flat of tempered minor third)
- Gershwin's Rhapsody in Blue final chord: justly tuned as 2:3:5:7:9:11

# Common Confusions
- Higher primes do not always produce intervals far from the keyboard -- 17/16 and 19/16 are closer to tempered notes than many 5-limit intervals
- "Exotic" does not mean "useless" -- these intervals appear naturally in the harmonic series and contribute to the character of complex chords
- The sub-minor third (19/16 ~ 298 cents) is a better approximation of the tempered minor third than the just minor third (6/5 ~ 316 cents)
