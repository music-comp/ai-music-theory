# Quick Definition

Exotic intervals are just intervals involving primes greater than 7, producing unusual sonorities that are often far from any equal-tempered keyboard note. They are described using the prefixes "sub-" and "super-" before the nearest keyboard interval names.

# Core Definition

Wright describes exotic intervals as those involving "higher primes" beyond 7: "Although the primes > 7 may be considered remote, they can contribute intervals which have legitimate uses in music. These intervals, unnamed for the most part, will be referred to as 'exotic.' We often describe them by using the prefixes sub- and super- before the names of keyboard intervals" (p. 144). The human ear can perceive ratios up to about 1000, so there are many audible possibilities.

# Prerequisites

- **Septimal intervals** -- Exotic intervals extend beyond the 7-limit
- **p-limit tuning** -- Understanding which primes are involved in interval classification

# Key Properties

1. Involve primes p >= 11 in their frequency ratios
2. Often far from any 12-tone equal-tempered pitch
3. Named with "sub-" and "super-" prefixes relative to nearest keyboard intervals
4. Higher primes do not always mean greater deviation from equal temperament
5. The human ear can perceive ratios up to about 1000
6. Primes 17 and 19 happen to give near-keyboard intervals

# Construction / Recognition

1. Identify ratios involving the 11th, 13th, 17th, 19th (etc.) harmonics
2. Compare with the nearest tempered interval
3. Apply "sub-" (slightly below) or "super-" (slightly above) prefix to the nearest keyboard interval name

# Context & Application

Exotic intervals have legitimate musical uses despite their strangeness. The 11th and 13th harmonics contribute to complex jazz voicings. Notably, the primes 17 and 19 give near-keyboard intervals that are actually better approximated by equal temperament than the just semitone or just minor third. Gershwin's "Rhapsody in Blue" final chord can be tuned justly as 2:3:5:7:9:11, using the 11th harmonic.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 144): Exotic tritone 11/8: 1200 * log2(11/8) ~ 551.32 cents, almost halfway between the keyboard's fourth and tritone.

**Example 2** (p. 144): Exotic super-minor sixth 13/8: 1200 * log2(13/8) ~ 840.53 cents, about 41 cents sharp of the keyboard minor sixth.

**Example 3** (p. 144): Super-semitone 17/16: 1200 * log2(17/16) ~ 104.96 cents, only about 5 cents sharp of the tempered semitone.

**Example 4** (p. 144): Sub-minor third 19/16: 1200 * log2(19/16) ~ 297.51 cents, only 2 cents flat of the tempered minor third.

**Example 5** (pp. 148-149): Gershwin's "Rhapsody in Blue" final chord tuned justly as 2:3:5:7:9:11 on E-flat.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming all higher-prime intervals are far from keyboard notes
  **Correction**: 17/16 (~5 cents from tempered semitone) and 19/16 (~2 cents from tempered minor third) are closer than many 5-limit intervals

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking exotic intervals are musically useless
  **Clarification**: They appear naturally in the harmonic series and contribute to complex chord character, as in Gershwin's "Rhapsody in Blue"

- **Confusion**: Assuming the sub-minor third 19/16 is the same as the just minor third 6/5
  **Clarification**: 19/16 ~ 298 cents is actually a better approximation of the tempered minor third than 6/5 ~ 316 cents
