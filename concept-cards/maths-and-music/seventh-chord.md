# Quick Definition
The seventh chord (also called the dominant seventh) is a four-note chord defined by the interval sequence (4, 3, 3, 2) in semitones, built by adding a minor seventh above the root of a major triad.

# Formal Definition
The seventh chord is defined by the sequence of modular intervals (4, 3, 3, 2) in Z_12:

root --(4)--> third --(3)--> fifth --(3)--> seventh --(2)--> (root)

This sequence has no non-trivial cyclic symmetries, so the root, third, fifth, and seventh are uniquely identifiable. The chord contains the major triad with the same root, third, and fifth.

# Mathematical Context
The interval sequence (4, 3, 3, 2) sums to 12. Its four cyclic permutations -- (4,3,3,2), (3,3,2,4), (3,2,4,3), (2,4,3,3) -- are all distinct, confirming a unique root. The chord is a superset of the major triad (4, 3, 5), where the final interval 5 is split into 3 + 2 by the insertion of the seventh.

# Musical Context
The seventh chord (labeled with superscript 7, e.g., G7) is one of the most important chords in Western harmony, particularly in its role as the dominant seventh (V7) which creates strong resolution to the tonic. The text notes that this chord plays a significant role in the development of Western harmony, and that tuning obstacles are associated with it. It is also enharmonically equivalent to the augmented sixth chord, depending on spelling.

# Examples
- G7: G-B-D-F, with intervals (4, 3, 3, 2)
- C7: C-E-G-Bb
- The seventh chord on V (dominant) resolving to I is a cornerstone of tonal harmony
- The progression V7 -> I appears in the circle-of-fifths pattern

# Common Confusions
- "Seventh chord" without qualifier typically means the dominant seventh (4, 3, 3, 2), not the minor seventh or major seventh
- The seventh chord contains a major triad plus a minor seventh; the major seventh chord contains a major triad plus a major seventh
- The augmented sixth chord is enharmonically equivalent to the seventh chord but spelled differently
