# Quick Definition
The relationship between two differently named notes that produce the same pitch, such as F-sharp and G-flat.

# Formal Definition
Two notes are enharmonically equivalent when they give the same pitch. This defines an equivalence relation on the set of note names. In equal temperament, enharmonic equivalence is exact; in other tuning systems (just intonation, Pythagorean), nominally enharmonic notes may differ slightly in pitch.

# Mathematical Context
Enharmonic equivalence is an equivalence relation distinct from octave equivalence. While octave equivalence identifies notes differing by 12 semitones, enharmonic equivalence identifies notes with the same pitch but different names. The quotient of all note names by enharmonic equivalence yields exactly 12 pitch classes (in equal temperament). This reflects the fact that the note-naming system (7 letters with accidentals) is redundant relative to the 12-tone chromatic system.

# Musical Context
Enharmonic equivalence arises because the 7-letter naming system with sharps and flats generates more names than there are distinct pitches in equal temperament. The choice between enharmonic spellings depends on musical context: key signature, harmonic function, and voice leading. The two sequences of key signatures (sharps and flats) wrap around and meet, yielding three pairs of enharmonically equivalent keys: $D^\flat \sim C^\sharp$, $G^\flat \sim F^\sharp$, and $C^\flat \sim B$.

# Examples
- $F^\sharp = G^\flat$ (same pitch, different names)
- $C^\flat_5 = B_4$ (same note, different notation)
- $B^\sharp_3 = C_4$
- Enharmonically equivalent key pairs: $D^\flat$ major / $C^\sharp$ major, $G^\flat$ major / $F^\sharp$ major, $C^\flat$ major / $B$ major

# Common Confusions
- Enharmonic equivalence is exact only in equal temperament; in just intonation or Pythagorean tuning, $F^\sharp$ and $G^\flat$ may differ slightly
- Enharmonic equivalence (same pitch, different name) vs. octave equivalence (different pitch, same name class) -- these are distinct equivalence relations
- The choice of enharmonic spelling is not arbitrary in practice: $F^\sharp$ is appropriate in the key of G, while $G^\flat$ is appropriate in the key of $D^\flat$
