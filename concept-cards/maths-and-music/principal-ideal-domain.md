# Quick Definition

A principal ideal domain (PID) is an integral domain in which every ideal is generated by a single element. The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ are the most important example, and this property is the foundation for the theory of greatest common divisors and unique factorization.

# Core Definition

"If $R$ is an integral domain in which every ideal is principal, we call $R$ a principal ideal domain, abbreviated PID" (Wright, Ch. 8, p. 101). The key theorem: "$\mathbb{Z}$ is a principal ideal domain." The proof uses the Division Algorithm and the Well-Ordering Principle.

# Prerequisites

- **Integral Domain** -- A PID is a special type of integral domain
- **Ideals and Principal Ideals** -- Understanding what it means for every ideal to be principal

# Key Properties

1. Every ideal $J \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ can be written as $J = n\mathbb{Z}$ for some $n \geq 0$
2. A non-zero ideal has a unique positive generator
3. The PID property guarantees existence and uniqueness of GCDs
4. The PID property enables the Unique Factorization Theorem
5. The proof relies on both the Division Algorithm and the Well-Ordering Principle

# Construction / Recognition

## Proof that Z is a PID (Wright, p. 102):
1. Let $J$ be an ideal in $\mathbb{Z}$. If $J = \{0\}$, then $J = 0\mathbb{Z}$
2. Otherwise $J$ contains non-zero integers; since $n \in J$ implies $-n \in J$, $J$ contains positive integers
3. Let $n$ be the smallest positive integer in $J$ (by Well-Ordering Principle)
4. Claim: $J = n\mathbb{Z}$. Clearly $n\mathbb{Z} \subseteq J$
5. For $m \in J$, write $m = qn + r$ with $0 \leq r < n$ (Division Algorithm)
6. Then $r = m - qn \in J$; by minimality of $n$, $r = 0$
7. Hence $m = qn \in n\mathbb{Z}$, so $J \subseteq n\mathbb{Z}$

# Context & Application

The PID property ensures that the GCD of any two integers exists uniquely, which is essential for the theory of generating intervals ($\gcd(m, n) = 1$ iff $[m]$ generates $\mathbb{Z}_n$) and for the analysis of m-on-n polyrhythmic patterns.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 102): $J = 6\mathbb{Z}$: principal ideal with generator 6.

**Example 2** (p. 103): $12\mathbb{Z} + 15\mathbb{Z} = 3\mathbb{Z}$: this ideal is principal with generator $\gcd(12, 15) = 3$.

**Example 3** (p. 101): $\mathbb{Q}$ has only two ideals ($\{0\}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$), both principal.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming all rings are PIDs
  **Correction**: The PID property is special; polynomial rings in two or more variables are typically not PIDs

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking a PID is just any integral domain
  **Clarification**: A PID is always an integral domain, but not conversely; the PID property requires every ideal to be principal
