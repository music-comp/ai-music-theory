# Quick Definition
An integral domain in which every ideal is principal (generated by a single element). The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ are the most important example, and this property underlies the theory of greatest common divisors.

# Formal Definition
An integral domain $R$ is a principal ideal domain (PID) if every ideal $J \subseteq R$ can be written as $J = aR$ for some $a \in R$. **Theorem:** $\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID. Proof: Given a non-zero ideal $J$, let $n$ be the smallest positive integer in $J$ (existing by WOP). For any $m \in J$, write $m = qn + r$ with $0 \leq r < n$. Then $r = m - qn \in J$, so by minimality of $n$, $r = 0$, giving $m \in n\mathbb{Z}$. Hence $J = n\mathbb{Z}$.

# Mathematical Context
The PID property of $\mathbb{Z}$ has profound consequences: it guarantees the existence and uniqueness of greatest common divisors, which in turn enables the Unique Factorization Theorem. Any non-zero ideal in $\mathbb{Z}$ has exactly two generators ($n$ and $-n$), hence a unique positive generator. The proof relies on both the Division Algorithm and the Well-Ordering Principle.

# Musical Context
The PID property ensures that the gcd of any two integers exists uniquely, which is essential for the theory of generating intervals ($\gcd(m, n) = 1$ iff $[m]$ generates $\mathbb{Z}_n$) and for the analysis of m-on-n polyrhythmic patterns. Without the PID property, the clean algebraic theory connecting number theory to chromatic scales would not hold.

# Examples
- $J = \{$multiples of 6$\} = 6\mathbb{Z}$: principal ideal with generator 6
- $12\mathbb{Z} + 15\mathbb{Z} = 3\mathbb{Z}$: this ideal is principal with generator $\gcd(12, 15) = 3$
- $\mathbb{Q}$ has only two ideals: $\{0\}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ itself (both principal)
- Every ideal in $\mathbb{Z}$ is of the form $n\mathbb{Z}$ for a unique non-negative integer $n$

# Common Confusions
- Not all rings are PIDs; polynomial rings in two or more variables are typically not
- The proof that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID requires both the Division Algorithm and the Well-Ordering Principle
- A PID is always an integral domain, but the converse is not true in general
