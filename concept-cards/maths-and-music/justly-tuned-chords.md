# Quick Definition
A justly tuned chord is a chord whose pitches can be expressed as a sequence of small positive integer ratios n1:n2:...:nk, arising naturally from the harmonic series.

# Formal Definition
A chord can be represented with specified voicing by writing a sequence of positive ratios n1:n2:...:nk, meaning the chord has k pitches and the ratio between the i-th and (i+1)-th pitch is ni. If n1, ..., nk are all "small" integers, the chord is called justly tuned. Every rational interval n:m (for m, n in Z+) is the interval from the m-th to the n-th harmonic of a sustained tone, so each justly tuned chord corresponds to a specific subset of harmonics.

# Mathematical Context
The consonance of a chord correlates inversely with the size of the integers in its ratios -- smaller integers produce more harmonious sounds. The harmonic series provides a natural ordering: chords involving lower harmonics (smaller integers) are more consonant. The irrationality theorem shows that equally-tempered chords with equal intervals (like augmented triads and diminished sevenths) cannot be justly tuned, since equal partitions of the octave require irrational ratios.

# Musical Context
The presence of justly tuned chords in the lower harmonics explains their fundamental role in music. The major triad (4:5:6) appears among the 4th, 5th, and 6th harmonics of any sustained tone. Instruments with continuous pitch control (voice, violin) allow performers to gravitate toward just tuning, especially in a cappella singing and barbershop harmony. The septimal seventh chord (4:5:6:7) has a "laser-like" consonance characteristic of blues and barbershop.

# Examples
- Major triad: 4:5:6
- Seventh chord (septimal): 4:5:6:7
- Half-diminished seventh: 5:6:7:9
- Minor triad: 10:12:15
- Minor seventh: 10:12:15:18
- Major seventh: 8:10:12:15
- Just minor seventh (non-septimal) seventh chord: 20:25:30:36 (root to seventh = 9:5)
- Ninth chord (jazz): 4:5:6:7:9
- Gershwin's Rhapsody in Blue chord: 2:3:5:7:9:11

# Common Confusions
- Justly tuned chords cannot be achieved on a keyboard -- they require continuous pitch control (voice, unfretted strings)
- The just seventh chord (4:5:6:7) uses the septimal seventh (~31 cents flat of the keyboard), which sounds flat to musicians accustomed to equal temperament but is more consonant
- Augmented and fully diminished chords, which divide the octave into equal parts, are inherently irrational and cannot be justly tuned -- they are arguably a product of equal temperament
- The "just minor seventh" interval (9:5) gives a less consonant seventh chord (20:25:30:36) than the septimal version (4:5:6:7)
