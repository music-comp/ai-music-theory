# Quick Definition

A justly tuned chord is a chord whose pitches can be expressed as a sequence of small positive integer ratios n1:n2:...:nk, arising naturally from the harmonic series.

# Core Definition

Wright defines: "A chord can be represented, with specified voicing, by writing a sequence of positive ratios n1:n2:...:nk. This means the chord has k pitches and the ratio between the i-th and the (i+1)-th pitch is ni. If the positive numbers n1, ..., nk are all 'small' integers ('small' being undefined) we say the chord is justly tuned. The smaller the integers in a chord's ratios, the more 'harmonious,' or consonant, it sounds" (p. 147). Each rational interval n:m is the interval from the m-th to the n-th harmonic of a sustained tone.

# Prerequisites

- **Just interval** -- Justly tuned chords are built from just intervals
- **Consonance and small integer ratios** -- The principle governing why small-integer chords sound consonant

# Key Properties

1. Pitches expressed as ratios of small positive integers
2. Smaller integers = more consonant chords
3. Every just chord corresponds to a specific subset of the harmonic series
4. Cannot be achieved on keyboard instruments (require continuous pitch control)
5. Chords with equal intervals (augmented, diminished) cannot be justly tuned

# Construction / Recognition

## Standard Just Chord Tunings (p. 147)

| Chord Type | Just Tuning |
|---|---|
| Major triad | 4:5:6 |
| Seventh (septimal) | 4:5:6:7 |
| Half-diminished seventh | 5:6:7:9 |
| Minor triad | 10:12:15 |
| Minor seventh | 10:12:15:18 |
| Major seventh | 8:10:12:15 |
| Ninth chord (jazz) | 4:5:6:7:9 |

# Context & Application

The presence of the major triad (4:5:6) among the low harmonics explains its fundamental role in music. Instruments with continuous pitch control (voice, violin) allow performers to gravitate toward just tuning. The septimal seventh chord (4:5:6:7) has a "laser-like" consonance heard in blues and barbershop, though the seventh is ~31 cents flat of the keyboard note. The 5-limit seventh chord (20:25:30:36), using larger integers, is decidedly less consonant.

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 147): The major triad as harmonics 4, 5, 6 of a fundamental tone, voiced as 4:5:6.

**Example 2** (p. 147): The same chord in "tenth voicing" (doubled root): 2:3:4:5.

**Example 3** (pp. 148-149): Gershwin's "Rhapsody in Blue" final chord tuned justly as 2:3:5:7:9:11 on E-flat.

**Example 4** (p. 149): The just minor seventh chord 20:25:30:36 with root-to-seventh ratio 9:5.

**Example 5** (p. 149): The V7 in just intonation: 36:45:54:64, which is "even less consonant."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming the 5-limit seventh chord is more consonant because it avoids prime 7
  **Correction**: The septimal chord 4:5:6:7 uses smaller integers overall and is more consonant

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking justly tuned chords can be played on a keyboard
  **Clarification**: Keyboards enforce equal temperament; just tuning requires continuous pitch control (voice, unfretted strings)

- **Confusion**: Believing all chords can be justly tuned
  **Clarification**: Augmented triads and fully diminished seventh chords, which divide the octave equally, require irrational intervals and cannot be justly tuned
