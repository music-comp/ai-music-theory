# Quick Definition
The two modes that became standard by the 18th century: major (Ionian, interval pattern 1,1,1/2,1,1,1,1/2) and minor (Aeolian, interval pattern 1,1/2,1,1,1/2,1,1), with each key signature representing one of each.

# Formal Definition
The major mode is the Ionian mode (the standard scale). The minor mode is the Aeolian mode. With only these two modes in use, each key signature determines exactly two possibilities: a major key and a minor key. The minor key sharing a key signature with a given major key is called the relative minor. The tonic of the relative minor lies a minor third (3 semitones) below the tonic of the corresponding major key.

# Mathematical Context
Restricting from 7 modes to 2 simplifies the key system: each of the 12 key signatures now represents exactly 2 keys (one major, one minor), giving $12 \times 2 = 24$ major/minor keys total. The relative minor relationship defines a bijection between the set of major keys and the set of minor keys, with the transformation being a fixed transposition of $-3$ semitones (modulo 12).

# Musical Context
The restriction to major and minor modes became standard during the Common Practice Period (roughly 1600-1900). The character of the music determines which mode prevails. Major mode is often perceived as bright or happy; minor mode as dark or sad. The distinction between major and minor is one of the most fundamental in Western tonal music.

# Examples
- No sharps or flats: C major or A minor (A is a minor third below C)
- One sharp ($F^\sharp$): G major or E minor
- Three flats ($B^\flat, E^\flat, A^\flat$): $E^\flat$ major or C minor
- The "character of music" -- cadences, melodic tendencies, harmonic progressions -- determines which mode applies

# Common Confusions
- "Relative minor" (shares key signature) vs. "parallel minor" (shares tonic): C major and A minor are relative; C major and C minor are parallel
- The natural minor (Aeolian) is only one form of the minor scale; harmonic and melodic minor are later modifications not discussed in this chapter
- Wright uses "standard scale" to mean the major (Ionian) scale specifically
