# Quick Definition
The wolf fifth is the anomalously large fifth that appears in the mean-tone chromatic scale, approximately two-fifths of a semitone sharp of a just or tempered fifth, named after the animal's howl for its harsh dissonance.

# Formal Definition
In the classical mean-tone scale, eleven fifths are tuned to 5^(1/4) (~696.58 cents each), but since twelve such fifths would fall short of seven octaves by 128/125 (~41.06 cents), one position on the circle of fifths must accommodate a "large fifth" that compensates for this shortfall. This wolf fifth is approximately 696.58 + 41.06 ~ 737.64 cents, about 35.68 cents sharp of the just fifth and about 37.64 cents sharp of the tempered fifth.

# Mathematical Context
The wolf fifth arises because three just major thirds do not equal an octave: (5/4)^3 = 125/64 < 128/64 = 2. The deficit is 2/(125/64) = 128/125 ~ 41.06 cents, called the lesser diesis. This is nearly twice the comma of Pythagoras (~23.46 cents), making the mean-tone wolf fifth more disruptive than the Pythagorean scale's weak fifth. The wolf fifth is typically placed between the 8 and 9 o'clock positions on the circle of fifths, away from diatonic notes.

# Musical Context
The wolf fifth was long ago dubbed after the animal's howl due to its harsh, dissonant quality. It represents the unavoidable cost of optimizing major thirds in the mean-tone system. Strategic placement of the wolf fifth (away from commonly used keys) was essential for practical music-making. If placed between G-sharp and E-flat, for example, keys using those notes as fifths would sound terrible while keys near C would sound excellent. This key-dependence was a major motivation for the eventual adoption of equal temperament.

# Examples
- Wolf fifth ~ 737.64 cents (vs. just fifth ~ 701.96 cents, tempered fifth = 700 cents)
- Excess over just fifth: ~35.68 cents (more than a third of a semitone)
- The deficit 128/125 ~ 41.06 cents (lesser diesis) is absorbed by a single fifth
- Typically placed between 8 and 9 o'clock positions on the circle of fifths (e.g., between G# and Eb)

# Common Confusions
- The wolf fifth is not a feature of equal temperament -- it exists only in unequally tempered systems like the mean-tone scale
- The wolf fifth (~41 cents excess) is much worse than the Pythagorean scale's weak fifth (~23 cents deficit)
- The wolf fifth moves depending on where the comma is placed -- it is a design choice, not a fixed property
- "Wolf" refers to the unpleasant beating, not to any specific ratio
