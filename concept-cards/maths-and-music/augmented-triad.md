# Quick Definition
The augmented triad is a three-note chord defined by the interval sequence (4, 4, 4) in semitones, consisting of three notes each separated by a major third. It has no discernible root.

# Formal Definition
The augmented triad is defined by the sequence of modular intervals (4, 4, 4) in Z_12. Every cyclic permutation of this sequence yields the same sequence (4, 4, 4). Consequently, the augmented triad has complete cyclic symmetry and no discernible root -- any of its three notes could equally serve as root.

# Mathematical Context
The sequence (4, 4, 4) is the unique partition of 12 into three equal parts. Its full cyclic symmetry group is Z_3 (the cyclic group of order 3). This means the augmented triad divides the octave into three equal parts, and its three notes form an orbit under the action of transposition by 4 semitones. There are only 4 distinct augmented triads (since 12/3 = 4), as compared to 12 distinct major or minor triads.

# Musical Context
The augmented triad creates a sense of ambiguity and suspension due to its symmetric structure. It is labeled with suffix "aug" or "+" (e.g., Caug or C+). When labeling is needed, the root is conventionally assigned to the lowest note in the voicing. Context may also override this convention -- for example, the text notes that a chord might be labeled E aug even though it is spelled as C aug.

# Examples
- C augmented: C-E-G#, with intervals (4, 4, 4)
- The same pitch classes {C, E, G#} could equally be labeled E aug (E-G#-C) or Ab aug (Ab-C-E)
- There are only 4 distinct augmented triads in the 12-tone system

# Common Confusions
- The augmented triad has no inherent root; the "root" is assigned by voicing or context, not by the interval structure
- It should not be confused with a major triad with a raised fifth, though enharmonically they contain the same notes
- Only 4 distinct augmented triads exist (unlike 12 for major or minor triads)
