# Quick Definition
A rational interval is a musical interval whose frequency ratio can be expressed as a ratio of positive integers, i.e., as an element of Q+.

# Formal Definition
An interval I is called rational if its corresponding ratio lies in Q+ (the positive rational numbers). Otherwise, I is called an irrational interval. Since elements of R+ are in one-to-one correspondence with musical intervals via the group isomorphism from the group of intervals to (R+, *), rational intervals form the subgroup (Q+, *) within this correspondence.

# Mathematical Context
The positive rationals Q+ form a subgroup of (R+, *) under multiplication. Every element x in Q+ has a unique prime factorization x = p1^a1 * p2^a2 * ... * pr^ar where the exponents ai are nonzero integers (positive or negative), unlike Z+ where exponents must be positive. This factorization is the key tool for analyzing rational intervals.

# Musical Context
Two pitches sounded simultaneously create a consonant effect when the ratio of their frequencies can be expressed as n:m where m and n are small positive integers. Such intervals are called just intervals. Rational intervals could be accurately created in ancient times using a vibrating string of length L -- the interval n/m (where n/m >= 1) is obtained by fretting the string at distance (m/n)*L from one end. By contrast, equally-tempered intervals like the semitone (ratio 2^(1/12)) were not accessible to ancient mathematicians.

# Examples
- The just fifth 3/2 is a rational interval (ratio of small integers 3 and 2)
- The just major third 5/4 is a rational interval
- The tempered semitone 2^(1/12) is an irrational interval
- The fraction 1,222,452/11,180,400 factors as (7^2 * 3^3)/(11^2 * 5^2 * 2^2) after prime factorization and cancellation

# Common Confusions
- Not all rational intervals sound consonant -- consonance depends on the size of the integers involved, not merely on rationality
- A rational interval is defined by its frequency ratio being rational, not by its cent measurement being rational (in fact, the cent measurement of a rational interval is always irrational, except for multi-octaves)
