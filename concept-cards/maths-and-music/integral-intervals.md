# Quick Definition
Musical intervals whose frequency ratio is a positive integer, forming a subset of all intervals that connects number theory to the physics of sound.

# Formal Definition
An integral interval is an interval whose interval ratio $x$ lies in $\mathbb{Z}^+ \subset \mathbb{R}^+$. Since intervals are identified with positive real numbers (via frequency ratio), each positive integer $n$ defines an interval. The set of integral intervals inherits the monoid structure of $(\mathbb{Z}^+, \cdot)$ under interval composition, but does not form a group (since $1/n \notin \mathbb{Z}^+$ for $n > 1$).

# Musical Context
The simplest integral intervals are musically fundamental: $2$ is the octave, $3$ is approximately an octave-and-a-fifth ($\approx 1901.96$ cents), $4$ is two octaves, $5$ is approximately two octaves and a major third, $6$ is approximately two octaves and a fifth. These small-integer ratios correspond to the intervals of the harmonic series and are perceived as consonant.

# Examples
- Ratio $2$: exactly the octave ($1200$ cents)
- Ratio $3$: $1200 \log_2 3 \approx 1901.96$ cents (about 2 cents above an octave + fifth)
- Ratio $4$: exactly two octaves ($2400$ cents)
- Ratio $5$: $1200 \log_2 5 \approx 2786.31$ cents (two octaves + just major third)
- Ratio $6 = 2 \cdot 3$: composition of octave and the interval of ratio 3

# Common Confusions
- Integral intervals form a monoid (under composition/multiplication) but NOT a group, since there are no inverses in $\mathbb{Z}^+$
- An integral interval is not the same as an interval measured in integers (like "integer number of semitones"); it is an interval with an integer RATIO
- The interval of ratio 3 is NOT exactly an octave + fifth in equal temperament; it differs by about 2 cents
