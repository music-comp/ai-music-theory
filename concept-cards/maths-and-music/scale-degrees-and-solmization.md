# Quick Definition

Scale degrees number the notes of the diatonic scale using circumflex notation ($\hat{1}, \hat{2}, \ldots, \hat{7}$), while solmization names them with syllables (do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti).

# Core Definition

Numbers with a circumflex $\hat{1}, \hat{2}, \hat{3}, \ldots, \hat{7}$ denote specific notes of the diatonic major scale relative to a given key. Chromatic alterations are denoted by preceding the number with $\sharp$ or $\flat$ (e.g., $\flat\hat{6}$ in G major denotes $E^\flat$). *Solmization* names the scale tones by syllables: do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti (Wright, pp. 27-28).

- **Diatonic and Chromatic Scales** — Scale degrees number the diatonic scale tones

# Key Properties

1. $\hat{1}$ through $\hat{7}$ label the seven diatonic notes of the major scale
2. Chromatic alterations: $\sharp\hat{n}$ raises by a semitone, $\flat\hat{n}$ lowers by a semitone
3. Chromatic alterations can coincide with diatonic degrees: $\sharp\hat{3} = \hat{4}$ in major
4. Under octave equivalence, only 7 numbers are needed
5. Without octave identification, larger numbers may be used: $\hat{9}$ = a ninth above $\hat{1}$
6. Solmization: do = $\hat{1}$, re = $\hat{2}$, mi = $\hat{3}$, fa = $\hat{4}$, sol = $\hat{5}$, la = $\hat{6}$, ti = $\hat{7}$

# Construction / Recognition

## To identify scale degrees in a given key:

1. Determine the key and its tonic
2. The tonic is $\hat{1}$
3. Number successive diatonic notes $\hat{2}$ through $\hat{7}$
4. For chromatic notes, use $\sharp$ or $\flat$ before the nearest diatonic degree number
5. For solmization, substitute the corresponding syllable

# Context & Application

Scale degree numbers are fundamental to music analysis, allowing discussion of harmonic and melodic relationships independent of key. The tonic ($\hat{1}$) is the most stable scale degree. Solmization provides singable syllables for ear training and sight-reading. Wright uses moveable do (do = $\hat{1}$ in any key). Solmization also provides names for chromatic tones, but Wright does not give them.

# Examples

- In the key of $A^\flat$: $\hat{1} = A^\flat$, $\hat{2} = B^\flat$, $\hat{3} = C$, $\hat{4} = D^\flat$, $\hat{5} = E^\flat$, $\hat{6} = F$, $\hat{7} = G$ (p. 28)
- In the key of G: $\flat\hat{6} = E^\flat$ (a half step below diatonic $\hat{6} = E$) (p. 28)
- $\sharp\hat{3} = \hat{4}$: raising the third by a semitone lands on the fourth (p. 28)
- $\hat{9}$ in C major indicates $D_5$ when $\hat{1} = C_4$ (without octave equivalence) (p. 28)

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Thinking $\hat{3}$ refers to the same note in every key
  **Correction**: Scale degrees are relative: $\hat{3}$ in C major is E, but $\hat{3}$ in $E^\flat$ major is G

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Confusing the circumflex notation ($\hat{n}$) with interval numbers or chord labels
  **Clarification**: The circumflex specifically denotes scale degrees, not intervals or chord roots
- **Confusion**: Expecting $\sharp\hat{n}$ to always produce a chromatic note
  **Clarification**: $\sharp\hat{3} = \hat{4}$ because the interval from $\hat{3}$ to $\hat{4}$ is a half step in major
- **Confusion**: Thinking solmization names chromatic tones
  **Clarification**: Wright provides solmization syllables only for the diatonic tones
