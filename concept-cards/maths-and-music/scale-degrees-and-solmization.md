# Quick Definition
Scale degrees number the notes of the diatonic scale using circumflex notation (^1, ^2, ..., ^7), while solmization names them with syllables (do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti).

# Formal Definition
Numbers with a circumflex $\hat{1}, \hat{2}, \hat{3}, \ldots, \hat{7}$ denote specific notes of the diatonic major scale relative to a given key. In the key of $A^\flat$: $\hat{1} = A^\flat$, $\hat{2} = B^\flat$, $\hat{3} = C$, and so on. Chromatic scale tones a half step above or below diatonic notes are denoted by preceding the number with $\sharp$ or $\flat$ (e.g., $\flat\hat{6}$ in G major denotes $E^\flat$). Solmization assigns the syllables do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti to $\hat{1}$ through $\hat{7}$ respectively.

# Mathematical Context
Scale degree numbering establishes a bijection between $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7\}$ and the diatonic notes of a given key, providing a key-independent way to refer to scale positions. Under octave equivalence, only 7 numbers are needed. Without octave identification, larger numbers may be used: $\hat{9}$ indicates the note a ninth above the tonic $\hat{1}$. The chromatic alteration $\sharp\hat{n}$ or $\flat\hat{n}$ shifts by one semitone, and these can coincide with diatonic notes (e.g., $\sharp\hat{3} = \hat{4}$).

# Musical Context
Scale degree numbers are fundamental to music analysis, allowing discussion of harmonic and melodic relationships independent of key. The tonic ($\hat{1}$) is the most stable scale degree. Solmization (do-re-mi-fa-sol-la-ti) provides singable syllables for ear training and sight-reading. "Moveable do" solmization sets do = $\hat{1}$ in any key; "fixed do" sets do = C always. Wright uses moveable do.

# Examples
- In the key of $A^\flat$: $\hat{1} = A^\flat$, $\hat{2} = B^\flat$, $\hat{3} = C$, $\hat{4} = D^\flat$, $\hat{5} = E^\flat$, $\hat{6} = F$, $\hat{7} = G$
- In the key of G: $\flat\hat{6} = E^\flat$ (the note a half step below the diatonic $\hat{6} = E$)
- $\sharp\hat{3} = \hat{4}$: raising the third scale degree by a semitone lands on the fourth
- $\hat{9}$ in C major indicates $D_5$ when $\hat{1} = C_4$ (without octave equivalence)

# Common Confusions
- Scale degrees are relative to a key; $\hat{3}$ in C major is E, but $\hat{3}$ in $E^\flat$ major is G
- The circumflex notation ($\hat{n}$) distinguishes scale degrees from interval numbers or chord labels
- $\sharp\hat{n}$ and $\flat\hat{n}$ can coincide with diatonic degrees: $\sharp\hat{3} = \hat{4}$ because the interval from $\hat{3}$ to $\hat{4}$ is a half step in major
- Solmization provides syllables for chromatic tones as well, but Wright does not give these names
