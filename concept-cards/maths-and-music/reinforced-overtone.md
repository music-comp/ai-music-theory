# Quick Definition
A reinforced overtone is a pitch that appears in the overtone series of two or more notes in a chord simultaneously. This reinforcement can make the overtone audible as a distinct pitch, causing the chord to "ring."

# Formal Definition
A pitch p is a reinforced overtone of a chord if p = k1*F1 = k2*F2 for two or more notes in the chord with fundamental frequencies F1, F2 and positive integers k1, k2. The overtone is present in multiple harmonic series simultaneously, amplifying its audibility.

# Mathematical Context
For a root C3 (fundamental F) and fifth G3 (fundamental 3F/2), the pitch G4 (frequency 3F) appears as the 3rd harmonic of C3 and the 2nd harmonic of G3. The coincidence 3*F = 2*(3F/2) is exact in just intonation, and off by only about 2 cents in equal temperament.

# Musical Context
When a reinforced overtone is audible, a cappella singers describe the chord as "ringing." The phenomenon is especially noticeable in well-tuned chords in resonant spaces. The audibility is enhanced if the reinforced overtone falls within a formant of the vowel being sung or the instrument being played.

# Examples
- Root C3 + fifth G3: G4 is the 3rd harmonic of C3 and the 2nd harmonic of G3
- The small note G4 appears in the overtone series of both C3 and G3
- A cappella choirs in reverberant spaces (churches, cathedrals) often experience this
- The effect is strongest when the chord is well-tuned (close to just intonation)

# Common Confusions
Reinforced overtones are not separately played notes -- they emerge from the overtone series of the notes actually being sounded. Students sometimes confuse this acoustic phenomenon with combination tones (difference tones), which arise from a different mechanism. Also, reinforcement requires the overtone frequencies to be close to identical, which is better achieved in just intonation than equal temperament.
