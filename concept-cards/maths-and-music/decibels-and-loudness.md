# Quick Definition
Loudness is a subjective perception that varies from person to person and is not directly proportional to amplitude. Physics measures it using sound pressure in decibels, which also depends on frequency.

# Formal Definition
Loudness is the subjective perception of a sound's intensity. It is not directly proportional to the amplitude of the waveform. Physics attempts to quantify it as a function of "sound pressure," measured in decibels (dB), as well as frequency. The term is difficult to define precisely because it varies from person to person.

# Mathematical Context
The decibel scale is logarithmic: an increase of 10 dB corresponds to a tenfold increase in sound pressure level. The perceived loudness also depends on frequency -- the human ear is most sensitive to frequencies in the 1-4 kHz range and less sensitive to very low or very high frequencies. Equal-loudness contours (Fletcher-Munson curves) map this frequency dependence.

# Musical Context
Vertical stretching of a waveform (multiplying by a constant c) adjusts amplitude and hence loudness, with "very little effect, if any, on the timbre of the tone." However, the relationship between amplitude and perceived loudness is not linear. Formant amplification also affects perceived loudness of specific harmonics.

# Examples
- Doubling amplitude does not double perceived loudness
- A soft clarinet and a loud clarinet have similar timbre (same harmonic profile) but different loudness
- The ear is less sensitive to bass frequencies, which is why bass instruments need more power to sound equally loud

# Common Confusions
Students often equate amplitude with loudness. While they are related, the relationship is not proportional and depends on frequency. A 100 Hz tone at a given amplitude sounds much quieter than a 2000 Hz tone at the same amplitude due to the ear's frequency-dependent sensitivity.
