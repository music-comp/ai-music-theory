# Quick Definition

Each unequal temperament (Pythagorean, just intonation, mean-tone) has specific tuning advantages but suffers from poorly tuned intervals and asymmetry across keys, making free modulation impossible on fixed-pitch instruments.

# Core Definition

Wright identifies two categories of drawbacks (p. 158): (1) "some intervals are poorly tuned" -- the Pythagorean scale has poor thirds, just intonation has poor II and III triads, mean-tone has one very bad fifth; (2) the "more serious problem" is "asymmetry with respect to the choice of key. A keyboard instrument cannot be conveniently retuned between songs, or in the middle of a piece that changes key." The key-dependence problem "fed the gradual adoption of equal temperament."

# Prerequisites

- **Pythagorean scale** -- One of the three systems with drawbacks
- **Just intonation scale** -- Another system with drawbacks
- **Mean-tone scale** -- The third system with drawbacks

# Key Properties

1. Pythagorean: poor thirds (81/64 ~ 22 cents sharp), one weak fifth
2. Just intonation: II and III major triads poorly tuned, V7 is bad (36:45:54:64)
3. Mean-tone: one very bad wolf fifth (~41 cents excess)
4. All three: asymmetric across keys -- cannot serve all keys equally
5. A keyboard cannot be retuned mid-piece
6. The key-dependence problem is more serious than individual interval problems
7. Equal temperament resolves key dependence at the cost of uniform imperfection

# Construction / Recognition

The drawbacks are identified by:
1. Checking which intervals deviate significantly from just (>10 cents)
2. Testing whether transposing a piece changes the quality of its chords
3. Attempting to modulate through the circle of fifths

# Context & Application

Wright states: "If the keyboard is tuned to the just intonation scale in the key of C, a song in D major has serious problems because the tonic triad is distractingly out of tune" (p. 158). Equal temperament's acceptance, despite its "imperfect tuning" being "a bitter pill to swallow," was necessary because "it allowed composers and performers to use extensive harmonic variety and freely modulate from one key to another. A great deal of 19th and 20th century music is deeply entrenched in this liberation" (p. 158).

# Examples

**Example 1** (p. 158): Pythagorean thirds (81/64) produce audible dissonance in I, IV, V triads.

**Example 2** (p. 158): Just intonation: II major triad has flat fifth, III major triad has sharp third.

**Example 3** (p. 158): Mean-tone: wolf fifth (~41 cents excess) at one position on the circle.

**Example 4** (p. 158): "A keyboard instrument cannot be conveniently retuned between songs, or in the middle of a piece that changes key."

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Thinking the drawbacks are implementation flaws
  **Correction**: They are mathematical necessities arising from the nature of primes; no conceivable unequal temperament can avoid them

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking equal temperament "solves" the tuning problem
  **Clarification**: Equal temperament distributes the error evenly rather than eliminating it; all non-octave intervals are irrational and slightly imperfect

- **Confusion**: Thinking unequal temperaments are obsolete
  **Clarification**: They remain relevant for period-instrument performance and specialized contexts
