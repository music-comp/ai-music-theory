# Quick Definition
Each unequal temperament system (Pythagorean, just intonation, mean-tone) has specific tuning advantages but suffers from poorly tuned intervals and asymmetry across keys, making free modulation impossible.

# Formal Definition
The three unequal temperament systems each optimize certain intervals at the expense of others. The Pythagorean scale gives just fifths almost everywhere but poor thirds (81/64). The just intonation scale gives many perfectly tuned chords but renders major triads II and III with poor tuning. The mean-tone scale gives tolerably tuned major triads on most diatonic roots but has one very bad (wolf) fifth. The more serious problem common to all three is asymmetry with respect to key choice: a keyboard tuned in one key cannot serve other keys equally well.

# Mathematical Context
The root cause of all these drawbacks is mathematical: the commas of Pythagoras (3^12/2^19) and Didymus (81/80), along with the lesser diesis (128/125), represent irreconcilable differences between powers of different primes. No rational tuning system can simultaneously achieve just fifths, just thirds, and octave closure across all keys. Equal temperament resolves this by making all intervals irrational but uniform -- distributing the inevitable error equally.

# Musical Context
A keyboard instrument cannot be conveniently retuned between songs or during a piece that modulates. If tuned to just intonation in C, a song in D major has a distractingly out-of-tune tonic triad. This key-dependence drove the gradual adoption of equal temperament, where all chords of the same type are tuned identically regardless of root. While the imperfect tuning of equal temperament (sharp thirds, irrational fifths) was a "bitter pill to swallow," it liberated composers to use extensive harmonic variety and freely modulate between keys.

# Examples
- Pythagorean: just fifths (3:2) everywhere except one weak fifth, but major third at 81/64 (~22 cents sharp of just)
- Just intonation: I, IV, V perfectly tuned as 4:5:6, but II and III triads are poor (II has flat fifth, III has sharp third)
- Mean-tone: perfect major thirds (5/4), but wolf fifth (~41 cents sharp) at one position
- All three: cannot modulate freely between keys on a fixed-pitch instrument
- 19th-20th century music is "deeply entrenched" in the liberation provided by equal temperament

# Common Confusions
- Equal temperament does not "solve" the tuning problem -- it distributes the error evenly rather than concentrating it
- The drawbacks are not flaws in implementation but mathematical necessities arising from the nature of primes
- Unequal temperaments are not obsolete -- they remain relevant for period-instrument performance and specialized contexts
- The issue is not that any single key sounds bad, but that a fixed keyboard cannot serve all keys
