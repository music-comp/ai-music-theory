# Quick Definition
The major seventh chord is a four-note chord defined by the interval sequence (4, 3, 4, 1) in semitones, built by adding a major seventh above the root of a major triad. It has a characteristic dissonance from the semitone between the seventh and the root.

# Formal Definition
The major seventh chord is defined by the sequence of modular intervals (4, 3, 4, 1) in Z_12:

root --(4)--> third --(3)--> fifth --(4)--> seventh --(1)--> (root)

This sequence admits no non-trivial cyclic symmetries. Like the seventh chord, it contains the major triad, but the interval from the fifth to the seventh is a major third (4 semitones) rather than a minor third (3 semitones).

# Mathematical Context
The interval sequence (4, 3, 4, 1) sums to 12. The four cyclic permutations are all distinct: (4,3,4,1), (3,4,1,4), (4,1,4,3), (1,4,3,4). The presence of the interval 1 (semitone) between the seventh and root is the source of the chord's characteristic dissonance.

# Musical Context
The major seventh chord became popular in the twentieth century and is one of the characteristic sounds of "smooth jazz." The dissonance arises from the semitone interval between the note class of the seventh and that of the root. It is labeled with suffix M7 (e.g., CM7, GM7). Despite containing a major triad, the semitone clash gives it a more complex, sophisticated sound.

# Examples
- CM7: C-E-G-B, with intervals (4, 3, 4, 1) -- the B is one semitone below C
- GM7: G-B-D-F#
- The chord is characteristic of smooth jazz and modern popular harmony

# Common Confusions
- The major seventh chord (4, 3, 4, 1) is not the same as the seventh/dominant seventh chord (4, 3, 3, 2) -- the former has a major seventh interval, the latter a minor seventh
- "Major" in "major seventh" refers to the quality of the seventh interval, not just the triad
- The dissonance comes from the semitone (1) between seventh and root, not from the triad itself
