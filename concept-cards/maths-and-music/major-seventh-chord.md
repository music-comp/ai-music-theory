# Quick Definition

The major seventh chord is a four-note chord defined by the interval sequence (4, 3, 4, 1) in semitones, built by adding a major seventh above the root of a major triad. Its characteristic dissonance comes from the semitone between the seventh and the root.

# Core Definition

The major seventh chord is defined by the sequence of modular intervals (4, 3, 4, 1) in Z_12:

root --(4)--> third --(3)--> fifth --(4)--> seventh --(1)--> (root)

This sequence admits no non-trivial cyclic symmetries. Like the seventh chord, it contains the major triad, but the interval from the fifth to the seventh is a major third (4 semitones) rather than a minor third (3 semitones). The dissonance arises from the semitone interval (1) between the note class of the seventh and that of the root (Wright, p. 48).

# Prerequisites

- **Major Triad** -- The major seventh chord extends the major triad by adding a major seventh

# Key Properties

1. Contains exactly four distinct note classes
2. Interval sequence: (4, 3, 4, 1) in semitones, summing to 12
3. Contains the major triad (4, 3, 5) with the interval 5 split as 4 + 1
4. Root to seventh: major seventh (11 semitones = 4 + 3 + 4)
5. The interval 1 (semitone) between seventh and root creates the characteristic dissonance
6. No non-trivial cyclic symmetries; root is uniquely identifiable
7. Labeled with suffix M7 (e.g., CM7, GM7)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct a Major Seventh Chord

1. Build a major triad (root, up 4, up 3)
2. From the fifth, go up 4 more semitones (major third) to the seventh
3. Verify: the seventh is 1 semitone below the root (completing the cycle)

# Context & Application

The major seventh chord became popular in the twentieth century and is one of the characteristic sounds of "smooth jazz." Despite containing a major triad, the semitone clash between the seventh and root gives it a more complex, sophisticated sound (Wright, p. 48).

## Contrasts With

- **Seventh Chord** -- (4, 3, 3, 2): has a minor seventh (interval of 3 from fifth to seventh) instead of a major seventh (interval of 4)
- **Minor Seventh Chord** -- (3, 4, 3, 2): has both a minor triad base and a minor seventh interval

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Confusing M7 (major seventh) with m7 (minor seventh) in notation
  **Correction**: M7 denotes the major seventh chord (4, 3, 4, 1); m7 denotes the minor seventh chord (3, 4, 3, 2)

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Thinking "major" in "major seventh" refers only to the triad quality
  **Clarification**: "Major" refers to the quality of the seventh interval (11 semitones = major seventh), not just the triad; both the M7 and the dominant seventh contain major triads
- **Confusion**: Assuming the major seventh chord is more consonant than the dominant seventh because "major" sounds positive
  **Clarification**: The major seventh chord is actually described as "somewhat dissonant" due to the semitone between seventh and root
