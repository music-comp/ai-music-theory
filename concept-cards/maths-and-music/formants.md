# Quick Definition
Formants are fixed frequency ranges in which harmonics are amplified by an instrument's or voice's resonating chambers. Unlike the harmonic series (which moves with pitch), formants stay in the same frequency range regardless of the note being played or sung.

# Formal Definition
A formant is a frequency range within which the harmonics of a musical tone are consistently given larger amplitudes (weights d_k) than harmonics outside that range. Formants depend only on the physical characteristics of the instrument or vocal tract, not on the fundamental pitch F. As F varies, different harmonics k will fall within a formant, so each d_k changes from note to note.

# Mathematical Context
In the Fourier representation g(t) = C + sum d_k*sin(2*pi*F*k*t + beta_k), if the d_k values were fixed (independent of F), the timbre would be constant across all pitches (like a square wave at different frequencies). Formants break this: the amplitude d_k is enhanced when the frequency kF falls within a formant range, making timbre pitch-dependent.

# Musical Context
Formants are created by resonating chambers (inside instruments or the human mouth/throat). A chamber's size and shape favor a specific frequency range. Musical sounds typically have two or three formants. Formants explain why instruments and vowels maintain their characteristic identity across different pitches, while still sounding slightly different at different pitches.

# Examples
- Clarinet formants: 1500-1700 Hz
- Trumpet formants: 1200-1400 Hz and narrowly around 2500 Hz
- Human "oo" vowel: formants near 310, 870, and 2250 Hz
- Human "ah" vowel: formants near 710, 1100, and 2640 Hz
- Human "ee" vowel: formants near 280, 2250, and 2900 Hz

# Common Confusions
Students often confuse formants with harmonics. Harmonics are integer multiples of the fundamental and move with the pitch. Formants are fixed frequency bands that stay put as pitch changes. A given harmonic may or may not fall within a formant depending on what note is being played.
