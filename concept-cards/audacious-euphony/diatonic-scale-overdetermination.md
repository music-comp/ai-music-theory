# Quick Definition
The diatonic scale, like the consonant triad, is doubly determined: arising through fifth-generation (acoustic) but proving uniquely suited for modulatory variety (structural) due to its "unique multiplicity" property in equal-tempered chromatic space.

# Formal Definition
The diatonic scale "evidently originated at a moment when modulation was inconceivable," yet once equal-tempered chromatic space arose, it had properties that "proved both unique and functionally powerful." Milton Babbitt (2003[1965]) observed: "The major scale is a maximal structure possessing [the property of unique multiplicity] in the usual equal-tempered division of the octave." Unique multiplicity means each interval class appears a different number of times: six perfect fourths, five whole steps, four minor thirds, etc. This ensures maximum diversity of common tones under transposition - for any diatonic collection, there exists another sharing n tones with it, where n ranges from 2 to 6.

# Construction/Recognition
Acoustic origin:
- Fifth-generated scale: F-C-G-D-A-E-B
- Arose through Pythagorean tuning considerations
- "Knew nothing" of future chromatic-modulatory role

Structural property (discovered later):
- Unique multiplicity: Different count for each interval class
- Common-tone retention under transposition is determined by interval multiplicity
- Maximum intervallic multiplicity -> maximum diversity of common tones
- Among seven-tone scales, only the chromatic heptachord shares this potential

Parallel to triadic overdetermination:
- Triad: Acoustic consonance AND voice-leading parsimony
- Scale: Fifth-generation AND modulatory variety
- Both are homophonous diamorphs between acoustic and structural systems

# Musical Context/Application
The diatonic scale's overdetermination "played a central role in the new modulatory system of early-eighteenth-century harmonic tonality, just as the consonant triad's overdetermination played a central role in the early nineteenth century's discovery of an alternative to it."

The scale was preadapted for modulatory function:
- Original purpose: Organizing melodic/harmonic material acoustically
- Discovered capacity: Achieving "maximum variety and depth" in modulation
- The same structure serves both purposes

# Examples
- The diatonic scale has 6 perfect fourths (maximum common tones with fifth-related keys)
- It has 5 whole steps (different from fourths count)
- It has 4 minor thirds (different from both)
- Continuing: 3 major thirds, 2 minor seconds, 1 tritone
- This unique distribution enables the modulatory richness of 18th-century practice

# Common Confusions
- The diatonic scale's modulatory properties were not the reason it was originally adopted
- "Unique multiplicity" is a structural property, not an acoustic one
- The property only becomes relevant once chromatic modulation is conceivable
- The chromatic heptachord also has unique multiplicity but lacks acoustic motivation
