# Quick Definition
Schubert's 1816 song "Liedesend'" demonstrates a departure-return scheme realized as a palindromic motion of voice-leading zones across its nine strophes, connecting c minor (beginning) to f minor (center) via cascading major triads, then ascending through the same zones to e minor (conclusion).

# Formal Definition
The song is sectionalized and episodic, with each of nine strophes having its own mood and tempo. Seven strophes are preceded by key-signature changes. The modulatory scheme, which George Grove selected as an example of Schubert's inscrutable modulatory practice, reveals a palindromic structure when analyzed through voice-leading zones. Minor tonics appear at the beginning, center, and end. Major triads cascade from zone 8 to 11 in the first half and upshift through the same zones in reverse in the second half.

# Construction/Recognition
Figure 6.13 presents the structure:
- Nine strophes indicated by Roman numerals
- Where successive tonics lack common tones, they are bridged by prolonged dominants sharing a tone with both
- The zonal palindrome is ruffled only by D major at the conclusion of stanza VIII, which defers arrival at zone 10
- This perturbation reprises and ultimately reverses the exact triadic succession from the initial stanza

# Musical Context
Suzannah Clark's analysis (2011b) identifies the song as structured by common-tone modulations, extended here by charting voice-leading zones. The palindromic voice leading suggests that the modulatory scheme need not be arbitrary even when its logic is not monotonal.

# Common Confusions
- Any attempt to organize the tonics around a single tonal center (c minor or e minor) would be "tendentious to an extreme degree"
- The palindromic structure is at the level of voice-leading zones, not at the level of specific triads
- The song closes in a key (e minor) first introduced only at the beginning of the final couplet
