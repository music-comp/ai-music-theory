# Quick Definition
Francois-Joseph Fetis's classification of tonality into four historical species (unitonality, transitonality, pluritonality, omnitonality), each with distinct syntactic principles and affective properties.

# Core Definition
Fetis (1832, 1844) divided tonality into four species representing stages of historical development:
1. **Unitonality**: Early music where triads succeed without establishing attractions; "no attraction is evident, because every perfect chord is a harmony of repose" (Fetis 2008 [1844], 163, qtd. p. 31)
2. **Transitonality**: Classical tonality with modulation between established keys
3. **Pluritonality**: Multiple simultaneous key implications
4. **Omnitonality**: Maximum chromaticism with "multiplicity, or even the universality of the keys" (Fetis 2008 [1844], 190, qtd. p. 30), predicting "total destruction of the scale" and division into "twelve equal semitones"

Each species has its own syntactic principles and affective properties.

# Prerequisites
- Basic understanding of historical periods of Western music

# Key Properties
1. The species are cumulative, not mutually exclusive -- "the best composers know how to combine them in a single work of art" (p. 31)
2. Transitonality (classical tonality) is not the only legitimate syntax
3. Listeners can switch between syntactic modes spontaneously, "like a carpenter exchanging a screwdriver for a hammer" (p. 31)
4. Sequences suspend the "law of tonality" in favor of a "law of uniformity"
5. Unitonality (not omnitonality) is most relevant to pan-triadic consonant chromaticism
6. Omnitonality concerns chromatically intensified dissonant harmonies

# Construction / Recognition
Fetis's framework proposes:
- Each species defines a distinct mode of tonal organization
- Later species do not supplant earlier ones -- they are cumulative
- Classical tonality ("transitonality") is a category whose elements are musical moments, not entire compositions
- The faculty of tonal listening is "capable of spontaneous suspension and reengagement without notice or fuss" (p. 31)

# Context & Application
Fetis provides the earliest systematic precedent for understanding how 19th-century music combines different organizational principles. His recognition that diatonic sequences suspend the "law of tonality" in favor of a "law of uniformity" anticipates Cohn's double syntax: "The mind, absorbed in the contemplation of the progressive series, momentarily loses the feeling of tonality, and regains it only at the final cadence" (Fetis 2008 [1844], 27, qtd. p. 31). Fetis's work first appeared as articles in 1832 and then in his 1844 harmony treatise.

# Examples
- **Unitonality**: Marenzio and Gesualdo (late 16th century) -- chromatic successions without tonal function
- **Transitonality**: Classical period music with functional modulation
- **Omnitonality**: Chromatically intensified dissonant harmonies leading toward "total destruction of the scale"
- **Combined species**: Wagner and 19th-century composers mixing local tonal function with chromatic larger-scale organization

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Fetis's "omnitonality" describes consonant triadic chromaticism
  **Correction**: Omnitonality concerns dissonant chromaticism; it is unitonality that describes tonally indeterminate consonant triadic successions

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: "Unitonality" means "one key"
  **Clarification**: It means the absence of functional tonal attraction -- each chord is an independent "harmony of repose"

- **Confusion**: The species are mutually exclusive historical periods
  **Clarification**: They are cumulative; all coexist within later compositions
