# Quick Definition
Francois-Joseph Fetis's classification of tonality into four historical species (unitonality, transitonality, pluritonality, omnitonality), each with distinct syntactic principles and affective properties.

# Formal Definition
Fetis (1832, 1844) divided tonality into four species representing stages of historical development:
1. **Unitonality**: Early music where triads succeed without establishing attractions between them; "every perfect chord is a harmony of repose"
2. **Transitonality**: Classical tonality with modulation between established keys
3. **Pluritonality**: Multiple simultaneous key implications
4. **Omnitonality**: Maximum chromaticism with "multiplicity, or even the universality of the keys," predicting "total destruction of the scale" and division into "twelve equal semitones"

Each species has its own syntactic principles and affective properties.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Fetis's framework proposes:
- Each species defines a distinct mode of tonal organization
- Later species do not supplant earlier ones - they are cumulative
- The best composers combine multiple species in a single work
- Tonal listening can shift between species spontaneously, "like a carpenter exchanging a screwdriver for a hammer"

This implies that classical tonality ("transitonality") is not the only legitimate syntax and that listeners can switch between syntactic modes.

# Musical Context/Application
Fetis's model provides historical precedent for understanding:
- How 19th-century music combines different organizational principles
- Why chromatic passages need not be forced into diatonic categories
- The cognitive capacity for "double syntax" (switching between organizational modes)
- The relationship between chromatic sequences and suspended tonal gravity

Fetis also recognized that diatonic sequences suspend the "law of tonality" in favor of a "law of uniformity" - the mind, "absorbed in the contemplation of the progressive series, momentarily loses the feeling of tonality."

# Examples
- Unitonality: Marenzio and Gesualdo (late 16th century) - chromatic successions without tonal function
- Transitonality: Classical period music with functional modulation
- Omnitonality: Chromatically intensified dissonant harmonies leading toward "total destruction of the scale"
- Combined species: Wagner and other 19th-century composers mixing local tonal function with chromatic larger-scale organization

# Common Confusions
- Fetis's "omnitonality" refers primarily to dissonant chromaticism, not consonant triadic chromaticism
- The species are cumulative, not mutually exclusive
- "Unitonality" does not mean "one key" but rather the absence of functional tonal attraction
- Fetis's framework is descriptive/historical, not prescriptive
