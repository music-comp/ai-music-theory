# Quick Definition
A supplementary dissonance is a tone added to a consonant triad that does not alter the triad's structural identity but smooths voice leading by reducing the work required to connect two triads -- the dissonant tone bridges what would otherwise be a disjunct triadic progression.

# Core Definition
Cohn introduces the concept of **supplementary dissonance** through the example at Figure 7.7: "Taken on its own terms, the progression involves only two units of voice-leading work: two upper voices descend by semitone; two lower ones are stationary. The progression between its constituent triads, however, involves five units of work, the maximum possible for two triads of opposite species" (p. 166). The supplementary dissonances "smooth the voice leading of a progression that is otherwise disjunct." This provides a motivation for seeking an approach that "takes dissonant harmonies as they are, rather than reducing them to something simpler and more familiar" (p. 166) -- the approach that leads to the substitution strategy and Tristan-genus theory.

# Key Properties
1. Added to a consonant triad without changing its identity
2. Reduces voice-leading work between successive harmonies
3. The dissonant chord may have lower voice-leading work than its triadic reduction
4. Motivates the substitution strategy: taking dissonant harmonies "as they are"

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify a dissonant chord with a unique embedded consonant triad
- Compare voice-leading work of the full dissonant progression with that of the reduced triadic progression
- If the dissonant version has fewer voice-leading units, the extra tones are "supplementary" -- smoothing dissonances

Example (Figure 7.7):
- Full progression (V7 to V7): 2 units of work
- Triadic reduction: 5 units of work (maximum for opposite-species triads)
- The sevenths are supplementary dissonances that dramatically reduce voice-leading distance

# Context & Application
The concept bridges the reduction and substitution strategies. When supplementary dissonances reduce voice-leading work, reduction to the triadic subset misses a crucial structural feature. This motivates the substitution strategy, where dissonant harmonies are modeled "based on an analogy with the triadic case, rather than an extension of it" (p. 166).

# Examples
- **Figure 7.7**: Classical prototype where V7-to-V7 motion involves 2 units of work vs. 5 for the embedded triads (p. 166)
- **Parsifal hexatonic poles with dissonance**: Over-sevenths and under-sevenths smooth the voice leading of hexatonic pole progressions that would be maximally disjunct as triads (pp. 163-164)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming all dissonant tones are supplementary
  **Correction**: The term applies specifically when the dissonant tone smooths voice leading without changing the chord's structural identity

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking supplementary dissonance is a standard music-theory term
  **Clarification**: It is Cohn's descriptive framing, not a widely established term; the concept is implicit in the discussion rather than formally defined
