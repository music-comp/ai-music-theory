# Quick Definition
The state in which a succession of triads cannot be unambiguously assigned to a single diatonic collection or tonic, leaving their scale-degree identities undetermined.

# Core Definition
Diatonic indeterminacy occurs when a triadic progression fails to establish a clear diatonic framework. By default, an isolated triad signals tonic status of its root (as Gottfried Weber described). However, tonic status requires confirmation through the remaining tones of its associated diatonic collection, local cadences, and structural repetition. When triads proliferate without such confirmation, or when enharmonic contradictions arise, their diatonic identities remain undetermined. "The more tones put in play, the less likely their alignment with respect to a diatonic collection that organizes their position and role with respect to some tonic. Until such a collection emerges and is cadentially crowned, the triadic progressions are diatonically indeterminate" (p. 28).

# Prerequisites
- **Chromatic vs. Diatonic Organization**: Understanding that triadic proximity can be measured by two independent metrics

# Key Properties
1. Measured by counting how many triadic pairs share membership in at least one diatonic collection
2. Lower counts indicate greater indeterminacy (higher "entropy")
3. Indeterminacy is not incoherence -- passages may be highly organized by voice-leading criteria
4. Essential enharmonicism evolves indeterminacy into contradiction
5. Scale-degree assignment becomes impossible or arbitrary
6. A passage can be locally determinate but globally indeterminate

# Construction / Recognition
Conditions for diatonic determinacy:
- Triads align with a single diatonic collection (7 pcs)
- A tonic is established through cadential confirmation
- Scale degrees can be consistently assigned to all chord tones

Conditions leading to indeterminacy:
- Triads do not share membership in any single diatonic collection
- Multiple potential tonics compete without resolution
- Enharmonic contradictions prevent consistent scale-degree assignment
- Essential enharmonicism distributes pitch-class conversion "everywhere and nowhere"

Measurement: For n triads, count pairs sharing membership in some diatonic collection. Lower counts indicate greater indeterminacy.

# Context & Application
Diatonic indeterminacy does not mean that a passage lacks syntax or is "random." It means that diatonic categories are inapplicable -- a different metric is needed. Fetis (1844) recognized "omnitonality" with its "multiplicity, or even the universality of the keys." Kurth (1920) described "absolute progressions" that bring about "total disruption of the original embracing tonal unity." After Kurth, "it became a commonplace of German musicology that neither the appearance of consonant triads nor their framing by occasional cadential progressions was sufficient to justify the judgment that their syntax was governed by the principles of classical tonality" (p. 30).

# Examples
- **Schubert, Piano Sonata in Bb, recapitulation**: Of 6 triadic pairs, only A major/f# minor shares any diatonic collection -- "among the most entropic" possible (p. 22)
- **Fetis's "unitonality"**: 16th-century chromatic madrigals (Marenzio, Gesualdo) where "no attraction is evident, because every perfect chord is a harmony of repose" (p. 31)
- **Chromatic sequences**: internally patterned but externally indeterminate

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Indeterminacy means artistic failure or random composition
  **Correction**: It describes the inapplicability of diatonic categories, not the absence of organization

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Roman numerals can resolve indeterminacy
  **Clarification**: Analysts can assign Roman numerals to indeterminate passages, but such labels describe rather than explain

- **Confusion**: Enharmonic notation creates indeterminacy
  **Clarification**: It reveals indeterminacy that exists in the pitch-class relationships
