# Quick Definition
The state in which a succession of triads cannot be unambiguously assigned to a single diatonic collection or tonic, leaving their scale-degree identities undetermined.

# Formal Definition
Diatonic indeterminacy occurs when a triadic progression fails to establish a clear diatonic framework. By default, an isolated major or minor triad signals tonic status of its root (as Gottfried Weber described). However, this tonic status requires confirmation through the remaining tones of its associated diatonic collection, local cadences, and structural repetition. When triads proliferate without such confirmation, or when enharmonic contradictions arise, their diatonic identities remain undetermined. The triads then constitute "tubs floating on their own bottoms" - internally coherent but relationally random from a diatonic perspective.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Conditions for diatonic determinacy:
- Triads align with a single diatonic collection (7 pc's)
- A tonic is established through cadential confirmation
- Scale degrees can be consistently assigned to all chord tones

Conditions leading to indeterminacy:
- Triads do not share membership in any single diatonic collection
- Multiple potential tonics compete without resolution
- Enharmonic contradictions prevent consistent scale-degree assignment
- Essential enharmonicism distributes pitch-class conversion "everywhere and nowhere"

Measure of indeterminacy: For n triads, count pairs sharing membership in some diatonic collection. Lower counts indicate greater indeterminacy.

# Musical Context/Application
Diatonic indeterminacy characterizes many 19th-century chromatic passages:
- Fetis (1844): "omnitonality" involves "multiplicity, or even the universality of the keys"
- Kurth (1920): "absolute progressions" bring about "total disruption of the original embracing tonal unity"
- Such passages are "extratonal" - their relation to bounding tonics is not tonally determined

Indeterminacy does not mean incoherence. Passages indeterminate diatonically may be highly organized by voice-leading criteria.

# Examples
- Schubert, Piano Sonata in Bb, recapitulation: of 6 triadic pairs, only A major/f# minor shares any diatonic collection - among the most entropic possible
- Fetis's "unitonality": 16th-century chromatic madrigals (Marenzio, Gesualdo) where "no attraction is evident, because every perfect chord is a harmony of repose"
- Chromatic sequences: internally patterned but externally indeterminate

# Common Confusions
- Indeterminacy is not chaos or randomness - it describes the inapplicability of diatonic categories, not the absence of organization
- A passage can be locally determinate but globally indeterminate (each span internally tonal, but their relationship is chromatic)
- Enharmonic notation does not create indeterminacy - it reveals indeterminacy that exists in the pitch-class relationships
- Analysts can still assign Roman numerals to indeterminate passages, but such labels describe rather than explain
