# Quick Definition
Ernst Kurth's concept of chromatic progressions, particularly those involving third relations, that derive their identity from internal structure and local connections rather than tonal function.

# Formal Definition
Ernst Kurth (1920) proposed that many chromatic progressions, particularly those involving root relations by third, introduce "rifts, wedges, and fissures into the fabric of tonality." The identity and function of these chords are found in their internal structure and immediate local connections to antecedents and successors, not in their relation to a governing tonic. When concatenated with sufficient intensity and persistence, such "absolute progressions" bring about "the total disruption of the original embracing tonal unity." Their relation to the tonal pillars that bound them is not tonally determined - they are "extratonal."

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Characteristics of absolute progressions:
- Root relations by third (major or minor)
- Identity derived from internal chord structure
- Connection based on local voice-leading to adjacent chords
- Governed by the logic of repetition (sequences) rather than tonal function
- "Extratonal" relation to bounding tonal pillars

Kurth discovered that chromatic sequences, like Fetis's diatonic ones, operate by the logic of repetition, not tonal attraction.

# Musical Context/Application
Kurth's work (primarily on Wagner's Tristan) became foundational for German musicology:
- Established that consonant triads and occasional cadences do not automatically justify tonal interpretation
- Recognized that multiple syntactic principles can operate within a single composition
- Influenced Adorno, Dahlhaus, and subsequent German scholars
- Provided theoretical grounding for understanding chromatic passages as systematically organized (not merely "coloristic")

After Kurth, it became "a commonplace of German musicology that neither the appearance of consonant triads nor their framing by occasional cadential progressions was sufficient to justify the judgment that their syntax was governed by the principles of classical tonality."

# Examples
- Wagner, *Tristan und Isolde*: Kurth's primary analytical focus
- Wagner, *Die Walkure*, Magic Sleep: Katz called it lacking "tonal implication"; Abbate described its "harmonic irrationality"
- Chromatic sequences generally: governed by repetition logic, not tonal function
- Third-related progressions throughout 19th-century repertoire

# Common Confusions
- "Absolute" does not mean "atonal" - the progressions involve consonant triads
- The term does not imply arbitrariness but rather a different basis for organization
- Kurth's analysis of Wagner influenced both theoretical understanding and practical composition
- "Extratonal" passages can be bounded by tonal passages without being integrated into tonal logic
