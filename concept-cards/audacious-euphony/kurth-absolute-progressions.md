# Quick Definition
Ernst Kurth's concept of chromatic progressions, particularly those involving third relations, that derive their identity from internal structure and local connections rather than tonal function.

# Core Definition
Ernst Kurth (1920) proposed that many chromatic progressions, particularly those involving root relations by third, "introduced rifts, wedges, and fissures into the fabric of tonality" (p. 30). "The identity and function of these chords are found in their internal structure and in their local connections to their immediate antecedents and successors" (p. 30). When concatenated with sufficient intensity and persistence, such absolute progressions bring about "the total disruption of the original embracing tonal unity" (Kurth 1991, 120, qtd. p. 30). Their relation to the tonal pillars bounding them is not tonally determined -- they are "extratonal."

# Prerequisites
- **Fetis's Tonal Species**: Kurth extends Fetis's recognition of multiple tonal principles

# Key Properties
1. Identity derived from internal chord structure, not tonal function
2. Connection based on local voice-leading to adjacent chords
3. Governed by the logic of repetition (sequences) rather than tonal attraction
4. "Extratonal" relation to bounding tonal pillars
5. Root relations by third are characteristic
6. Chromatic sequences are the primary vehicle

# Construction / Recognition
Characteristics of absolute progressions:
- Root relations by third (major or minor)
- Identity derived from internal chord structure
- Connection based on local voice-leading
- Governed by repetition logic, not tonal function
- "Extratonal" -- relation to bounding tonics is not tonally determined

Kurth discovered that chromatic sequences, like Fetis's diatonic ones, operate by the logic of repetition rather than tonal attraction.

# Context & Application
Kurth's *Romantische Harmonik und ihre Krise in Wagners "Tristan"* (1920) became foundational for German musicology. After it, "it became a commonplace of German musicology that neither the appearance of consonant triads nor their framing by occasional cadential progressions was sufficient to justify the judgment that their syntax was governed by the principles of classical tonality; other factors were necessary in addition" (p. 30). This view was shared by Adorno, Dahlhaus, and influenced Katz, Mitchell, Salzer, and Schachter in America.

# Examples
- **Wagner, Tristan und Isolde**: Kurth's primary analytical focus
- **Wagner, Die Walkure, Magic Sleep**: Katz called it lacking "tonal implication" (1945, 213); Abbate described its "harmonic irrationality"
- **Mitchell on circle of fifths**: "can be arrested at any point or it can just as easily go on in perpetuity" (1962, 9)
- **Salzer and Schachter**: "we register the equal intervallic progressions without referring them to a supposed diatonic original. This temporary lack of a diatonic frame of reference creates, as it were, a suspension of tonal gravity" (1969, 215)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: "Absolute" means "atonal"
  **Correction**: The progressions involve consonant triads; "absolute" means their identity is self-contained rather than referential to a tonic

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: "Extratonal" means outside tonality
  **Clarification**: It means the relation to bounding tonal pillars is not tonally determined; the passage may be bounded by tonal passages

- **Confusion**: Kurth's analysis applies only to Wagner
  **Clarification**: The principle of absolute progressions applies to chromatic sequences throughout the 19th-century repertoire
