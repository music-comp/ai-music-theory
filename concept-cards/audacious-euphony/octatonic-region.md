# Quick Definition
An octatonic region bridges two adjacent Boretz regions, containing eight Tristan-genus chords (four half-diminished from one region, four same-rooted dominant sevenths from the neighboring region) whose tones are drawn from an octatonic scale -- analogous to hexatonic regions bridging Weitzmann regions.

# Core Definition
**Octatonic regions** bridge adjacent Boretz regions. "Regions that combine the half-diminished seventh chords of one system with the same-rooted dominant seventh chords of the neighboring system form bridges between adjacent Boretz regions. As the eight chords of this bridging region draw their tones from an octatonic scale, they are referred to as octatonic regions. Their role with respect to the Boretz regions is exactly analogous to the role of the hexatonic regions with respect to the Weitzmann regions, although their individual transformations lack the minimal work of the hexatonic transformations L and P" (p. 172).

# Prerequisites
- **Boretz region**: The regions being bridged
- **Tristan genus**: The chords populating the regions

# Key Properties
1. Three octatonic regions exist (corresponding to three octatonic scales)
2. Each contains 8 chords: 4 ø7 from one Boretz region + 4 V7 from the adjacent region
3. All tones draw from a single octatonic collection
4. Transformations within involve 2 voices moving by semitone (n-2 where n=4)
5. Less parsimonious than hexatonic bridges (which involve only 1 moving voice)

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify two adjacent Boretz regions (cores differing by semitone)
- Take the 4 upshifters (ø7 chords) from the lower region
- Take the 4 downshifters (V7 chords) from the higher region
- These 8 chords share tones from one octatonic scale
- The progression from chord 5 to chord 6 in the Tristan Prelude is one such octatonic bridge

# Context & Application
Octatonic regions enable interregional modulation in the tetrachordal voice-leading system. They appear at the 12:00, 4:00, and 8:00 positions on 4-Cube Trio, where minor seventh chords and French sixth chords also serve as alternative bridges. "Because the octatonic transformations involve two voices moving by semitone in parallel motion, they jump voice-leading zones, leaving a gap that is not present in the triadic case" (p. 175).

# Examples
- **Tristan Prelude, chords 5-6**: The Aø7 to C#7 progression bridges two Boretz regions via an octatonic transformation, marking the first "interregional modulation" (pp. 169, 172)
- **Brunnhilde's Immolation**: Eb7 to C#ø7 to C#7 crosses from one Boretz region into another via octatonic bridge, sustaining double-semitone voice leading (pp. 173-174)
- **Figure 7.15**: Three Boretz spiders in union with three octatonic pools, showing interlocking regions subject to figure-ground reversals (p. 174)

**Octatonic scale properties** (from pp. 172-173): The underlying octatonic (diminished) scale has interval pattern H-W-H-W-H-W-H-W, is symmetric under T3/T6/T9, and contains four interlocking diminished seventh chord subsets. Three distinct collections exist: Oct 0,1 {C, Db, Eb, E, F#, G, A, Bb}, Oct 1,2 {Db, D, E, F, G, Ab, Bb, B}, Oct 2,3 {D, Eb, F, Gb, Ab, A, B, C}. The scale appears prominently in late Romantic practice, Russian nationalist composers (Rimsky-Korsakov), and early twentieth-century music (Stravinsky, Bartok, Messiaen). The combined pitch content of any dominant seventh and its octatonic pole half-diminished seventh exhausts one octatonic scale

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming octatonic regions replace Boretz regions
  **Correction**: They bridge Boretz regions; the two structures interlock and are subject to figure-ground reversals

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking octatonic transformations have minimal voice-leading work like hexatonic L and P
  **Clarification**: Octatonic bridges require n-2 = 2 moving voices, not 1; this is why triads have special voice-leading properties
