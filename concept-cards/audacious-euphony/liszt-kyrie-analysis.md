# Quick Definition
Liszt's 1879 *Missa pro organo lectarum* Kyrie exemplifies a departure-return script realized as a palindrome of voice-leading zones, where each individual voice forms a palindrome but the staggered firing order prevents strict triadic palindrome.

# Formal Definition
The brief wordless Kyrie setting consists of three phrases (Kyrie-Christe-Kyrie), each containing three parallel segments plus a cadential fourth segment (twelve segments total). The composition is entirely triadic, beginning and ending in Bb major. Ramon Satyendra (1992) observes that each individual voice constitutes a palindrome whose axis is the double bar where the key signature converts from flats to sharps. However, because the voices' entries are staggered, the triadic progression itself is not strictly palindromic.

# Construction/Recognition
- Individual voices: Each is a strict palindrome about the central axis
- Triadic level: The palindrome is not strict; deviations occur in the third and fourth triads
- Deviations: Db major -> f minor is mirrored by c# minor -> A major (retrograded transposition down a diminished fourth)
- Voice-leading zones: The palindrome IS strict at the zonal level
- All voice leading downshifts in the first two phrases and upshifts in the final phrase

# Musical Context
The Kyrie demonstrates that departure-return schemes can operate in chromatic music with little connection to diatonic scale degrees or Riemannian functions. The palindromic structure is evident to the organist's hand through the kinetic complementarity of downshifting and upshifting.

# Common Confusions
- The palindrome is strict for individual voices but not for the triadic progression
- The palindrome IS strict at the level of voice-leading zones
- The composition has little functional harmony beyond the cadences; structure comes from voice-leading trajectory
