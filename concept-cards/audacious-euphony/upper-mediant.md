# Quick Definition

Upper mediant triads have roots a third above the tonic and often serve as off-tonic harmonic goals in place of the traditional dominant, particularly in nineteenth-century chromatic music.

# Core Definition

**Upper mediant** (Kopp's term, 2002) refers to triads whose roots lie a major or minor third above the tonic. In chromatic practice, upper mediants appropriate the structural role traditionally assigned to the dominant, serving as off-tonic harmonic goals in expositions, development section platforms, and alternatives to dominant prolongation. For a C tonic, the four upper mediants are E major, E minor, Eb major, and Eb minor (Cohn, pp. 152–153).

# Prerequisites

- **Voice-leading zones** — Upper mediants occupy specific zones relative to tonic, enabling systematic tracking of their structural role
- **Hexatonic cycle** — Many upper mediant relationships involve hexatonic connections (e.g., tonic to its hexatonic pole via mediant steps)

# Key Properties

1. Root lies a major or minor third above the tonic
2. Four possible upper mediants for any tonic (two major, two minor)
3. Can substitute for dominant as structural secondary key area
4. Sequential presentation in upshifting order creates large-scale chromatic trajectories
5. Voice-leading connections often involve common tones with tonic triad

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify Upper Mediants:
1. Determine the tonic pitch class
2. Find triads rooted a minor third above (e.g., C → Eb major/minor)
3. Find triads rooted a major third above (e.g., C → E major/minor)
4. These four triads are the upper mediants

## To Recognize Upper Mediant Function:
1. Check if an off-tonic key area has a root a third above the tonic
2. Verify it serves a structural role (secondary theme, development platform, etc.)
3. Note whether dominant function is weakened or absent in the movement

# Context & Application

- **Typical contexts**: Sonata forms where dominant is avoided as secondary key; development sections using mediant platforms
- **Common applications**: Large-scale tonal plans organized by ascending thirds; chromatic key schemes replacing dominant polarity
- **Historical/stylistic notes**: Reflects nineteenth-century trends toward weakened dominant function, third-related key schemes, and chromatic voice-leading logic over root-based function

# Examples

**Example 1** (pp. 152–153): Dvorak's "New World" Symphony, first movement — the movement avoids tonicizing dominant B entirely. Off-tonic roles are filled by four upper mediants in upshifting order: g minor (m. 91), G major (m. 137), then transposed up by chromatic semitone in the recapitulation, with Ab major (proxy for G#, hexatonic pole of tonic) closing the recapitulation. The progression through zones 7 → 8 → 10 → 11 forms part of a P/S chain.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming upper mediants always replace dominant function
  **Correction**: Both upper mediant and dominant function can coexist in a single movement; upper mediants supplement rather than necessarily replace

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Conflating upper mediant with relative major/minor
  **Clarification**: Relative major/minor involves a specific diatonic relationship; upper mediants include chromatic mediants (e.g., C major → E major) that are not diatonic relatives

- **Confusion**: Thinking "upper" refers to register
  **Clarification**: "Upper" refers to the direction of root motion (ascending third from tonic), not to pitch register
