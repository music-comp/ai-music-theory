# Quick Definition
The omnibus progression (also called the "devil's mill") is a sequential template that transposes dominant seventh chords by minor third, featuring contrary semitonal motion in two voices while two voices remain stationary, producing balanced voice leading without net directional shift.

# Formal Definition
The **omnibus progression** is a chromatic sequence connecting dominant seventh chords related by T3 (minor third transposition). It is the first of three sequential templates Cohn presents for Tristan-genus chords (figure 7.8(a)). Unlike the T11 and T5 sequences that alternate V7 and half-diminished seventh chords, the omnibus uses only dominant seventh chords and produces balanced (non-circumnavigatory) voice leading through contrary motion.

The progression's distinctive voice-leading properties derive from a principle Cohn italicizes for emphasis: the minor third "divides the octave into as many equal parts as a seventh chord has tones."

# Construction/Recognition
How to construct the omnibus:
1. Begin with any dominant seventh chord (e.g., C7: C-E-G-Bb)
2. Hold two voices stationary
3. Move the remaining two voices by semitone in opposite directions
4. The result is a dominant seventh chord a minor third away (Eb7: Eb-G-Bb-Db)

Recognition features:
- Only dominant seventh chords (no half-diminished)
- Minor third transposition between successive chords
- Two voices move, two hold (contrary semitonal motion)
- No net upshift or downshift (balanced)
- Chromaticized voice exchanges may embellish the progression

# Musical Context
The omnibus arises already in the eighteenth century and was extensively elaborated by nineteenth-century composers. When embellished by chromaticized voice exchanges, it becomes known as the "omnibus" or "devil's mill." The progression shares anomalous features with triadic major-third transpositions: just as major thirds divide the octave into as many equal parts as a triad has notes (producing contrary motion in triadic progressions), minor thirds divide the octave into as many parts as a seventh chord has notes.

# Common Confusions
- The omnibus is balanced (no circumnavigation), unlike the T11 and T5 seventh-chord sequences
- "Omnibus" and "devil's mill" are traditional names for the embellished version of this progression
- The contrary motion arises because minor third divides the octave into four parts (matching the chord's cardinality), paralleling how major third relates to triads
- The omnibus involves only dominant seventh chords, not the alternation of V7 and half-diminished seventh found in other Tristan-genus sequences
