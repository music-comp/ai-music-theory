# Quick Definition
Candace Brower's (2008) model generating the diatonic Tonnetz in stages from a seed node, retracing pitch system development across historical time and exploring the interplay of centrifugal (acoustic/consonance) and centripetal (closure-seeking) forces.

# Core Definition
Rather than accepting the Tonnetz as a space given all at once, Brower generates it in stages from a seed node. Her stages "roughly retrace the development of pitch systems across historical time" (Brower 2008, 71). She recognizes a centrifugal force operating on purely tuned triads, transposing them by purely tuned intervals, balanced by a centripetal force that "relies on our psychophysiological propensity to ignore pitch differences beneath a threshold of just-noticeability, treats similarity as equivalence, and induces systemic closure through symmetric completion" (Cohn, p. 192). Cohn reframes this as the tension between acoustic consonance and melodic proximity that is "central to this book from the outset" (p. 193).

# Prerequisites
- **Tonnetz**: Must understand the basic pitch-class network
- **Consonant triad**: The seed object from which the Tonnetz grows
- **Generating interval**: The perfect fifth as the interval that extends the space

# Key Properties
1. Generates the Tonnetz incrementally rather than presenting it all at once
2. Centrifugal force: acoustic consonance driving outward extension
3. Centripetal force: closure-seeking through identification of near-unisons
4. Each stage of closure involves accepting a pseudo-identity
5. The process reveals how acoustic and voice-leading properties collaborate

# Construction / Recognition
**Stage 1** (Figure 8.4a): C major transposed by perfect fifth yields F major (left) and G major (right). Neighboring triads share a single point. E is unshared.

**Stage 2** (Figure 8.4b): E's fifth relations with A (left) and B (right) close two local triangles, creating a trapezoid periphery of seven tones.

**Stage 3** (Figure 8.4c-d): Adding d minor (preferred over b minor) "replicates a tone already present," creating the first closure. The two D tones differ by a syntonic comma in just intonation. Accepting their identity creates a parallelogram tiled into six consonant triads -- the diatonic collection.

**Final closure**: Accepting the pseudo-fifth (B-F tritone) as if perfect closes the chain of fifths into a circuit, transforming the parallelogram into a Mobius strip (pp. 193-195).

# Context & Application
The genesis narrative reveals that the diatonic Tonnetz is "dually determined by the properties of the overdetermined diatonic triad that generates it" (p. 196). Even before chromatic extensions, the potential reciprocity of acoustics and voice leading is visible: triads sharing an edge are related by a single unit of voice-leading work on the diatonic Mobius strip, and the mobile voice moves by minimal diatonic step.

Brower's derivation is appropriated from Hauptmann and Riemann (Harrison 1994, 44-45). Brower's orientation rotates Cohn's Tonnetz 90 degrees to place dominants above tonics (p. 193, n. 6).

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the genesis stages as merely historical narrative
  **Correction**: Each stage reveals a principled structural decision about closure that has ongoing analytical consequences

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The final Mobius strip topology is Brower's invention
  **Clarification**: It follows necessarily from accepting the pseudo-fifth; Brower identifies and describes the topology (Brower 2008, 73-74)
