# Quick Definition
A distortion inherent in planar representations of cyclic pitch-class space, where points that are actually identical (like a Tonnetz strip's top and bottom edges) appear separated on the page -- analogous to how Eurocentric world maps distort the distance between Alaska and Siberia.

# Formal Definition
The Bering Strait problem names the unavoidable distortion arising when cyclic (toroidal) musical spaces are represented on a two-dimensional plane. In a Tonnetz strip, each tone and edge at the top reappears at the bottom, "masking identities and distorting distances." Were these identities honored by "gluing together," the strip would convert to a cylinder. The failure to do so is "a concession to the dimensional limits of the printed page." Geometric models of pitch space have been in use for some 1,500 years, and the cyclic structure of chromatic space will create such problems in any planar representation.

# Construction/Recognition
Recognize the Bering Strait problem whenever:
- A graph has edges or vertices that represent the same pitch class but appear at different locations
- Distances on the graph do not accurately reflect distances in the underlying musical space
- A linear or planar layout attempts to represent a cyclic or toroidal structure
- Tonnetz strips show identical augmented triads at top and bottom boundaries

# Musical Context
Despite this flaw, planar representations are worth tolerating because of the many advantages they afford: visual accessibility, capacity for tracking compositional paths, and revelation of parallel motion. Cohn notes that "these limitations are worth tolerating because of the many advantages that the triangularly tiled planar representations afford," and that they can be mitigated with supplementary "legends" guiding map interpretation.

# Common Confusions
- The Bering Strait problem is a limitation of the representation, not of the underlying musical space
- "Gluing" the edges would solve the problem topologically (creating a cylinder or torus) but would sacrifice the visual clarity of the planar format
- Different maps foreground different relationships; the Bering Strait problem affects some representations more than others
- The problem becomes more acute when multiple cyclic dimensions must be represented simultaneously
