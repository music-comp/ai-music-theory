# Quick Definition
A distortion inherent in planar representations of cyclic pitch-class space, where points that are actually identical appear separated on the page -- analogous to how Eurocentric world maps distort the distance between Alaska and Siberia.

# Core Definition
The Bering Strait problem names the unavoidable distortion arising when cyclic (toroidal) musical spaces are represented on a two-dimensional plane. In a Tonnetz strip, "each tone and edge at the top of a strip reappears at its bottom, masking identities and distorting distances" (p. 46). Were these identities honored by "gluing together," the strip would convert to a cylinder. The failure to do so is "a concession to the dimensional limits of the printed page" (p. 46). In two dimensions, the distortion compounds: locations at left replicate those at right, and locations at bottom replicate those at top. Correcting both would wrap the plane into a torus. Geometric models of pitch space have been in use for some 1,500 years, and the cyclic structure of chromatic space creates such problems in any planar representation.

# Key Properties
1. Arises whenever cyclic (toroidal) spaces are represented on a plane
2. Masks pitch-class identities at graph boundaries
3. Distorts distances between points near opposite edges
4. Affects one dimension (cylinder) or two dimensions (torus)
5. Can be mitigated with supplementary "legends" but not fully eliminated in 2D
6. A tradeoff: tolerated for the advantages planar representations afford

# Construction / Recognition
Recognize the Bering Strait problem whenever:
- A graph has edges or vertices that represent the same pitch class but appear at different locations
- Distances on the graph do not accurately reflect distances in the underlying musical space
- A linear or planar layout attempts to represent a cyclic or toroidal structure
- Tonnetz strips show identical augmented triads at top and bottom boundaries

# Context & Application
"These limitations are worth tolerating because of the many advantages that the triangularly tiled planar representations afford" (p. 46). Cohn notes that in cyclic space, "even a second dimension introduces falsifications and distortions, like the Bering Strait problem familiar from Eurocentric world maps" (p. 33). The connected Tonnetz (figure 5.6) suffers this distortion in two dimensions, correctable by gluing to create a cylinder and then a torus. Cube Dance avoids the Bering Strait distortion because it gives augmented triads explicit locations and does not require imagination across boundaries.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: The Bering Strait problem indicates a flaw in the underlying musical space
  **Correction**: It is a limitation of the representation, not the space itself

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: "Gluing" the edges would solve everything
  **Clarification**: Gluing creates a cylinder or torus, which solves the topological problem but sacrifices visual clarity of the planar format

- **Confusion**: The problem affects all representations equally
  **Clarification**: Different representations foreground different relationships; Cube Dance avoids this problem where the Tonnetz does not
