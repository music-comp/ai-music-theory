# Quick Definition
The Nebenverwandt transformation takes a major triad to its "next-related" minor triad (or vice versa), moving two voices by semitone in contrary motion while keeping one tone fixed.

# Formal Definition
N (Nebenverwandt) is a triadic transformation defined by Weitzmann as:
- The inversion about the root of a major triad
- The inversion about the fifth of a minor triad

N maps:
- A major triad to the minor triad a perfect fifth below its root (e.g., C major to f minor)
- A minor triad to the major triad a perfect fifth above its fifth (e.g., f minor to C major)

# Construction/Recognition
Voice-leading action:
- C major {CEG} -> f minor {FAbC}: E moves down to F (semitone), G moves up to Ab (semitone), C retained
- f minor {FAbC} -> C major {CEG}: F moves up to E, Ab moves down to G, C retained

N requires 2 units of voice-leading work (two semitone motions in contrary direction).
N is the L-inverse (L'): its semitonally moving and stationary voices swap those of L.

# Musical Context
The nebenverwandt relationship connects a major tonic to its minor subdominant (or minor tonic to major dominant). This classical progression underlies authentic cadential patterns. Weitzmann used the term to describe key relationships; his student Oettingen later interpreted it transformationally. The N/R alternation generates the complete Weitzmann region cycle.

# Examples
- Schubert Fourth Symphony, mm. 86-106: N/R chain where each major triad is prolonged as local tonic, minor triads serve as subdominant
- Schubert Ninth Symphony trombone solo: N/R cycle with dominant sevenths attached to major triads
- Classical syntax: V-i motion (major dominant to minor tonic) embodies N

# Common Confusions
- N is not the same as functional dominant-tonic; it describes a triadic relationship, not a tonal function
- N can be conceived as L', the inverse of L (swapping moving and stationary voices)
- The term comes from German "Nebenverwandt" (next-related), coined by Weitzmann
