# Quick Definition
A three-note chord consisting of a major third and a minor third (in either order), either major or minor, that constitutes the primary harmonic object in both classical diatonic tonality and pan-triadic syntax.

# Core Definition
A consonant triad is a three-note chord that divides the octave nearly evenly, consisting of a major third (4 semitones) and a minor third (3 semitones) stacked in either order. The 24 consonant triads (12 major, 12 minor) are the central objects of *Audacious Euphony*. They possess two distinct "natures": acoustic consonance (generating classical diatonic syntax) and near-evenness (generating pan-triadic syntax). Throughout the book, "triad" in its unmodified form "refers restrictively to consonant triads" (synthesized from Glossary, p. 229). The book's central claim is that triads are "homophonous diamorphs" (Ch. 2, p. 59) — identical sonic objects with dual syntactic functions.

# Key Properties
1. Major triad: root + major third (4 semitones) + minor third (3 semitones) = [0, 4, 7]
2. Minor triad: root + minor third (3 semitones) + major third (4 semitones) = [0, 3, 7]
3. Both divide 12 semitones into 3 + 4 + 5 or 4 + 3 + 5
4. "Nearly even": as close to equal division as possible without being perfectly even
5. Each consonant triad is one semitone away from exactly one augmented triad
6. 24 total in 12-tone equal temperament

# Construction / Recognition
- Major triad: root + M3 + P5 (e.g., C-E-G)
- Minor triad: root + m3 + P5 (e.g., C-Eb-G)
- Notation: Major = uppercase (C, G, Eb) or plus sign (C+); Minor = lowercase (c, g, eb) or minus sign (c-)
- Excludes augmented triads (perfectly even) and diminished triads

# Context & Application
Consonant triads serve as the exclusive harmonic vocabulary of pan-triadic passages, nodes in all geometric representations (Tonnetz, Cube Dance), and the domain over which transformations operate. They are the link between classical and romantic harmonic syntax, functioning as "homophonous diamorphs" that participate in both systems.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 2, p. 59): Cohn likens the triad to overdetermined organs in human physiology: "Mouths are for eating, talking, and breathing" — triads similarly serve multiple syntactic functions.

**Example 2** (p. 60): Cohn will "continue to refer to them collectively as consonant triads, even though we are more interested in their extensionally identical but intensionally distinct status as nearly even trichords."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Including augmented or diminished triads as "consonant triads"
  **Correction**: Only major and minor triads qualify; augmented triads play a structural role but are not members of this class

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking "consonant" is the only relevant property
  **Clarification**: Triads also possess near-evenness (the "second nature"), which is equally important for pan-triadic syntax
- **Confusion**: Assuming the German *Klang* always means "consonant triad"
  **Clarification**: In Cohn's usage, the terms are synonymous, but *Klang* has broader meanings in other contexts
