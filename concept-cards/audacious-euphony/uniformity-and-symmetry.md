# Quick Definition
Fetis's concepts of uniformity and symmetry describe the forces that can override tonal hierarchy, allowing sequences to suspend the distinction between consonance and dissonance through systematic repetition.

# Formal Definition
Francois-Joseph Fetis identified uniformity and symmetry as compositional resources that can "override the forces of tonality." In a sequence, "the succession and... movement fix the attention of the mind, which holds on to the form so strongly that any irregularity of tonality is not noticed" (Fetis 2008 [1844]). The listener's attention is "diverted from the feeling of tonality by symmetry of movement and succession," allowing dissonances to behave like consonances and chromatic progressions to escape diatonic gravity.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Two types of sequence distinguished:

1. Diatonic sequence:
- Pattern iterations replicate generic intervals
- Specific intervals channeled within diatonic scale
- Uniformity is "kept in check" by prior commitment to the scale
- Dissonances still require resolution (but may be tolerated more freely)

2. Chromatic sequence:
- Pattern iterations replicate exact intervals
- "The law of uniformity has a monolithic force"
- Rapid chromatic turnover "ruptures any ability of the diatonic collection to hold a focus on a particular global tonic"
- Consonance/dissonance distinction is leveled

This duality is formulated by later theorists as:
- Diatony versus repetition (Schenker 1954 [1906])
- Magnetism versus inertia (Larson 1994)

# Musical Context/Application
Uniformity and symmetry explain how chromatic sequences (like hexatonic cycles) can suspend normal tonal expectations. The systematic repetition creates its own logic that overrides the default hierarchies of diatonic tonality.

Fetis's phenomenological description:

On consonance/dissonance leveling:
"Fetis writes that a sequence levels the distinction between consonance and dissonance. A diminished fifth no longer requires resolution; in this context, its behavior is indistinguishable from that of the perfect fifth."

# Common Confusions
- Uniformity is not the same as "unity" in a general sense; it specifically refers to systematic repetition
- Symmetry here means regularity of pattern, not (necessarily) inversional or retrogressive symmetry
- The suspension of tonal feeling is temporary; sequences typically resolve back to tonal orientation
- Fetis's terms describe compositional resources, not automatic perceptual effects
