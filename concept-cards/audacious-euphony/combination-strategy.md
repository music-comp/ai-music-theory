# Quick Definition
The combination strategy generates dissonant harmonies from the union of two consonant triads, producing tetrachords that occupy compact, determinate parallelogram positions on the Tonnetz.

# Core Definition
**Combination** generates dissonant harmonies from the union of two triads. Rameau advanced this in his 1760 *Code pratique*, viewing major and minor seventh chords as combining two complete triads. Though "only occasionally sighted in contemporary harmonic theory," it was common in the nineteenth century, underlying analyses by Hauptmann, Helmholtz, Riemann, Capellen (1908, *Doppelklang*), and Schenker (p. 158). On the Tonnetz, dissonances generated by combination "benefit from compact and determinate locations": specifically, tetrachords formed by combining two edge-adjacent triads create parallelograms from their two triangles (p. 160).

# Prerequisites
- **Four approaches to dissonance**: Understanding combination as one of four complementary strategies
- **Consonant triad**: The building blocks that are combined
- **Tonnetz representation**: The geometric space where combination-generated chords have compact locations

# Key Properties
1. Generates only three specific tetrachord types from edge-adjacent triads on the Tonnetz
2. These tetrachords have compact parallelogram Tonnetz locations, unlike most dissonant harmonies
3. Most dissonant harmonies are "represented on the standard Tonnetz by shapes that are irregular, sprawling, or even disconnected" (p. 160)
4. Gollin (1998) extends the Tonnetz to three dimensions to represent dominant and half-diminished sevenths as tetrahedra

# Construction / Recognition
Three tetrachords generated by combination of edge-adjacent triads:

1. **Major seventh chord** = union of L-related triads
   - Shared edge: minor third
   - Tonnetz: parallelogram bisected by minor-third edge

2. **Minor seventh chord** = union of R-related triads
   - Shared edge: major third
   - Tonnetz: parallelogram bisected by major-third edge

3. **Split-third chord [0347]** = union of P-related triads
   - Shared edge: perfect fifth
   - Lendvai's "alpha" sonority in Bartok
   - Tonnetz: parallelogram bisected by fifth edge

# Context & Application
Although combination has not figured heavily in Cohn's overall thinking, it illustrates how the Tonnetz can represent certain dissonant harmonies compactly. The strategy is most useful when a tetrachord mediates between two triads sharing a Tonnetz edge, and when compact geometric representation aids analysis. The split-third chord, the least familiar of the three, does not arise prominently until the twentieth century.

# Examples
- **Schreker's Kammersymphonie (1916)**: "The C# split third fills a gap that is left along the major third axis by the juxtaposition of the two previous triads, the hexatonic poles A major and f minor, with both of which it shares two common tones" (p. 160)
- **Rameau's identification (1760)**: Major and minor seventh chords as combining two complete triads
- **Hauptmann (1853)**: Major and minor seventh chords generated through combination

## Contrasts With
- Reduction (isolates one triad from a larger chord; combination unites two triads)
- Substitution (relates dissonances to perfectly even chords; combination relates them to triadic pairs)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming all seventh chords can be generated by combination
  **Correction**: Only three types (major seventh, minor seventh, split-third) arise from edge-adjacent triads; dominant and half-diminished sevenths do not have compact Tonnetz locations

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the split-third chord is a common-practice sonority
  **Clarification**: It "does not arise to prominence until the twentieth century" (p. 160); it is Lendvai's "alpha" sonority found in Bartok
