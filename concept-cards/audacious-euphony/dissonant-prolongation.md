# Quick Definition
Dissonant prolongation occurs when a dissonant harmony (such as an augmented triad) governs a span of music, with consonant triads subordinating to it, reversing the normal diodic relationship between consonance and dissonance.

# Formal Definition
Robert P. Morgan's concept of "dissonant prolongation" (1976) describes situations where "consonant triads subordinate to dissonant ones, not only locally but across spans of significant duration." This reverses the classical assumption that only consonant harmonies can be prolonged (the basis of Schenkerian analysis). In such passages, the augmented triad may function as the "controlling harmony" even though "the score contains no vertical slice or contiguous patch, even an egregiously gerrymandered one, to be circled and labeled as a 'controlling harmony'" (p. 63).

# Construction/Recognition
Conditions for dissonant prolongation:
1. A dissonant harmony (e.g., augmented triad) provides structural coherence
2. Consonant triads function as elaborations or passing chords
3. The dissonant harmony may or may not appear as a surface sonority
4. The passage cannot be coherently explained by diatonic prolongational logic

Schenker's implicit acceptance:
- His graphs of passages from Beethoven and Wolf show "dissonant prolongations"
- These "cannot be reconciled to his pronouncements elsewhere that only consonant harmonies are susceptible to composing out"
- Nevertheless, "Schenker found dissonant prolongations aurally and conceptually plausible, even if they 'prolong' an idea that dissonates with the fundaments of tonality" (p. 63)

# Musical Context
Dissonant prolongation allows analysts to make sense of chromatic passages where consonant triads seem to orbit around an augmented triad. The concept challenges strict Schenkerian assumptions while extending prolongational thinking to chromatic repertoire.

# Examples
From the text (pp. 63-64):

Schubert, Symphony No. 2, finale:
"If prolongation is at work in this passage in any form, then its more plausible object is the FAC# triad that unites the three tonics. In graphs of similar passages by Beethoven and Wolf, Heinrich Schenker implied that he understood the arpeggiated augmented triad as the prolonged displacement, by chromatic neighbor, of a major triad."

"When Db major is tonicized at m. 312, C is displaced by a Db neighbor, which continues to be locally supported (qua C#) when A major is tonicized at m. 332, and only returns to C at m. 352, when F major is retonicized. If, as Schenker implies in analogous passages, FADb is the prolonged harmony from m. 312 to m. 347, then it follows that both Db major and A major are subordinated to a controlling dissonance."

Morgan's insight:
"The relation between consonance and dissonance, then, is fluid in principle. The potential for this fluidity opens up a compositional dynamic, where a terrain of fixed relations is transformed into a site for negotiation."

# Common Confusions
- Dissonant prolongation does not mean the dissonance is literally sustained; it may be implied by surrounding consonances
- Not all chromatic passages involve dissonant prolongation; the concept applies to specific structural situations
- The concept extends Schenkerian analysis rather than rejecting it
- "Prolongation" of a dissonance is somewhat metaphorical; some prefer alternative terminology
