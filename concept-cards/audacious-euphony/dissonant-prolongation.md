# Quick Definition
Dissonant prolongation occurs when a dissonant harmony (such as an augmented triad) governs a span of music, with consonant triads subordinating to it, reversing the classical assumption that only consonant harmonies can be prolonged.

# Core Definition
Robert P. Morgan's concept of "dissonant prolongation" (1976) describes situations where "consonant triads subordinate to dissonant ones, not only locally but across spans of significant duration" (p. 64). In such passages, the augmented triad may function as a "controlling harmony" even though "the score contains no vertical slice or contiguous patch, even an egregiously gerrymandered one, to be circled and labeled as a 'controlling harmony'" (p. 63). The augmented triad's status is "virtual and liminal" -- it exists as "a collocation of bass pitches or triadic roots or local tonics" rather than as a sounded sonority.

# Prerequisites
- **Consonant triad**: Must understand the classical assumption that only consonant harmonies are susceptible to prolongation
- **Augmented triad**: The dissonant harmony that is prolonged in these passages

# Key Properties
1. A dissonant harmony provides structural coherence for a span of music
2. Consonant triads function as elaborations or passing chords within the dissonant framework
3. The dissonant harmony may or may not appear as a surface sonority
4. The passage cannot be coherently explained by diatonic prolongational logic alone
5. Schenker's own analyses implicitly accept dissonant prolongation despite his pronouncements to the contrary

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify a passage where consonant triads are organized around an augmented triad
2. Check whether the succession of local tonics or bass pitches outlines an augmented triad
3. Verify that no diatonic prolongational explanation adequately accounts for the passage
4. The controlling dissonance may never sound as a vertical simultaneity

# Context & Application
Dissonant prolongation extends Schenkerian thinking to chromatic repertoire by allowing dissonant harmonies to control spans of music. It is essential for analyzing passages where consonant triads orbit an augmented triadic center, such as development sections organized by major-third divisions.

# Examples
**Schubert, Symphony No. 2, finale** (pp. 63-64): "If, as Schenker implies in analogous passages, FADb is the prolonged harmony from m. 312 to m. 347, then it follows that both Db major and A major are subordinated to a controlling dissonance."

**Schenker's implicit acceptance** (p. 63): His graphs of passages from Beethoven's "Appassionata" and "Spring" Sonatas and Wolf's "Standchen" show dissonant prolongations that "cannot be reconciled to his pronouncements elsewhere that only consonant harmonies are susceptible to composing out." Nonetheless, "Schenker found dissonant prolongations aurally and conceptually plausible."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the dissonance must be literally sustained as a chord
  **Correction**: The dissonance may be implied by the succession of consonant harmonies that elaborate it

- **Error**: Treating dissonant prolongation as a rejection of Schenkerian analysis
  **Correction**: It extends rather than rejects Schenkerian thinking; Schenker himself implicitly accepted it

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: "Prolongation" of a dissonance functions identically to consonant prolongation
  **Clarification**: The term is somewhat metaphorical; some theorists prefer alternative terminology
- **Confusion**: All chromatic passages involve dissonant prolongation
  **Clarification**: The concept applies to specific structural situations where consonant triads clearly orbit a dissonant center
