# Quick Definition
The linguistic phenomenon of alternating between different languages or linguistic varieties within a conversation, appropriated by Cohn as a model for understanding how listeners move between diatonic and chromatic syntaxes within a musical passage.

# Formal Definition
Code switching is a term from linguistics referring to the ability "to alternate between different linguistic varieties at the drop of a hat, and to make use of several simultaneously when it suits them to do so" (Gardner-Chloros 2009). In music, code switching describes how listeners shift between organizing triadic progressions according to diatonic functional syntax and chromatic transformational syntax. The linguistic evidence challenges Smith's (1986) objection that music cannot operate under multiple syntaxes because languages are "rigidly discrete, autonomous systems." Research shows most people routinely coprocess languages, and the behavior often occurs beneath conscious awareness.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Key findings from bilingualism research relevant to music:
1. Speakers routinely switch 15+ times in rapid speech without interrupting flow
2. Switching behavior is largely unconscious; speakers are surprised by playback
3. Asymmetric (matrix + insertion) and symmetric (equal shuttling) models exist
4. Transitions between languages may be fuzzy, not crisp

The musical parallel:
- Asymmetric: Diatonic syntax as "matrix" with chromatic insertions
- Symmetric: Equal movement between syntaxes
- Fuzzy transitions: Overdetermined chords as pivot points

Evidence suggests listeners adjust as smoothly as speakers: "If the Alsatian office worker is code-switching rapidly in an effective conversation, it is likely that her interlocutor is receiving her switches with as few hitches as she has in creating them."

# Musical Context/Application
Code switching provides a model for:
- Moving between diatonic encapsulation and chromatic navigation
- Understanding how the same triad can function in both syntaxes
- Explaining why listeners do not experience "grinding of gears" at syntactic boundaries
- Framing the convertible Tonnetz as representation of code-switching possibilities

The evidence "does not in itself mandate any conclusions about music, which is no language." But given that "music and language are closely related" as cognitive/neural systems (Patel 2008), the linguistic evidence provides "strong motivation for reevaluating" dismissals of double syntax.

# Examples
- Gardner-Chloros (2009): Alsatian office worker switching 15 times between French and Alsatian without interrupting phone conversation
- The Schubert C major Symphony Scherzo exits diatonic syntax, crosses enharmonic seam, and returns without jarring discontinuity
- Every tonicized hexatonic station in Liszt's Consolation represents a code switch from chromatic to diatonic syntax

# Common Confusions
- "Code switching" is used despite acknowledged limitations of the term (Gardner-Chloros 2009)
- The musical application is analogical, not claiming music literally is language
- Code switching in music may be even smoother than in language due to lexical continuity (same triads in both syntaxes)
- The phenomenon challenges intuitions but is empirically well-documented in linguistics
