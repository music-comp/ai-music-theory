# Quick Definition
The linguistic phenomenon of alternating between different languages within a conversation, appropriated by Cohn as a model for how listeners move between diatonic and chromatic syntaxes. Evidence from bilingualism shows this occurs beneath conscious awareness and is facilitated by shared lexical items (homophonous diamorphs).

# Core Definition
Code switching is a term from linguistics referring to "the ability 'to alternate between different linguistic varieties at the drop of a hat, and to make use of several simultaneously when it suits them to do so'" (Gardner-Chloros 2009, 177, qtd. in Cohn, p. 219). Cohn appropriates the concept to model how listeners shift between diatonic and chromatic syntactic organizations. Two theoretical models exist: the "classical" model maintains asymmetric languages (one "matrix" with insertions from the other); more recent evidence suggests symmetric shuttling between equals (p. 220). Critically, "Speakers are on the whole not very aware of their code-switching behaviour, and tend to be surprised at their own performance if you play it back to them" (Gardner-Chloros 2009, 121, qtd. p. 221).

# Key Properties
1. Most people code-switch routinely (monolingualism is the exception, not the norm)
2. Switching occurs beneath conscious awareness
3. Two models: asymmetric (matrix + insertions) and symmetric (equal shuttling)
4. Transitions may be fuzzy, not crisp -- facilitated by homophonous diamorphs
5. The musical application is analogical, not claiming music is literally language

# Construction / Recognition
**The musical parallel** (p. 220):
- Asymmetric: Diatonic syntax as "matrix" with chromatic insertions
- Symmetric: Equal movement between syntaxes
- Fuzzy transitions: Overdetermined chords as pivot points (like homophonous diamorphs)

Key evidence: Gardner-Chloros recorded an Alsatian office worker making fifteen code switches in rapid speech "without in any way interrupting the flow" (p. 221). If the speaker switches this smoothly, "it is likely that her interlocutor is receiving her switches with as few hitches as she has in creating them" (p. 221).

# Context & Application
Code switching responds directly to Smith's objection: his assumption that languages are "rigidly discrete, autonomous systems" is "empirically false" (p. 219). The evidence shows that "most people have the ability to alternate between different linguistic varieties" and that for most of the world's population, monolingualism is the exception: "you are left with small islands of monolingualism in a multilingual sea" (Gardner-Chloros 2009, 5-7, qtd. p. 219).

The evidence "does not in itself mandate any conclusions about music, which is no language" (p. 222). But given that "as cognitive and neural systems, music and language are closely related" (Patel 2008, 417), it provides "strong motivation for reevaluating" dismissals of double syntax (p. 222).

# Examples
- **Alsatian office worker**: 15 switches between French and Alsatian in rapid phone conversation (p. 221)
- **Schubert C major Symphony Scherzo**: Exits diatonic syntax, crosses enharmonic seam, returns without jarring discontinuity
- **Liszt Consolation**: Each hexatonic station represents a code switch from chromatic to diatonic syntax

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Code switching in music is identical to modulation
  **Correction**: Modulation moves between keys within one syntax; code switching moves between syntactic systems

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The musical application claims music is language
  **Clarification**: It is analogical, supported by evidence that music and language share cognitive/neural systems
