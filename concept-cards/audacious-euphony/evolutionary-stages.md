# Quick Definition
Cohn's provisional outline of four stages by which chromatic syntax emerged from diatonic tonality: (1) diatonic sequence, (2) Tristan-genus promotion, (3) scalar crystallization, and (4) atonal absorption - each representing a further remove from diatonic tonality.

# Formal Definition
The soft revolution proceeded through identifiable stages, each exploiting properties discovered at the previous stage:

**Stage 1 - Diatonic sequence**: Triads exercise voice-leading capacity within diatonic space. As Fetis observed, sequential repetition suspends the tritone's appellative powers. The arrow of orientation flips from tonic to voice-leading, then back at cadence. This dynamic is replicated and intensified in chromatic space.

**Stage 2 - Tristan-genus promotion**: If consonant triads can serve as voice-leading objects, so can other nearly even chords. Dominant and half-diminished seventh chords (Tristan genus) become primary, beginning with omnibus progressions (Telesco 1998) and eventuating in late Wagner.

**Stage 3 - Scalar crystallization**: L/P-chaining (major third cycle) creates hexatonic scales; R/P-chaining (minor third cycle) creates octatonic scales. Initially by-products of harmonic progressions, these scales "become things in themselves." Individual tones relate directly to these new scales without triadic mediation.

**Stage 4 - Atonal absorption**: Hexatonic and octatonic scales are absorbed into broader atonality. Chords and scales become sets and set classes of Second-Viennese-School atonality, taking meaning directly from the chromatic 12-gamut.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Arrow of orientation across stages:
- Stage 1: Arrow temporarily flips within diatonic context, then returns
- Stages 2-4: Arrow remains "sprung," pointing from smaller to larger cardinalities
- All later stages are "atonal" in this sense, though only gradually acquiring prototypical atonal sound

Lexical vs. syntactic change:
- Stage 1: Syntactic change (arrow flip) with lexical continuity (same triads)
- Stage 2: Lexical change (Tristan genus promoted) with syntactic continuity (voice-leading still governs)
- Stage 3: Lexical change (new scales) with syntactic continuity
- Stage 4: Full integration into set-theoretic framework

Point of no easy return: "Once composers begin to use [0148]-type chords in a hexatonic context, or [0146]-type chords in an octatonic one, the imposition of a diatonic cadence often sounds like the grinding of gears that Smith describes."

# Musical Context/Application
The staging explains:
- Why Schubert sounds "tonal" though exploiting chromatic syntax (Stage 1)
- Why Wagner sounds more distant (Stage 2)
- Why Rimsky-Korsakov/Debussy sound different from Wagner (Stage 3)
- Why Schoenberg/Webern represent a culmination (Stage 4)

The process is not teleological: "At the risk of bleaching the nuance out of a complex set of musical transformations through a few broad strokes."

# Examples
- Stage 1: Baroque Fortspinnung; Schubert C major Symphony Scherzo
- Stage 2: Omnibus progressions (18th century); Wagner Tristan
- Stage 3: Liszt's scalar tonality; Rimsky-Korsakov octatonic usage; French impressionism (Tymoczko 2011b, chapter 9)
- Stage 4: Second Viennese School atonality

# Common Confusions
- The stages are analytical, not necessarily corresponding to individual composers' awareness
- Stages coexisted historically; later stages did not replace earlier ones
- "Atonal" in this context refers to arrow orientation, not necessarily to dissonance or lack of tonal center
- The staging is provisional ("at the risk of bleaching nuance")
