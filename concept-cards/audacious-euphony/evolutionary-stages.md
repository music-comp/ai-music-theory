# Quick Definition
Cohn's provisional outline of four stages by which chromatic syntax emerged from diatonic tonality: (1) syntactic flip in diatonic sequence, (2) Tristan-genus lexical promotion, (3) hexatonic/octatonic scalar crystallization, and (4) atonal absorption into set theory -- each a further remove from diatonic tonality.

# Core Definition
The soft revolution proceeded through identifiable stages (pp. 228-230):

**Stage 1 -- Syntactic flip**: Triads exercise voice-leading capacity within diatonic space. The arrow of orientation flips during sequential passages (Fetis's "law of uniformity"), then flips back at cadence. This is replicated and intensified in chromatic 12-space, where voice leading achieves even greater parsimony.

**Stage 2 -- Lexical promotion**: "If consonant triads can serve as objects in a system of relations defined by common-tone preservation and voice-leading work, so too can other nearly even chords, the members of the Tristan genus" (p. 229). Beginning with omnibus progressions (Telesco 1998) and eventuating in late Wagner.

**Stage 3 -- Scalar crystallization**: L/P-chaining creates hexatonic scales; R/P-chaining creates octatonic scales. "Although arising initially as secondary by-products of harmonic progressions... eventually these scales become things in themselves" (p. 229). Individual tones relate directly to these new scales without triadic mediation.

**Stage 4 -- Atonal absorption**: Hexatonic and octatonic scales are absorbed into broader atonality. Chords and scales become the sets and set classes of Second-Viennese-School atonality, "which take their meaning directly... in relation to the chromatic 12-gamut" (p. 230).

# Prerequisites
- **Soft revolution**: The overarching framework
- **Preadaptation**: The mechanism enabling the first stage
- **Tristan genus**: The chord class promoted at Stage 2

# Key Properties
1. The arrow of orientation remains "sprung" (outward-pointing) in all later stages
2. Stage 1 is syntactic change with lexical continuity; later stages involve lexical change with syntactic continuity
3. Point of no return: "[0148]-type chords in a hexatonic context... the imposition of a diatonic cadence often sounds like the grinding of gears" (p. 227)
4. The staging is provisional ("at the risk of bleaching the nuance")
5. Stages coexisted historically; later stages did not replace earlier ones

# Construction / Recognition
**Lexical vs. syntactic change across stages**:
- Stage 1: Syntactic change (arrow flip) + lexical continuity (same triads) = key that unlocks the capsule
- Stage 2: Lexical change (Tristan genus promoted) + syntactic continuity (voice-leading still governs)
- Stage 3: Lexical change (new scales) + syntactic continuity
- Stage 4: Full integration into set-theoretic framework

# Context & Application
The staging explains stylistic differences: "why Schubert sounds 'tonal' though exploiting chromatic syntax (Stage 1), why Wagner sounds more distant (Stage 2), why Rimsky-Korsakov/Debussy sound different from Wagner (Stage 3), and why Schoenberg/Webern represent a culmination (Stage 4)."

The "road of return becomes bumpy" at later stages (p. 230): once composers use non-triadic set-types in hexatonic/octatonic contexts, diatonic cadences create the gear-grinding that Smith described.

# Examples
- **Stage 1**: Baroque Fortspinnung; Schubert C major Symphony Scherzo
- **Stage 2**: Omnibus progressions (18th century); Wagner Tristan
- **Stage 3**: Liszt's scalar tonality; Rimsky-Korsakov octatonic usage; French impressionism (Tymoczko 2011b, ch. 9)
- **Stage 4**: Second Viennese School atonality

# Common Errors
- **Error**: The stages are strictly chronological
  **Correction**: They coexisted historically; composers at any period could deploy multiple stages

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: "Atonal" means the same thing at each stage
  **Clarification**: All later stages are "atonal" in the sense of outward-pointing arrows, but only gradually acquire prototypical atonal sound
