# Quick Definition
The intertwining of diatonic tonal syntax and pan-triadic chromatic syntax in nineteenth-century music, where both systems organize triadic relations simultaneously without collapsing into each other.

# Core Definition
Syntactic interaction describes how the two syntaxes governing consonant triads -- classical diatonic tonality (based on acoustic consonance and root relations) and pan-triadic chromaticism (based on voice-leading parsimony and near-evenness) -- interpenetrate in compositional practice. As Steven Rings emphasized, a model of syntactic interaction "must do more than dump transformational labels into the same cage with Roman numerals or Schenkerian representations; it needs to study how they get along within those confines" (Rings 2007, 34). Without such modeling, "the theoretical divide is thus reified in analytical practice, resulting in a curiously bifocal view of chromatic harmony, one in which the triad seems not so much 'overdetermined' as dichotomous" (Cohn, p. 186).

# Key Properties
1. Both syntaxes operate on the same set of objects (consonant triads) but organize them by different principles
2. The challenge is modeling their co-presence without privileging one syntax over the other
3. Local diatonic encapsulation can coexist with global chromatic organization
4. Rings arrows (tonal-gravitational forces) can activate or deactivate depending on context
5. The convertible Tonnetz provides the geometric model for representing syntactic interaction

# Construction / Recognition
Recognizing syntactic interaction requires identifying:
- Passages where diatonic function and chromatic voice-leading logic both apply
- Moments where cadential processes erect or dissolve diatonic walls
- Triads functioning simultaneously as voice-leading objects and acoustic/functional objects
- Sequential passages that temporarily suspend diatonic orientation while using diatonic materials

The Ernst Kurth epigraph captures the essence: "Everywhere, Romanticism exploits the ability to hear one and the same phenomenon in two and more ways; it is fond of this coexistence and its indefiniteness."

# Context & Application
Syntactic interaction is the central analytical concern of Chapter 8. Previous models (Cohn 1999, Rings 2007) positioned chromatic and diatonic axes as separate dimensions, but these models had limitations:
1. Orientation to hexatonic configuration limits applicability to octatonic-axis repertory
2. Rings arrows implicitly commit to tonal-gravitational forces whenever motion occurs along the fifths axis
3. Third relations are relegated to only one axis, preventing diatonic third divisions from being modeled

The convertible Tonnetz overcomes these limitations by situating each object and relation in a unique location while allowing the space to change shape in response to the listener's hearing.

# Examples
- **Schubert Bb Sonata** (Cohn 1999): Hexatonic cycles positioned as cross-sections of a cylinder with fifth relations connecting horizontally (Figure 8.1a, p. 187)
- **Schubert Eb Impromptu** (Rings 2007): Triads positioned at intersection of "vertical tonal-functional" and "horizontal hexatonic" axes (Figure 8.1b, p. 187)
- **Liszt Consolation no. 3**: Each hexatonic station is locally tonicized, functioning simultaneously as voice-leading object in Db hexatonic space and acoustic object in local diatonic space (Figure 8.9, pp. 204-206)
- **Wagner Parsifal Faith Proclamation**: Plagal drift through L/R chain halted by diatonic encapsulation forces (Figure 8.11, pp. 207-210)
- **Brahms Symphony no. 1 finale**: Thematic statements erect diatonic walls on hypermetric downbeats; liquidations dissolve into L/R chains; Eb major to B major as hexatonic stations with liquidations filling the alleys (Figures 8.12-8.13, pp. 209-211)
- **Schubert C major Symphony Scherzo** (figure 9.2, mm. 117-140): The hexatonic alley at the interior represents "transient emergence into the hexatonic light" -- voice-leading perspective inverts the monotonal perspective so that "what we initially conceived as tunneling into diatonic/tonal darkness becomes... a transient emergence into the hexatonic light"
- **Hexatonic alley as corridor** (pp. 203-207): A bounded L/P path through six triads of a hexatonic region, with balanced voice leading suspending directional upshift/downshift. The alley "sprouts" from a diatonic station, with triads bobbing as "sealed capsules on the pan-triadic sea." Each station can be locally tonicized; the wave of voice-leading efficiency "crests at the hexatonic alley." Unlike the unbounded L/R chain (24 triads), the hexatonic alley is bounded (6 triads)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Dumping transformational labels alongside Roman numerals without modeling their interaction
  **Correction**: The model must show how the two systems "get along within those confines," not merely coexist as labels

- **Error**: Assuming one syntax is always primary and the other secondary
  **Correction**: Both syntaxes are equally real aspects of the music; neither is inherently primary

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Syntactic interaction means choosing between diatonic and chromatic analyses
  **Clarification**: It means understanding how both operate simultaneously on the same musical surface

- **Confusion**: Interaction implies the syntaxes blend into a single hybrid
  **Clarification**: They remain distinct systems that can be separately traced even when co-present
