# Quick Definition
A syntactic system governing progressions of major and minor triads that operates independently of diatonic tonal centers, using triads without determining a key.

# Formal Definition
Pan-triadic syntax refers to the organizational principles governing chromatic progressions of consonant triads that do not establish or require reference to a diatonic tonal center. The term, first used by Evan Copley (1991), designates music that uses fundamental materials of tonality (triads) in tonally indeterminate ways. It is the obverse of "pan-diatonic" (Slonimsky 1937): pan-diatonic music uses diatonic scales without triads, while pan-triadic music uses triads without diatonic scales.

# Construction/Recognition
Pan-triadic syntax emerges from properties intrinsic to consonant triads themselves, specifically their voice-leading characteristics in chromatic space. The 24 consonant triads (12 major + 12 minor) form a complete universe of harmonic objects whose relationships are determined by:
- Common-tone retention between triads
- Minimal voice-leading work (semitonal displacement)
- Position within geometric representations (Tonnetz, hexatonic cycles, Weitzmann regions)

These relationships operate independently of root-interval logic or diatonic scale membership.

# Musical Context/Application
Pan-triadic syntax characterizes passages in 19th-century music where triads progress according to voice-leading proximity rather than diatonic function. Such passages often appear in:
- Transitional or developmental sections
- Music associated with the supernatural, magical, or uncanny
- Sequential passages with chromatic root motion
- Passages that traverse enharmonic seams

The syntax coexists with classical diatonic tonality, sometimes in alternation, sometimes in overlay. Composers use pan-triadic progressions to create effects of destabilization, wonder, or transcendence while maintaining the consonant triadic surface valued by 19th-century aesthetics.

# Examples
- Schubert's Piano Sonata in Bb major, D. 960, first movement recapitulation (mm. 217-256): progression through Gb major, f# minor, A major back to Bb major operates by voice-leading logic rather than diatonic function
- Wagner's Magic Sleep music from Die Walkure: "lacks tonal implication" (Katz 1945)
- Programmatic associations throughout 19th-century repertoire: Schubert's magic harp, Wagner's magic sleep, Rimsky-Korsakov's exotic kingdoms, Liszt's mountain-top meditations

# Common Confusions
- Pan-triadic is not synonymous with "neo-Riemannian." Neo-Riemannian theory is an analytical approach; pan-triadic describes the musical phenomenon itself.
- Pan-triadic passages are not "atonal" - they consist entirely of consonant triads but organize them by non-diatonic principles.
- Pan-triadic does not mean "random" or "incoherent" - it follows its own systematic logic based on voice-leading proximity.
- The presence of triads does not guarantee diatonic tonality; pan-triadic music demonstrates that triads can organize without establishing keys.
