# Quick Definition
A syntactic system governing chromatic progressions of consonant triads that operates independently of diatonic tonal centers, organizing triads by voice-leading proximity rather than diatonic function.

# Core Definition
Pan-triadic syntax refers to the organizational principles governing chromatic progressions of consonant triads that do not establish or require reference to a diatonic tonal center. The term, first used by Evan Copley (1991), designates music that uses fundamental materials of tonality (triads) in tonally indeterminate ways. It is the obverse of "pan-diatonic" (Slonimsky 1937): "both terms designate music that uses fundamental materials of tonality in tonally indeterminate ways, one by using diatonic scales without triads, and the other by using triads without diatonic scales" (Cohn, p. 15). Cohn prefers "pan-triadic" over "neo-Riemannian" as a descriptor for the musical phenomenon itself (p. 15).

# Key Properties
1. Operates on the 24 consonant triads as its complete harmonic vocabulary
2. Relationships between triads are determined by voice-leading proximity, not root-interval distance or diatonic function
3. Triads progress according to common-tone retention and minimal semitonal displacement
4. Generates geometric representations: hexatonic cycles, Weitzmann regions, Tonnetz, Cube Dance
5. Coexists with classical diatonic syntax rather than replacing it
6. The term describes the musical phenomenon, not the analytical approach (which Cohn calls "neo-Riemannian")

# Construction / Recognition
## To recognize pan-triadic syntax in a passage:
1. Check whether triadic progressions follow voice-leading proximity rather than diatonic function
2. Look for chromatic root motion (especially by major or minor third)
3. Check for tonal indeterminacy: no single triad asserts clear tonic function
4. Look for smooth voice leading with common-tone retention across non-diatonic progressions
5. Check whether the passage resists coherent Roman numeral analysis

# Context & Application
Pan-triadic syntax characterizes passages in nineteenth-century music where triads progress according to voice-leading proximity rather than diatonic function. Such passages often appear in transitional or developmental sections, music associated with the supernatural or uncanny, and sequential passages with chromatic root motion. The syntax "follows from an internal property that consonant triads hold independently of the acoustic properties that are claimed to generate classical syntax" (p. 14).

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 15): Cohn's terminological distinction: "'pan-triadic' properly labels the compositions or passages that invite such modes of audition, conceptualization, and representation."

**Example 2** (p. 12): Programmatic associations include "Schubert's magic harp, Wagner's magic sleep, Rimsky-Korsakov's magic and exotic kingdoms, Liszt's mountain-top meditations."

**Example 3** (pp. 27-31): The Schubert B-flat Sonata recapitulation (Bb - Gb - f# - A - Bb) operates by voice-leading logic rather than diatonic function.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Equating "pan-triadic" with "neo-Riemannian"
  **Clarification**: Neo-Riemannian theory is an analytical approach; pan-triadic describes the musical phenomenon itself (p. 15)
- **Confusion**: Thinking pan-triadic passages are "atonal"
  **Clarification**: They consist entirely of consonant triads but organize them by non-diatonic principles
- **Confusion**: Assuming pan-triadic means "random" or "incoherent"
  **Clarification**: Pan-triadic syntax follows systematic logic based on voice-leading proximity
