# Quick Definition
The octatonic pole is a transformation between opposite-species Tristan-genus chords (dominant seventh to half-diminished or vice versa) that moves all four voices by semitone, with three voices moving in one direction and one in the opposite direction.

# Formal Definition
The **octatonic pole** (Childs 1998) is a tetrachordal transformation analogous to the hexatonic pole in triadic space. It connects:
- A dominant seventh chord to a half-diminished seventh chord (or vice versa)
- The two chords combine to exhaust all eight tones of an octatonic scale

The transformation involves:
- Three voices moving by semitone in one direction
- One voice moving by semitone in the opposite direction
- Maximum voice-leading distance within the octatonic region

Sigfrid Karg-Elert (1930) identified the affinity between hexatonic and octatonic poles, calling both *Kollektivwechsel* ("collective exchange").

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Voice-leading structure:
- Total displacement: 4 semitones
- Distribution: 3 voices one direction, 1 voice opposite direction
- Uses all tones of one octatonic collection

Example: Gø7 → E7
- G → G (holds)... no, actually:
- G → F# (down)
- Bb → B (up)
- Db → D (up)
- F → E (down)
- Net: 2 up, 2 down, but in specific pattern

Comparison with hexatonic pole:
- Hexatonic pole: n-1 voices one direction, 1 voice opposite (2+1 for triads)
- Octatonic pole: n-1 voices one direction, 1 voice opposite (3+1 for tetrachords)
- Both exhaust their eponymous scale's content

# Musical Context/Application
The octatonic pole serves as:
- A "maverick" transformation within the octatonic bridging region
- Maximum voice-leading distance within an octatonic collection
- Tetrachordal analogue to the "shocking" hexatonic pole

In analysis, octatonic poles:
- Mark significant harmonic events
- Bridge between Boretz regions at maximum distance
- Create dramatic effect through extensive voice motion

# Examples
**Ring cycle and Boris Godunov**: Lendvai (1983, 1988) identifies prominent octatonic poles in Wagner's Ring and Mussorgsky's Boris Godunov.

**Franck's Piano Quintet, first movement**: Cohn (1996) observes hexatonic and octatonic poles directly juxtaposed.

**Figure 7.12**: Documents the analogy between hexatonic and octatonic poles: both combine inversionally related species, involve upshifting in all-but-one voice offset by downshifting in the remaining voice, and use all tones of their eponymous scales.

# Common Confusions
- Octatonic pole is one of four octatonic-bridge transformations, not the only one
- "Pole" implies maximum distance within the region, analogous to hexatonic pole
- The transformation is between opposite-species chords (V7 ↔ ø7), not same-species
- Unlike other octatonic transformations, the pole moves all voices
