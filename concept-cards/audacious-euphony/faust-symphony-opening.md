# Quick Definition
The opening of Liszt's *Faust* Symphony (1854) explicitly inverts the classical consonance/dissonance hierarchy: augmented triads dominate the surface while consonant minor triads occupy the position of suspensions in a troped suspension figure.

# Core Definition
The *Faust* opening "famously and explicitly inverts the values classically accorded these sonorities" (p. 69). It presents "two slow rotations through three segments of material (marked A, B, and C), each of which extends approximately four measures. Augmented triads dominate the surface." The pitch-class pool draws "exclusively on the CEG# and FAC# augmented triads, which combine to form a hexatonic collection" (exceptions at mm. 1 and 13). "Of particular interest are the four boxed figures, whose staggered downward motion tropes a suspension figure, but with consonance and dissonance inverted with respect to formal function (but not metric location): the position of preparation and resolution is occupied by dissonant augmented triads; that of the suspension, by consonant minor triads" (Morgan 1976, 60; p. 69).

# Prerequisites
- **Augmented triad**: The augmented triad dominates the surface texture
- **Consonance/dissonance reciprocity**: The passage is an extreme case of reciprocity
- **Hexatonic cycle**: The pitch content draws on a hexatonic collection

# Key Properties
1. Two rotations through three texturally differentiated segments
2. Pitch-class pool: two augmented triads combining into a hexatonic collection
3. Suspension figure with inverted consonance/dissonance roles
4. Metric positions preserved (dissonance on strong beat expectations), chord types swapped
5. The second rotation transposes the first segment up by minor sixth

# Construction / Recognition
Inverted suspension figure:
- Traditional: consonance (preparation) -> dissonance (suspension) -> consonance (resolution)
- *Faust*: dissonance (preparation/augmented) -> consonance (suspension/minor triad) -> dissonance (resolution/augmented)

# Context & Application
The *Faust* opening represents the most explicit case of consonance/dissonance reciprocity in the repertoire, becoming a model for later chromatic composers. Rimsky-Korsakov's *Antar* (1868) was likely modeled on it.

# Examples
**Figure 3.4** (p. 69): Liszt, *Faust* Symphony, 1st mvt., mm. 1-22, showing the two rotations and the inverted suspension figures.

**Rimsky-Korsakov connection** (p. 70): "It seems likely that Rimsky-Korsakov had the opening of Faust in his ear when he wrote the opening measures of his Symphony no. 2... subtitled Antar. Antar, like Faust, begins with two slow rotations through a series of three texturally differentiated segments."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Describing the passage as "atonal"
  **Correction**: It uses consonant and dissonant triads with reversed roles; the inversion is of function, not of tonal language

- **Error**: Treating the metric inversion as complete
  **Correction**: "Consonance and dissonance [are] inverted with respect to formal function (but not metric location)" -- metric positions are preserved

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The hexatonic collection provides the innovative element
  **Clarification**: The structural inversion of the suspension figure is the innovative element; the hexatonic collection provides the pitch-class framework
- **Confusion**: Later composers replicate the systematic inversion
  **Clarification**: Later composers often allude to the *Faust* opening without replicating its full systematic inversion
