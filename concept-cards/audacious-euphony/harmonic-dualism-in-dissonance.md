# Quick Definition
The application of harmonic dualism to dissonance analysis interprets half-diminished seventh chords as minor triads with under-sevenths rather than as seventh chords built from stacked thirds, a view associated with Oettingen and Riemann that Cohn rehabilitates through musical evidence from Wagner.

# Core Definition
The dualist interpretation of the half-diminished seventh chord was developed by Arthur von Oettingen and Riemann in the second half of the nineteenth century. "For Riemann, the DFA chord is generated downward from A. When B is added to that chord, it functions as a characteristically dissonant under-seventh" (Rehding 2003, 94-96) (p. 160). The interpretation "was hardy enough to survive well into the twentieth century, even among theorists otherwise uncommitted to a dualist theory of harmony." It was "ultimately overcome by a renewed third-stacking monolithy, to the point that its peremptory dismissal became self-evident" (p. 160). However, "recent empirical work has motivated a revival of Riemann's proposal, grounding it in perceptual experiments rather than physical or metaphysical speculation (Parncutt 1989, 149)" (p. 160).

# Key Properties
1. Reverses standard third-stacking: the "root" of a ø7 becomes the dissonant addition
2. The minor triad is heard as the structural core, not the diminished triad built on the bass
3. The under-seventh resolves upward (plagal motion), mirroring the over-seventh's downward resolution (authentic motion)
4. Empirical support from Parncutt (1989) perceptual experiments
5. Musical support from Wagner's treatment of minor triads and ø7 chords as interchangeable

# Construction / Recognition
The dualist analysis reframes the ø7 chord:
- **Standard**: B-D-F-A = B root, diminished triad + minor seventh
- **Dualist**: B-D-F-A = D minor triad (D-F-A) with B as under-seventh
- Identifying feature: when the bass tone (B) resolves upward while the triad remains stable, the dualist analysis is supported

Musical contexts supporting the dualist hearing:
- The bass tone does not resolve as a conventional seventh-chord root
- The minor triad is timbrally, registrally, or durationally isolated from the bass
- Plagal voice-leading patterns are present

# Context & Application
Cohn rehabilitates the dualist interpretation not through metaphysical argument but through musical evidence. Wagner's treatment of minor triads and their half-diminished supersets "as interchangeable in his late music" (p. 162) provides the strongest case. The analytical payoff is the ability to track hexatonic pole relationships through dissonant surfaces, expanding motivic networks in works like Parsifal.

# Examples
- **Dahlhaus (1990)**: "The notion that B is the 'characteristic dissonance' of the subdominant seventh chord b-d-f-a is frustrated by musical reality, in which it is evident that a is the chord's actual dissonance" (p. 159) -- Dahlhaus argues against the dualist view
- **Harrison (2011)**: Suggests the recourse to subposed dissonances "exemplifies 'analytic difficulties'" (p. 159)
- **Wagner's Tristan final cadence**: The half-diminished behaves as a minor triad with under-seventh, not as a seventh chord (p. 161)
- **Wagner's Parsifal Communion theme**: Appears both as triadic and with timbrally isolated under-seventh, suggesting interchangeability (p. 162)
