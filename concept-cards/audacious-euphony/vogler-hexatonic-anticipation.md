# Quick Definition
Georg Vogler's 1802 *Handbuch zur Harmonielehre* was the first treatise to explore the augmented triad's potential for enharmonic reinterpretation, presenting a progression that anticipates the hexatonic cycle.

# Core Definition
Vogler (1749-1814) recognized that the augmented triad "appears to consist of three similar major thirds" and can be "multiply interpreted as a III chord in three different keys" (1802, 103, 109; p. 62). His *Handbuch* includes a progression (Figure 3.1) whose "anacrusis/downbeat combinations form a hexatonic cycle. The third beat of each measure hosts some spelling of the CEG# augmented triad, acting successively as dominant of each triad on the following beat" (p. 62). This is the first theoretical acknowledgment of what would later be understood as hexatonic structure.

# Prerequisites
- **Augmented triad**: Vogler's exploration depends on understanding the augmented triad's symmetric structure
- **Mehrdeutigkeit**: Vogler's concept of multiple meaning is the theoretical framework for his exploration

# Key Properties
1. Vogler was "the first treatise to explore the augmented triad's potential for enharmonic reinterpretation" (p. 62)
2. He claimed the augmented triad's "proper roost was the third scale degree of harmonic minor"
3. His progression forms a hexatonic cycle through successive enharmonic reinterpretations
4. The CEG# augmented triad appears on beat 3 of each measure, acting as dominant to each subsequent tonic
5. Vogler did not use the term "hexatonic" -- this is a modern analytical description

# Construction / Recognition
Vogler's key observations:
1. The augmented triad consists of "three similar major thirds"
2. Its normative position is as III of harmonic minor
3. "Each III chord in minor... can be multiply interpreted as a III chord in three different keys"
4. The progression demonstrates this through successive respellings of one augmented triad

# Context & Application
Vogler's treatise provides the theoretical foundation that Schubert and other early Romantic composers would exploit compositionally. The connection is potentially historical as well as theoretical.

# Examples
**Figure 3.1** (p. 62): Vogler's progression from the *Handbuch zur Harmonielehre*, with anacrusis/downbeat combinations forming a hexatonic cycle.

**Influence on Schubert** (p. 63): "Vogler's progression serves as a template for the passage presented in linear reduction at figure 3.2, from the finale of Schubert's Second Symphony in Bb major (1812)."

**Biographical connection** (p. 63): "Vogler was a peripatetic, ambitious, and charismatic personality who lived in Vienna from 1802 to 1805 and later taught composition to such prominent figures as Carl Maria von Weber, Gottfried Weber, and Meyerbeer." Wason (1985, 19) "speculates on a possible lineage from Vogler to Schubert."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Attributing the concept of hexatonic cycles to Vogler
  **Correction**: Vogler explored enharmonic reinterpretation (*Mehrdeutigkeit*), not voice-leading structure; "hexatonic" is a modern term

- **Error**: Treating the Vogler-Schubert connection as established fact
  **Correction**: "Wason speculates on a possible lineage"; the connection is plausible but not documented

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Vogler understood the augmented triad's role in voice-leading systems
  **Clarification**: His focus was on enharmonic reinterpretation, not on the voice-leading properties that Cohn emphasizes
- **Confusion**: Vogler's III chord interpretation is the same as later understandings
  **Clarification**: Vogler located the augmented triad as III of harmonic minor; later theory reframed it as a generator of consonant triads
