# Quick Definition
Two fundamentally different ways of organizing triadic harmony: diatonic organization derives relations from scale membership and root motion, while chromatic organization derives relations from voice-leading proximity in the twelve-tone chromatic universe.

# Core Definition
Diatonic organization governs classical tonality, measuring triadic proximity by mutual membership in diatonic collections (sharing at least 6 of 7 scale tones) and interpreting chromatic harmonies as transformations of underlying diatonic ones. Chromatic organization measures triadic proximity by voice-leading work and common-tone retention within the chromatic collection of twelve pitch classes. The two systems generate divergent proximity judgments: triads closely related diatonically may be distant chromatically, and vice versa. The book's "principal thesis is that major and minor triads are not only the familiar acoustic consonances of eighteenth-century classical theory. They also have a second property, equally rare and equally generative of syntax" (Introduction, p. 12).

# Prerequisites
- Basic understanding of diatonic scales and triadic harmony
- Familiarity with the concept of key relations in classical theory

# Key Properties
1. Diatonic distance: based on shared scale membership (close = keys sharing 6+ of 7 tones)
2. Chromatic distance: based on voice-leading work (semitonal displacements) and common-tone retention
3. The two metrics are independent and can produce contradictory proximity judgments
4. Diatonic collections play no role in the chromatic metric
5. Harmonic roots play no role in the chromatic metric
6. Both systems organize consonant triads (neither is "atonal")

# Construction / Recognition
Diatonic measurement:
- Distance based on shared scale membership
- Close relation = triads whose keys share 6+ of 7 tones
- The diatonic collection serves as the template for measuring distance

Chromatic measurement:
- Distance based on voice-leading work (semitonal displacements)
- Close relation = triads connected by minimal voice motion
- The chromatic collection (12 pitch classes) serves as the template

Example: C major to E major
- Diatonically: distant (no shared diatonic collection)
- Chromatically: close (one voice moves by semitone: G to G#)

# Context & Application
The distinction is the central framing of Audacious Euphony. It becomes crucial for analyzing 19th-century music where passages may be locally diatonic but globally chromatic, where triadic successions "make sense" by voice-leading criteria while "defying definition" in diatonic terms. Riemann called his progression "audacious" precisely because the triads are closely related chromatically (by common tones and voice leading) yet distant diatonically, producing a paradox that gives the book its title.

# Examples
- **Schubert, Piano Sonata in Bb, D. 960**: The recapitulation's key areas (Bb-Gb-f#-A-Bb) share minimal diatonic membership (only A/f# coexist in one diatonic collection) but progress smoothly by voice-leading criteria -- total voice-leading work of only 14 units
- **Riemann's "audacious" progression**: triads judged "closely related" despite scales sharing only 3 of 7 tones
- **Galeazzi (1796)**: rated C major to E major as "regular and good" despite no shared diatonic membership, because they share a common tone

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Chromatic organization means "more chromatic notes"
  **Correction**: It is a fundamentally different principle for relating triads, not a matter of quantity of chromaticism

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The distinction is equivalent to "tonal vs. atonal"
  **Clarification**: Both systems organize consonant triads; neither is atonal

- **Confusion**: Roman numeral analysis captures chromatic organization
  **Clarification**: Roman numerals can describe chromatically-organized passages but obscure the chromatic logic by imposing diatonic labels

- **Confusion**: Diatonic passages cannot exist within chromatic frameworks
  **Clarification**: Most 19th-century chromatic music is locally diatonic but globally chromatic (layered tonality)
