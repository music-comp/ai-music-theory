# Quick Definition
Two fundamentally different ways of organizing triadic harmony: diatonic organization derives relations from scale membership and root motion, while chromatic organization derives relations from voice-leading proximity in the twelve-tone chromatic universe.

# Formal Definition
Diatonic organization governs classical tonality, measuring triadic proximity by mutual membership in diatonic collections (sharing at least 6 of 7 scale tones) and interpreting chromatic harmonies as transformations of underlying diatonic ones. Chromatic organization, by contrast, measures triadic proximity by voice-leading work and common-tone retention within the chromatic collection of twelve pitch classes. The two systems generate different proximity judgments: triads closely related diatonically may be distant chromatically, and vice versa.

# Construction/Recognition
Diatonic measurement:
- Distance based on shared scale membership
- Close relation = triads whose keys share 6+ of 7 tones
- The diatonic collection serves as the template for measuring distance

Chromatic measurement:
- Distance based on voice-leading work (semitonal displacements)
- Close relation = triads connected by minimal voice motion
- The chromatic collection (12 pitch classes) serves as the template

Example: C major to E major
- Diatonically: distant (no shared diatonic collection)
- Chromatically: close (one voice moves by semitone: G to G#)

# Musical Context/Application
The distinction becomes crucial for analyzing 19th-century music where:
- Passages may be locally diatonic but globally chromatic
- Triadic successions "make sense" by voice-leading criteria while "defying definition" in diatonic terms
- Enharmonic relations create paradoxes that diatonic theory cannot resolve
- Sequential patterns follow chromatic rather than diatonic logic

The analytical challenge is recognizing when each organizational principle applies and how they interact in the same composition.

# Examples
- Schubert, Piano Sonata in Bb major, D. 960: The recapitulation's key areas (Bb - Gb - f# - A - Bb) share minimal diatonic membership (only one pair, A major/f# minor, belongs to any shared diatonic collection) but progress smoothly by voice-leading criteria
- Riemann's "audacious" progression: triads judged "closely related" despite their scales sharing only 3 of 7 tones
- Galeazzi (1796): rated C major to E major as "regular and good" despite lacking shared diatonic membership, because they share a common tone

# Common Confusions
- Chromatic organization is not simply "more chromatic notes" but a fundamentally different principle for relating triads
- Diatonic passages can exist within larger chromatic frameworks, and vice versa
- The distinction is not equivalent to "tonal vs. atonal" - both systems organize consonant triads
- Roman numeral analysis can obscure the distinction by assigning diatonic labels to chromatically-organized progressions
