# Quick Definition
A method for calculating triadic distance based on the number of pitch classes shared between two triads: more common tones indicates closer relationship.

# Formal Definition
The common-tone approach measures triadic proximity by the cardinality of pitch-class intersection between two triads. Two consonant triads can share 0, 1, or 2 pitch classes (they cannot share all 3 without being identical). Under this approach, triads sharing 2 tones are most closely related, those sharing 1 tone are intermediate, and those sharing 0 tones are most distant. This metric operates independently of diatonic collection membership or root relations.

# Construction/Recognition
For triads X and Y:
- |X ∩ Y| = 2: closest (e.g., C major {C, E, G} and a minor {A, C, E} share C and E)
- |X ∩ Y| = 1: intermediate (e.g., C major and E major share E)
- |X ∩ Y| = 0: most distant (e.g., C major {C, E, G} and f# minor {F#, A, C#} share nothing)

The common-tone metric can produce different proximity judgments than the diatonic metric:
- C major to d minor: diatonically close (both in C major scale), but 0 common tones
- C major to E major: diatonically distant (no shared scale), but 1 common tone

# Musical Context/Application
The common-tone approach was dominant in 19th-century harmonic theory:
- Galeazzi (1796): rated C-E as "regular and good" (1 common tone) while C-d was "very irregular and poor" (0 common tones)
- Krause (1827): "most closely related consonant triads are those that have two notes in common"
- Marx (1837): common tones provide "a more distinct tie" than mere co-occurrence in a diatonic collection
- Hauptmann (1853) and Helmholtz (1877): both dedicated to common-tone basis of harmonic proximity

This approach motivates analytical attention to pitch-class connections independent of root motion.

# Examples
- Schubert, Piano Sonata in Bb, recapitulation: f# minor shares 2 common tones with both Gb major (predecessor) and A major (successor), making the progression coherent by common-tone metric
- The "law of least motion" in voice leading pedagogy: hold common tones in same voice
- Third-related triads (C-E, C-Ab) share one common tone, contributing to their 19th-century prominence

# Common Confusions
- Common tones are counted as pitch classes, not pitches - octave equivalence applies
- Maximum common-tone retention (2) does not guarantee minimum voice-leading work (the moving voice might move by whole tone rather than semitone)
- The approach does not specify voice assignment - common tones need not be held in the same register
- Common-tone counting assumes idealized voice leading, abstracting from actual registral realization
