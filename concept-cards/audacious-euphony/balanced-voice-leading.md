# Quick Definition
Voice leading in which upward and downward motion alternate between adjacent triads, creating no net directional drift through pitch space, a property exclusive to major-third transposition cycles (hexatonic cycles).

# Core Definition
Balanced voice leading describes voice leading where "one voice moves up (G to G#) and one moves down (C to B), while the third voice, E, holds its place" (Cohn, p. 37). This occurs exclusively when transposing by major third (T4 or T8). "Under least-motion voice leading, recursive transposition by any interval other than major third generates uniformly directed voice leading. It is transposition by four or eight semitones that is special: these alone generate transposition cycles whose voice leading is balanced" (p. 37). The distinction between balanced and uniform voice leading "has gone unrecognized by the many scholars who have studied third relations during the last thirty years" (p. 37).

# Key Properties
1. For every voice that rises, another falls by the same magnitude
2. Exclusive to major-third transpositions (T4/T8)
3. Produces contrary motion (entailment of balance)
4. Circumnavigation produces "neither upward nor downward motion through the pitch spectrum" (p. 44)
5. The center of balance "toggles back and forth between two pitches separated by one-third of a semitone" (p. 44)

# Construction / Recognition
## Balanced (major-third transposition):
- C major to E major: G rises to G#, C falls to B, E holds
- Result: one voice up, one voice down, one stationary

## Uniform (minor-third transposition, for contrast):
- C major to Eb major: C falls to Bb, E falls to Eb, G holds
- Result: both moving voices move in the same direction (down)

The key insight: "the major third has this special status because *it divides the octave into as many equal parts as the triad has notes*" (p. 37).

# Context & Application
Balanced voice leading underlies both the syntactic role of major-third relations in pan-triadic progressions and their semiotic association with the supernatural. Cohn uses the analogy: "Balanced voice leading corresponds to balls atop an arch. Some released balls will fall east, some west... Uniform voice leading corresponds to the situation of balls on a tilted plane" (p. 37, footnote 2).

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 37): C major to E major: "under idealized voice leading one voice moves up (G to G#) and one moves down (C to B), while the third voice, E, holds its place."

**Example 2** (p. 37): C major to Eb major (contrasting uniform case): "both moving voices move down (C to Bb and E to Eb) while G holds its place."

**Example 3** (Fig. 2.3, pp. 39-40): All three types of hexatonic juxtaposition (adjacent, next-adjacent, diametric) exhibit balanced voice leading.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming all "smooth" progressions have balanced voice leading
  **Correction**: Only major-third transpositions produce balanced voice leading; minor-third and fifth transpositions produce uniform voice leading

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking balanced voice leading is the same as contrary motion in counterpoint pedagogy
  **Clarification**: Balanced voice leading is a structural property of specific transposition types, not a pedagogical prescription
- **Confusion**: Conflating balanced voice leading with static harmony
  **Clarification**: Voices move actively, but their aggregate motion cancels; local fluctuation underlies global stasis
