# Quick Definition
The hypnotic, suspended quality of passages that cycle through triads within a single hexatonic system, lacking cadential resources of classical tonality and creating tonal multistability.

# Core Definition
When a musical passage confines itself to the six triads of a single hexatonic system, it enters a state characterized by: absence of fifth-related roots, absence of dissonance requiring resolution, absence of diatonic coordination, and equal potential for any constituent triad to serve as tonic. "A hexatonic cycle abjures the cadential resources of classical tonality, such as fifth-related roots, dissonances, and diatonic coordination. A composer can suggest one of its constituent triads as a tonic 'factitiously by virtue of its recurrence' (Taruskin 1996, I: 259) but can secure it only by recruiting external syntactic routines" (Cohn, p. 43). The six triads are "equally likely recipients of rhetorical or cadential benefaction; the progression itself is neutral with respect to its potential tonics" (p. 43).

# Key Properties
1. 3:2 phasing: triple periodicity within voices, duple periodicity between them
2. Hocket canon structure: voices enter canonically at two-beat intervals
3. Balanced voice leading: alternating up and down, no net directional motion
4. Center of balance remains roughly constant despite local fluctuations
5. "Circumnavigation of a hexatonic cycle thus produces neither upward nor downward motion through the pitch spectrum" (p. 44)
6. Tonal multistability: each triad contains the other's leading tone

# Construction / Recognition
Structural features producing the trance state:
1. Each individual voice holds its pitch for three "beats" then displaces by semitone (Fig. 2.2)
2. The triple periodicity within voices interlocks with duple periodicity of the cycle
3. Voices execute displacements "two beats later and four semitones lower than its predecessor"
4. The result: "a hocket canon, a structure familiar to music historians from the caccia of late-medieval polyphony" (p. 38)

# Context & Application
Cohn draws a speculative analogy to African musical traditions: "hexatonic cycles embed a hocket canon that projects a 3:2 phasing" similar to patterns "associated with spirit possession and trance" (Rouget 1985, cited on p. 44). In post-Enlightenment Europe, Dr. Mesmer's theories of animal magnetism held interest; "one can imagine, then, that a composer of the early nineteenth century might be interested in exploring ways to depict states or sensations associated with hypnosis" (p. 44).

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 43): The Tarnhelm progression depicts invisibility through tonal instability; "each Tarnhelm triad contains the other's leading tone and hence signifies the other as tonic, like the Escher hands drawing each other's sleeves."

**Example 2** (p. 43): Comparison to the West African "standard" bell pattern (*bembe*, *gagokoe*) whose temporal "tonic" (downbeat) can occur at several points along the cycle.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the trance requires literal, complete cycling through all six triads
  **Correction**: Even partial traversal of a hexatonic system can produce the effect

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Taking "trance" as a claim about actual psychological states in listeners
  **Clarification**: It is an analytical metaphor describing the suspended, tonally ambiguous quality of the passage
- **Confusion**: Equating the hexatonic trance with general "hypnotic" effects in music
  **Clarification**: Hexatonic trance specifically requires confinement to a single hexatonic system
