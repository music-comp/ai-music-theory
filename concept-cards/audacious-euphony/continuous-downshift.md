# Quick Definition
Continuous downshift is a voice-leading pattern where all voices consistently move downward through voice-leading zones, typically associated with departure from tonic and dominant-ward motion in traditional tonal contexts.

# Formal Definition
**Continuous downshift** is a compositional pattern where triadic progressions lead uniformly through a series of adjacent voice-leading zones in a downward direction. In idealized voice leading, downshifting involves aggregate voice motion that descends through pitch space.

Downshifting is associated with:
- Departure from tonic in classical departure → return schemes
- Motion toward dominant function
- Sequential passages that descend chromatically

The concept represents one pole of the fundamental voice-leading duality (down/up) that Cohn argues can replace or supplement the Riemannian duality of subdominant/dominant.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Continuous downshift involves:
1. Progressive motion through voice-leading zones in descending order (e.g., 10 → 7 → 5 → 2 → ...)
2. Aggregate voice motion predominantly downward
3. Can be realized through sequences or free progressions

In the downshift/upshift duality:
- Downshifting may correlate with authentic (dominant-ward) motion
- The relationship emerges from: pitch motion exists on a linear continuum; pitch-class motion exists on a cyclical continuum; motion along either can occur in two opposite directions

# Musical Context/Application
Continuous downshift serves several compositional purposes:
- Creating sense of departure from tonic
- Building toward structural dominants
- Establishing the "departure" phase of departure → return scripts
- Generating sequential passages with chromatic voice leading

The technique appears prominently in:
- Exposition sections moving from tonic to dominant
- Development sections before the retransition
- Any passage establishing distance from a tonal center

# Examples
**Schubert's "Auf dem Flusse"**: The first quatrain executes downshifting from zone 10 to zone 5 through N → L → N transformations (e minor → B major → d# minor). This establishes the departure leg of the song's departure → return scheme.

**Schubert's Bb major Piano Sonata, D. 960, first movement, exposition**: A two-phase descent in voice-leading space: the f# minor second theme displaces Bb major downward by one voice-leading degree (5 → 4), then F major displaces f# minor downward by two degrees (4 → 2). The nadir is reached with c# minor at the opening of the development.

**Chopin's e minor Prelude, Op. 28 no. 4**: The chromatic linear progressions from tonic to structural subdominant are entirely semitonal downshifts, with the overall trajectory covering fifteen semitones of voice leading descent in the first phrase.

**Chopin's f minor Mazurka, Op. 68 no. 4**: The passage at mm. 33-40 executes T5 then T11 sequences of dominant seventh chords, maintaining continuous downshift trajectory.

# Common Confusions
- Downshift refers to aggregate voice motion, not melodic contour; soprano may ascend while the overall texture downshifts
- Downshift correlates with but does not equal dominant function; the correlation is statistical, not absolute
- Scalar descent (descending scales) differs from voice-leading downshift (triadic zone motion)
- Classical cadences often involve local upshift even within a broader downshifting context
