# Quick Definition
Single semitonal displacement is the movement of exactly one voice by exactly one semitone between two triads, representing the minimal unit of voice-leading work in triadic progressions.

# Formal Definition
In a progression between two consonant triads, single semitonal displacement occurs when one and only one pitch class changes, and that change is by one semitone (a half step). The resulting progression preserves two common tones and produces the smallest possible measurable change in pitch content. This is the defining characteristic of the L and P transformations.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
- Voice-leading work = 1 (one voice moves one semitone)
- Common tones retained = 2
- Connecting triads must be of opposite mode
- The displacement may be chromatic (as in P: E to Eb) or diatonic (as in L: G to G# between C major and c# minor)
- Total semitonal displacement in a hexatonic cycle progression of n steps = n semitones

# Musical Context/Application
Single semitonal displacement is the basic building block of hexatonic progressions. When composers chain together transformations that each involve single semitonal displacement, they create the smooth, hypnotic voice-leading characteristic of pan-triadic harmony. This minimal motion allows for maximal harmonic change (from major to minor or vice versa) with minimal melodic disruption.

# Examples
- C major to c minor: only the third moves (E to Eb) - P transformation
- C major to e minor: only the root moves (C to B) - L transformation
- c minor to Ab major: only the fifth moves (G to Ab) - L transformation
- ab minor to Ab major: only the third moves (Cb to C) - P transformation

From the text: "c minor can reach both C major and Ab major by a single semitonal displacement" (p. 36)

# Common Confusions
- Single semitonal displacement always connects triads of opposite mode; there is no single-semitone connection between two major triads or two minor triads
- Do not confuse the semitone species: chromatic semitone (same letter name, e.g., E-Eb) versus diatonic semitone (different letter name, e.g., C-B)
- A "semitone" in this context always refers to one half step, regardless of enharmonic spelling
