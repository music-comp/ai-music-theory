# Quick Definition
Extensions of the diatonic Tonnetz along the fifth axis, allowing modulation to closely related keys through incremental shifts of the diatonic parallelogram and enabling recursive migrations through the L/R chain.

# Formal Definition
Horizontal extensions arise when the chain of perfect fifths is extended outside the diatonic parallelogram, a process historically begun in Renaissance modality and compounded over centuries (Lowinsky 1946, 1989). Under classical tonality, limited mobility along these tracks embeds the diatonic capsule into a larger "modulatory" shell. Sharps to the right and flats to the left temporarily displace diatonic letter-name equivalents, shifting the parallelogram horizontally. With each shift comes a new pair of syntonic images, new potential tonics, and reversal of Rings arrows and their syntactic and semantic forces.

# Construction/Recognition
Close modulation:
- Shifts limited to single degree on either side of tonic
- Each shift creates new syntonic images
- May be characterized as secondary chords, tonicizations, or modulations

Recursive extensions along L/R chain:
- Pairs of shifts in single direction exit closely related keys
- Sequential tonicization of fifth-related, modally matched tonics compounds the process
- "Moving window begins to resemble a local train, migrating incrementally in a uniform direction and locking temporarily into a series of adjacent stations"
- Track is the L/R chain (chapter 5)

The chain extends to 24 stations before replication occurs.

# Musical Context
Three modes of L/R chain navigation:
1. **Locking migrations**: Each station cadentially confirmed (classical development sections)
2. **Sliding surveillance**: Window slows to inspect each station but alights at none (Bach d minor Toccata BWV 913)
3. **Slippery slope**: Extended excursion where triads fly by without diatonic gathering (Beethoven Ninth Symphony Scherzo mm. 159-71)

As the passage progresses: "Exiting the initial diatonic space, the parallelogram slides incrementally left or right. Flying past multiple diatonic spaces, its diagonal edges ease open, and the horizontal track converts to an alley of indefinite extension. Rings arrows retract into the body of the moving vehicle."

# Examples
- Liszt Consolation no. 3, first sentence: Tonicization of f minor shifts syntonic images from Eb to Bb (Figure 8.7c)
- Bach d minor Toccata (BWV 913): After establishing g minor, moves incrementally leftward through six flats, then reverses rightward, none cadentially confirmed
- Beethoven Ninth Symphony Scherzo (mm. 159-71): L/R chain as "slippery slope" - triads fly by until pattern breaks and tonic-seeking radar reawakens

# Common Confusions
- Horizontal extensions are not merely theoretical; they trace historical expansion of tonal practice from Renaissance through Classical era
- The L/R chain is not inherently chromatic; it moves through a series of diatonic spaces
- Not every passage traversing the L/R chain need be heard as a sequence; some are "slippery slopes" where diatonic attunement is abandoned mid-journey
- Common-tone relations among triads provide overdetermination with acoustic and scalar common-tone motivations
