# Quick Definition
A four-triad subset of a Weitzmann region featuring a pair of R-related triads (interior) flanked by their N-related triads (exterior), connected by enharmonic reinterpretation of a "double agent" pitch that changes its directional tendency.

# Core Definition
The double-agent complex consists of four triads from a Weitzmann region arranged as a contiguous N/R chain segment (Figure 4.10). The "interior" pair are R-related (relative major/minor); the "exterior" pair are N-related to the interior triads and S-related to each other. The common third of the S-related exterior triads "is obscured by the enharmonic distinction" (p. 87): the same pitch presses downward in one context and upward in the other, acting as a "double agent" in Lewin's Cold War allegory.

# Prerequisites
- **Weitzmann region**: The complex is a four-triad subset
- **Agents (Harrison)**: The directional tendencies of triadic thirds
- **N/R cycle**: The complex is a four-triad segment of the cycle
- **S transformation**: Connects the exterior triads

# Key Properties
1. Four triads arranged as N-R-N chain segment
2. Interior pair: R-related (relative major/minor)
3. Exterior pair: N-related to their adjacent interior triads, S-related to each other
4. The S-related exterior triads share a common third that must be enharmonically respelled
5. The complex is permutationally malleable (elements can be reordered)
6. The complex is tonally adaptable (can center on any of its four triads)
7. The complex can appear even when none of its triads is global tonic

# Construction / Recognition
Structure around c minor/Eb major (Figure 4.10):
- G major (exterior) --(N)--> c minor (interior) --(R)--> Eb major (interior) --(N)--> ab minor (exterior)
- G major and ab minor are S-related: common third Bb/Cb is the double agent
- Bb (third of G major) presses upward; Cb (third of ab minor) presses downward

# Context & Application
The double-agent complex is "remarkably malleable, both in the ordering of its elements and in the tonal environments in which they operate" (p. 90). It appears in canonical, permuted, and nontonic forms throughout 19th-century music, from Chopin to Brahms.

# Examples
**Chopin, Mazurka Op. 6 No. 1** (pp. 87-88): Complex around f# minor/A major; avoids direct enharmonic contact ("teases the seam").
**Chopin, Nocturne Op. 9 No. 1** (pp. 87-88): Complex around bb minor/Db major; Bbb transforms directly into A ("plunges through" the seam).
**Wagner, Tarnhelm** (pp. 91-92): Permuted order; relative major follows rather than precedes minor subdominant.
**Schubert, Impromptus Op. 90 Nos. 2 and 3** (pp. 93-95): Exterior triads tonicized.
**Schumann, "Im wunderschonen Monat Mai"** (pp. 95-96): Nontonic double-agent complex.
**Brahms, "Von ewiger Liebe"** (pp. 95-96): Tonally remote double-agent complex.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking the "double agent" is a chord
  **Correction**: "The 'double agent' is a pitch, not a chord -- it's the enharmonically respelled third" (cf. p. 89)

- **Error**: Assuming the complex must appear in canonical ordering
  **Correction**: The complex is "permutationally malleable" -- elements can appear in any order

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The complex can only center on the interior minor triad
  **Clarification**: It can "in principle center around any of its four triadic constituents" (p. 95)
- **Confusion**: The complex requires tonic function
  **Clarification**: It can appear "where none of its constituent chords serves as tonic" (p. 95)
