# Quick Definition
The group of three transformations (R, N, S) that preserve Weitzmann region membership while shifting hexatonic region, complementary to the H-group (L, P, H).

# Formal Definition
The W-group (Weitzmann group) consists of three triadic transformations:
- R (Relative): one voice moves by whole step
- N (Nebenverwandt): two voices move by semitone in contrary motion
- S (Slide): two voices move by semitone in parallel motion

All W-group transformations:
- Require 2 units of voice-leading work
- Preserve Weitzmann region membership
- Shift hexatonic region membership
- Change mode (major to minor or vice versa)

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
W-group properties:
- Closed under combination: any sequence of W-group operations remains in same Weitzmann region
- Complementary to H-group: alternating W and H moves between adjacent regions
- Each W-group transformation shifts the voice-leading zone by 2

Combination with H-group generates chromatic sequences:
| H\W | R | N | S |
|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| L | T5 | T1 | T3 |
| P | T3 | T5 | T1 |
| H | T1 | T3 | T5 |

# Musical Context/Application
The W-group/H-group distinction is fundamental to understanding chromatic sequences in 19th-century music. Any chromatic sequence results from alternating one H-group and one W-group transformation. The W-group contains two classically normative transformations (R, N) and one maverick (S), parallel to the H-group's structure (L, P normative; H maverick).

# Examples
- R and N alternate in N/R cycles (Schubert symphonies)
- S appears in Schubert, Liszt, Wagner chromatic progressions
- W-group combined with H-group generates all odd-transposition sequences

# Common Confusions
- W-group and H-group are not opposites but complements
- W-group transformations preserve Weitzmann region (not hexatonic)
- The label "W" comes from Weitzmann, who first described these regional structures
