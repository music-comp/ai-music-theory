# Quick Definition
The principle that the soft revolution was possible because it changed syntax (organizational principles) while maintaining the same lexicon (consonant triads), allowing composers and listeners to cross between syntactic systems without encountering unfamiliar sonic material.

# Core Definition
The central suggestion of Chapter 9's penultimate section is that "lexical continuity can bridge over a syntactic impasse along the historical axis, as well as within a phrase of Schubert" (p. 227). A theory of double syntax "allows that at the same time that triads are root-indexing consonances, they have the capacity to serve as something else entirely: nearly even chords participating in a system of tight voice leading. Under cover of a unified lexicon, a composer learns to hear a different set of syntactic possibilities, and to train listeners to that hearing" (p. 226).

# Prerequisites
- **Double syntax hypothesis**: The framework within which lexical continuity operates
- **Homophonous diamorph**: The specific items providing lexical continuity

# Key Properties
1. The same triads appear in both syntactic systems
2. No new sonic material is introduced when switching syntaxes
3. Lexical continuity bridges syntactic change both within phrases and across historical time
4. Only at later evolutionary stages (Stage 2+) does lexical change accompany syntactic change
5. When lexical change does occur (Tristan genus, hexatonic/octatonic scales), the road of return becomes bumpy

# Construction / Recognition
Three central arguments summarized (p. 227):
1. "The consonant triad has two natures, and those two natures lead to two syntaxes."
2. "The juxtaposition of syntaxes is not as problematic as is sometimes thought, particularly when smoothed over by lexical continuity."
3. "Lexical continuity can bridge over a syntactic impasse along the historical axis, as well as within a phrase of Schubert."

The point of no return: "Once composers begin to use [0148]-type chords in a hexatonic context, or [0146]-type chords in an octatonic one, the imposition of a diatonic cadence often sounds like the grinding of gears" (p. 227) -- because lexical continuity has been broken.

# Context & Application
Lexical continuity explains why the first stage of the soft revolution (syntactic flip) is the critical one: "It is the syntactic substitution at the first stage that provides the key that unlocks the capsule of diatonic tonality" (p. 227). Later stages involve increasingly lexical change, which makes the transitions rougher and the return to diatonic syntax more difficult.

# Examples
- **Schubert's instrumental music**: Chromatic syntax uses the same triads as diatonic syntax
- **Diatonic sequence (Baroque)**: Arrow flip occurs entirely within a familiar triadic vocabulary
- **Contrast with late Wagner**: Tristan-genus promotion (Stage 2) introduces new primary objects
- **Contrast with Schoenberg/Webern**: Full lexical change to set classes (Stage 4)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Lexical continuity means the music sounds the same in both syntaxes
  **Correction**: The objects are the same but they are organized differently -- which changes how they sound in context

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Lexical continuity means syntax doesn't matter
  **Clarification**: Syntax determines the relations between objects, which is where the musical meaning resides
