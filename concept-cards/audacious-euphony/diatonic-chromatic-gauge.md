# Quick Definition
The concept that diatonic and chromatic systems provide different "measuring sticks" for triadic distance, and that passages crossing the enharmonic seam require relinquishing the diatonic gauge for a chromatic one, where enharmonic equivalents become notational variants of a single object.

# Core Definition
When chromatic progressions cross the enharmonic seam, "seven-tone diatonic space provides no adequate stick for measuring distances" (p. 215). "The solution comes easily once we relinquish the diatonic gauge for a chromatic one. C-sharp and D-flat become notational variants of a single object. Like the Morning Star and the Evening Star, they achieve identity by intension as well as by extension, by sense as well as by reference" (p. 215). Root distances follow automatically: the unified C-sharp/D-flat object is heard as equidistant from F and A. "What is compromised in this move is the syntactic position and semantic force of that tone" (p. 215).

# Prerequisites
- **Double syntax hypothesis**: The framework within which gauge-switching operates

# Key Properties
1. The diatonic gauge measures distance by scale-degree position and root consonance
2. The chromatic gauge measures distance by semitonal voice-leading work
3. The diatonic gauge fails at the enharmonic seam
4. Switching gauges preserves distance measurement but compromises syntactic position
5. The same triad functions differently under each gauge

# Construction / Recognition
Under the diatonic gauge: C-sharp minor and D-flat minor are distinct entities with different functions relative to different keys. The "dotted arrow" connecting them on the diatonic Tonnetz "seems to travel a great distance" (p. 215).

Under the chromatic gauge: they are a single entity equidistant from its neighbors. "Although the chord still retains its forked syntactic and appellative residues in diatonic space, what comes to the fore as syntactic driver is the minimal change relation between adjacent triads: each motion requires only a single semitonal motion" (p. 215).

# Context & Application
The gauge concept is central to the Schubert Scherzo analysis. The c-sharp/d-flat minor triad at m. 123 cannot be diatonically construed in C major, f minor, or d minor, "or even in the parallel modes of any of those three keys" (p. 214). Only by switching to the chromatic gauge does the distance become measurable and the passage coherent.

The concept generalizes: "Once attuned to the minimal change at the center of the passage, that attunement disperses toward its exterior" (p. 216), revealing upshift patterns throughout.

# Examples
- **Schubert Scherzo m. 123**: C-sharp/D-flat minor -- inscrutable diatonically, transparent chromatically (pp. 214-216)
- **Morning Star/Evening Star analogy**: Identity by intension as well as extension (p. 215)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Gauge switching means the diatonic gauge was wrong
  **Correction**: Both gauges are valid for different aspects of the same passage; neither replaces the other

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The chromatic gauge eliminates all syntactic meaning
  **Clarification**: The triad retains "forked syntactic and appellative residues" even under the chromatic gauge; what changes is which property drives perception
