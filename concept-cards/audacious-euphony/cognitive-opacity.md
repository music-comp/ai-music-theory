# Quick Definition
The phenomenon whereby syntactic switching, causal reversal, and overdetermination occur beneath the threshold of conscious awareness, so that listeners experience smooth transitions without noticing when organizational principles have changed.

# Core Definition
Cognitive opacity describes the sub-threshold nature of processes involved in double syntax. Code switchers "tend to be surprised at their own performance if you play it back to them" (Gardner-Chloros 2009, 121). Similarly, musical transitions between syntaxes may transpire "beneath the horizon of awareness" (p. 221). Cohn argues through quotidian analogies that not just language-switching but overdetermination generally is cognitively opaque: wearing garments for warmth, concealment, or adornment "may feel pretty much like a unitary act" despite distinct functions (p. 222). "A cunning composer or crafty performer knows how to take advantage of the opacity of causal reversal, to flip the arrow of causality without rocking a listener's boat" (p. 224).

# Prerequisites
- **Double syntax hypothesis**: The claim whose cognitive feasibility opacity supports
- **Code switching**: The linguistic phenomenon demonstrating opacity

# Key Properties
1. Syntactic switching occurs beneath conscious awareness
2. Causal reversal (arrow flipping) is similarly opaque
3. Overdetermination of multiple functions merges into unitary experience
4. Evidence from both linguistics and everyday cognition
5. Opacity does not mean the processes are insignificant -- they shape perception

# Construction / Recognition
**Evidence from non-musical domains** (pp. 222-224):
1. Garment functions: Wearing for warmth, concealment, or adornment "feels like a unitary act"
2. Causal reversals: "Do you want money to get power, or power to get money?" -- reversals "float below the threshold of consciousness"
3. Driving: Taking car to shop vs. car taking you to barber -- "from a cognitive standpoint, one hardly notices the difference"

**Musical manifestations**:
- G as neighbor to Ab becomes Ab as neighbor to G (Parsifal)
- Eb as dominant of Ab becomes subdominant of Eb
- Arsis becomes thesis; strong beats become weak
- Triad shifts from acoustic object to voice-leading object

# Context & Application
Cognitive opacity directly addresses the Dahlhaus/Lerdahl objection that syntactic switching is psychologically implausible. If "musical cognition is not entirely remote from other sorts of mental processing (Zbikowski 2002), then we cannot dismiss the possibility that switching between musical syntaxes might transpire beneath our horizon of awareness, especially when the switching is routed through an overdetermined object such as a triad or a dominant seventh chord" (p. 224).

# Examples
- **Bilingual code switching**: Speakers unaware of their 15+ switches (Gardner-Chloros 2009, 121)
- **Garment analogy**: Dressing for warmth, concealment, or adornment feels like one act (p. 222)
- **Driving analogy**: Taking the car vs. the car taking you (p. 223)
- **Parsifal opening**: G/Ab neighbor reversal; Eb/Ab dominant relationship reversal (p. 224)
- **Schubert Scherzo m. 117**: Arrow reversal as diatonic lock releases (p. 222)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Cognitive opacity means listeners are unaware of harmonic change
  **Correction**: They are unaware of syntactic mode change, not of the music itself

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Opacity makes analytical investigation pointless
  **Clarification**: Opacity explains why analysis can uncover structures not consciously perceived -- it motivates rather than undermines analysis
