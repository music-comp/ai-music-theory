# Quick Definition
The phenomenon whereby syntactic switching or causal reversal in music occurs beneath the threshold of conscious awareness, so that listeners experience smooth transitions without noticing when organizational principles have changed.

# Formal Definition
Cognitive opacity describes the sub-threshold nature of processes involved in double syntax. Drawing on bilingualism research, Cohn notes that code switchers are "on the whole not very aware of their code-switching behaviour, and tend to be surprised at their own performance if you play it back to them" (Gardner-Chloros 2009, 121). Similarly, musical transitions between diatonic and chromatic syntax, or reversals of causal/hierarchical direction, may transpire "beneath the horizon of awareness." This opacity explains why listeners do not experience the "grinding of gears" that Smith (1986) feared would accompany syntactic switching.

# Construction/Recognition
Evidence for cognitive opacity in non-musical domains:
1. Bilingual code switching: 15+ switches in rapid speech without interrupting flow
2. Garment functions: Wearing clothes for warmth, concealment, or adornment feels like "a unitary act" despite distinct purposes
3. Causal reversals: "Do you want money to get power, or power to get money?" - reversals float below consciousness

Musical manifestations:
- Neighbor becomes tone, tone becomes neighbor (Parsifal opening)
- Arsis becomes thesis, strong becomes weak
- Triad shifts from acoustic object to voice-leading object
- Overdetermined chords mediate between syntaxes without discrete switch point

"A cunning composer or crafty performer knows how to take advantage of the opacity of causal reversal, to flip the arrow of causality without rocking a listener's boat."

# Musical Context
Cognitive opacity addresses objections to double syntax:
- Dahlhaus (1967): "The supposition that the musical hearing switches between tonal and non-tonal apprehension during a composition or a phrase would be problematic"
- Lerdahl (2001): Switching between systems is "implausible as a psychological position"

Cohn responds that these objections assume switching must be conscious and jarring. But "if musical cognition is not entirely remote from other sorts of mental processing... then we cannot dismiss the possibility that switching between musical syntaxes might transpire beneath our horizon of awareness."

# Examples
- Schubert C major Symphony Scherzo: Transition from diatonic to hexatonic organization at m. 117 occurs without jarring discontinuity
- The enharmonic seam (Db becoming C#) is crossed cognitively without conscious reinterpretation
- Liszt Consolation: Each hexatonic station is locally diatonic without listeners tracking syntactic mode
- Wagner Parsifal opening: G as neighbor to Ab becomes Ab as neighbor to G; Eb as dominant of Ab becomes subdominant of Eb

# Common Confusions
- Cognitive opacity does not mean listeners are unaware of harmonic change; they are unaware of syntactic mode change
- Opacity applies to the switching process, not to the musical content itself
- The phenomenon is not unique to music; it characterizes many cognitive processes
- Opacity does not make analysis impossible; it explains why analysis can uncover structures not consciously perceived
