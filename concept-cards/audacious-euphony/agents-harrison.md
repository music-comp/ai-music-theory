# Quick Definition
In Daniel Harrison's theory of harmonic function, "agents" are the thirds of triads, which carry the most intense melodic energy: major thirds strive upward with dominant energy, while minor thirds press downward with subdominant "attitude."

# Core Definition
Harrison (1994) proposes that "the melodic energy invested in thirds bestows agency upon their constituent harmonies: triadic major thirds strive upward, bearing dominant energy, while triadic minor thirds press downward with subdominant 'attitude'" (p. 89). These "appellative tendencies are intensified when the thirds are chromatic inflections that displace their diatonic equivalents" (p. 89). In the nebenverwandt relation between C major and f minor, the agents are "the upward-pressing E, borrowed from f minor's parallel major, and the downward-pressing Ab, borrowed from C major's parallel minor" (p. 65).

# Prerequisites
- **Consonant triad**: Must understand the distinction between root, third, and fifth to identify which tones carry agency

# Key Properties
1. The **major third** of any triad is the dominant agent, pressing upward by semitone
2. The **minor third** (from the root) of any minor triad is the subdominant agent, pressing downward
3. In a nebenverwandt pair, the two agents point in opposite directions, creating reciprocal tension
4. Agency is intensified when the third is a chromatic inflection displacing a diatonic equivalent
5. The concept originates in dualist thinking but was adopted by non-dualist theorists (Louis and Thuille, Kurth, Lorenz)

# Construction / Recognition
To identify agents:
1. Locate the third of the triad
2. Determine if it is a major third (presses up = dominant agent) or serves as a minor third from the root (presses down = subdominant agent)
3. In chromatic contexts, check for enharmonic respelling that redirects agency (double-agent phenomenon)

# Context & Application
The concept of agency is central to understanding the double-agent complex in Weitzmann regions (Chapter 4). When a pitch functions as both an upward-pressing agent in one triadic context and a downward-pressing agent in another (through enharmonic respelling), it becomes a "double agent" -- a musical version of a Cold War spy whose loyalties are uncertain.

# Examples
**Nebenverwandt pair C major / f minor** (p. 65): E is the upward-pressing agent (dominant energy toward F), Ab is the downward-pressing agent (subdominant energy toward G).

**Chopin Nocturne Op. 9 No. 1** (p. 89): "The downward agency of Bbb is converted instantly into the upward agency of A." Cohn notes: "I find that I need to sing the pitch continuously in order to convince myself that the two pitches are identified; and even then, it feels like a trick."

**Harrison's broader context** (p. 64): "The conception originates in dualist thinking but was sensible enough that it was taken up by theorists with no commitment to dualism, such as Louis and Thuille [1913], Kurth 1923, and Lorenz 1933."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating any active tone as an "agent"
  **Correction**: "Agent" refers specifically to the third of a triad, not to any melodically active pitch

- **Error**: Assuming agency is only relevant in chromatic contexts
  **Correction**: Agency exists in diatonic contexts too but is intensified by chromatic inflection

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Agency is purely a voice-leading concept
  **Clarification**: Agency is directional and functional (dominant vs. subdominant energy), not merely about semitonal motion
- **Confusion**: The concept requires commitment to harmonic dualism
  **Clarification**: Although it originates in dualist thinking, it was adopted by non-dualist theorists
