# Quick Definition
A hexatonic cycle is a closed chain of six consonant triads connected by single semitonal displacement, alternating between major and minor mode, whose pitch content draws from a six-note hexatonic scale.

# Formal Definition
Beginning with any consonant triad and connecting it to triads via the minimal-work relation, one generates a chain of alternating major and minor triads. This chain closes into a cycle after six triads when the seventh triad would be enharmonically equivalent to the first. The four hexatonic cycles partition all twenty-four consonant triads into four groups of six, with no overlap between cycles.

# Construction/Recognition
Structure of a hexatonic cycle (starting from C major):
- C major - c minor - Ab major - ab minor - E major - e minor - [C major]

Properties:
- 6 triads per cycle (3 major, 3 minor)
- Roots form an augmented triad (e.g., C-E-Ab)
- Adjacent triads share 2 common tones
- Opposite triads in the cycle (3 steps apart) are hexatonic poles
- All triads draw from a single hexatonic collection [014589]
- The cycle is generated by alternating P and L transformations

# Musical Context
Hexatonic cycles appear throughout nineteenth-century chromatic music, particularly in passages depicting supernatural, uncanny, or altered states of consciousness. The cycle's inherent tonal ambiguity - where no single triad asserts dominance - creates a sense of floating or suspension. Composers exploit this quality for expressive purposes, especially when depicting dreams, magic, or transcendence.

# Examples

# Common Confusions
- Hexatonic cycles are distinct from chromatic sequences generated by minor thirds; major-third cycles exhibit balanced voice leading while minor-third cycles have uniform voice leading
- The "hexatonic" label refers both to the six triads in the cycle AND the six-note scale they collectively articulate
- Do not confuse with diatonic progressions that happen to involve third relations; hexatonic cycles specifically require chromatic semitonal connections
