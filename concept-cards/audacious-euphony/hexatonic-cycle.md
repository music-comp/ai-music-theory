# Quick Definition
A closed chain of six consonant triads connected by single semitonal displacement, alternating between major and minor mode, whose combined pitch content forms a six-note hexatonic scale.

# Core Definition
A hexatonic cycle is generated by chaining the minimal-work relation. "Each consonant triad is thus situated in a chain of alternating major and minor triads" (Cohn, p. 36). Starting from C major: "C major is flanked by c minor and e minor, producing the three-element chain {c minor, C major, e minor}." Extending further: "{Ab major, {c minor, C major, e minor}, E major}." The chain continues to "{ab minor, {Ab major, {c minor, C major, e minor}, E major}, g# minor}. The external elements of that septet are identified under enharmonic equivalence, and so the outer elements are 'glued together,' converting the seven-element chain into a six-element cycle" (p. 36). Four such cycles partition all 24 consonant triads.

- **consonant-triad** — The cycle consists exclusively of consonant triads

# Key Properties
1. 6 triads per cycle (3 major, 3 minor)
2. Adjacent triads share 2 common tones and differ by 1 semitone
3. Roots of the 3 major triads form an augmented triad (e.g., C-E-Ab)
4. Generated by alternating P and L transformations
5. All triads draw from a single hexatonic collection [014589]
6. Triads 3 positions apart are hexatonic poles (0 common tones, 3 units of work)
7. The cycle is neutral with respect to tonic; any triad can serve as center
8. The four cycles partition all 24 consonant triads with no overlap

# Construction / Recognition
## To construct a hexatonic cycle from any starting triad:
1. Start with a major triad (e.g., C major: C-E-G)
2. Apply P: move the third by chromatic semitone -> c minor (C-Eb-G)
3. Apply L: move the fifth by diatonic semitone -> Ab major (Ab-C-Eb)
4. Apply P: -> ab minor (Ab-Cb-Eb)
5. Apply L: -> E major (E-G#-B)
6. Apply P: -> e minor (E-G-B)
7. Apply L: -> C major (return to start)

Result: C - c - Ab - ab - E - e - [C]

# Context & Application
Hexatonic cycles appear throughout nineteenth-century chromatic music, particularly in passages depicting supernatural, uncanny, or altered states. The cycle's inherent tonal ambiguity creates a sense of floating or suspension. Derek Waller first identified these cycles in a 1978 article in the *Mathematical Gazette* (p. 36, footnote 1).

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 43-45): Mozart, Symphony K. 543, finale, mm. 109-126: traverses a hexatonic cycle with diatonic buffering between stations.

**Example 2** (p. 44): Haydn, Symphony no. 98, finale, mm. 148-198: developmental core traversing a hexatonic cycle.

**Example 3** (p. 44-45): Beethoven, "Spring" Sonata Op. 24, 2nd mvt., mm. 38-54: block transpositions of lyrical material through a hexatonic cycle.

**Example 4** (p. 45): Schubert, Piano Trio in Eb, Op. 100, 1st mvt., mm. 584-595: three phrases traverse a complete hexatonic cycle.

**Example 5** (p. 48): Brahms, Concerto for Violin and Cello, Op. 102, mm. 270-79: hexatonic cycle without diatonic buffering, sounding only the six hexatonic-scale tones.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Confusing hexatonic cycles with minor-third cycles
  **Correction**: Hexatonic (major-third) cycles exhibit balanced voice leading; minor-third cycles have uniform voice leading (p. 37)

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking "hexatonic" refers only to the six triads
  **Clarification**: The label refers both to the six triads in the cycle AND the six-note scale they articulate (p. 38)
- **Confusion**: Assuming hexatonic cycles require literal, unbuffered presentation
  **Clarification**: Early examples (Mozart, Haydn) have diatonic buffering between stations; later examples (Brahms) are unbuffered
