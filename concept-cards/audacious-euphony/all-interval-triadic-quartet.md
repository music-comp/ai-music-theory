# Quick Definition
A quartet of triads whose voice-leading zones form an all-interval tetrachord ([0146] or [0137]), distributing their six pairwise voice-leading intervals as evenly as possible across all six interval classes, achieving maximum entropy.

# Core Definition
Taking inspiration from Elliott Carter's practice, Robert Morris (1990) showed that any nonintersecting union of a tritone with a minor third creates an all-interval tetrachord. "Piping this observation through our homology, we learn that a maximally entropic ensemble of four triads consists of two zone-diametric (T2- or T6-related) triads of one mode, combined with two triads of the opposite mode that are related to each other by an odd transposition" (p. 130). Both all-interval tetrachord types ([0146] and [0137]) are octatonic subsets, and the eight consonant-triad voice-leading zones have octatonic structure, guaranteeing the existence of such quartets.

# Prerequisites
- **Voice-leading zones**: The metric space enabling interval measurement
- **Zone-diametric relations**: The tritone/whole-step maximum distance used in construction
- **Disjunction and entropy**: The broader framework of disjunction analysis

# Key Properties
1. Zones form all-interval tetrachord: [0146] or [0137]
2. Six pairwise intervals distributed across all six interval classes
3. Construction: tritone (or whole-step) pair of one mode + odd-transposition pair of opposite mode
4. Both tetrachord types are octatonic subsets
5. Exhausts (to within transposition and reordering) the ways of distributing the pitch-class aggregate among four consonant triads

# Construction / Recognition
1. Select two zone-diametric triads of one mode (e.g., two major triads 6 zones apart)
2. Select two triads of the opposite mode, related by odd transposition (3 zones apart)
3. Check that the four zones form [0146] or [0137]

# Context & Application
These quartets connect atonal pitch-class theory to triadic voice-leading analysis, demonstrating that "atonal pitch-class theory has the capacity to stimulate, undergird, or nuance" poststructural attributions of disjunction in nineteenth-century music (p. 131).

# Examples
- **Beethoven "Tempest" Sonata finale, mm. 8-12** (figure 5.26a): {g minor = 7, Eb major = 8, A major = 2, d minor = 4} = [0146]; a i-iv-bII-V6 progression
- **Wagner *Gotterdammerung* opening** (figure 5.26b): {eb minor = 7, Cb major = 8, db minor = 1, D major = 5} = [0137]; eb minor alternating with Cb major, db minor, and D major (Fate motive)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: The all-interval property applies to the pitch classes of the triads
  **Correction**: It applies to the voice-leading ZONES, not the pitch-class content

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Only [0146] or only [0137] works
  **Clarification**: Both all-interval tetrachord types can be realized as triadic quartets
