# Quick Definition
A linguistic phenomenon (Muysken 2000) where overlapping language populations share cognate terms (homophonous diamorphs) that facilitate code switching -- the musical parallel being the consonant triad as shared lexical item enabling smooth transition between diatonic and chromatic syntaxes.

# Core Definition
Where language populations overlap or associate through trade, colonization, or migration, "there often exist words that have similar form and meaning in both languages. These cognate terms, or homophonous diamorphs, are thought to 'cause, or at least facilitate, a codeswitch from one language to the other'" (Broersma and de Bot 2006, 2; Muysken 2000, 123, qtd. in Cohn, p. 220). Their function resembles pivot chords: "a dual-language zone intercedes between language 1 and language 2, so that 'it is very possible to know that a definite codeswitch has occurred even when we are unable to say at what point it occurred'" (Woolard 1998, 7-8, qtd. p. 220).

# Prerequisites
- **Code switching**: The broader phenomenon of alternating between systems
- **Homophonous diamorph**: The specific type of shared element enabling congruent lexicalization

# Key Properties
1. Shared lexical items bridge two systems
2. The pivot function creates fuzzy (not crisp) transition boundaries
3. The item performs distinct roles in each system
4. Functions as a seam through which one system slips into another
5. "The entire process is cognitively opaque, transacted beneath the horizon of awareness" (p. 221)

# Construction / Recognition
In the musical application:
- The consonant triad is the cognate term shared by both diatonic and chromatic syntaxes
- It has similar "form" (same sonic surface) and related "meaning" (harmonic function) in both
- It enables transition without a discrete switching point
- The triad "brings along her appropriately acculturated interlocutor without stumble" (p. 221)

Katherine Woolard's challenge: "alternation between rigidly discrete systems [is] a mythic ground of analysis that is as empirically and theoretically ill-founded as the idealized speaker/hearer" (1998, 6, qtd. p. 220).

# Context & Application
Congruent lexicalization explains the mechanism by which double syntax operates smoothly. The shared lexicon (consonant triads) means that syntactic switching requires no lexical change -- unlike the later evolutionary stages where lexical substitution (Tristan genus, hexatonic/octatonic scales) creates rougher transitions.

# Examples
- **Every consonant triad**: Functions as a congruent lexical item between diatonic and chromatic syntaxes
- **The c-sharp/d-flat minor triad** in Schubert's Scherzo (m. 123): Functions in both f minor's and d minor's diatonic capsules, enabling transition without discrete switch
- **Dominant seventh chords**: Similarly span both systems with appellative and voice-leading functions

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Congruent lexicalization means the two syntaxes are the same
  **Correction**: They share lexical items but organize them by fundamentally different principles

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Congruent lexicalization implies syntactic boundaries are nonexistent
  **Clarification**: It implies they are "fuzzy" rather than crisp, not that they are absent
