# Quick Definition
A linguistic phenomenon identified by Pieter Muysken where overlapping language populations share cognate terms (homophonous diamorphs) that facilitate code switching -- appropriated by Cohn to explain how consonant triads facilitate switching between diatonic and chromatic syntaxes.

# Formal Definition
Congruent lexicalization occurs where language populations overlap, share a significant geographic boundary, or associate through trade, colonization, or migration patterns. In such contexts, there often exist words that have similar form and meaning in both languages. These cognate terms, or homophonous diamorphs, are thought to "cause, or at least facilitate, a codeswitch from one language to the other" (Broersma and de Bot 2006, 2; see also Muysken 2000, 123). Their function resembles that of pivot chords in theories of modulation: "a dual-language zone intercedes between language 1 and language 2, so that 'it is very possible to know that a definite codeswitch has occurred even when we are unable to say at what point it occurred'" (Woolard 1998, 7-8).

# Construction/Recognition
In the musical application:
- The consonant triad is the cognate term shared by both diatonic and chromatic "languages"
- It has similar "form" (same sonic surface) and related "meaning" (harmonic function) in both systems
- It facilitates transition between syntactic modes without a discrete switching point
- The triad performs distinct roles in each syntax while functioning as a seam through which one system slips into the other

Katherine Woolard polemically responds that "alternation between rigidly discrete systems [is] a mythic ground of analysis that is as empirically and theoretically ill-founded as the idealized speaker/hearer" (1998, 6) -- challenging the assumption that syntactic boundaries are crisp.

# Musical Context
Congruent lexicalization is particularly relevant to the approach presented in the book because it explains how overdetermined objects enable seamless transitions between organizational systems. The consonant triad, as a congruent lexical item shared by both syntaxes, "brings along her appropriately acculturated interlocutor without stumble." The concept supports the argument that double syntax is cognitively feasible precisely because the two musical "languages" share their primary lexical units.

# Examples
- Every consonant triad functions as a congruent lexical item between diatonic and chromatic syntaxes
- The dominant seventh chord similarly spans both systems (appellative power in diatonic syntax, voice-leading object in chromatic syntax)
- The c#/db minor triad in Schubert's Scherzo (m. 123): Functions in both the diatonic capsule of f minor and the hexatonic alley, enabling transition without discrete switching point
- Alsatian/French bilingualism: Words existing in both languages facilitate the 15+ code switches in a single phone conversation

# Common Confusions
- Congruent lexicalization is not the same as simple bilingualism; it specifically concerns shared lexical items that bridge systems
- The musical application is analogical; music is not language, though cognitive parallels are well-supported
- The concept does not imply that syntactic boundaries are nonexistent, only that they are "fuzzy" rather than crisp
- Congruent lexicalization explains the mechanism of smooth transition, not the existence of double syntax itself
