# Quick Definition
The Hostinsky Tonnetz is an 1879 tonal network diagram that represents pitch-class neighborhoods by positioning consonant tones around a central pitch, with diagonal alignment of major and minor thirds similar to modern Tonnetz representations.

# Formal Definition
The **Hostinsky Tonnetz** (or *Tonschema*) is a tonal network from Czech theorist Ottokar Hostinsky's 1879 treatise *Die Lehre von den musikalischen Klängen*. It is a variation on Riemann's Tonnetz with diagonal positioning of major and minor thirds that echoes modern Tonnetz representations.

The diagram grows from a central pitch class:
1. Six edges radiate from the center, connecting to the six tones with which it is consonant
2. Six further edges form a hexagon binding those six tones to each other
3. The hexagons and radii together form six triangles representing the six triads containing the central pitch
4. Each of the six surrounding tones generates its own neighborhood, with overlapping hexagons extending outward

# Construction/Recognition
Structure of the Hostinsky Tonnetz:
- Central node: any pitch class (e.g., C)
- First tier: six consonant pitch classes (thirds and fifths above/below)
- Connections: edges represent consonant intervals (thirds and fifths)
- Triangles: each represents a major or minor triad
- Extension: process radiates outward indefinitely

The diagonal positioning allows:
- Horizontal axis: perfect fifths
- Diagonal axes: major and minor thirds
- Triangular triads: adjacency represents voice-leading proximity

# Musical Context
The Hostinsky Tonnetz provides a visualization framework for:
- Understanding pitch-class neighborhoods
- Tracking accumulation of pitch classes and dyads
- Analyzing pan-triadic harmonizations of static pitches
- Representing triadic assemblies of atomistic pitch-class components

The diagram's neutrality makes it suitable for:
- Music where triads are presented as dyads gradually completed
- Analysis where tonal hierarchy is ambiguous or contested
- Tracking systematic pitch-class aggregations independent of triadic constituency

# Examples
**Schubert's "Der Doppelganger"**: The Tonnetz adapts well to surfaces featuring dyads and individual tones. Rather than deciding whether the initial dyad signifies b minor or B major, the Tonnetz documents its presence and invites inference of plausible completions.

**Verdi's "Ah si, ben mio"**: The Tonnetz models pitch retention loops by showing the cyclical tour through a neighborhood's six triads.

**Brahms's Second Symphony development**: The synoptic Tonnetz model shows how the entire sixty-measure passage tours every triad in the double neighborhood (D and A retention loops) counterclockwise from subdominant to subdominant.

# Common Confusions
- The Hostinsky Tonnetz is historical (1879), predating modern neo-Riemannian adaptations
- It differs from Riemann's original Tonnetz in its diagonal orientation of thirds
- The Tonnetz is neutral regarding tonal centricity; it need not express or mandate key interpretations
- Triads appear as triangles, dyads as edges, individual pitches as vertices
