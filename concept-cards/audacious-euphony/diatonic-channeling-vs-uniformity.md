# Quick Definition
The fundamental tension between two forces governing melodic and harmonic motion: diatonic channeling (conforming to a governing scale) and the law of uniformity (exact transposition preserving interval sizes), whose interaction determines whether a passage remains diatonic or becomes chromatic.

# Formal Definition
Fetis (2008 [1844]) described how sequential patterns absorb the mind's attention so strongly that "any irregularity of tonality is not noticed ... The mind, absorbed in the contemplation of the progressive series, momentarily loses the feeling of tonality." In a diatonic sequence, the law of uniformity is kept in check by prior commitment to the diatonic scale: each pattern iteration replicates the generic intervals of its predecessor, but its specific intervals are "channeled within the banks of the diatonic scale." In chromatic sequences (hexatonic cycles, major-third divisions), "the law of uniformity has a monolithic force, and the rapid turnover of chromatic pitch classes ruptures any ability of the diatonic collection to hold a focus on a particular global tonic."

# Construction/Recognition
The binary distinction between diatonic and chromatic sequences is a particular case of a more general dynamic:
- **Diatonic channeling**: Motivic fragments conform to the locally governing scale; generic intervals are preserved but specific intervals adjust
- **Law of uniformity**: Motivic fragments preserve exact interval sizes across transposition, potentially rupturing diatonic control
- Later theorists formulate this same duality as **diatony vs. repetition** (Schenker 1906) and **magnetism vs. inertia** (Larson 1994)

The dynamic applies not only to sequences but to any situation where a motivic fragment migrates across transpositional levels, including fugal imitation.

# Musical Context
The tension between channeling and uniformity manifests directly in the relationship between consonant and augmented triads. When a major-third interval (e.g., C to E) is replicated starting on E, diatonic channeling produces E to G (consonant triad), while uniformity produces E to G# (augmented triad). This fulcrum separates two compositional logics.

# Examples
- **Fetis on diatonic sequences**: "The succession and ... movement fix the attention of the mind ... any irregularity of tonality is not noticed ... the mind, absorbed in the contemplation of the progressive series, momentarily loses the feeling of tonality"
- **Fetis on consonance/dissonance**: A diatonic sequence levels the distinction so that "a diminished fifth no longer requires resolution; in this context, its behavior is indistinguishable from that of the perfect fifth"
- **Hexatonic cycles** (Chapter 2): The law of uniformity operates with "monolithic force"
- **Schubert, Symphony No. 2, finale** (Figure 3.2): Three transpositionally identical phrases divide the octave by major thirds -- the law of uniformity unchecked by diatonic channeling

# Common Confusions
- Diatonic channeling and uniformity are not mutually exclusive; in diatonic sequences, both operate simultaneously with channeling constraining uniformity
- The distinction is not between "tonal" and "atonal" but between two modes of organization, both of which use consonant triads
- Chromatic sequences are not "lawless"; they follow the law of uniformity more purely than diatonic ones
- The tension is not unique to the 19th century but becomes foregrounded when composers privilege uniformity over diatonic channeling
