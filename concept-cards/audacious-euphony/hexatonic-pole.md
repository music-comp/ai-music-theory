# Quick Definition
A hexatonic pole is a triad diametrically opposite another in a hexatonic cycle, sharing no common tones but drawing from the same six-note hexatonic collection, connected by the H transformation.

# Formal Definition
Within a hexatonic cycle of six triads, any two triads three positions apart are hexatonic poles of each other. These triads are of opposite mode (one major, one minor), share zero common tones, yet together exhaust the six pitch classes of their hexatonic scale. The hexatonic pole relationship represents maximum distance within a hexatonic system while maintaining minimal voice-leading work (three semitones total).

# Construction/Recognition
Properties of hexatonic poles:
- Cyclic distance: 3 positions apart in hexatonic cycle
- Common tones: 0
- Voice-leading work: 3 semitones (each voice moves by semitone)
- Pitch-class union: 6 (complete hexatonic collection)
- Motion type: contrary (two voices one direction, one opposite)

Examples of hexatonic pole pairs:
- C major and ab minor (Northern system)
- E major and c minor (Northern system)
- Ab major and e minor (Northern system)
- Db major and a minor (Southern system)

# Musical Context
The hexatonic pole relationship creates a distinctive paradox: maximum harmonic "distance" (no common tones, mode change) achieved through minimal melodic motion (semitones in all voices). This paradox makes hexatonic poles particularly effective for depicting liminal or uncanny states in Romantic music - moments of transformation, death, or supernatural occurrence.

# Examples
From the text (p. 40-41, 49-50):
- Wagner's Tarnhelm progression: "aural sense of eerie power" and "radical disjunction... the motif seems almost to have fallen in from another world"
- Schubert Sanctus (Eb major Mass): Eb major to b minor at movement opening
- Wagner Parsifal: Grail distortion, same progression

The text notes: "The diametric progression... depicts a range of uncanny phenomena" including supernatural and death-related imagery in opera and concert music (p. 41).

# Common Confusions
- Hexatonic poles share NO common tones (unlike L and P relations, which share 2 common tones each)
- The "pole" is relational: every triad in a hexatonic system has exactly one hexatonic pole
- Each hexatonic system contains three hexatonic pole pairs
- Do not confuse with tonal "polar" keys (like tonic and dominant); hexatonic poles are defined by voice-leading geometry, not tonal function
