# Quick Definition
Two triads diametrically opposite in a hexatonic cycle, three positions apart, sharing no common tones but drawing from the same hexatonic collection, connected by the H transformation with all three voices moving by semitone in contrary motion.

# Core Definition
"Two intermediate triads on the hexatonic cycle are omitted, and all three voices move simultaneously by semitone, as in figure 2.3(c). I will describe this relation as a *hexatonic pole*, and the corresponding transformation with the label **H** (after Cook 1994)" (Cohn, p. 49). Within a hexatonic cycle, any two triads three positions apart are hexatonic poles. They are of opposite mode, share zero common tones, yet together exhaust the six pitch classes of their hexatonic scale. The relationship represents maximum distance within a hexatonic system while maintaining minimal individual voice-leading displacements.

# Prerequisites
- **hexatonic-cycle** — Hexatonic poles are defined by position within a hexatonic cycle

# Key Properties
1. Cyclic distance: 3 positions apart in hexatonic cycle
2. Common tones: 0
3. Voice-leading work: 3 semitones (each voice moves by semitone)
4. Mode: always opposite (major-minor pair)
5. Pitch-class union: 6 (complete hexatonic collection)
6. Motion type: contrary (balanced voice leading in all three voices)
7. Each hexatonic system contains 3 hexatonic pole pairs
8. Every triad has exactly one hexatonic pole

# Construction / Recognition
## To find a triad's hexatonic pole:
1. Identify the hexatonic cycle containing the triad
2. Count 3 positions around the cycle in either direction
3. The resulting triad is the hexatonic pole

Examples of hexatonic pole pairs:
- C major and ab minor
- E major and c minor
- Ab major and e minor
- Eb major and b minor (as in Schubert Sanctus and Parsifal)

# Context & Application
The hexatonic pole paradox: maximum harmonic "distance" (no common tones, mode change) achieved through minimal individual melodic motion (semitones in all voices). This paradox makes hexatonic poles particularly effective for depicting liminal or uncanny states. "The diametric progression at figure 2.3(c) depicts a range of uncanny phenomena" (p. 40).

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 49-50): Schubert, Sanctus from Eb major Mass (1828): Eb major to b minor at the opening.

**Example 2** (pp. 49-50): Wagner, Parsifal (1882): Grail distortion, same progression Eb major to b minor.

**Example 3** (p. 40): Uncanny depictions cited in Cohn 2004 include "Kundry's de-souling (*Parsifal*), the dead Siegfried shaking his fist (*Gotterdammerung*), Scarpia's murder (*Tosca*), Aase's arrival at St. Peter's Gates (*Peer Gynt*), Strauss's Salome singing to the severed head of Jochanaan, and Schoenberg's self-portrait in death (String Trio, Op. 45)."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming hexatonic poles share at least one common tone
  **Correction**: Hexatonic poles share ZERO common tones (the only triadic pair within a hexatonic system with this property)

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Confusing hexatonic "poles" with tonal poles (tonic-dominant)
  **Clarification**: Hexatonic poles are defined by voice-leading geometry, not tonal function
- **Confusion**: Thinking the hexatonic pole is a relationship between same-mode triads
  **Clarification**: Hexatonic poles are always opposite mode (one major, one minor)
