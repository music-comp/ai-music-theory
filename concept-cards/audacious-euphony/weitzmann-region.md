# Quick Definition
A six-triad group centered on an augmented triad, containing three major and three minor triads, where all pairs of triads stand exactly two voice-leading units apart.

# Core Definition
A Weitzmann region is one of four collections of six consonant triads that can be reached by displacing one tone of an augmented triad by one semitone. Each region "contains three major-third-related triads of the same species" paired with "a triplet featuring triads of the opposite species" (p. 77). Unlike hexatonic regions, "Weitzmann regions pair triads whose roots lie an odd number of semitones apart" (p. 77). The region is "a flat, uniform space: all of its triadic pairs stand exactly two voice-leading units apart. The triads can be cyclically ordered in several ways, but there is no ordering that is more natural than the others, from the standpoint of voice leading" (p. 78).

# Prerequisites
- **Augmented triad**: Each Weitzmann region is centered on one of four augmented triads
- **Weitzmann's six-triad cluster**: The historical antecedent of the formalized region

# Key Properties
1. Contains 6 consonant triads (3 major, 3 minor)
2. Centered on one of four augmented triads (CEG#, C#FA, DF#Bb, EbGB)
3. All internal triadic connections require exactly 2 voice-leading units
4. Major triads are related to each other by major third (T4)
5. Minor triads are related to each other by major third (T4)
6. Cross-modal connections shift the "center of balance" by two units (more unsettling than hexatonic cross-modal connections)
7. The augmented triad functions as a hub routing all intra-regional progressions

# Construction / Recognition
To identify which Weitzmann region a triad belongs to:
1. Take any consonant triad
2. Find the augmented triad reachable by moving one voice by semitone
3. That augmented triad defines the region
4. Example: C major (C-E-G) -> C augmented (C-E-G#) by raising G; CEG# region contains C major

The four regions:
- **CEG# region**: C major, E major, Ab major, a minor, c# minor, f minor
- **C#FA region**: C#/Db major, F major, A major, bb minor, d minor, f# minor
- **DF#Bb region**: D major, F#/Gb major, Bb major, b minor, eb minor, g minor
- **EbGB region**: Eb major, G major, B major, c minor, e minor, g# minor

# Context & Application
Weitzmann regions complement hexatonic regions as a second way of partitioning the 24 consonant triads into four groups of six. Both models pair major-third-related triads of the same species, but differ in how they pair cross-modal triads. Hexatonic regions pair triads with same roots or roots four semitones apart; Weitzmann regions pair triads whose roots differ by an odd number of semitones.

# Examples
**Schubert's Fourth Symphony** (pp. 80-81): Complete N/R cycle through one Weitzmann region (mm. 86-106).

**Schubert's Ninth Symphony trombone solo** (pp. 81-82): Complete N/R cycle traversing an entire region with dominant sevenths producing whole-tone scales.

**Richard Strauss's "Fruhling"** (pp. 98-100): First stanza organized around the GBEb region, second stanza shifts to the CEAb region.

**Wagner's Parsifal, Act 1** (pp. 97-98): Nonsequential exploration of a complete Weitzmann region.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming Weitzmann regions have natural cyclic ordering
  **Correction**: Unlike hexatonic regions, Weitzmann regions are flat spaces; no ordering is privileged by voice leading

- **Error**: Treating R as closer than N and S within a Weitzmann region
  **Correction**: All intra-regional moves require 2 voice-leading units; R's apparent proximity on the Tonnetz is an illusion

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Weitzmann regions and hexatonic regions partition triads the same way
  **Clarification**: They partition differently: hexatonic pairs same-root cross-modal triads; Weitzmann pairs odd-semitone-root cross-modal triads
- **Confusion**: Weitzmann regions are named for their constituent triads
  **Clarification**: They are named for their central augmented triad
