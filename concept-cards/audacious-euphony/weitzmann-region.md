# Quick Definition
A six-triad group centered on an augmented triad, containing three major and three minor triads related by major thirds, where all pairs of triads stand exactly two voice-leading units apart.

# Formal Definition
A Weitzmann region is one of four collections of six consonant triads that can be reached by displacing one tone of an augmented triad by one semitone. Each region contains three major triads whose roots form an augmented triad and three minor triads whose roots form another augmented triad. Unlike hexatonic regions, Weitzmann regions are flat, uniform spaces where all triadic pairs are equidistant (two semitones of voice-leading work).

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Each Weitzmann region:
- Contains 6 consonant triads (3 major, 3 minor)
- Is centered on one of four augmented triads (CEG#, C#FA, DGbBb, EbGB)
- Has all internal triadic connections requiring exactly 2 voice-leading units
- Major triads are related to each other by major third (T4)
- Minor triads are related to each other by major third (T4)
- Cross-modal connections involve whole-step motion in one voice, or two voices moving by semitone

# Musical Context/Application
Weitzmann regions pair triads whose roots lie an odd number of semitones apart, contrasting with hexatonic regions which pair triads with same roots or roots four semitones apart. The augmented triad serves as a hub through which all intra-regional progressions are routed. Composers from C.P.E. Bach (1763) to Richard Strauss (1949) explored Weitzmann regions with increasing thoroughness.

# Examples
- C major's Weitzmann region contains: C major, E major, Ab major, a minor, c# minor, f minor (all centered on CEG# augmented)
- Schubert's Fourth Symphony (1st mvt., mm. 86-106): Complete N/R cycle through one Weitzmann region
- Schubert's Ninth Symphony trombone solo: Complete N/R cycle traversing an entire region
- Richard Strauss's "Fruhling" (1949): Opening stanza organized around a complete Weitzmann region

# Common Confusions
- Unlike hexatonic regions which have natural cyclic ordering based on voice-leading proximity, Weitzmann regions are flat spaces with no inherent ordering
- The R transformation appears closer on the Tonnetz than other Weitzmann transformations, but this is an illusion; all intra-regional moves require 2 voice-leading units
- Weitzmann regions are named after Carl Friedrich Weitzmann (1808-1880), who first described these six-triad groups in 1853
