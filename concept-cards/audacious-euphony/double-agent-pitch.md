# Quick Definition
A pitch whose enharmonic respelling redirects its melodic energy from upward-pressing (dominant agent) to downward-pressing (subdominant agent) or vice versa, creating the central drama of the double-agent complex.

# Core Definition
A double agent is a pitch that "functions as a triadic third in two different harmonic contexts" within a double-agent complex (cf. p. 89). In one context it is spelled as a leading tone pressing upward; in the other it is spelled as a flatted scale degree pressing downward. Lewin's Cold War allegory captures the concept: "You enter... wearing your F# cloak, as leading tone to G; but you abruptly hurl the cloak away and reveal yourself in a suit underneath as Gb, upper neighbor to F" (2006, 107; p. 89). Cohn comments: "It is the story of the double agent. All is not what it seems. Or perhaps it is what it seems, in which case it is not what it seemed not to seem" (p. 90).

# Prerequisites
- **Agents (Harrison)**: The directional tendencies that the double agent redirects
- **Double-agent complex**: The four-triad structure in which the double agent operates
- **Mehrdeutigkeit**: The principle of multiple meaning that enables the double agent

# Key Properties
1. The double agent is a specific pitch, not a chord
2. It is the third of two different triads (the S-related exterior pair)
3. Its directional energy reverses through enharmonic respelling
4. The acoustic identity masks the functional inversion
5. "The redirection of an energized tone through enharmonic transformation is one of Vogler's principal genera of Mehrdeutigkeit" (p. 89)

# Construction / Recognition
Example: Bb/Cb as double agent
- As B-natural (third of G major): presses upward toward C (dominant agent)
- As Cb (third of ab minor): presses downward toward Bb (subdominant agent)
- The respelling occurs at the S connection between exterior triads

# Context & Application
Double agent pitches create some of the most rhetorically powerful moments in 19th-century music, where enharmonic respelling dramatically redirects melodic energy within a single voice.

# Examples
**Chopin, Nocturne Op. 9 No. 1** (pp. 88-89): Bbb (third of gb minor) transforms directly into A (third of F major) in the same voice. "I find that I need to sing the pitch continuously in order to convince myself that the two pitches are identified; and even then, it feels like a trick."

**Lewin on Beethoven's Fifth** (p. 89): "You enter wearing your F# cloak, as leading tone to G; but you abruptly hurl the cloak away and reveal yourself in a suit underneath as Gb."

**Schubert, Impromptu Op. 90 No. 2** (pp. 93-94): Ebb/D double agency at the measure following the half cadence.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the double agent as a chord or harmonic entity
  **Correction**: The double agent is a single pitch functioning as a triadic third in two contexts

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The double agent effect works without enharmonic respelling
  **Clarification**: The effect requires actual enharmonic respelling in notation, not just acoustic identity
