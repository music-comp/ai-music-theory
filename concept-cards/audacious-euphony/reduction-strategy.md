# Quick Definition
The reduction strategy handles dissonance by identifying the unique consonant triad embedded within a dissonant chord, treating the extra tone as a supplementary addition to an underlying triadic structure.

# Core Definition
**Reduction to a subset** is Rameau's strategy and method: "a dissonant harmony reduces to a consonant triad when the latter is uniquely contained in the former" (p. 159). In the *Traite* of 1722, Rameau generated dissonant harmonies by adding thirds above the fifth or below the root of a consonant triad. The procedure is simple when the triadic subset is unique (as with dominant and half-diminished sevenths), but indeterminate when no consonant triad is contained (diminished seventh) or multiple triads are contained (major and minor sevenths). The strategy underlies Cohn's treatment of dominant seventh chords as representing major triads (ignoring the seventh) and, more controversially, half-diminished seventh chords as representing minor triads (ignoring the putative root).

# Prerequisites
- **Consonant triad**: The strategy depends on identifying consonant triads embedded within larger sonorities
- **Four approaches to dissonance**: Understanding reduction as one of four complementary strategies

# Key Properties
1. Works only when a consonant triad is *uniquely* contained in the dissonant chord
2. Dominant seventh uniquely embeds a major triad; half-diminished uniquely embeds a minor triad
3. Diminished seventh chords contain no consonant triads; major/minor sevenths contain multiple -- both are problematic for reduction
4. The dualist interpretation of half-diminished as minor triad + under-seventh was "hardy enough to survive well into the twentieth century" before being overcome by "renewed third-stacking monolithy" (p. 160)

# Construction / Recognition
1. Take a dissonant chord and identify all consonant triadic subsets
2. If exactly one consonant triad is found, reduce the chord to that triad
3. The remaining tone(s) are treated as supplementary dissonance (over-seventh or under-seventh)
4. If no unique subset exists, the reduction strategy does not apply

Application by chord type:
- **Dominant seventh** (G-B-D-F): unique subset G-B-D (G major) with over-seventh F
- **Half-diminished seventh** (B-D-F-A): unique subset D-F-A (D minor) with under-seventh B
- **Diminished seventh**: no consonant subset -- strategy fails
- **Major/minor seventh**: multiple subsets -- strategy indeterminate

# Context & Application
Reduction broadens the motivic network of pieces like *Parsifal* by revealing hexatonic pole relationships embedded in dissonant formations. When the consonant-triadic subset is tracked through dissonant surfaces, voice-leading connections emerge that stacked-third analysis would miss. Cohn develops this extensively for Wagner's late music, showing how the under-seventh interpretation illuminates passages in *Tristan*, *Parsifal*, and the *Ring*.

# Examples
- **Tristan final cadence**: Minor subdominant receives added major sixth (D#), creating a "half-diminished" sonority where "the 'seventh' of that chord, B, does not resolve, nor does it feel any pressure to do so. It is the putative root, C#, that is transient: as an under-seventh, it passes stepwise upward to the third of the resolution chord" (p. 161)
- **Tristan act 3 opening**: Bb minor triad with added G; "there is little temptation to hear this as a passing formation, as the G does not evidently issue from F, and it endures for three very slow beats" (p. 161)
- **Parsifal Communion theme**: Appears both as triadic and with timbrally isolated under-seventh, showing Wagner treated minor triads and their half-diminished supersets as interchangeable (p. 162)
- **Figure 7.2**: Six chords with uniquely embedded consonant triads, presented in inversionally related pairs (p. 159)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming all seventh chords can be reduced to a unique triadic subset
  **Correction**: Diminished sevenths (no consonant subset) and major/minor sevenths (multiple subsets) are not amenable to this strategy

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the reduction strategy is identical to standard third-stacking
  **Clarification**: Reduction identifies the unique consonant *subset*, which may reverse standard root assignments (e.g., Bø7 reduces to D minor, not B diminished)
- **Confusion**: Believing the dualist analysis of half-diminished chords is merely theoretical
  **Clarification**: Cohn presents musical evidence from Wagner showing the minor triad as perceptually and functionally primary, supported by recent empirical work (Parncutt 1989)
