# Quick Definition
The reduction strategy handles dissonance by identifying a consonant triad uniquely embedded within a dissonant chord, treating the dissonant tone as an addition to an underlying triadic structure.

# Formal Definition
**Reduction to a subset** is a strategy for incorporating dissonant harmonies into triadic theory. The method involves:
1. Identifying when a consonant triad is uniquely contained within a dissonant harmony
2. Reducing the dissonance to its consonant subset
3. Treating the additional tone(s) as supplementary dissonance

The strategy derives from Rameau's Traite (1722), where dissonant harmonies are generated by adding thirds above the fifth or below the root of a consonant triad.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Application by chord type:
- **Dominant seventh** (G-B-D-F): unique subset G-B-D (major triad) → reduce to G major with added seventh
- **Half-diminished seventh** (B-D-F-A): unique subset D-F-A (minor triad) → reduce to D minor with added sixth/under-seventh
- **Diminished seventh**: no unique consonant subset (contains no consonant triad)
- **Major/minor seventh**: multiple consonant subsets (reduction indeterminate)

The crucial condition: the dissonant chord must contain exactly one consonant triad as a subset.

# Musical Context/Application
Reduction is appropriate when:
- The dissonant tone functions as supplementary color
- The consonant subset is perceptually or functionally primary
- Voice-leading continuity depends on the embedded triad

The approach supports:
- Analysis of hexatonic poles embedded in dissonant formations
- Understanding seventh chords as triads with additions
- Tracking triadic voice-leading through dissonant surfaces

Historical support from dualist theory:
- Oettingen and Riemann: half-diminished = minor triad generated downward from fifth, with under-seventh
- Recent empirical work (Parncutt 1989) provides perceptual support for this hearing

# Examples
**Wagner's Tristan und Isolde, final cadence**: Minor subdominant (f# minor) receives added major sixth (D#) creating a "ø7" sonority. The "seventh" (C#) passes upward to the third of B major. The progression behaves like a plagal cadence with rising under-seventh, not a half-diminished seventh chord resolution.

**Wagner's Tristan, act 3 opening**: Bb minor triad with added G. The G endures for three slow beats without evident passing function; it is registrally and timbrally isolated from the Bb minor triad.

**Wagner's Parsifal, horn motive**: The Communion theme appears both as triadic and with timbrally isolated under-seventh, suggesting Wagner treated minor triads and their ø7 supersets as interchangeable.

**Wagner's Tarnhelm motive**: Initial g# minor → e minor sometimes adds under-seventh to the initial chord, treating the minor triad and half-diminished as variants.

# Common Confusions
- Reduction reverses standard third-stacking: the "root" of a ø7 becomes a dissonant addition, not a generating bass
- Not all dissonances reduce to unique triadic subsets; diminished sevenths and major/minor sevenths are problematic
- Reduction is an analytical strategy, not a claim about compositional process
- The strategy works best when musical context supports hearing the triad as primary
