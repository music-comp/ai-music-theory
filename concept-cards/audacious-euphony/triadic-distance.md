# Quick Definition
The measurement of relatedness between two consonant triads, which can be calculated using different metrics (root-interval, common-tone, or voice-leading) that may yield conflicting judgments.

# Formal Definition
Triadic distance quantifies the proximity or remoteness between two consonant triads. Chapter 1 identifies three historical approaches to calculating this distance:
1. Root-interval: distance between roots on the circle of fifths
2. Common-tone: number of shared pitch classes (0, 1, or 2)
3. Voice-leading: aggregate semitonal displacement under idealized voice leading

These metrics are not equivalent and can produce contradictory proximity judgments. The classical tradition privileges root-interval (and implicitly diatonic) distance, while 19th-century theory increasingly valued common-tone and voice-leading metrics.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
For triads X and Y:

| Metric | Formula | Range |
|--------|---------|-------|
| Root-interval | Fifths between roots | 0-6 |
| Common-tone | \|X ∩ Y\| | 0-2 |
| Voice-leading | Σ semitonal motion | 1-6 |

The metrics can conflict:
- C major to G major: root-close (1 fifth), common-tone medium (1), voice-leading medium (4)
- C major to c minor: root-identical (0 fifths), common-tone close (2), voice-leading close (1)
- C major to E major: root-distant (4 fifths), common-tone medium (1), voice-leading close (2)

# Musical Context/Application
Understanding multiple distance metrics helps explain:
- Why 19th-century music sounds coherent despite violating diatonic expectations
- How composers created effects of both continuity and surprise
- Why chromatic third relations became prominent
- The aesthetic of "audacious euphony" - distant by one metric, close by another

The choice of metric reflects different conceptions of what makes harmonies "related" and different historical/stylistic contexts.

# Common Confusions
- There is no single "true" triadic distance - different metrics capture different aspects of relatedness
- A progression can be simultaneously close and distant depending on the metric applied
- Textbook descriptions of "close" and "remote" keys typically assume the root-interval/diatonic metric without acknowledging alternatives
- The "law of least motion" conflates common-tone and voice-leading approaches, but they can diverge
