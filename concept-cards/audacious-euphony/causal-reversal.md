# Quick Definition
The phenomenon where the direction of causality, subordination, or orientation between musical elements reverses, as when a tone changes from being a neighbor to being the main note, or when a triad changes from being hierarchically subordinate to tonic to being hierarchically superordinate to its constituent tones.

# Formal Definition
Causal reversal describes the flip in hierarchical relationships that accompanies syntactic switching. In diatonic syntax, individual tones are subordinate to triads, which are subordinate to tonic; causality flows inward. In chromatic syntax, individual tones take meaning from triads, which take meaning from the chromatic collection; causality flows outward. The reversal is captured by the "arrow reversal" metaphor: "It is as if at one moment the person wears the garment and at the next the garment wears the person."

Crucially, causal reversal can be cognitively opaque - occurring beneath conscious awareness. Evidence from quotidian experience: "On Tuesday, you drive your car to town for a tune-up. On Wednesday, you drive your car to town so that you can get your hair cut. In the first case, you take the car... In the second case, the car takes you... Yet, from a cognitive standpoint, one hardly notices the difference."

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Musical manifestations of causal reversal:
- G is neighbor to Ab -> Ab is neighbor to G (Parsifal opening)
- Eb major is dominant of Ab -> Ab major is subdominant of Eb
- Arsis and thesis flip: Strong beats become weak, weak become strong
- Triad as acoustic object (subordinate to tonic) -> Triad as voice-leading object (superordinate to tones)

The garment analogy:
- Clothing for warmth: Garment serves person (person wears garment)
- Clothing for adornment: Person serves social function (garment wears person)
- Same physical act (dressing), different causal direction

"These reversals are insidious; they float below the threshold of consciousness unless one exerts some effort through analysis."

# Musical Context/Application
Causal reversal helps explain why:
- Double syntax encounters deep resistance (it suggests fundamental reorientation)
- Switching between syntaxes can nonetheless feel seamless (reversal is opaque)
- Composers can exploit both directions within a single phrase
- Analysts may disagree about hierarchical relationships

"A cunning composer or crafty performer knows how to take advantage of the opacity of causal reversal, to flip the arrow of causality without rocking a listener's boat."

# Examples
- Parsifal opening: G/Ab neighbor relationship reverses; Eb/Ab dominant relationship reverses
- Schubert C major Symphony Scherzo: At m. 117, tones cease orienting toward C major tonic and begin orienting toward chromatic collection
- Diatonic sequence: During Fortspinnung, arrow temporarily flips outward, then back at cadence
- Any overdetermined chord serving as pivot between syntactic modes

# Common Confusions
- Causal reversal is not ambiguity; it is determinate change of direction
- The reversal is often not audible as a discrete event; it emerges through context
- Opacity does not mean reversal is insignificant; it means it operates beneath awareness
- The quotidian analogies are structural, not claiming music is like driving
