# Quick Definition
The triad is a homophonous diamorph: a single sound with two distinct forms - it is simultaneously an optimal acoustic structure (consonant) and an optimal voice-leading structure (nearly even), providing two independent reasons for its centrality in Western music.

# Formal Definition
Borrowing from linguistic code-switching theory, Cohn describes the triad as a "homophonous diamorph" - one sound, two forms (p. 59). The triad's dual nature means that "there are two distinct, independent reasons for selecting major and minor triads as primary structures on which to build a musical syntax" (p. 59): acoustic consonance and near evenness. This dual potential constitutes a case of overdetermination, where multiple independent factors converge on the same object.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
The two "forms" of the triad:

1. As consonant object:
- Acoustic foundation (harmonic series, just intonation)
- Generates diatonic syntax
- Basis for Riemann's functions, Schenker's Ursatze, etc.

2. As nearly even object:
- Voice-leading foundation (minimal perturbation of augmented triad)
- Generates pan-triadic syntax
- Basis for hexatonic cycles, chromatic progressions

Tymoczko's Deist parable (p. 59-60): If God offered a suitcase of chords optimized for either voice-leading efficiency OR acoustic consonance, "he would hand you the very same chords, no matter which choice you made."

# Musical Context/Application
The homophonous diamorph concept explains why triadic music can operate in two different syntactic modes - diatonic (consonance-based) and pan-triadic (voice-leading-based) - while using the same sonic objects. The transformation between these modes typically occurs "well beneath the threshold of awareness," like the multiple functions of human organs (eating, talking, breathing with the mouth).

# Examples
From the text (p. 58-60):
On overdetermination in other domains:
- "Mouths are for eating, talking, and breathing"
- "Ears serve auditory and vestibular functions"
- "Male urethrae channel both excretory and reproductive fluids"

In music theory, the phenomenon is called "overdetermination," "robustness," or "Babylonianism" - "the use of multiple means of determination to 'triangulate' on the existence and character of a common phenomenon" (Wimsatt 1981).

# Common Confusions
- The two "forms" are not audibly distinct - the triad sounds the same regardless of which syntactic role it plays
- The diamorph is not about major vs. minor, but about consonant-triad-as-acoustic-object vs. nearly-even-trichord-as-voice-leading-object
- Recognition of the dual nature does not mean composers consciously chose one mode or the other; the transformation may be unconscious
- The coincidence of near evenness and consonance in triads is mathematically contingent, not necessary
