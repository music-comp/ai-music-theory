# Quick Definition
The central, most harmonically active section of a sonata-form development, where hexatonic cycles frequently appear in late-18th-century practice -- providing a structural home for pan-triadic progressions within an otherwise diatonically tonal framework.

# Core Definition
The developmental core (Caplin 1998's "core," Hepokoski and Darcy 2006's "central action zone") is the portion of a development section where the most intensive harmonic activity occurs, typically featuring modulation through multiple key areas and thematic fragmentation. Cohn observes that "the earliest hexatonic cycles in the classical repertoire characteristically appear in developmental cores" (p. 42), where their harmonically open, texturally diverse character suits tonal exploration. These passages exhibit layered tonality: "classically tonal at both the most global and the most local level but chromatic at the level of the local modulations" (Satyendra 1992, qtd. p. 42).

# Prerequisites
- **Hexatonic Cycle**: The cyclic structure that appears within developmental cores

# Key Properties
1. Harmonically open (not closed on the same key)
2. Texturally diverse (thematic fragmentation, stretti, motivic development)
3. Tonicizing dissonances "buffer the stations of the cycle" (p. 42)
4. Essential enharmonic transformation occurs within the passage
5. The passage typically initiates from one key area and arrives at another
6. Diatonic indeterminacy resides in the succession of tonics, not in the surface

# Construction / Recognition
Characteristics of hexatonic developmental cores:
- Located in the central portion of a development section
- Modulates through multiple key areas related by major third
- Local tonicizations buffer the hexatonic stations
- Essential enharmonicism distributes across the passage
- Each station locally diatonic, succession globally chromatic

# Context & Application
The developmental core provides a natural home for hexatonic progressions because its function already demands departure from the tonic and exploration of remote harmonic territory. In early examples (late 18th century), hexatonic progressions are embedded within conventional formal functions; in later 19th-century practice, they appear more freely. The "interior of a Classical sonata-form development... characteristically projects tonal instability and mounting tension, frequently through one or more sequences that sustain an upshift trajectory" (p. 150).

# Examples
- **Mozart, Symphony K. 543, finale, mm. 109-126**: "The excerpt initiates the developmental core of a monothematic sonata-form movement. Beginning in Ab major with a partial quotation of the principal theme, the music proceeds... through a hexatonic cycle" (p. 42)
- **Haydn, Symphony No. 98, finale, mm. 148-198**: "the passage likewise constitutes the developmental core of a final movement" -- series of four-measure thematic segments traversing a hexatonic cycle (p. 44)
- **Beethoven, "Tempest" Sonata, development**: compressed upshift bracketed by downshifts (p. 150)
- **Beethoven, "Waldstein" Sonata, development**: T5 chain accelerates through the core (p. 150)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: All developmental cores contain hexatonic progressions
  **Correction**: The term designates a formal function, not a harmonic requirement

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The hexatonic progression operates at the musical surface
  **Clarification**: It is typically at the middleground level; the surface features diatonic tonicizations

- **Confusion**: "Development" in the general sense is the same as "developmental core"
  **Clarification**: The core refers specifically to the most harmonically active portion
