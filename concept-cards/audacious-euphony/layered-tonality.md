# Quick Definition
Music that is classically tonal at both the most global and most local levels but chromatic at the level of local modulations, with diatonic indeterminacy residing in the succession of tonics.

# Core Definition
Ramon Satyendra's (1992) concept of layered tonality describes a common situation in 19th-century chromatic music where different structural levels operate according to different syntactic principles. "It is characteristic of these chronologically early examples that tonicizing dissonances buffer the stations of the cycle, which are presented at a thinly veiled layer of the middleground. Like the Schubert excerpt examined in chapter 1, each of these excerpts instantiates what Ramon Satyendra (1992) calls layered tonality: classically tonal at both the most global and the most local level but chromatic at the level of the local modulations" (p. 42). The essential enharmonic transformation required by hexatonic cycles is symptomatic of this layered condition.

# Prerequisites
- **Hexatonic Cycle**: The chromatic middleground structure
- **Diatonic Indeterminacy**: The condition at the middleground level

# Key Properties
1. Background level: Tonal (clear global tonic)
2. Middleground level: Chromatic (hexatonic or pan-triadic)
3. Foreground level: Tonal (local tonicizations with diatonic buffering)
4. Tonicizing dissonances "buffer the stations of the cycle" (p. 42)
5. Essential enharmonic transformation required
6. Local cadences confirm each station as a tonic

# Construction / Recognition
Structure of layered tonality:
- Background: global tonic established
- Middleground: succession of local tonics follows chromatic logic (e.g., hexatonic cycle)
- Foreground: each station is locally diatonic, confirmed by tonicizing dissonances and cadences

Characteristic of chronologically early hexatonic examples (late 18th/early 19th century).

# Context & Application
Layered tonality describes the analytical situation in many early hexatonic examples, where composers embed pan-triadic progressions within a diatonic framework. This creates the characteristic sound of "Romantic chromaticism" -- locally tonal, globally tonal, but chromatically adventurous at the level of modulation. In later 19th-century practice, the layers may become more thoroughly chromatic.

# Examples
- **Mozart, Symphony K. 543, finale, mm. 109-126** (p. 42-44): Developmental core with hexatonic cycle buffered by tonicizing dissonances
- **Haydn, Symphony No. 98, finale, mm. 148-198** (p. 44): Four-measure thematic segments traversing a hexatonic cycle
- **Beethoven, "Spring" Sonata Op. 24, 2nd mvt.** (p. 44-45): Block-transposed lyrical material through hexatonic stations
- **Schubert, Piano Trio Op. 100, 1st mvt.** (p. 45): Three phrases each modulating down a major third

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Layered tonality means bitonality or polytonality
  **Correction**: There is one governing tonic at the global level; the "layers" are structural levels, not simultaneous strata

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The "layers" are heard simultaneously like polyphonic layers
  **Clarification**: They are analytical levels (foreground/middleground/background), experienced as a single unified progression

- **Confusion**: All chromatic music exhibits layered tonality
  **Clarification**: Some music is chromatic at all levels; layered tonality specifically describes the combination of diatonic surface with chromatic middleground
