# Quick Definition
Layered tonality describes music that is classically tonal at both the most global and most local levels but chromatic at the level of local modulations, with diatonic indeterminacy residing in the succession of tonics.

# Formal Definition
Ramon Satyendra's (1992) concept of layered tonality describes a common situation in nineteenth-century chromatic music where different structural levels operate according to different syntactic principles. The foreground and background may be diatonically tonal, while the middleground (the succession of local key areas) follows pan-triadic or chromatic logic. The essential enharmonic transformation required by hexatonic cycles is symptomatic of this layered condition.

# Construction/Recognition
Structure of layered tonality:
- Background level: Tonal (clear global tonic)
- Middleground level: Chromatic (hexatonic or pan-triadic)
- Foreground level: Tonal (local tonicizations with diatonic buffering)

Characteristic features:
- Tonicizing dissonances "buffer the stations of the cycle" (p. 43)
- Essential enharmonic transformation required (e.g., g# minor = ab minor)
- Local cadences confirm each station as a tonic
- The succession of tonics follows chromatic logic

# Musical Context/Application
Layered tonality describes the analytical situation in many chronologically early hexatonic examples (late 18th/early 19th century), where composers embed pan-triadic progressions within a diatonic framework. This creates the characteristic sound of "Romantic chromaticism" - locally tonal, globally tonal, but chromatically adventurous at the level of modulation.

# Examples
From the text (p. 43):
"It is characteristic of these chronologically early examples that tonicizing dissonances buffer the stations of the cycle, which are presented at a thinly veiled layer of the middleground."

Specific examples analyzed:
- Mozart, Symphony K. 543, finale, mm. 109-126 (p. 43-44)
- Haydn, Symphony No. 98, finale, mm. 148-198 (p. 44)
- Beethoven, "Spring" Sonata Op. 24, 2nd movement (p. 44-45)
- Schubert, Piano Trio Op. 100, 1st movement (p. 45)

Each excerpt "instantiates what Ramon Satyendra (1992) calls layered tonality: classically tonal at both the most global and the most local level but chromatic at the level of the local modulations" (p. 43).

# Common Confusions
- Layered tonality is not "bitonal" or "polytonal" - there is one governing tonic at the global level
- The "layers" are analytical levels (foreground/middleground/background), not simultaneous strata
- Not all chromatic music exhibits layered tonality; some is chromatic at all levels
- The concept describes analytical situation, not compositional intent
