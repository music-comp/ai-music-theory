# Quick Definition
Lewin's transformations representing dominant and subdominant relations: DOM (equivalent to T5) indicates a chord "becomes the dominant of" its target; SUBD (T7) indicates a chord "becomes the subdominant of" its target. Unlike L, P, R, these are directional and not their own inverses.

# Formal Definition
David Lewin (1982, 1987) defined DOM as equivalent to T5: the source chord "becomes the dominant of" the target chord. SUBD, its inverse, is equivalent to T7 = T-5: the source chord "becomes the subdominant of" the target chord. These transformations capture not only the syntactic flow of tonal harmony but also the semantic charge associated with classical tonality - the forces of summoning, leading, gravity, magnetism, attraction, and desire. The single-headed arrows in Rings's model indicate these transformations work in only one direction; they are not involutions.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
- DOM: T5 (root descends by fifth / ascends by fourth)
  - C major DOM yields F major (C major "becomes dominant of" F major)
- SUBD: T7 = T-5 (root ascends by fifth)
  - C major SUBD yields G major (C major "becomes subdominant of" G major)

Key distinction from Neo-Riemannian transformations:
- L, P, R are involutions (applying twice returns to origin)
- DOM and SUBD are not involutions; they have distinct inverses

According to Lewin's definition: "G major is not the dominant of C minor, even though musicians frequently refer to that relation in those terms" - DOM preserves mode.

# Musical Context/Application
Lewin proposed dividing DOM into two MED (mediant) transformations, each taking the root down a diatonic third. Inversely, SUBD divides into two SUBM (submediant) transformations. This creates potential for modeling third relations within a tonal framework.

However, integrating mediants into Rings's model creates complications:
- c minor to Ab major projects both eastward (hexatonic) and southward (toward subdominant f minor)
- This forces arbitrary choices or cultivates ambiguity

Lewin (1992) suggested c minor to Ab major is heard as L with Amfortas's suffering in Parsifal but as MED otherwise - but this offers little guidance in general cases.

# Common Confusions
- DOM does not simply mean "dominant"; it is a transformation indicating the source becomes dominant of the target
- These transformations preserve mode; G major is DOM of C major but not of C minor
- The mediants (MED, SUBM) are not equivalent to L, P, R; they measure diatonic thirds, not chromatic common-tone relationships
- Single-headed arrows indicate non-involutory nature; applying DOM twice yields T10, not identity
