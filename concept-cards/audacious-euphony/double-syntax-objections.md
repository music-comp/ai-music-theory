# Quick Definition
The set of objections to the double syntax hypothesis from Dahlhaus, Smith, and Lerdahl -- ontological (music must be unified) and cognitive (switching is implausible) -- and Cohn's three-part response via bilingualism evidence, quotidian cognitive opacity, and historical framework.

# Core Definition
Objections come in two forms (pp. 217-218):
1. **Ontological/aesthetic** (Smith 1986): "Music of high aesthetic value is organically unified and hence cannot be generated from multiple sources." Smith: "I cannot believe that any chromatic master conceived of his musical terrain as so partitioned; I hear no grinding of gears."
2. **Epistemological/cognitive** (Dahlhaus 1967, Lerdahl 2001): "The supposition that the musical hearing switches between tonal and non-tonal apprehension during a composition or a phrase would be problematic" (Dahlhaus). Lerdahl: "implausible as a psychological position."

Cohn's three-part response (pp. 219-225):
1. **Bilingualism**: Smith's appeal to autonomous languages is empirically false; most humans code-switch routinely
2. **Quotidian cognitive opacity**: Causal reversal is opaque in everyday life
3. **Historical framework**: Double syntax provides the only plausible account of how Mozart-era music became Webern-era music

# Prerequisites
- **Double syntax hypothesis**: Must understand the claim being challenged

# Key Properties
1. Convergence from three independent intellectual traditions suggests the hypothesis "touches a broad cultural nerve"
2. Neither Dahlhaus nor Lerdahl presents empirical evidence; they rely on common sense
3. The organic unity metaphor has become historically bracketed but still works "subliminally"
4. The three-part response addresses the objections from different angles

# Construction / Recognition
The convergence is significant: "Dahlhaus, Smith, and Lerdahl evidently voice their objections to double syntax independently of one another and converge on those objections at three distinct historical moments, from three distinctive eras and intellectual traditions" (p. 218).

# Context & Application
The objections frame the rest of Chapter 9: code switching (responding to Smith), cognitive opacity (responding to Dahlhaus/Lerdahl), and the soft revolution (the historical argument that makes double syntax necessary).

# Common Errors
- **Error**: The convergent objections constitute empirical evidence against double syntax
  **Correction**: They represent cultural consensus, not empirical findings; "Neither Dahlhaus nor Lerdahl presents evidence"

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: "No grinding of gears" is a cognitive finding
  **Clarification**: It is an aesthetic judgment about how music sounds, not a claim about cognitive processes
