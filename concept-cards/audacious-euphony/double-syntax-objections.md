# Quick Definition
The set of objections to the double syntax hypothesis from Dahlhaus, Smith, and Lerdahl - ontological (music must be unified), cognitive (switching is implausible) - and Cohn's three-part response via bilingualism evidence, quotidian cognitive opacity, and historical framework.

# Formal Definition
Objections to double syntax come in two forms:

1. **Ontological/aesthetic** (Smith 1986): "High-value music cannot partake of two systemic modes of organization." Music of aesthetic value is organically unified; Proctor's approach "suggests the separateness of chromatic and diatonic tonalities... I cannot believe that any chromatic master conceived of his musical terrain as so partitioned; I hear no grinding of gears."

2. **Epistemological/cognitive** (Dahlhaus 1967, Lerdahl 2001): "The supposition that the musical hearing switches between tonal and non-tonal apprehension during a composition or a phrase would be problematic." Switching is "implausible as a psychological position" for music that "moves smoothly between diatonicism and chromaticism even within a single phrase."

The convergence of these objections across three distinct intellectual traditions suggests double syntax "touches a broad cultural nerve."

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Cohn's three-part response:

1. **Bilingualism evidence**: Smith's appeal to "rigidly discrete, autonomous" languages is empirically false. Most humans "alternate between different linguistic varieties at the drop of a hat" (Gardner-Chloros 2009). Code switching occurs beneath awareness; speakers are "surprised at their own performance if you play it back to them."

2. **Quotidian cognitive opacity**: Causal inversion is cognitively opaque in everyday life. "Do you want money to get power, or power to get money?" These reversals "float below the threshold of consciousness unless one exerts some effort through analysis."

3. **Historical framework**: Double syntax provides the only plausible account of how music of the age of Mozart became music of the age of Webern. The alternative - tabula rasa emergence - is "inconsistent with everything else we know about historical process and human cognition."

# Musical Context/Application
The objections reflect postwar music theory's commitment to organic unity:
- "Although that metaphor has become epistemically and historically bracketed in the last twenty years... I suspect that it nonetheless continues to subliminally work on the instinctive sensibilities of music theorists"

The objections also reflect intuitions about cognitive simplicity:
- Neither Dahlhaus nor Lerdahl presents evidence; they rely on "some species of common sense"
- But "there are other sorts of common sense than those to which Dahlhaus and Lerdahl implicitly appeal"

# Examples
- Smith (1986) responding to Proctor (1978): "virtually distinct languages" objection
- Dahlhaus (1967): "problematic" to suppose switching between apprehension modes
- Lerdahl (2001): "implausible as a psychological position"
- Gardner-Chloros (2009): Alsatian office worker switching 15+ times in rapid speech without interrupting flow

# Common Confusions
- The objections are not about whether chromatic progressions exist but whether they constitute a separate syntax
- "No grinding of gears" is an aesthetic judgment, not a cognitive finding
- The objections assume conscious awareness is necessary for syntactic processing
- Convergent objections suggest cultural consensus, not empirical truth
