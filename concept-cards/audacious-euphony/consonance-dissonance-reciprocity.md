# Quick Definition
Consonance/dissonance reciprocity is the condition where the normally asymmetric (diodic) relationship between consonant and dissonant harmonies becomes fluid, allowing consonant triads to subordinate to dissonant augmented triads in certain compositional contexts.

# Core Definition
In classical tonality, consonant harmonies provide the stable framework within which dissonant harmonies operate -- a one-directional ("diodic") relationship. However, as Robert P. Morgan showed in "Dissonant Prolongation" (1976), "there are situations where these priorities are reversed, and consonant triads subordinate to dissonant ones, not only locally but across spans of significant duration." Cohn argues that "the relation between consonance and dissonance, then, is fluid in principle. The potential for this fluidity opens up a compositional dynamic, where a terrain of fixed relations is transformed into a site for negotiation. Consonant and augmented triads gain the potential, in principle, for reciprocity" (p. 64).

# Prerequisites
- **Consonant triad**: Must understand the normative stability of major and minor triads to grasp how reciprocity challenges their priority
- **Augmented triad**: The principal dissonant sonority involved in reciprocal relationships with consonant triads

# Key Properties
1. The classical consonance/dissonance relationship is "diodic" (one-directional): consonance provides the framework for dissonance
2. Reciprocity makes this relationship fluid, opening a "site for negotiation"
3. The augmented triad can function as a structural center even when not sounded as a surface verticality
4. Reciprocity requires "external forces" such as uniformity and symmetry (Fetis) to level the inherent asymmetry
5. The scale between consonance and dissonance is "inherently out of balance and can only be leveled through the application of external forces" (p. 63)

# Construction / Recognition
Five types of reciprocity identified in 19th-century theory:
1. **Fifth-related triads** (Sechter 1853, following Kirnberger): C serves as dominant to F, which serves as subdominant to C -- recursive indeterminacy requiring external resolution
2. **Relative major/minor** (C major / a minor): Tonal indeterminacy focused at the whole-step melodic fulcrum
3. **Leittonwechsel** (C major / e minor): Indeterminacy focused at the semitone fulcrum; the leading-tone relationship is bidirectional in German theory
4. **Nebenverwandt** (C major / f minor, per Weitzmann): Reciprocal leading-tone energies divided between the agents
5. **Consonant/augmented triad**: The case explored in Chapter 3, where augmented triads can function as structural centers around which consonant triads are organized

# Context & Application
Consonance/dissonance reciprocity enables analysis of passages where consonant triads orbit around an augmented triad that never sounds as a surface verticality. The concept is central to understanding how chromatic passages in Schubert, Liszt, Rimsky-Korsakov, and Faure organize harmonies around augmented triadic centers rather than diatonic tonal ones.

# Examples
**Schubert, Symphony No. 2, finale** (Figure 3.2, pp. 63-64): "The core of the development consists of three transpositionally identical phrases that divide the octave by major thirds... If prolongation is at work in this passage in any form, then its more plausible object is the FAC# triad that unites the three tonics."

**Liszt, Faust Symphony opening** (pp. 69-70): "Consonance and dissonance [are] inverted with respect to formal function (but not metric location): the position of preparation and resolution is occupied by dissonant augmented triads; that of the suspension, by consonant minor triads."

**Schubert, "Die Stadt"** (p. 63): "A dissonant harmony achieves a quasi stability by squatting like a brute and appropriating the rhetorical garments normally reserved for consonances (first, last, loudest, longest)."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking reciprocity means consonance and dissonance are "the same"
  **Correction**: The scale is inherently imbalanced; reciprocity requires external forces to level it

- **Error**: Assuming the augmented triad must sound as a surface harmony to be structurally central
  **Correction**: "The center of a circle is equally orienting to a set of dancers, whether marked by a pole, a hole, or the imagination of the dancers" (p. 74)

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Reciprocity is a perceptual reality in all contexts
  **Clarification**: It is a compositional possibility requiring specific conditions (sequential uniformity, hexatonic structure, etc.)
- **Confusion**: Reciprocity is unique to augmented triads
  **Clarification**: Cohn identifies five types of reciprocity, four involving pairs of consonant triads
