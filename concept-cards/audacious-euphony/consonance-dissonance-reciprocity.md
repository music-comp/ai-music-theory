# Quick Definition
Consonance/dissonance reciprocity describes the condition where the normally asymmetric relationship between consonant and dissonant harmonies becomes fluid, allowing consonant triads to subordinate to dissonant ones in certain compositional contexts.

# Formal Definition
In classical tonality, consonant harmonies provide the stable framework within which dissonant harmonies operate - the relationship is "diodic" (one-directional). However, as Robert P. Morgan showed in "Dissonant Prolongation" (1976), there are situations where these priorities reverse and "consonant triads subordinate to dissonant ones, not only locally but across spans of significant duration" (p. 64). The relation between consonance and dissonance thus becomes "fluid in principle," opening "a compositional dynamic, where a terrain of fixed relations is transformed into a site for negotiation."

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Types of reciprocity identified in 19th-century theory:

1. Fifth-related triads (Sechter 1853, following Kirnberger):
C serves as dominant to F, which serves as subdominant to C - recursive indeterminacy requiring external resolution.

2. Relative major/minor (C major/a minor):
Tonal indeterminacy focused at the whole-step melodic fulcrum.

3. Leittonwechsel (C major/e minor):
Indeterminacy focused at the semitone fulcrum; leading-tone relationship is bidirectional in German theory.

4. Nebenverwandt relation (C major/f minor, per Weitzmann):
Reciprocal leading-tone energies divided between the agents (upward-pressing E from f minor's parallel major, downward-pressing Ab from C major's parallel minor).

5. Consonant/augmented triad:
The case explored in Chapter 3, where augmented triads can function as structural centers around which consonant triads are organized.

# Musical Context/Application
Consonance/dissonance reciprocity opens compositional possibilities that classical theory does not accommodate. Passages can be organized around dissonant harmonies (like augmented triads) that never sound as surface verticalities but nonetheless govern the progression's logic.

# Examples
From the text (pp. 63-65):

Schubert, Symphony No. 2, finale (Figure 3.2):
"The core of the development consists of three transpositionally identical phrases that divide the octave by major thirds... Immediately preceding each tonic is a locally appropriate spelling of the CEG# augmented triad."

The text notes: "If prolongation is at work in this passage in any form, then its more plausible object is the FAC# triad that unites the three tonics."

Schenker's analyses of similar passages (Beethoven, Wolf) imply "dissonant prolongations" that "cannot be reconciled to his pronouncements elsewhere that only consonant harmonies are susceptible to composing out."

# Common Confusions
- Reciprocity does not mean consonance and dissonance are "the same"; the scale is inherently imbalanced
- The reciprocity is a compositional possibility, not a perceptual reality in all contexts
- Even when augmented triads govern structure, they need not sound as surface harmonies
- Reciprocity requires "external forces" to level the inherent asymmetry (Fetis's uniformity and symmetry)
