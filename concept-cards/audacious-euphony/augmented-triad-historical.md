# Quick Definition
The augmented triad emerged gradually from the 17th through 19th centuries, evolving from a marginalized passing dissonance to a recognized primary sonority with distinctive character and enharmonic potential.

# Core Definition
The historical emergence of the augmented triad traces its transformation from a "harsh harmony" (Sorge 1745) to a primary sonority capable of enharmonic reinterpretation and structural organization. Cohn identifies key milestones: Sorge's reluctant recognition (1745), French theorists' acceptance as a fundamental sonority (1770s), Vogler's exploration of its enharmonic *Mehrdeutigkeit* (1802), and Weitzmann's full advocacy for "granting [the augmented triad] an abiding place in the kingdom of tones" (1853). Late-nineteenth-century composers such as Liszt and Wolf ultimately incorporated augmented triads into their "normative sonic core" (p. 61).

# Key Properties
1. The augmented triad was first located in France as early as 1636, typically as a displaced dominant fifth
2. Figured bass treatises consistently required that +5 be rendered with a seventh or ninth
3. Sorge (1745) was the first to recognize it as a primary harmony, though reluctantly
4. French theorists of the 1770s accepted it as a fundamental sonority with distinctive character
5. Vogler (1802) first explored its potential for enharmonic reinterpretation (*Mehrdeutigkeit*)
6. Weitzmann (1853) advocated for its compositional emancipation in a full monograph

# Construction / Recognition
Historical development timeline:
1. **1636 (France)**: Augmented triad appears when dominant fifth is displaced up by diatonic semitone
2. **1745 (Sorge)**: First recognition as primary harmony: "The best thing about this harsh harmony... is that it seldom appears"
3. **1760 (Sorge)**: Tolerable when chromatic passing tone connects fifth-related major triads
4. **1770s (French theorists)**: Accepted as fundamental sonority with distinctive character and capacity for accretions
5. **1802 (Vogler)**: First exploration of enharmonic reinterpretation: "each III chord in minor... can be multiply interpreted as a III chord in three different keys"
6. **1853 (Weitzmann)**: Full treatise advocating emancipation, portraying augmented triad as a "serf" deserving freedom

# Context & Application
In neo-Riemannian theory, the augmented triad's historical trajectory matters because its gradual acceptance as a structural harmony laid the groundwork for understanding it as the perfectly even chord around which consonant triads are organized as minimal perturbations. The shift from "tolerated passing dissonance" to "structural center" is the same conceptual shift that Cohn's theory formalizes.

# Examples
**Mozart Minuet K. 355/576b** (attribution uncertain, p. 62): "The short composition contains seven augmented triads, of which only the first behaves in the manner sanctioned by contemporaneous treatises. The remaining six dissonances are anomalously accented in three independent ways: each initiates a phrase, occurs on a metric downbeat, and is marked sforzando."

**Late 19th-century usage** (p. 61): Brahms in the Alto Rhapsody (motivic emblem), Wagner in *Siegfried Idyll* and portraits of the Valkyries and Amfortas, Liszt and Wolf incorporating augmented triads into their "normative sonic core."

**Moomaw's research** (p. 61): Charles Moomaw's 1985 dissertation is the most comprehensive English-language source on the augmented triad's origins and early history.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the augmented triad as "invented" at a specific moment
  **Correction**: It emerged gradually through changing theoretical and compositional practice over two centuries

- **Error**: Equating theoretical acceptance with consonant status
  **Correction**: "Primary harmony" status does not mean the chord was considered consonant; it was accepted as a legitimate independent sonority

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Early tolerance for the chord was general
  **Clarification**: Acceptance was context-dependent; accented and structurally prominent usage was innovative
- **Confusion**: The chord's enharmonic potential was understood from the start
  **Clarification**: Enharmonic reinterpretation (Vogler 1802) was recognized long after the chord's basic acceptance
