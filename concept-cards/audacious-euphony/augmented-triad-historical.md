# Quick Definition
The augmented triad emerged gradually as an accepted harmony from the 17th through 19th centuries, initially as a passing dissonance regulated by figured bass conventions, later gaining status as a primary harmony with distinctive character and enharmonic potential.

# Formal Definition
The historical emergence of the augmented triad traces its transformation from a marginalized, "harsh harmony" (Sorge 1745) to a recognized primary sonority with compositional potential. Key figures include Georg Andreas Sorge (first to recognize it as a primary harmony, 1745), French theorists of the 1770s (accepted it as fundamental), Georg Vogler (explored its enharmonic reinterpretation, 1802), and Carl Friedrich Weitzmann (advocated for its emancipation, 1853).

# Construction/Recognition
Historical development timeline:

1636 (France): Augmented triad appears when dominant fifth is displaced up by diatonic semitone. Figured bass treatises instruct that +5 be rendered with seventh or ninth.

1745 (Sorge): First recognition as primary harmony, though reluctant: "The best thing about this harsh harmony... is that it seldom appears."

1760 (Sorge): Upgrade - tolerable when chromatic passing tone connects fifth-related major triads.

1770s (France): Accepted as fundamental sonority with distinctive character and capacity for accretions.

1802 (Vogler): First exploration of enharmonic reinterpretation (Mehrdeutigkeit): "each III chord in minor... can be multiply interpreted as a III chord in three different keys."

1853 (Weitzmann): Full treatise advocating emancipation, comparing augmented triad to a "serf" deserving "an abiding place in the kingdom of tones."

# Musical Context/Application
The historical trajectory shows the augmented triad moving from regulated dissonance to expressive resource. By the late nineteenth century, some composers (Brahms, Wagner, Liszt, Wolf) featured augmented triads as motivic emblems or incorporated them into their normative sonic vocabulary.

# Common Confusions
- The augmented triad was not "invented" at a specific moment; it emerged gradually through changing theoretical and compositional practice
- Early tolerance for the chord was context-dependent (passing, unaccented); accented usage was innovative
- "Primary harmony" status does not mean the chord was considered consonant
- The chord's enharmonic potential was recognized before its structural role in voice leading
