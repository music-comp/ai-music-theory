# Quick Definition
Brian Hyer's (1989) model of the Tonnetz as a geometry capable of changing shape in response to whether the listener hears triads in relation to a tonic (planar/extended) or as participants in pan-triadic space (cylindrical/closed).

# Core Definition
Hyer's 1989 dissertation developed a geometry capable of simultaneously modeling both of Lewin's spaces (Riemannian tonal space and scale-degree space) while situating each object and relation in a unique location. Each triad is positioned as a point connected to its L, P, and R associates and to its modally matched fifth. The Tonnetz models chromatic space by identifying enharmonically and syntonically equivalent points. Hyer writes: "the [transformational] group as a whole disperses the functional 'significance' of [a single] triad among the harmonic consonances woven together to form its algebraic fabric; there is no one triad that forms a tonic for the group as a whole" (Hyer 1995, 127, qtd. in Cohn, p. 191).

# Prerequisites
- **Tonnetz**: Must understand the basic pitch-class graph with consonant-interval edges
- **L, P, R transformations**: These define the connections in Hyer's model

# Key Properties
1. Each triad occupies a unique position; each direct triadic relation occupies a unique position
2. The interpretation of each relation is contingent on whether the space is closed or open
3. The structure cannot be inferred from the standpoint of a single triad or even a single relation
4. Two states: cylindrical (pan-triadic) and planar (tonal)

# Construction / Recognition
**Cylindrical/closed state** (pan-triadic): Enharmonic and syntonic points identified (glued); "no one triad that forms a tonic for the group as a whole."

**Planar/extended state** (tonal): "To assert a given triad as a tonic... forces us to imagine transformational relations with regard to the tonic, and to calculate them in scale degrees rather than generic semitones, in effect decircularizing [the Tonnetz], extending its [axes] in all directions" (Hyer 1995, 127). This "impos[es] a sense of perspective on the surrounding terrain, a point of view from which all the other triads appear to be near, more or less remote, or over the horizon" (Hyer 1995, 127-28).

**Conversion trigger**: "When it becomes strained to hear relations between triads with respect to a given tonic triad, then we in fact no longer hear that triad as a tonic. At that moment... the circularized form of the lattice comes back into play" (Hyer 1989, 215).

# Context & Application
Hyer's geometry is "ordered up to Lewin's blueprint in almost every respect" except that where Lewin envisions a Mobius strip, Hyer constructs a plane. Cohn suggests imagining Hyer's plane closed into a loop with a half-twist to bring the vision to full realization, a motivation provided by Brower's (2008) consideration of the Tonnetz as diatonic space (p. 192).

The distinction between circular and planar interpretations is equivalent to Harrison's (2002a) conforming/nonconforming distinction (p. 191, n. 5).

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating Hyer's model as a static representation
  **Correction**: Its shape changes dynamically in response to listener perception

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Decircularization means the chromatic Tonnetz disappears
  **Clarification**: It means tonal perspective is imposed on the space, not that chromatic relations cease to exist

- **Confusion**: The "glue" bonding enharmonic/syntonic equivalents is physical
  **Clarification**: It is cognitive -- the listener's capacity to treat enharmonic equivalents as identical
