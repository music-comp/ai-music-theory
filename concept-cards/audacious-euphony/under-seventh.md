# Quick Definition
An under-seventh is a dissonant tone added a major second below the root of a minor triad, creating what standard theory calls a half-diminished seventh chord but analyzed as a minor triad with supplementary bass dissonance.

# Formal Definition
The **under-seventh** is a concept from harmonic dualism (Oettingen, Riemann) that interprets the bass note of a half-diminished seventh chord as a dissonant addition to a minor triad, rather than as the chord's root.

For the chord B-D-F-A (typically labeled B half-diminished seventh):
- Dualist analysis: D minor triad (D-F-A) with B as under-seventh
- Standard analysis: B as root, with D-F-A as stacked thirds above

The under-seventh is generated downward from the triad's "root" (which dualists consider the fifth), symmetrical to the over-seventh generated upward from a major triad.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Dualist generation:
- Minor triad generated downward from its fifth: A → F → D (d minor)
- Under-seventh added a major second below the dualist root: D → B
- Result: B-D-F-A (enharmonically "half-diminished seventh")

Voice-leading behavior:
- Under-seventh typically resolves upward by step (to the third of the resolution chord)
- This mirrors the over-seventh's downward resolution
- Creates plagal voice-leading symmetrical to authentic voice-leading

# Musical Context/Application
The under-seventh interpretation is supported when:
- The "seventh" does not resolve; the "root" does
- The minor triad is perceptually/functionally primary
- Voice-leading mirrors plagal (IV-I) progressions
- Timbre or register isolates the under-seventh from the triadic core

The concept appears in:
- Wagner's late music (Tristan, Parsifal, Ring)
- Brahms's plagal cadences
- Film music cadential progressions
- Any plagal progression with rising bass voice

# Examples
**Tristan und Isolde, final cadence**: F# minor subdominant receives added D# (major sixth/under-seventh), creating a "ø7" sonority. The B does not resolve or feel pressure to do so. The C# (putative root) passes upward to D# (third of B major), behaving like an under-seventh, not a seventh-chord root.

**Tristan, act 3 opening**: Bb minor with added G. The G endures without resolution and is registrally/timbrally isolated from the Bb minor core.

**Parsifal, horn motive**: Communion theme appears both as pure minor triad and with timbrally isolated under-seventh, suggesting interchangeability.

**Tallis's Third Tune (1557)**: Concludes with a minor → E major cadence featuring passing F# in the cantus - an early example of plagal under-seventh voice-leading.

# Common Confusions
- Under-seventh analysis reverses standard third-stacking; what looks like a root becomes a dissonance
- The concept requires dualist orientation; standard theory has no equivalent
- Under-seventh ≠ added sixth; same pitches, different analytical orientation
- Perceptual primacy of the triad, not theoretical commitment, justifies the analysis
