# Quick Definition
An under-seventh is a dissonant tone added a major second below the root of a minor triad, creating what standard theory calls a half-diminished seventh chord but analyzed dualistically as a minor triad with supplementary bass dissonance.

# Core Definition
The **under-seventh** is a concept from harmonic dualism (Oettingen, Riemann) that interprets the bass note of a half-diminished seventh chord as a dissonant addition to a minor triad. For the chord B-D-F-A: dualist analysis treats D-F-A (D minor) as the core, with B as a characteristically dissonant under-seventh generated downward from A (Rehding 2003, 94-96). Carl Dahlhaus writes that "the notion that B is the 'characteristic dissonance' of the subdominant seventh chord b-d-f-a is frustrated by musical reality, in which it is evident that a is the chord's actual dissonance" (1990 [1967], 56), though Cohn presents evidence from Wagner suggesting otherwise.

# Prerequisites
- **Reduction strategy**: The under-seventh is an application of reduction to the half-diminished seventh chord
- **Half-diminished seventh chord**: The chord type being reanalyzed
- **Harmonic dualism**: The theoretical framework generating the under-seventh concept

# Key Properties
1. Mirrors the over-seventh symmetrically: over-seventh adds above major triad (authentic), under-seventh adds below minor triad (plagal)
2. The under-seventh resolves *upward* by step, not downward like a conventional seventh
3. The minor-to-major plagal progression with rising under-seventh mirrors the major-to-minor authentic with falling seventh
4. The interpretation survived "well into the twentieth century, even among theorists otherwise uncommitted to a dualist theory of harmony" before being overcome by "renewed third-stacking monolithy" (p. 160)

# Construction / Recognition
- Take a minor triad (e.g., D-F-A)
- Add a major second below the lowest note of the dualist derivation: D down to B
- Result: B-D-F-A (conventionally labeled B half-diminished)
- The under-seventh (B) resolves upward, while the triadic core resolves conventionally

Voice-leading behavior:
- Under-seventh passes stepwise upward to the third of the resolution chord
- This mirrors the dominant seventh's downward resolution, but in reverse direction
- Creates plagal cadential motion symmetrical to authentic cadential motion

# Context & Application
The under-seventh interpretation is supported when: the "seventh" does not resolve or feel pressure to do so; the "root" is transient and passes upward; the minor triad is perceptually primary due to timbral isolation, register, or duration. Wagner "often treats minor triads and their ø7 supersets as interchangeable in his late music" (p. 162). Recent empirical work (Parncutt 1989, 149) has motivated a revival of Riemann's proposal, grounding it in perceptual experiments.

# Examples
- **Tristan final cadence**: Minor subdominant receives added major sixth creating a "ø7" sonority. "The 'seventh' of that chord, B, does not resolve, nor does it feel any pressure to do so. It is the putative root, C#, that is transient: as an under-seventh, it passes stepwise upward to the third of the resolution chord" (p. 161)
- **Tristan act 3 opening**: Bb minor triad with added G; "there is little temptation to hear this as a passing formation, as the G does not evidently issue from F, and it endures for three very slow beats" (p. 161)
- **Parsifal horn motive**: Communion theme appears both as triadic and with timbrally isolated under-seventh (p. 162)
- **Tarnhelm motive**: Wagner frequently adds under-seventh to initial g# minor chord (Rothfarb 1988, Lewin 1992) (p. 162)
- **Tallis's Third Tune (1557)**: Concludes with a minor to E major progression featuring passing F# -- an early example (p. 161 n. 3)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Dismissing the under-seventh as merely an "added sixth"
  **Correction**: Though the pitches are the same, the analytical orientation differs -- the under-seventh emphasizes the minor triad's primacy and the bass tone's dissonant, resolving character

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking the under-seventh requires commitment to harmonic dualism
  **Clarification**: The interpretation can be grounded in perceptual evidence (Parncutt 1989) rather than metaphysical speculation about undertone series
