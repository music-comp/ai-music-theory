# Quick Definition
The boundary between diatonic spellings where enharmonic equivalence must be invoked, distinguishing passages that "tease" the seam from those that "plunge through" it.

# Formal Definition
The enharmonic seam is the point in chromatic space where a pitch must be respelled enharmonically to maintain locally consonant and diatonic connections. In Weitzmann regions, the seam appears at the S-related exterior triads of a double-agent complex. The four-chord complex may tease the seam (avoiding direct enharmonic contact), while the five- or six-chord N/R chain necessarily plunges through it.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Example seam in Bb minor context:
- Gb major contains Bbb (minor third)
- F major contains A (major third)
- These are enharmonically equivalent but spell differently
- Direct Gb -> F motion crosses the seam (S transformation)

The seam exists because:
- S-related triads share no diatonic scale membership
- The common third must be respelled when S is traversed
- No notation can maintain consonance and diatonic consistency for both triads

# Musical Context/Application
The expansion from four-chord double-agent complexes to six-chord N/R cycles can be understood as an incremental historical process moving from Classical diatonic tonality into symmetric chromatic space. The four-chord complex may avoid the seam; the complete cycle cannot. This progression maps the development from C.P.E. Bach through Schubert.

# Examples
- Chopin Mazurka Op. 6 No. 1: Teases the seam, avoiding direct enharmonic contact by substituting F# for F
- Chopin Nocturne Op. 9 No. 1: Plunges through with Bbb -> A in same voice
- Haydn Symphony No. 99 and Benda Sonata: Five-chord chains that cross the seam

# Common Confusions
- The seam is notational, not acoustic (the pitches are the same)
- "Teasing" vs. "plunging" describes compositional treatment, not absolute categories
- The seam location depends on which triads frame the double-agent complex
