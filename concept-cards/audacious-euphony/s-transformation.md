# Quick Definition
The Slide transformation (S) connects a major triad to a minor triad one semitone higher (or vice versa), moving root and fifth by semitone in parallel motion while preserving the shared third.

# Core Definition
S is "the remaining mode-reversing relation" within a Weitzmann region (p. 82). Sigfrid Karg-Elert (1930) called it *Terzgleich* (same-third), and David Lewin (1987) proposed *Slide*; both suggest the letter S. S "retains 'maverick status' among the Weitzmann transformations, similar to H within the hexatonic group" (p. 82). "S-related triads share no membership in a diatonic scale," which is why the "direct pairing is not classically normative" (p. 82). S emerged prominently with Schubert and became more frequent in "Liszt, Wagner, and Prokofiev" and "a staple of film music" (p. 82).

# Prerequisites
- **Consonant triad**: S operates on consonant triads

# Key Properties
1. S maps a major triad to the minor triad one semitone higher (e.g., C major -> c# minor)
2. S maps a minor triad to the major triad one semitone lower (e.g., c# minor -> C major)
3. Root and fifth move by semitone in parallel motion; third retained
4. S requires 2 units of voice-leading work
5. S is an involution: S(S(X)) = X
6. S preserves Weitzmann region and shifts hexatonic region
7. S-related triads share no diatonic scale membership, giving S its "maverick status"
8. S = LP (the compound of L and P)

# Construction / Recognition
Voice-leading action:
- C major {C, E, G} -> c# minor {C#, E, G#}: C->C# (up semitone), G->G# (up semitone), E retained
- c# minor {C#, E, G#} -> C major {C, E, G}: C#->C (down semitone), G#->G (down semitone), E retained

# Context & Application
S lacks classical syntactic normality because S-related triads share no diatonic scale. In the 18th century, S-related triads substituted for one another rather than juxtaposing directly. Schubert pioneered direct S progressions; they became a staple of Romantic and film music. S connects the exterior triads of the double-agent complex.

# Examples
**C.P.E. Bach, Piano Sonata in f minor (1763)** (p. 86): Ab major to Fb/E major involves S in a larger context; Forkel "found no beauty in this passage" but defended it as written by a great composer.

**Chopin, Nocturne Op. 9 No. 1** (p. 88): gb minor to F major enacts S transformation, transforming Bbb into A in the same voice.

**Karg-Elert's influence** (p. 82): "Karg-Elert's treatment of the common-third progression has had a particular influence on harmonic theory in Russia."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating S as a common Classical progression
  **Correction**: S has "no classical syntactic basis" and only emerged prominently with Schubert

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The common third is always spelled the same in both triads
  **Clarification**: When S crosses the enharmonic seam, "the common third may be spelled enharmonically" (e.g., Bbb vs. A)
- **Confusion**: S and LP are different operations
  **Clarification**: "LP = S" -- the compound of L and P produces the same result as S
