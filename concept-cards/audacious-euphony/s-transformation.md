# Quick Definition
The Slide transformation connects a major triad to a minor triad a semitone higher (or vice versa), moving two voices by semitone in parallel motion while preserving the shared third.

# Formal Definition
S (Slide) is a triadic transformation that maps:
- A major triad to the minor triad whose root is one semitone higher (e.g., C major to c# minor)
- A minor triad to the major triad whose root is one semitone lower (e.g., c# minor to C major)

S is characterized by:
- Preservation of the common third (in same-third or Terzgleich progressions)
- Two voices moving by semitone in parallel motion
- Modal change (major to minor or vice versa)

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Voice-leading action:
- C major {CEG} -> c# minor {C#EG#}: C moves up to C# (semitone), G moves up to G# (semitone), E retained
- c# minor {C#EG#} -> C major {CEG}: C# moves down to C, G# moves down to G, E retained

S requires 2 units of voice-leading work.
S-related triads share no membership in a diatonic scale, giving S its maverick status.

# Musical Context/Application
S lacks classical syntactic normality because S-related triads share no diatonic scale. In the 18th century, S-related triads typically substituted for one another rather than juxtaposing directly. Schubert pioneered direct S progressions, which became more frequent in Liszt, Wagner, and Prokofiev. S has become a staple of film music. S retains "maverick status" within the Weitzmann group, similar to H within the hexatonic group.

# Examples
- C.P.E. Bach Piano Sonata in f minor (1763): Ab major to Fb/E major (S in larger context)
- Chopin Nocturne Op. 9 No. 1, m. 8: gb minor to F major (S transformation with enharmonic respelling of Bbb to A)
- Liszt's "Il Penseroso": a minor to G# major via S
- Schubert Lebenssturme, m. 260: direct S juxtaposition

# Common Confusions
- S is labeled after both Karg-Elert's "same-third" (Terzgleich) and Lewin's "Slide" - both suggest the letter S
- Unlike R and N, S has no classical syntactic basis and only emerged prominently with Schubert
- The common third may be spelled enharmonically (Bbb vs. A) when S crosses enharmonic boundaries
