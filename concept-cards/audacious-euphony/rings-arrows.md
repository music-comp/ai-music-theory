# Quick Definition
Directional arrows in Steven Rings's (2007) model representing the syntactic flow and semantic charge of tonal harmony -- the gravity, magnetism, and attraction associated with dominant-to-tonic and subdominant-to-tonic motion in classical tonality.

# Core Definition
Rings arrows, adapted from Lewin's DOM and SUBD transformations, accompany the vertical (tonal-functional) axis of Rings's model. They capture not only syntactic flow but also the "semantic charge" of classical tonality, "roughly stand[ing] in for what musicians elsewhere capture through such terms as summoning, leading, gravity, magnetism, attraction, desire, and so forth" (Cohn, p. 188). Crucially, these arrows can be activated or deactivated depending on musical context: they are active within encapsulated diatonic space and retract during sequential or chromatic passages.

# Prerequisites
- **DOM and SUBD transformations**: Rings arrows are the visual representation of these Lewinian transformations
- **Diatonic encapsulation**: Arrows activate only within encapsulated diatonic space

# Key Properties
1. Single-headed: represent directional, non-involutory motion
2. Carry semantic content beyond mere syntactic position (gravitation, desire, leading)
3. Can be activated (within diatonic capsule) or deactivated (during chromatic sequences)
4. Default component that can be deactivated, not an inherent property of fifth-related motion
5. Retract "into the body of the moving vehicle" during chromatic navigation

# Construction / Recognition
- Active: Within diatonic encapsulation, when cadential processes confirm tonal centers
- Deactivating: During sequential motion (Fetis's "law of uniformity"), L/R chains, hexatonic traversals
- Reactivating: When approaching diatonic stations, cadential labor relocks the arrows

On the convertible Tonnetz: "Exiting the initial diatonic space, the parallelogram slides incrementally left or right. Flying past multiple diatonic spaces, its diagonal edges ease open... Rings arrows retract into the body of the moving vehicle. Approaching a new diatonic station, doors flip closed, Rings arrows reemerge" (p. 200).

# Context & Application
A key limitation of the original Rings model is that the arrows "implicitly commit to the presence of tonal-gravitational forces whenever motion occurs along the fifths axis, excluding the possibility that those forces are slackened in the case of sequences" (p. 188). The convertible Tonnetz addresses this by making arrow activation contingent on diatonic encapsulation.

# Examples
- **Liszt Consolation no. 3, basic idea** (mm. 3-6): T-S-D-T paradigm with arrows indicating syntactic flow (Figure 8.7b, p. 198)
- **S-to-D motion**: The overshooting arrow vaults across the syntonic comma on the planar Tonnetz; on the Mobius strip, it continues uniformly around the backside (Figure 8.5, p. 194)
- **Hexatonic journeys** (Consolation mm. 23-40): Arrows temporarily suspend; cadential confirmation at each station temporarily reactivates them locally
- **Bach d minor Toccata**: Arrows deactivate during sliding migration through keys, none cadentially confirmed (p. 199)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming Rings arrows are always active whenever fifths motion occurs
  **Correction**: They function as a default that can be deactivated by sequential or chromatic contexts

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Rings arrows are simply labels for DOM/SUBD
  **Clarification**: They carry semantic content (gravitation, desire) beyond syntactic position, and critically they are context-dependent
