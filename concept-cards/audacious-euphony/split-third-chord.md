# Quick Definition
The split-third chord is a tetrachord of type [0347] formed by combining P-related triads (major and minor sharing a root), containing both major and minor thirds above the root, identified by Lendvai as the "alpha" sonority in Bartok's music.

# Core Definition
The **split-third chord** is the third type of tetrachord generated by combining edge-adjacent triads on the Tonnetz. It is "formed by the union of two P-related triads" sharing a perfect-fifth edge, producing the [0347]-type tetrachord. Cohn identifies it as "the 'alpha' sonority that Lendvai 1971 locates prominently in the music of Bartok" (p. 160). Unlike the major seventh (L-related) and minor seventh (R-related) combinations, the split-third "does not arise to prominence until the twentieth century."

# Prerequisites
- **Combination strategy**: The split-third chord is generated through the combination of two triads
- **P transformation**: The two triads are related by the Parallel transformation

# Key Properties
1. Contains both major and minor thirds above the same root (hence "split-third")
2. Set-class 4-17, prime form [0347]
3. Creates a parallelogram on the Tonnetz bisected by the perfect-fifth edge
4. Shares two common tones with each generating triad
5. Can mediate between hexatonic poles

# Construction / Recognition
- Take a major triad and its parallel minor: C major (C-E-G) + C minor (C-Eb-G)
- Union: C-Eb-E-G
- Shared edge: C-G (perfect fifth)
- Contains both minor third (C-Eb) and major third (C-E)

# Context & Application
The split-third chord appears primarily in twentieth-century music. Its compact Tonnetz location distinguishes it from most dissonant harmonies, which sprawl irregularly on the Tonnetz. It can mediate between hexatonic poles by sharing two common tones with each.

# Examples
- **Schreker's Kammersymphonie (1916)**: "The C# split third fills a gap that is left along the major third axis by the juxtaposition of the two previous triads, the hexatonic poles A major and f minor, with both of which it shares two common tones. Figure 7.1(b) indicates this hybrid chord as a parallelogram" (p. 160)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Classifying the split-third chord as a nearly even tetrachord
  **Correction**: It is generated by combination of triads, not by displacement of a diminished seventh; it belongs to a different set class ([0347] vs. [0258])

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking "alpha sonority" is Cohn's term
  **Clarification**: "Alpha" is Lendvai's term for this chord in Bartok analysis; "split-third" emphasizes the interval structure
