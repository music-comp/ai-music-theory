# Quick Definition
The split-third chord is a tetrachord formed by combining P-related triads (major and minor triads sharing a root), containing both major and minor thirds above the root, identified by Lendvai as the "alpha sonority" in Bartok's music.

# Formal Definition
The **split-third chord** (set-class 4-17, prime form [0347]) is formed by the combination of two P-related triads (a major triad and its parallel minor). It contains:
- Root
- Minor third
- Major third
- Perfect fifth

The chord represents the third type of tetrachord generated by combining edge-adjacent triads on the Tonnetz, alongside:
- Major seventh (L-related triads, minor-third edge)
- Minor seventh (R-related triads, major-third edge)
- Split-third (P-related triads, perfect-fifth edge)

Lendvai (1971) identifies this as the "alpha" sonority prominent in Bartok's music.

# Construction/Recognition
Generation by combination:
- C major (C-E-G) + C minor (C-Eb-G) = C-Eb-E-G
- Shared edge: C-G (perfect fifth)
- Tonnetz representation: parallelogram bisected by fifth edge

Interval content:
- [0, 3, 4, 7] in pitch-class integers
- Contains both minor third (0-3) and major third (0-4)
- "Splits" the third between major and minor

Voice-leading relationships:
- Shares two common tones with both generating triads
- Can mediate between hexatonic poles

# Musical Context/Application
The split-third chord:
- Is rare in common-practice music but prominent in early twentieth century
- Appears in Bartok as a characteristic sonority
- Can mediate between chromatic voice-leading relationships
- Has compact Tonnetz location (unlike most seventh chords)

In Cohn's discussion, the split-third illustrates how the combination strategy generates determinate Tonnetz locations, as opposed to other seventh chord types that sprawl irregularly.

# Examples
**Schreker's Kammersymphonie (1916)**: A C# split-third chord fills the gap left by juxtaposition of hexatonic poles A major and f minor. The split-third shares two common tones with each adjacent triad, functioning as a combinational bridge.

**Bartok's music generally**: Lendvai's analysis identifies the alpha sonority ([0347]) as a characteristic Bartokian chord, arising from the superimposition of major and minor thirds.

# Common Confusions
- Split-third is NOT a nearly even tetrachord (it's a combination, not a displacement)
- The chord does not arise from nineteenth-century practice; it's primarily twentieth-century
- "Alpha" is Lendvai's term; "split-third" emphasizes the interval structure
- The chord has a compact Tonnetz location, unlike most dissonances
