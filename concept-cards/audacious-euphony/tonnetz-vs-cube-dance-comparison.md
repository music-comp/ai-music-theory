# Quick Definition
The Tonnetz and Cube Dance are two essentially equivalent but complementarily advantageous representations of triadic voice-leading space: the Tonnetz excels at tracking individual pitch classes, common tones, and sequential patterns, while Cube Dance excels at modeling true voice-leading distance and cyclic closure.

# Formal Definition
Both graphs model voice-leading proximity among the twenty-four consonant triads (plus augmented triads). Their respective advantages:

**Tonnetz advantages**:
- More compact
- Shows individual pitch classes rather than fusing them into triads
- Preserves connections with historical modes of thought
- More amenable to tracing common tones
- Consistently correlates planar directions with transformations

**Cube Dance advantages**:
- More direct model of true voice-leading distance (no Bering Strait distortion)
- Gives augmented triads explicit locations
- Shows cyclic closure explicitly
- Clockwise/counterclockwise consistently maps to upshift/downshift

# Construction/Recognition
Choose the Tonnetz when:
- Tracking common tones or individual pitch classes
- Analyzing sequential patterns or transformation-specific progressions
- Working with pitch retention loops or neighborhoods
- Historical context is relevant

Choose Cube Dance when:
- Tracking voice-leading trajectories and cyclic closure
- Comparing distances between triads
- Assessing voice-leading zones
- The specific triadic progression matters less than the trajectory

# Musical Context
Chapters 5 and 6 move back and forth between these representations in response to the characteristics of each composition. The choice is analogous to tracking pitch succession in pitch space vs. pitch-class space.

# Examples
- Liszt *Lelio Fantasy*: Cube Dance (figure 5.23) shows cyclic closure invisible on the Tonnetz (figure 5.22)
- Brahms Symphony No. 2 retransition: Tonnetz (figures 6.6-6.7) shows neighborhood structure and common-tone relationships
- Schubert "Der Doppelganger": Tonnetz preferred for tracking dyadic and pitch-class accumulation
- Liszt Kyrie: Cube Dance (figure 6.12) shows departure-return trajectory clearly

# Common Confusions
- Neither representation is strictly superior; each has distinct analytical advantages
- The Tonnetz's directional convention (rightward = downshift) confounds Western orthographic intuitions, but this is maintained for historical reasons
- Both are "true" models of voice-leading distance, but Cube Dance does not require imagination across the Bering Strait
