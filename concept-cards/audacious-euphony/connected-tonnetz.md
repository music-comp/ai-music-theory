# Quick Definition
A Tonnetz representation that conjoins adjacent hexatonic systems at their shared major-third axes, with the option to interpret those axes as boundaries (for common-tone relations) or as objects (for voice-leading distance).

# Formal Definition
The connected Tonnetz (Figure 5.5) expands and unites the hexatonic and Weitzmann segments presented in earlier chapters. Perfect fifths rise to the right, major thirds rise toward the northeast, and minor thirds "rise" toward the southeast. Major triads are up-tipped triangles; minor triads are down-tipped. Adjacent hexatonic systems are conjoined at shared major-third axes.

# Construction/Recognition
Two interpretations of major-third axes:
1. As boundaries: Tonnetz is a relation graph of 24 triads under maximal pitch-class intersection (adjacent triads share 2 tones)
2. As locations: Each axis represents an augmented triad, and Tonnetz becomes a model of 28 objects (24 consonant + 4 augmented) under single semitonal displacement

Under interpretation 2, the Tonnetz shares Cube Dance's property: "every distance can be interpreted as representing voice-leading size."

Topological structure:
- Left and right edges are identified (Bering Strait distortion)
- Top and bottom edges are identified
- Gluing these creates a torus (doughnut shape)

# Musical Context
The connected Tonnetz enables analysis of passages that cross regional boundaries, which is the typical case in 19th-century chromatic music. Figure 5.6 shows how the Tonnetz can be assembled from the four Weitzmann regions, with broken edges indicating where corresponding points are glued together.

# Common Confusions
- The distortions require imagining glued edges to see true distances
- Rightward motion = downshifting (counterintuitive, retained for historical reasons)
- The two interpretations of major-third axes give different analytical perspectives
