# Quick Definition
A Tonnetz that conjoins adjacent hexatonic systems at their shared major-third axes, uniting all twenty-four consonant triads and four augmented triads into a single planar graph with toroidal topology.

# Core Definition
The connected Tonnetz (figure 5.5) "expands and unites the segments presented in chapters 2 and 4" (p. 105). Perfect fifths rise to the right, major thirds toward the northeast, minor thirds toward the southeast. Major triads are up-tipped triangles; minor triads are down-tipped. Adjacent hexatonic systems are conjoined at shared major-third axes. If these axes are interpreted as boundaries, the Tonnetz is a relation graph under maximal pitch-class intersection; if interpreted as locations representing augmented triads, it becomes a model of twenty-eight objects under single semitonal displacement, sharing the property that "every distance can be interpreted as representing voice-leading size" (Tymoczko, cited p. 106).

# Prerequisites
- **Tonnetz strip**: Individual hexatonic strips that are now conjoined
- **Hexatonic-Weitzmann interaction**: Understanding why the strips must be united
- **Augmented triad as hub**: The major-third axes can represent augmented triads

# Key Properties
1. Two interpretations of major-third axes: boundaries (common-tone graph) or locations (voice-leading distance graph)
2. Bering Strait distortion in two dimensions: left/right edges replicate, top/bottom edges replicate
3. Gluing both pairs of edges creates a torus (doughnut shape)
4. Rightward motion = downshifting (counterintuitive, retained for historical reasons)
5. The staircase metaphor: each hexatonic strip is a tread, each augmented triad is a riser

# Construction / Recognition
1. Take the four hexatonic Tonnetz strips from chapter 2
2. Conjoin adjacent strips at their shared major-third axes
3. Alternatively, assemble from four Weitzmann regions (figure 5.6), with broken edges indicating where points are glued
4. Observe the toroidal topology requiring mental identification of left-right and top-bottom edges

# Context & Application
The connected Tonnetz is used alongside Cube Dance throughout chapters 5-6. It is preferred when individual pitch classes matter, when tracking common tones, when passages involve non-triadic sonorities (dyads, seventh chords), and when historical Tonnetz conventions are relevant. Its directional vectors reflect tight patterning of chromatic sequences and the disruption of sequences when substitutions occur.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Forgetting the Bering Strait distortions when measuring distances
  **Correction**: Locations at left/right edges and top/bottom edges replicate; mental gluing is required for accurate distance assessment

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Rightward motion should mean upshifting (by convention of reading direction)
  **Clarification**: On the Tonnetz, rightward = downshifting. This counterintuitive convention is retained for historical reasons: early Tonnetz designers (Euler, Oettingen, Riemann) conceived C to B as a rising acoustic ratio, projecting rightward (p. 107n4)
