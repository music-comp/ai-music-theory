# Quick Definition
Three different ways of measuring triadic distance: common-tone count, Tonnetz edge-traversals, and voice-leading work, each distributing triadic pairings differently across categories.

# Formal Definition
Figure 4.7 compares three distance metrics for the twenty-three types of triadic pairings from any fixed triad:
(a) Common tones: Counts shared pitch classes (0, 1, or 2)
(b) Tonnetz distance: Counts edge-traversals representing double-common-tone retention
(c) Voice-leading work: Counts semitonal displacements

Voice-leading work creates the most categories and distributes pairings most evenly.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Distribution of 23 triadic pairings:
- Common tones: 3 categories (0, 1, 2 common tones)
- Tonnetz distance: Varies based on edge-traversals
- Voice-leading work: 7 categories (0-6 semitones)

Discrepancies:
- C major to F major: closer on Tonnetz than C major to f minor
- But C major to f minor involves less voice-leading work
- These discrepancies become more acute in generalized n-note universes

# Musical Context/Application
Tymoczko advocates for voice-leading measures over Tonnetz distance for generality and intuitive satisfaction. However, Cohn argues for retaining the Tonnetz for several reasons: historical connections, individual pitch-class tracking, directional axis consistency, and tuning explorations.

# Examples
- C major to a minor (R): 2 common tones, but 2 units voice-leading work
- C major to f minor (N): 1 common tone, but also 2 units voice-leading work
- The expanded Tonnetz (Figure 4.6c) aligns Tonnetz distance with voice-leading work

# Common Confusions
- Common-tone counting and voice-leading work give different rankings
- R maximizes common tones but requires more voice-leading work than L and P
- Tonnetz distance aligns with voice-leading work only when augmented triads are treated as objects with area
