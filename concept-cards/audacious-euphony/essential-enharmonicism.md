# Quick Definition
Enharmonic conversion that is compositionally necessary (not merely notational convenience) and that cannot be localized to a single moment, being distributed across the entire progression.

# Core Definition
Essential enharmonicism is distinguished from notational enharmonicism by the fact that "the composer has no choice but to convert between sharps and flats in order to retain global diatonic logic" (p. 28). The exact point where the conversion is notated is pragmatic; "in a phenomenological sense, such a conversion happens everywhere and nowhere, which is tantamount to saying that it is distributed evenly across all of the possible moments when it could occur" (Proctor 1978, Telesco 1998, Harrison 2002a, qtd. p. 28). When enharmonically paired pitch classes are directly juxtaposed, the ear cannot avoid identifying them as a single tone, and "the splitting of a tone's scale-degree constituency can have a ripple effect, destabilizing the diatonic collection and the tonic that is claimed to anchor it" (p. 29).

# Prerequisites
- **Diatonic Indeterminacy**: Essential enharmonicism evolves indeterminacy into contradiction

# Key Properties
1. Compositionally necessary, not a notational choice
2. The moment of conversion is not locatable -- it is distributed across the passage
3. Reflects the incommensurability between cyclic chromatic space (mod 12) and linear diatonic logic
4. Destabilizes scale-degree assignment through tone-splitting
5. Symptomatic of layered tonality (Satyendra 1992)
6. Often occurs in sequences traversing large portions of chromatic space

# Construction / Recognition
Essential enharmonicism arises when:
- A progression returns to its starting point
- But the diatonic accounting produces a different "address" (e.g., Bb becomes Cbb)
- The discrepancy must be absorbed somewhere, but no single moment is the "true" location

Example from Schubert Bb Sonata:
- Following diatonic logic: Bb to Gb to gb to Bbb to Cbb
- But the final chord sounds like Bb, not Cbb
- The "error" is distributed across the enharmonic conversions at Gb/F#, A/Bbb

# Context & Application
Essential enharmonicism is a hallmark of 19th-century chromatic practice and a defining feature of hexatonic cycles. "It is characteristic of these chronologically early examples that tonicizing dissonances buffer the stations of the cycle... each of these excerpts instantiates what Ramon Satyendra (1992) calls layered tonality: classically tonal at both the most global and the most local level but chromatic at the level of the local modulations" (p. 42). Schenker (1906) recognized essential enharmonicism as distinct from notational convenience.

# Examples
- **Schubert, Piano Sonata in Bb major, D. 960, recapitulation**: The sustained bass pitch Gb2 would need to "fracture" into Gb and F# to maintain diatonic consistency, which is perceptually implausible
- **Hexatonic cycles**: The essential enharmonic transformation that every hexatonic cycle requires (e.g., g# minor = ab minor)
- **Any cyclic progression** that returns to its starting triad after traversing chromatic space

# Common Errors
- **Error**: The "moment" of enharmonic conversion can be identified
  **Correction**: It is distributed across the passage; "happens everywhere and nowhere"

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Essential enharmonicism is a notational choice
  **Clarification**: It is a structural necessity arising from the incommensurability of chromatic and diatonic logics

- **Confusion**: Equal temperament eliminates the significance of enharmonicism
  **Clarification**: The paradox arises from applying diatonic categories to chromatic progressions, not from tuning systems
