# Quick Definition
The choriambus (or Hungarian choriambus) is a distinctive long-short-short-long rhythmic pattern that Cohn identifies as linking thematic materials in Dvorak's "New World" Symphony development.

# Formal Definition
The **choriambus** is a rhythmic pattern consisting of long-short-short-long durations (in classical prosody: stressed-unstressed-unstressed-stressed, or in musical notation: quarter-eighth-eighth-quarter or equivalent). The pattern is associated with Hungarian musical idioms.

In Dvorak's "New World" Symphony, the choriambus links:
- The pastoral third theme
- The principal theme

These themes are also related by contour inversion, creating both rhythmic and melodic connections across the development.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Choriambic rhythm:
- Pattern: LONG - short - short - LONG
- Metric notation: ♩ ♪♪ ♩ or equivalents
- Duration ratio: approximately 2:1:1:2

The pattern creates:
- Strong-weak-weak-strong accent pattern
- Characteristic "rocking" or "galloping" feel
- Recognition across different melodic contexts

# Musical Context/Application
The choriambus serves analytical purposes:
- Identifying thematic relationships across development sections
- Understanding motivic unity beneath surface variety
- Connecting passages with different melodic content but shared rhythmic identity

In the "New World" Symphony analysis, the choriambus:
- Structures the development into four paragraphs
- Links the first two paragraphs (dominated by third theme) with final two (dominated by principal theme)
- Provides coherence across the ninety-six-measure span

# Examples
**Dvorak's "New World" Symphony, first movement development**: Hortatory thematic incipits share the choriambic rhythm. The first two paragraphs are dominated by the pastoral third theme, the final two by the principal theme. Both themes share the long-short-short-long rhythmic pattern and relate by contour inversion.

# Common Confusions
- Choriambus is a rhythmic, not melodic, pattern; different melodies can share it
- The term derives from classical prosody (Greek/Latin poetry), not specifically Hungarian music
- "Hungarian choriambus" refers to its association with Hungarian musical styles
- Rhythmic identity can create thematic connection even when pitches differ entirely
