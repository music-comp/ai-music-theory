# Quick Definition
Passages in Schubert's music where the octave is divided into three equal parts by major thirds, creating sequences of transpositionally identical phrases that arpeggiate an augmented triad through their tonal centers.

# Core Definition
Major-third divisions occur when a passage presents transpositionally identical material at three tonal levels related by major third (T4), collectively dividing the octave into three equal segments. These divisions exemplify the law of uniformity overriding diatonic channeling. The augmented triad formed by the succession of local tonics functions as a "controlling dissonance" -- a dissonant prolongation -- even though "the score contains no vertical slice or contiguous patch, even an egregiously gerrymandered one, to be circled and labeled as a 'controlling harmony'" (p. 63).

# Prerequisites
- **Augmented triad**: The succession of local tonics outlines an augmented triad
- **Dissonant prolongation**: The controlling augmented triad may never sound as a surface verticality

# Key Properties
1. Three transpositionally identical phrases or sections
2. Tonal centers related by major third (e.g., F, Db, A)
3. Each local tonic preceded by a locally appropriate spelling of one augmented triad
4. The overall root motion outlines an augmented triad
5. The passage typically returns to the starting tonal center

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify three transpositionally identical segments
2. Verify their tonal centers are related by T4 (major third)
3. Check for the augmented triad appearing (variously spelled) before each tonic
4. Note whether the passage completes the cycle by returning to the initial center

# Context & Application
Major-third divisions appear frequently in Schubert's development sections and represent early systematic explorations of equal division of the octave. They realize compositionally the *Mehrdeutigkeit* that Vogler theorized in 1802 and connect to the hexatonic cycles of Chapter 2.

# Examples
**Schubert, Symphony No. 2, finale** (Figure 3.2, pp. 63-64): "The core of the development consists of three transpositionally identical phrases that divide the octave by major thirds, beginning and ending with the F major that terminates the exposition. Immediately preceding each tonic is a locally appropriate spelling of the CEG# augmented triad."

**Schubert, Piano Sonata D. 959, 1st mvt.** (Figure 3.3, pp. 68-69): Two extended expansions "each initiated by a chromatic sequence that arpeggiates downward through the stations of an E augmented triad," involving B-to-C displacements that trigger "significant motivic reverberations."

**Relationship to Vogler** (p. 63): "Vogler's progression serves as a template for the passage" in the Second Symphony.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Calling the augmented triad "prolonged" in the strict Schenkerian sense
  **Correction**: "Prolongation does not capture the relation of these chords to one another, because the chord functions as a local dominant to a series of well-articulated tonics" (p. 63)

- **Error**: Calling the relationship merely "motivic association"
  **Correction**: "Motivic association is too static to capture the phenomenology of the passage. More than waving their hands and crying, 'Remember me, here I am again,' these augmented triads are also saying, 'You thought I was this; well think again, 'cause I can be that too'" (p. 63)

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The augmented triad must sound as a chord to be structurally central
  **Clarification**: Its status is "virtual and liminal" -- a "collocation of bass pitches or triadic roots or local tonics" (p. 64)
- **Confusion**: Major-third divisions are the same as diatonic sequences
  **Clarification**: They are chromatic sequences where uniformity overrides diatonic channeling
