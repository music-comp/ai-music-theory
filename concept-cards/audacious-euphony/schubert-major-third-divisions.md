# Quick Definition
Passages in Schubert's music where the octave is divided into three equal parts by major thirds, creating sequences of transpositionally identical phrases that arpeggiate an augmented triad through their tonal centers.

# Formal Definition
Major-third divisions occur when a passage presents transpositionally identical material at three tonal levels related by major third (T4), collectively dividing the octave into three equal segments. These divisions exemplify the law of uniformity overriding diatonic channeling, producing a chromatic sequence that arpeggiates an augmented triad through its succession of local tonics. The augmented triad thus formed functions as a virtual controlling harmony -- a dissonant prolongation -- even though it may never appear as a surface sonority.

# Construction/Recognition
Characteristics of major-third divisions:
- Three transpositionally identical phrases or sections
- Tonal centers related by major third (e.g., F, Db, A)
- Each local tonic preceded by a locally appropriate spelling of one augmented triad
- The overall bass or root motion outlines an augmented triad
- The passage typically begins and ends on the same tonal center, completing the division

# Musical Context
Major-third divisions appear frequently in Schubert's development sections and are among the earliest systematic explorations of equal division of the octave. They represent a compositional realization of the *Mehrdeutigkeit* that Vogler theorized in 1802 and relate directly to the hexatonic cycles studied in Chapter 2.

# Examples
- **Schubert, Symphony No. 2 in Bb major, finale** (Figure 3.2, mm. 300-12): "The core of the development consists of three transpositionally identical phrases that divide the octave by major thirds, beginning and ending with the F major that terminates the exposition. Immediately preceding each tonic is a locally appropriate spelling of the CEG# augmented triad."
- **Schubert, Piano Sonata D. 959, 1st mvt.**: Two extended expansions in the exposition, "each initiated by a chromatic sequence that arpeggiates downward through the stations of an E augmented triad," involving B-to-C displacements that trigger significant motivic reverberations (Figure 3.3)
- **Relationship to Vogler**: "Vogler's progression serves as a template" for the Second Symphony passage; Wason speculates on a possible lineage from Vogler to Schubert

# Common Confusions
- The augmented triad being "prolonged" is not a surface verticality but a virtual collocation of bass pitches or local tonics
- *Prolongation* in the strict Schenkerian sense does not capture this relationship, since each augmented triad functions as a local dominant to well-articulated tonics
- *Motivic association* is also inadequate: "More than waving their hands and crying, 'Remember me, here I am again,' these augmented triads are also saying, 'You thought I was this; well think again, 'cause I can be that too'"
- The technique is distinct from diatonic sequences, which channel uniformity within a governing scale
