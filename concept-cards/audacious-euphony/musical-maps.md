# Quick Definition
Geometric and graphical representations of musical space that reflect proximity judgments between musical objects, enabling visualization of syntactic relationships and compositional paths.

# Core Definition
Musical maps are geometric models of pitch space that have been in use for some 1,500 years. "The supreme advantage afforded by musical maps is their capacity to reflect judgments about the psychological proximity of musical objects or states" (p. 33). When structural and psychological conditions align, a map can draw together a family of pairwise distance assessments and represent syntactic judgments (conjunct vs. disjunct, normal vs. unusual). A good musical map "does not merely sit there, as a static structure. It acts as a stage upon which imaginative performances are mounted, thus serving the same function as a geographical map for a child with a toy car" (p. 34).

# Prerequisites
- None (framing concept for the entire book)

# Key Properties
1. Spatial distance reflects psychological proximity
2. Number of dimensions manageable (preferably 2-3)
3. Cyclic structures create Bering Strait distortions in planar formats
4. Triangle inequality may be violated when psychological and geometric logics diverge
5. Advantages over algebraic models: visual accessibility, exploratory potential, capacity to represent parallel paths
6. Different maps foreground different relationships -- no single map captures all information

# Construction / Recognition
Requirements for effective musical maps:
- Spatial distance correlates with psychological proximity
- Manageable number of dimensions
- Cyclic structures represented with minimal distortion
- Triangle inequality preserved (or violations are psychologically meaningful)

Types used for triadic relations:
- **Tonnetz**: nodes are pitch classes, edges are consonant intervals
- **Cube Dance**: nodes are triads and augmented triads, edges are single semitonal displacements
- **Orbifolds**: continuous geometric constructions

# Context & Application
Musical maps illuminate compositional decisions as selections from a finite menu. They invite questions central to composition and analysis: "How many ways are there to connect these two chords?" "What chord stands halfway between these two?" "How can I form a cycle with some desired number of elements?" "Is this a step or a leap?" "Are these two paths parallel?" "Is this path an embellishment of that one?" (p. 34). These questions concern corpus-wide ideals and norms as well as "motivic" elements that individuate particular compositions -- "the musical equivalents of *langue* and *parole*" (p. 34).

# Examples
- **The Tonnetz**: maps pitch classes as nodes, with edges representing perfect fifths (horizontal), major thirds (diagonal), minor thirds (diagonal)
- **Cube Dance**: all 28 triads (24 consonant + 4 augmented) connected by single semitonal displacement
- **Weber's "Chart of Regions"**: 18th-19th century key-relation diagram (adapted by Schoenberg)
- **Hostinsky's Tonschema (1879)**: variation on the Tonnetz used in Chapter 6

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Musical maps are claims about how listeners literally perceive space
  **Correction**: They are tools for exploration and communication, reflecting proximity judgments

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: One map can capture all relevant relationships
  **Clarification**: Different maps foreground different relationships; Tonnetz and Cube Dance are complementary

- **Confusion**: Spatial distance on a map always perfectly reflects musical proximity
  **Clarification**: Bering Strait distortions and triangle inequality violations mean the correlation is approximate
