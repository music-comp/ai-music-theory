# Quick Definition
Geometric and graphical representations of musical space that reflect proximity judgments between musical objects, enabling visualization of syntactic relationships and compositional paths.

# Formal Definition
Musical maps are geometric models of pitch space that have been used for some 1,500 years. When structural and psychological conditions align, a map can draw together a family of pairwise distance assessments and represent syntactic judgments (conjunct vs. disjunct, normal vs. unusual). Good musical maps serve as stages for "imaginative performances" - like geographical maps for a child with a toy car - illuminating compositional decisions as selections from a finite menu and inviting analytical questions about paths, steps, leaps, and embellishments.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Requirements for effective musical maps:
- Spatial distance reflects psychological proximity
- Number of dimensions manageable (preferably 2-3)
- Cyclic structures represented without excessive distortion (the "Bering Strait problem")
- Triangle inequality preserved (or violations psychologically meaningful)

Types of musical maps used for triadic relations:
- Tonnetz: nodes are pitch classes, edges are consonant intervals
- Cube Dance: nodes are triads and augmented triads, edges are single semitonal displacements
- Orbifolds and other geometric constructions

Advantages over algebraic models: visual accessibility, exploratory potential, capacity to represent parallel paths and alternative routes.

# Musical Context/Application
Musical maps invite questions central to composition and analysis:
- "How many ways are there to connect these two chords?"
- "What chord stands halfway between these two?"
- "How can I form a cycle with some desired number of elements?"
- "Is this a step or a leap?"
- "Are these two paths parallel?"
- "Is this path an embellishment of that one?"

These questions concern corpus-wide ideals and norms as well as "motivic" elements that individuate particular compositions - the musical equivalents of *langue* and *parole*.

# Examples
- The Tonnetz: maps pitch classes as nodes, with edges representing perfect fifths (horizontal), major thirds (diagonal), minor thirds (diagonal)
- Cube Dance: all 28 triads (24 consonant + 4 augmented) connected by single semitonal displacement
- 18th-19th century key relation diagrams
- Weber's "Chart of Regions" (adapted by Schoenberg)

# Common Confusions
- Musical maps are tools for exploration and communication, not claims about how listeners literally perceive space
- The "Bering Strait problem" (cyclic distortion) affects all 2D representations of pitch-class space
- Spatial distance on a map should correlate with psychological proximity, but the correlation is not always perfect
- Different maps foreground different relationships - no single map captures all relevant information
