# Quick Definition
An analytical approach to triadic harmony using transformational labels (P, L, R, etc.) to describe relationships between consonant triads, originating from David Lewin's reading of Hugo Riemann's harmonic writings.

# Core Definition
Neo-Riemannian theory is an analytical framework employing transformational labels to describe moves between consonant triads, emphasizing common tones and voice-leading proximity rather than root motion or diatonic function. Cohn explains that "it was David Lewin's reading of Riemann's harmonic writings that constituted the originary moment for this branch of theorizing" (p. 14). Cohn avoids the term in *Audacious Euphony* for two reasons: first, "it gives too much credit to Hugo Riemann," who "was functioning more as transmitter than generator" of the key ideas (p. 14); second, "the domain of neo-Riemannian theory has never been very stable" (p. 15), and not all practices performed under its name align with Cohn's approach.

# Key Properties
1. Originated with David Lewin's interpretation of Riemann's harmonic theory
2. Uses transformational labels (P, L, R, N, S, H) to describe triadic relationships
3. Emphasizes voice-leading proximity over root motion or diatonic function
4. Labels are "a bridge to a first approximation; what lies on the other side of the bridge, if one is lucky, is an understanding of how the moves designated by these labels behave as part of a compositional system" (p. 15)
5. The term has been applied to both an analytical approach and the musical progressions themselves; Cohn prefers "pan-triadic" for the latter (p. 15)
6. Does not require commitment to harmonic dualism despite some claims to the contrary (p. 15)

# Construction / Recognition
Core transformations:
1. **P** (Parallel): C major <-> c minor (same root, change mode)
2. **L** (Leittonwechsel): C major <-> e minor (shared minor third dyad)
3. **R** (Relative): C major <-> a minor (shared major third dyad)
4. **N** (Nebenverwandt): C major <-> f minor
5. **S** (Slide): C major <-> c# minor (shared third)
6. **H** (Hexatonic Pole): C major <-> ab minor (no shared tones, same hexatonic cycle)

All mode-switching operations are involutions: applying the same transformation twice returns to the starting triad.

# Context & Application
Neo-Riemannian theory provides vocabulary for analyzing chromatic triadic progressions that resist Roman numeral analysis. However, Cohn stresses that "although these labels are descriptively useful, they do not in themselves lead to an understanding of triadic syntax any more than Roman numeral or set-class designations alone constitute explanatorily adequate accounts" (p. 15). The goal is to understand "how the moves designated by these labels behave as part of a compositional system."

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 14): Cohn traces the theory's origins to his own 1990 observation about voice-leading properties of consonant triads in Schubert's E-flat Piano Trio, developed through conferences in Buffalo (1993, 1997) and contributions by Douthett and Lewin.

**Example 2** (p. 15): Tymoczko (2009a) claims "a commitment to harmonic dualism is at the heart of neo-Riemannian theory," which Cohn rejects: "On this basis, I am no neo-Riemannian."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating transformational labels as sufficient analytical explanations
  **Correction**: Labels are "a bridge to a first approximation," not an end in themselves (p. 15)

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Equating "neo-Riemannian" with the musical progressions themselves
  **Clarification**: "Neo-Riemannian" labels an analytical approach; "pan-triadic" labels the musical phenomenon (p. 15)
- **Confusion**: Assuming neo-Riemannian theory requires commitment to harmonic dualism
  **Clarification**: The theoretical apparatus can be grounded in voice-leading behavior and melodic dualism (pp. 14-15, expanded in Ch. 2)
- **Confusion**: Crediting Riemann as the primary originator
  **Clarification**: Lewin's reading of Riemann was the originary moment; Riemann "was functioning more as transmitter than generator" (p. 14)
