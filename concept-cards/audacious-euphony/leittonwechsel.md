# Quick Definition
The relation between two consonant triads that share a minor-third dyad (e.g., C major and e minor), or the transformation that produces one from the other; also called "leading-tone exchange."

# Formal Definition
The Leittonwechsel (German: "leading-tone exchange"), abbreviated L, describes both a relation and a transformation between consonant triads. Two triads stand in L relation when they share two pitch classes forming a minor third. For a major triad, L transforms it to the minor triad a major third above its root (C major to e minor); for a minor triad, L transforms it to the major triad a major third below its root (e minor to C major). The transformation involves single semitonal displacement and is an involution (applying L twice returns to the starting triad).

# Construction/Recognition
For major triad with root r:
- L(r+) = (r+4)- (minor triad with root a major third higher)
- Example: L(C+) = e- (C major to e minor)

For minor triad with root r:
- L(r-) = (r-4)+ (major triad with root a major third lower)
- Example: L(e-) = C+ (e minor to C major)

Voice leading:
- Two voices hold the common minor third
- One voice moves by semitone (the "leading tone exchange")
- Total voice-leading work: 1 semitone

Properties:
- Involution: L(L(x)) = x
- Preserves minor third dyad
- Member of H-group transformations

# Musical Context
The L transformation:
- Connects relative major/minor from the perspective of the minor mode (c minor's relative is Eb major, reached by L)
- Creates smooth voice leading with maximum common-tone retention
- Is a building block for hexatonic cycles (alternating L and P)
- Appears frequently in 19th-century chromatic progressions

The name "Leittonwechsel" refers to the leading-tone relationship: in C major to e minor, the root C moves to B (leading tone of C).

# Examples
- C major to e minor: {C, E, G} to {E, G, B} - E and G held, C moves to B
- LP chain: C - e - E - g# - Ab - c - C (hexatonic cycle)
- Brahms, Wagner, and others use L transformations extensively

# Common Confusions
- L is not the same as "relative major/minor" in the usual sense (R connects C major to a minor)
- The name refers to leading-tone motion, not to the leading tone of the target chord
- L and R both connect triads sharing a third, but L preserves minor thirds, R preserves major thirds
- L is sometimes called "leading-tone exchange" in English
