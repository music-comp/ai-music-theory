# Quick Definition
The opposition between two syntactic systems governing triadic progressions: classical syntax (based on acoustic consonance and diatonic function) and romantic syntax (based on voice-leading proximity and chromatic relations), which coexist and interact in nineteenth-century music.

# Core Definition
Cohn positions chromatic triadic progressions on "a field animated by opposing forces: of classical and romantic syntax, of normativity and aberration" (p. 12). Classical syntax generates triadic organization from acoustic properties of consonant triads: root relations, diatonic scales, tonic function, cadential closure. Romantic (pan-triadic) syntax generates organization from the near-evenness property: voice-leading proximity, chromatic space, hexatonic and Weitzmann regions. The two syntaxes share the same lexicon (consonant triads) but organize it according to different principles. "How do the old and the new syntaxes coexist and interact in nineteenth-century music, and what are the implications of this coexistence for our understanding of musical cognition and of the transformation of musical syntax across the long nineteenth century?" (p. 12).

# Key Properties
1. Both syntaxes share the same lexicon: the 24 consonant triads
2. They differ in how proximity and coherence are measured
3. Classical syntax: root-interval proximity, diatonic collections, tonic establishment, cadential closure
4. Romantic syntax: voice-leading proximity, chromatic space, tonal multistability, minimal semitonal displacement
5. They coexist in nineteenth-century music, "sometimes in alternation, sometimes in overlay"
6. Chromatic progressions "excited the Romantic imagination not because they conformed to expectations about triadic behavior and succession but because they confounded them" (p. 12)

# Construction / Recognition
## Classical syntax markers:
- Fifth-related root progressions
- Diatonic collection coherence
- Cadential closure (V-I)
- Dissonance requiring resolution

## Romantic (pan-triadic) syntax markers:
- Third-related root progressions (especially major third)
- Voice-leading parsimony
- Tonal indeterminacy
- No cadential function

# Context & Application
The coexistence of two syntaxes is central to understanding nineteenth-century harmonic practice. Chromatic progressions in song, opera, and programmatic music are "often explicitly affiliated with altered or heightened realities" (p. 12). The relationship between the syntaxes is explored explicitly in Chapters 8-9 of the book.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 12): Supernatural affiliations include "Schubert's magic harp, Wagner's magic sleep, Rimsky-Korsakov's magic and exotic kingdoms, Liszt's mountain-top meditations."

**Example 2** (p. 12): Even in nonprogrammatic instrumental music, chromatic progressions "are capable of evoking the strange, magical, and inscrutable."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Applying only one syntax to a passage that operates under both
  **Correction**: Nineteenth-century passages often operate under classical syntax at some structural levels and pan-triadic syntax at others

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking "romantic syntax" refers to emotional or expressive qualities
  **Clarification**: It refers to a specific set of voice-leading-based organizing principles
- **Confusion**: Assuming the two syntaxes are mutually exclusive
  **Clarification**: They coexist, sometimes in rapid alternation, sometimes in overlay
