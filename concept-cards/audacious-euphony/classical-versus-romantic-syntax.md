# Quick Definition
The opposition between two syntactic systems governing triadic progressions: classical syntax, based on acoustic consonance and diatonic function, and romantic syntax, based on voice-leading proximity and chromatic relations -- forces that coexist and interact in nineteenth-century music.

# Formal Definition
Cohn positions chromatic triadic progressions on "a field animated by opposing forces: of classical and romantic syntax, of normativity and aberration." Classical syntax generates triadic organization from the acoustic properties of consonant triads -- root relations, diatonic scales, tonic function, cadential closure. Romantic (pan-triadic) syntax generates triadic organization from the near-evenness property -- voice-leading proximity, chromatic space, hexatonic and Weitzmann regions. The two syntaxes share the same lexicon (consonant triads) but organize it according to different principles. Their coexistence and interaction in nineteenth-century music is a central concern of the book.

# Construction/Recognition
Distinguish the two syntaxes by their organizing principles:
- **Classical**: root-interval proximity, diatonic collection membership, tonic establishment through cadence, dissonance treatment, fifth-based progressions
- **Romantic**: voice-leading proximity, chromatic space, tonal multistability, minimal semitonal displacement, hexatonic and Weitzmann regions

# Musical Context
The two syntaxes are not merely historical labels but reflect different cognitive modes. Chromatic progressions "excited the Romantic imagination not because they conformed to expectations about triadic behavior and succession but because they confounded them." In song, opera, and programmatic music, such progressions affiliate with altered or heightened realities. The question of how the old and new syntaxes coexist and interact has implications for understanding musical cognition and the transformation of musical syntax across the long nineteenth century.

# Examples
- Schubert's music frequently operates at local and global levels in classical syntax while the middleground tonics adhere to pan-triadic logic
- Wagner's *Tarnhelm* progression: audacious by classical standards, euphonious by voice-leading standards
- The affiliations of chromatic progressions with the supernatural: Schubert's magic harp, Wagner's magic sleep, Rimsky-Korsakov's exotic kingdoms

# Common Confusions
- The two syntaxes are not mutually exclusive; they coexist, sometimes in rapid alternation, sometimes in overlay
- "Romantic syntax" does not mean emotional or expressive; it refers to a specific set of voice-leading-based organizing principles
- The classical syntax did not disappear in the nineteenth century; it continued to operate at certain structural levels
- Accepting double syntax does not require rejecting the validity of either system
