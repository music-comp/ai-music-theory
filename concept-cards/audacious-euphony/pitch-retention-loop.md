# Quick Definition
A pitch retention loop is a cyclical tour through a neighborhood's six triads using the three common-tone maximizing transformations (P, R, and L) placed in any order and then repeated.

# Formal Definition
A **pitch retention loop** is a progression that makes a cyclical tour of a pitch-class neighborhood by visiting all six triads that share the central tone. The transformational vehicles for such a tour are the three common-tone maximizers - **P** (Parallel), **R** (Relative), and **L** (Leittonwechsel) - placed in any order and then repeated in the same order. The name takes advantage of the acronymic fortuity PRL (pronounced "purl").

The loop maintains continuity through retention of the central pitch class while exploring all triadic possibilities within that neighborhood.

# Construction/Recognition
Starting from any triad containing the central pitch class, a pitch retention loop proceeds through transformations that:
1. Always preserve at least one common tone (the neighborhood's central pitch)
2. Use P, R, and L transformations in a fixed order
3. Return to the starting triad after six steps

Example loop order: P-R-L-P-R-L (or any permutation repeated)

For a neighborhood centered on C:
- Starting from C major: C major → c minor (P) → Eb major (R) → eb minor (L) → Gb major (P) → gb minor (R) → returns via (L)

# Musical Context
Pitch retention loops serve as compositional strategies for:
- Creating coherent chromatic progressions while maintaining audible continuity
- Exploring a neighborhood systematically rather than randomly
- Providing structural framework for development sections and modulatory passages

The technique appears prominently in nineteenth-century music where composers sought to create extended chromatic passages with underlying logic.

# Examples
**Verdi's "Ah si, ben mio" (Il Trovatore, act 3)**: The cantabile section models a pitch retention loop. The first quatrain modulates from f minor to Ab major; the second quatrain begins in ab minor, sounds Fb major through its consequent phrase, and repeats over db minor; the final quatrain prolongs Db major. A final L would return to the initial triad.

**Brahms's Second Symphony, first movement (mm. 246-302)**: The final part of the development executes an ambitious pitch retention loop. The timpani sounds only D and A, but these pitches serve not as roots of tonic and dominant triads but as thirds and fifths dispersing across their respective neighborhoods. The passage tours the D neighborhood (G major → g minor → Bb major) and then the A neighborhood, sounding every triad in the double neighborhood while maintaining the timpani pedals.

# Common Confusions
- A pitch retention loop is not the same as staying in one key; the loop traverses multiple tonal regions while maintaining a common pitch class
- The order of P, R, L matters for the specific path but not for achieving the loop (any order, repeated, will complete the cycle)
- Not all progressions through a neighborhood are loops; random visits don't constitute a systematic tour
- The "loop" returns to its starting triad; incomplete tours are departures that may or may not return
