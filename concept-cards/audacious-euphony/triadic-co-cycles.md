# Quick Definition
Co-cyclic triads are triads that belong to the same hexatonic cycle, meaning they share roots that form an augmented triad (roots are 0, 4, or 8 semitones apart) or they are the parallel major/minor of such triads.

# Formal Definition
Two triads are co-cyclic if they belong to the same hexatonic system. This occurs when their roots are the same or are separated by four semitones (major third). All six triads in a hexatonic cycle are mutually co-cyclic. The co-cyclic relationship determines which triads can be connected by P and L transformations (or their compounds) without leaving the hexatonic system.

# Construction/Recognition
Co-cyclic criteria:
- Same root: e.g., C major and c minor
- Roots 4 semitones apart: e.g., C major and E major, C major and Ab major
- Roots 8 semitones apart: equivalent to 4 semitones down, so also co-cyclic

For any major triad (e.g., C major), its co-cyclic partners are:
- c minor (same root, P transformation)
- E major (root +4, LP transformation)
- e minor (root +4, L transformation)
- Ab major (root +8, PLP transformation)
- ab minor (root +8, hexatonic pole)

Non-co-cyclic triads require different transformations (like R) to connect.

# Musical Context
The co-cyclic relationship determines the voice-leading possibilities within a hexatonic system. Progressions that remain co-cyclic maintain the characteristic smooth voice leading of hexatonic progressions; leaving the co-cyclic group requires larger voice-leading work and typically introduces pitches outside the hexatonic collection.

# Common Confusions
- Co-cyclic triads share a hexatonic collection, not necessarily a key or tonal center
- The co-cyclic relationship is determined by root distance, not by common tones
- All six triads in a cycle are mutually co-cyclic (the relationship is transitive within the cycle)
- Two triads from different cycles are never co-cyclic (the cycles partition the 24 triads)
