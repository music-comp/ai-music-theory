# Quick Definition
A method for calculating triadic distance based on the interval between the roots of two triads, typically measured on the circle of fifths.

# Formal Definition
The root-interval approach measures harmonic proximity according to the distance between triadic roots, typically using the circle of fifths as the metric. Triads whose roots are close on the circle of fifths (e.g., C major and G major, separated by one fifth) are considered closely related; those whose roots are distant (e.g., C major and F# major, separated by six fifths) are considered remote. This approach underlies the classical conception of key relations inherited from 18th-century theory.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Distance between roots measured in fifths:
- Adjacent on circle of fifths: 1 fifth apart (C-G, C-F)
- Two steps: 2 fifths apart (C-D, C-Bb)
- Maximum distance: 6 fifths (tritone relation, C-F#)

The root-interval approach assumes:
- Roots are acoustically generated from the harmonic series
- Root relation reflects fundamental bass motion
- Triads "represent" their eponymous keys (Rameau, Marx, Riemann, Schenker)

This approach aligns with the diatonic distance measure, since triads close on the circle of fifths tend to share diatonic collections.

# Musical Context/Application
The root-interval approach:
- Underlies Roman numeral analysis (I-V is a "strong" progression)
- Motivates circle-of-fifths sequences as normative
- Shapes expectations about modulation (to dominant = close, to tritone = remote)
- Is implicit in Schenkerian Ursatz (I-V-I as fundamental structure)

However, Tchaikovsky (1872) distinguished "inner" relations (based on root distance on the circle of fifths) from "external" connections (based on common tones), suggesting awareness that root-interval is not the only metric.

# Examples
- Classical pedagogy: the "closely related keys" are those one step away on the circle of fifths
- Rameau (1722): "every note that supports a perfect chord should be considered a tonic"
- Marx (1841-47): "every consonant triad is 'borrowed' from the key in which it is the tonic"
- Riemann (1897): "key relation is nothing other than the relation of their two tonic triads"

# Common Confusions
- Root-interval distance does not always correlate with voice-leading distance (C to G requires more voice-leading work than C to c)
- The approach works well for classical diatonic music but becomes problematic for chromatic progressions
- Equating triad with key obscures cases where triads function outside their "home" keys
- The approach privileges the root as the primary determinant of chord identity, which may not align with voice-leading intuitions
