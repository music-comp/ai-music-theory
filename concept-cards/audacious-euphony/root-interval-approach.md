# Quick Definition
A method for calculating triadic distance based on the interval between the roots of two triads, typically measured on the circle of fifths, representing the classical conception of harmonic proximity.

# Core Definition
The root-interval approach measures harmonic proximity according to the distance between triadic roots, typically using the circle of fifths as the metric. This approach reflects the classical principle that "triadic relations tracked those of their eponymous keys" (Cohn, p. 19). Rameau proclaimed in 1722 that "every note that supports a perfect chord should be considered a tonic" (p. 19). Marx (1841-47) held that "every consonant triad is 'borrowed' from the key in which it is the tonic, and he claims that these triads stand in the same relation to one another as the keys they represent" (Engebretsen 2002, 70, cited on p. 19). Riemann wrote "key relation is nothing other than the relation of their two tonic triads" (1897, 86, cited on p. 19).

# Key Properties
1. Measures distance in fifths: C-G = 1, C-D = 2, C-F# = 6 (maximum)
2. Equates triadic relations with key relations
3. Aligns with diatonic distance (triads close on the circle of fifths tend to share diatonic collections)
4. Underlies Roman numeral analysis and circle-of-fifths sequences
5. Works well for classical diatonic music but becomes problematic for chromatic progressions

# Construction / Recognition
Distance between roots measured in fifths:
1. Adjacent on circle of fifths: 1 fifth apart (C-G, C-F)
2. Two steps: 2 fifths apart (C-D, C-Bb)
3. Maximum distance: 6 fifths (tritone relation, C-F#)

The approach assumes that roots are acoustically significant and that root relation reflects fundamental bass motion.

# Context & Application
This approach represents the "eighteenth-century formulation" described on p. 9. Kirnberger (1771) expressed its core principle: "It is self-evident that those keys whose scales have most notes in common are most closely related" (p. 19). By the "age of Beethoven, the matter is more complicated" (p. 19), as alternative conceptions of distance gained traction.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 19): Rameau (1722): "every note that supports a perfect chord should be considered a tonic."

**Example 2** (p. 19): Schenker "collapsed the distinction altogether, regarding 'keys' as triads under prolongation."

**Example 3** (p. 20): Tchaikovsky (1872) distinguished "inner" relations (root distance on circle of fifths) from "external" connections (common tones), suggesting awareness that root-interval is not the only metric.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming root-interval distance predicts voice-leading smoothness
  **Correction**: C to G requires more voice-leading work (4 units) than C to c minor (1 unit), despite the former being root-closer

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Equating triad with key in all analytical contexts
  **Clarification**: This equation works for diatonic music but obscures the voice-leading properties central to chromatic practice
