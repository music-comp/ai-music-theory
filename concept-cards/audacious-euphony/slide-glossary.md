# Quick Definition
The relation between two consonant triads that share a common third, for example C major and c# minor; or the transformation that produces one of these triads from the other.

# Formal Definition
S (Slide) is defined in the Glossary as "the relation between two consonant triads that share a common third, for example, C major and c# minor; or the transformation that produces one of these triads from the other." The transformation is an involution, involves two semitones of voice-leading work (root and fifth both move by semitone in the same direction), and preserves two common tones (the third). S is one of the three W-group transformations that map triads within Weitzmann regions. The name "slide" captures the visual effect on the Tonnetz: the perfect-fifth dyad "slides" by semitone while the third is retained.

# Construction/Recognition
Transformation specification:
- Input: Consonant triad
- Output: Consonant triad sharing the third (enharmonically)
- Voice leading: Root and fifth both move by semitone in same direction
- Work: 2 semitones total
- Common tones: 2 (the third, shared as common tone)

Algebraic properties:
- S is an involution: S(S(X)) = X
- S is a member of the W-group (along with R and N)
- S preserves Weitzmann region membership
- LP = S (the compound of L and P equals S)

# Musical Context/Application
S is characteristic of Romantic chromaticism, particularly favored by Schubert. It produces the most distant-sounding of the common-tone relationships, connecting triads whose roots are a semitone apart. On the Tonnetz, S appears as diagonal motion across the augmented triad at the center of a Weitzmann region. The identity LP = S shows how slide relations arise from hexatonic cycles.

# Examples
- S(C major) = c# minor: (C, E, G) -> (C#, E, G#)
- S(c# minor) = C major: (C#, E, G#) -> (C, E, G)
- S(F major) = f# minor
- Schubert's characteristic key relationships often involve slide
- Movement between enharmonically equivalent thirds

# Common Confusions
- S is not the same as chromatic motion; it changes mode while moving root by semitone
- The common tone is the third (not root or fifth as in P or R)
- S appears distant on the circle of fifths but close in voice-leading space
- "Slide" refers to the visual motion on the Tonnetz, not to portamento
