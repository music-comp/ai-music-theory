# Quick Definition
Cube Dance is constructed by connecting the four hexatonic cycles through the four augmented triads, creating a unified graph where every distance represents voice-leading size and directional motion correlates consistently with melodic direction.

# Formal Definition
Cube Dance, created by Jack Douthett in 1992 (published in Douthett and Steinbach 1998), is a graph of twenty-eight nodes (twenty-four consonant triads plus four augmented triads) where edges connect triads related by single semitonal displacement. It is a "true" model of voice-leading distance: "Every distance can be interpreted as representing voice-leading size" (Tymoczko 2009b). The graph is constructed by taking figure 5.2's circular arrangement and connecting constituent triads of each hexatonic region into the familiar hexatonic cycles, with cosmetic distortions aligning modally matched triads on the same side of the figure.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Place the four augmented triads at cardinal points of a circle
2. Connect each augmented triad to the six triads of its Weitzmann region (minor triads clockwise, major counterclockwise)
3. Connect the triads within each hexatonic region into hexatonic cycles (shown as circuits of unbroken lines)
4. The Weitzmann regions appear as broken-line "water bugs"
5. Clockwise motion = upshifting in pitch space; counterclockwise = downshifting

# Musical Context
Cube Dance includes both hexatonic cycles and Weitzmann regions as contiguous subgraphs, modeling all passages internal to either system. More importantly, it models progressions that cross between regions. It excels at showing cyclic closure and voice-leading trajectories.

# Common Confusions
- Cube Dance is not merely decorative; it is a mathematically rigorous model of voice-leading distance
- The "cube" appearance arises from the hexatonic cycles, not from arbitrary design
- Unlike the Tonnetz, Cube Dance gives augmented triads explicit locations as nodes
- Cube Dance excels at cyclic closure but does not show the sequential nature of progressions as clearly as the Tonnetz
