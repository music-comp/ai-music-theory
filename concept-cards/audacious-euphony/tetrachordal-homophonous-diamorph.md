# Quick Definition
Tristan-genus chords, like consonant triads, are "overdetermined creatures, homophonous diamorphs" that can rapidly switch between chromatic voice-leading syntax and diatonic tonal function, setting up the syntactic exchange theory of Chapter 8.

# Core Definition
At the conclusion of the Chapter 7 analyses, Cohn writes that "the denizens of 4-Cube Trio, like those of Cube Dance, are overdetermined creatures, homophonous diamorphs through which composers and listeners can rapidly and radically exchange syntactic expectations" (pp. 182-183). Just as consonant triads can operate under either pan-triadic (chromatic) or diatonic syntax, Tristan-genus chords can operate under Boretz-region voice-leading logic or under classical tonal function. "In each of these compositions, the summons is eventually heard, and dissonant sonorities revert to their inborn behaviors, sometimes retrospectively projecting those behaviors onto their predecessors" (p. 182).

# Prerequisites
- **Tristan genus**: The chord type demonstrating overdetermination
- **Homophonous diamorph**: The general concept of syntactically ambiguous chords
- **4-Cube Trio**: The voice-leading space within which chromatic syntax operates

# Key Properties
1. Seventh chords can suspend their appellative (tonal-functional) properties during Tristan-genus passages
2. They can reassert those properties at any moment, "reverting to their inborn behaviors"
3. The reversion may retrospectively project tonal function onto preceding chords
4. This sets up Chapter 8's theory of syntactic interaction and convertibility
5. The phenomenon parallels triadic homophonous diamorphs in Cube Dance

# Construction / Recognition
Signs of syntactic exchange in tetrachordal passages:
- A passage of parsimonious seventh-chord voice leading (chromatic syntax active)
- A moment when a chord "hearkens to its appellative properties" (tonal syntax reasserts)
- The transition may be marked by interregional modulation, change of voice-leading zone pattern, or shift to whole-step bass motion

# Context & Application
The concept bridges Chapter 7 (dissonance) and Chapter 8 (syntactic interaction). It explains why Tristan-genus analysis and tonal analysis are not competing theories but complementary descriptions of the same chords operating under different syntactic modes. "Ways to conceive and represent these syntactic exchanges are taken up in chapter 8, in the tetrachordal as well as the triadic case" (p. 183).

# Examples
- **Brunnhilde's Immolation**: "The seventh chords hearken to their appellative properties after ignoring them for some measures" (p. 174)
- **Chopin e minor Prelude**: The G# "awakened from its omnitonal haze by the whole-step motion in the bass... remembers its monotonal responsibilities" (p. 180)
- **Chopin Mazurka**: Zone 1 chords serve structural roles partly because dominant seventh chords at zone 1 can assert tonal function

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking Tristan-genus analysis and tonal analysis are mutually exclusive
  **Correction**: The same chords participate in both syntactic systems; the question is which system is foregrounded at any moment

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming syntactic exchange is a gradual process
  **Clarification**: Exchange can be "rapid and radical" -- a single chord may switch from chromatic to tonal syntax instantaneously
