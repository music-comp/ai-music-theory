# Quick Definition
Cohn's appropriation of research on multilingualism and bilingual code switching to counter Smith's objection that languages (and by analogy, musical syntaxes) are "rigidly discrete, autonomous systems" -- showing that most humans routinely process multiple linguistic systems simultaneously.

# Core Definition
Gardner-Chloros (2009, 5-7) observes that monolingualism is the exception: "if you add together people who live in multilingual areas of the world...; people who speak a regional language or dialect on top of a national language...; and migrants and their descendants..., you are left with small islands of monolingualism in a multilingual sea" (qtd. in Cohn, p. 219). Two theoretical models of code switching exist (p. 220): the "classical" model maintains asymmetric languages (one "matrix" into which items from the other are inserted); more recent evidence suggests equivalent knowledge and symmetric shuttling.

Cohn deploys this evidence to "reopen the door that Smith attempted to close with his appeal to the autonomy of natural languages" (p. 219).

# Prerequisites
- **Double syntax objections**: The claims (especially Smith's) being addressed

# Key Properties
1. Monolingualism is the global exception, not the norm
2. Speakers routinely switch 15+ times in rapid speech without flow interruption
3. Switching behavior is largely unconscious
4. Both asymmetric and symmetric models exist for how languages interact
5. The evidence does not mandate conclusions about music but provides "strong motivation for reevaluating" dismissals

# Construction / Recognition
Two key parallels between linguistic and musical code switching:
- **Asymmetric**: One "matrix" language with insertions (analogous to diatonic syntax as matrix with chromatic insertions)
- **Symmetric**: Equal shuttling between two systems (analogous to equal treatment of diatonic and chromatic syntax)

Key distinction: "The evidence does not in itself mandate any conclusions about music, which is no language" (p. 222). But "as cognitive and neural systems, music and language are closely related" (Patel 2008, 417).

# Context & Application
The multilingualism analogy addresses the first of Cohn's three responses to double syntax objections. It is followed by the cognitive opacity argument (addressing Dahlhaus/Lerdahl) and the historical framework (the soft revolution).

Slobin (1992) is noted as the only musicologist to have mined bilingualism research; Cohn suggests "the theoretical and empirical explosion in bilingual studies suggests that a return visit might be productive" (p. 221, n. 4).

# Examples
- **Gardner-Chloros's Alsatian office worker**: 15 switches between French and Alsatian in rapid phone conversation without flow interruption (p. 221)
- **Woolard (1998)**: "alternation between rigidly discrete systems [is] a mythic ground of analysis" (p. 220)
- **Slobin (1992)**: The only prior musical application of bilingualism research (p. 221, n. 4)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: The analogy claims music is literally language
  **Correction**: The claim is about shared cognitive architecture, not linguistic identity

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Bilingual evidence proves double syntax in music
  **Clarification**: It provides "strong motivation for reevaluating" dismissals, not proof; music has distinctive properties
