# Quick Definition
The conceptualization of consonant triads as objects within twelve-tone chromatic pitch-class space, where relationships are determined by voice-leading proximity rather than diatonic scale membership.

# Formal Definition
Viewing triads in chromatic space means measuring distances and relationships against the chromatic collection of 12 pitch classes rather than against 7-note diatonic collections. This perspective declines to interpret triads primarily in terms of the number of diatonic degrees separating their roots from some tonic. Instead, triadic proximity is assessed by common-tone retention and voice-leading work within the chromatic universe. This approach, developed from post-tonal set theory, provides systematic tools for exploring properties and relations of chords within the chromatic universe.

# Construction/Recognition
In chromatic space:
- Distance is measured in semitones (the minimal unit)
- All 12 pitch classes are equidistant from their chromatic neighbors
- Enharmonic pairs (F#/Gb) are identical
- The space is cyclic (modulo 12 arithmetic)

Triads are positioned by:
- Their pitch-class content (as 3-element subsets of the 12-pc universe)
- Their voice-leading distances to all other triads
- Their common-tone connections
- Their relation to perfectly even divisions (augmented triads)

# Musical Context/Application
Viewing triads in chromatic space:
- Cuts against classical theory's diatonic privilege
- Enables systematic analysis of passages that resist diatonic interpretation
- Reveals voice-leading connections invisible to root-based analysis
- Motivates geometric representations (Tonnetz, orbifolds, Cube Dance)

This perspective has been developed since the late 18th century by theorists dissatisfied with purely diatonic explanations:
- Galeazzi (1796): common-tone relations independent of diatonic membership
- Fetis (1832-1844): recognized that chromaticism could lead to "total destruction of the scale"
- Kurth (1920): "absolute progressions" operating outside tonal determination

# Examples
- Schubert's enharmonic passages: triads progress smoothly in chromatic space while creating paradoxes in diatonic space
- Chromatic sequences: regular patterns that divide chromatic space evenly (by major thirds, minor thirds, etc.)
- The Tonnetz: a geometric representation that displays triads' chromatic relationships

# Common Confusions
- Chromatic space is not "atonal" - triads remain consonant, but their organization differs from diatonic tonality
- Chromatic space uses modular (mod 12) arithmetic, which listeners perform unconsciously (like clock arithmetic)
- Analyzing in chromatic space does not deny diatonic function where it applies; it provides an alternative for passages where diatonic analysis fails
- The chromatic collection is not merely the diatonic collection "with added notes" but an independent organizing framework
