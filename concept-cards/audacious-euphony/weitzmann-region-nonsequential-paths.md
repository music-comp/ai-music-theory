# Quick Definition
Paths through a Weitzmann region that do not follow cyclic or sequential patterns, exploiting the region's flat-terrain structure where all triadic pairs are equidistant.

# Formal Definition
Because Weitzmann regions are flat terrains (all pairs 2 voice-leading units apart), not all progressions through them will be sequential or cyclic. The N/R cycle is one possible ordering, but the region's structure permits many other trajectories. Composers can explore regions through nonsequential paths that take advantage of the uniform voice-leading distances.

# Construction/Recognition
In a Weitzmann region, any path through the six triads requires the same total voice-leading work if visiting each triad once:
- Sequential path (N/R cycle): 6 triads, 10 units total
- Nonsequential path: Same 6 triads, same total work, different order

The "flat terrain" allows compositional freedom in ordering regional triads.

# Musical Context/Application
Nonsequential paths became increasingly common as composers moved beyond the Classical syntactic routines that favored N/R ordering. Wagner and Strauss exploited this freedom extensively, creating progressions that explore regions without following predictable cyclic patterns.

# Examples
- Wagner, Parsifal, Act 1, mm. 404-13: e minor spins into its Weitzmann region, sounding all three minor triads in succession, then all three major triads, before resolving to F# major dominant via augmented sixth
- Strauss, "Fruhling" opening: Explores GBEb region through nonsequential path (c minor - ab minor - B major, then e minor - G major - Eb major)

# Common Confusions
- Nonsequential does not mean random; composers still create coherent voice-leading trajectories
- The flat structure enables but does not require nonsequential exploration
- Nonsequential paths may still partition the region into modal subsets (all minor, then all major)
