# Quick Definition
The uncanny semiotics of hexatonic progressions refers to the association of chromatic major-third relations with supernatural, altered, or liminal states in nineteenth-century music, rooted in the paradoxical properties of these progressions rather than mere convention.

# Formal Definition
Hexatonic progressions (especially hexatonic poles and LP/PL transformations) "frequently depict sublime, supernatural, or exotic phenomena" (p. 40) in Romantic music and film scores. Cohn argues this association is not arbitrary but iconic: the progressions signify altered states through a "homology between the signifying progression and the signified affect" (p. 42). The paradox of contrary motion producing consonance/dissonance ambiguity mirrors the psychological uncanny - "breakdowns in the division between otherwise securely demarcated categories, prototypically the boundary between reality and illusion, or life and death" (p. 42).

# Construction/Recognition
Sources of the uncanny effect:

1. Consonance/dissonance ambiguity:
- A perfect fifth expanding by contrary semitones "ought" to become a diminished seventh (diatonic logic)
- But the resulting nine-semitone interval is heard as a consonant major sixth
- "The ear is caught in a liminal space, where the binary distinction between consonance and dissonance is eroded" (p. 41)

2. Tonal multistability:
- Each triad contains the other's leading tone
- No triad can establish clear tonal dominance
- "Like the Escher hands drawing each other's sleeves" (p. 43)

3. Contrary motion without progression:
- The progressions "go somewhere" harmonically while "going nowhere" melodically
- Local fluctuation underlies global stasis

# Musical Context/Application
In Peircean terms, hexatonic progressions are "icons rather than symbols of altered or destabilized mental states" (p. 42). The semiotic relationship is motivated, not arbitrary, making these progressions particularly effective for depicting uncanny subject matter.

# Examples
From the text (pp. 40-42):
Supernatural/altered state depictions:
- Schubert, "Nacht und Traume": "nocturnal fixation on evanescent dreams"
- Wagner, Ring (Tarnhelm): invisibility, "fallen in from another world"
- Rimsky-Korsakov, Antar: "reclusive Antar adrift on the sands of the Sahara"
- Wagner, Parsifal: Klingsor, "recluse in the Arabian wasteland"

Hexatonic pole depictions (p. 41):
- Kundry's de-souling (Parsifal)
- Dead Siegfried shaking his fist (Gotterdammerung)
- Scarpia's murder (Tosca)
- Aase's arrival at St. Peter's Gates (Peer Gynt)
- Salome singing to Jochanaan's severed head (Strauss)
- Schoenberg's self-portrait in death (String Trio, Op. 45)

# Common Confusions
- The uncanny effect is not purely conventional (learned association) but has a structural basis
- Not all chromatic third relations are equally "uncanny" - the effect is strongest for hexatonic poles and LP progressions
- The semiotics developed historically alongside the musical practice; it was not predetermined
- Film score usage of these progressions often draws on this 19th-century tradition
