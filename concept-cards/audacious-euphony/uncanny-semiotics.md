# Quick Definition
The association of chromatic major-third relations with supernatural, altered, or liminal states in nineteenth-century music, rooted in the paradoxical properties of these progressions (consonance/dissonance ambiguity and tonal multistability) rather than mere convention.

# Core Definition
Hexatonic progressions "frequently depict sublime, supernatural, or exotic phenomena" in Romantic music and film scores (Cohn, p. 40). Cohn argues this association is iconic rather than arbitrary: "the affective power of the progressions in figure 2.3 derives from a paradoxical characteristic that is inherent to them, when they are heard against the expectations of classical diatonic tonality" (p. 41). The paradox: a perfect fifth expanding by contrary semitones "ought" to become a dissonant diminished seventh (by diatonic logic), yet the resulting nine-semitone interval is heard as a consonant major sixth. "The ear is caught in a liminal space, where the binary distinction between consonance and dissonance is eroded. Such breakdowns in the division between otherwise securely demarcated categories, prototypically the boundary between reality and illusion, or life and death, are a mark of the psychological uncanny" (p. 41). In Peircean terms, these progressions are "icons rather than symbols of altered or destabilized mental states" (p. 41).

# Key Properties
1. The effect is iconic (structurally motivated), not purely conventional
2. Sources of the uncanny: consonance/dissonance ambiguity, tonal multistability, contrary motion without directional progress
3. The "default principle" of diatonic hearing produces "contradictory information" when applied to hexatonic progressions (p. 41)
4. The effect is overdetermined: both consonance/dissonance ambiguity and tonal multistability contribute

# Construction / Recognition
Three sources of the uncanny effect:
1. **Consonance/dissonance ambiguity**: A perfect fifth expanding by semitones produces a nine-semitone interval that is simultaneously a dissonant diminished seventh (by diatonic logic) and a consonant major sixth (by acoustic default) (p. 41)
2. **Tonal multistability**: Each triad contains the other's leading tone, creating mutual tonic implications "like the Escher hands drawing each other's sleeves" (p. 43)
3. **Contrary motion without progression**: Voices rise and fall but the overall pitch center remains static

# Context & Application
While Taruskin (2005) recognizes the semantic charge of these progressions but implies the link is purely conventional, Cohn argues for a "homology between the signifying progression and the signified affect" (p. 41). The effect extends from nineteenth-century music to film scores.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 40): LP progressions depicting the supernatural: Schubert's "Nacht und Traume" (nocturnal dreams), Wagner's Tarnhelm (invisibility), Rimsky-Korsakov's Antar (desert solitude), Wagner's Klingsor (Arabian sorcerer).

**Example 2** (p. 40): Hexatonic pole (H) depictions: "Kundry's de-souling (*Parsifal*), the dead Siegfried shaking his fist (*Gotterdammerung*), Scarpia's murder (*Tosca*), Aase's arrival at St. Peter's Gates (*Peer Gynt*), Strauss's Salome singing to the severed head of Jochanaan, and Schoenberg's self-portrait in death."

**Example 3** (pp. 41-42): Wagner's Tarnhelm: "aural sense of eerie power" (Darcy), "as if excavated from primeval time" (Abbate).

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the uncanny effect is purely conventional (learned association)
  **Correction**: Cohn argues for a structural basis: "icons rather than symbols" (p. 41)

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking all chromatic third relations are equally "uncanny"
  **Clarification**: The effect is strongest for hexatonic poles and LP progressions; minor-third relations lack the balanced voice leading that produces the paradox
