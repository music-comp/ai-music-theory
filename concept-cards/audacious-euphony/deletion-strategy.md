# Quick Definition
The deletion strategy handles dissonance by suppressing dissonant harmonies from the analysis, treating nonadjacent consonant triads as if directly adjacent, thereby modeling a composition as a succession of consonant triads.

# Core Definition
**Deletion** is implicit in eighteenth-century modulatory theory, which interprets one level of a composition's structure as a succession of keys standing in one-to-one relationship with their eponymous consonant triads. Combined with Rameau's theory of imputed dissonance -- which held that only tonic triads are consonant, all others bearing an acoustically suppressed sixth or seventh -- the implication is that "a triadic progression is coextensive with its progression of tonics" (p. 157). The analyst's job is "not to assign triadic constituencies to dissonant harmonies... but rather to delete them from the analysis." Schenker adopted a different version: stipulating that consonances alone are capable of prolongation, he represented "nonadjacent consonant triads as register-specific pitches that participate in linear strands, or voices, whose coordinated behavior is available for study by analogy with the behavior of voices in a direct succession of consonances" (p. 158).

# Prerequisites
- **Consonant triad**: Deletion assumes a clear category of consonant triads that form the structural skeleton
- **Four approaches to dissonance**: Understanding deletion as one of four complementary strategies

# Key Properties
1. Deletion treats nonadjacent consonances as if directly connected
2. The strategy is implicit in much standard analysis without being named
3. Rameau, Riemann, and Schenker all adopted versions of deletion, though inflected differently
4. Unlike Rameau or Riemann, Schenker did not require deleted consonances to have local tonic status

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify structural consonant triads in the passage
2. Remove intervening dissonant harmonies from the analytical representation
3. Connect the remaining consonances as if adjacent
4. Represent the result in voice-leading graphs or key-scheme diagrams

# Context & Application
Cohn says little about deletion in Chapter 7 because it "tacitly underlies many analyses already presented and to which Anglophone readers are habituated by Schenkerian practice" (p. 158). The strategy is most useful when focusing on large-scale tonal structure, where dissonances function as embellishing or passing harmonies. Its limitation is that it may miss significant voice-leading relationships among the dissonances themselves.

# Examples
- **Modulatory analysis**: Analyzing a composition's key scheme as a list of tonics deletes the dominant sevenths and other dissonances connecting them (p. 157)
- **Rameau's imputed dissonance**: Only tonic triads are truly consonant; all others bear acoustically suppressed sixths or sevenths (p. 157)
- **Schenkerian graphs**: Register-specific pitches participate in linear strands, with dissonances either reduced to unstemmed notes or omitted entirely (p. 158)

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Rameau's modulatory theory (keys as proxy for triads)
- Schenkerian prolongation (consonances alone prolong)
## Enables
- Large-scale tonal analysis of chromatic music
- Pan-triadic analysis by suppressing dissonant surface
## Related
- Reduction strategy (alternative that assigns triadic identity rather than suppressing)
## Contrasts With
- Reduction strategy (reduction keeps the dissonant chord and identifies its triadic core; deletion removes it entirely)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming deletion means dissonances are unimportant
  **Correction**: Deletion is an analytical strategy, not a claim about musical insignificance; the same passage may reward analysis via other strategies

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Conflating deletion with reduction
  **Clarification**: Deletion removes dissonant harmonies from the analysis; reduction assigns them a triadic identity. These are fundamentally different operations.
- **Confusion**: Thinking Schenkerian deletion requires local tonic status
  **Clarification**: Schenker rejected local tonics; what matters is whether the consonance is capable of prolongation, not whether it bears local tonic function
