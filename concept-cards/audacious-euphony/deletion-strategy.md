# Quick Definition
The deletion strategy for handling dissonance involves suppressing or ignoring dissonant harmonies in analysis, treating nonadjacent consonant triads as if directly adjacent, thereby modeling composition as a succession of consonant triads.

# Formal Definition
**Deletion** is one of four historical strategies for incorporating dissonance into harmonic theory. It involves:
1. Modeling a composition as a succession of consonant triads (or keys)
2. Deleting dissonant harmonies from the analysis
3. Treating nonadjacent consonances as directly adjacent
4. Representing the resulting consonant progression as coordinated voice strands

Historical origins:
- Implicit in eighteenth-century modulatory theory (succession of keys = succession of triads)
- Related to Rameau's theory of imputed dissonance (only tonics are truly consonant)
- Systematized in Schenker's graphs (only consonances prolong)

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Deletion operates by:
1. Identifying structural consonant triads
2. Removing intervening dissonances from the analytical representation
3. Connecting the remaining consonances as if adjacent
4. Representing the result in voice-leading graphs

Unlike Rameau or Riemann, Schenker does not require deleted consonances to have local tonic status; what matters is their capacity for prolongation.

Schenkerian notation:
- Consonances appear as stemmed notes
- Dissonances appear as unstemmed passing/neighbor tones or are omitted
- Linear strands connect consonances across intervening dissonances

# Musical Context/Application
Deletion is useful when:
- Focusing on large-scale tonal structure
- Dissonances function as passing or embellishing harmonies
- The analyst wants to emphasize consonant pillars

Limitations:
- May miss significant dissonant events
- Assumes consonance/dissonance hierarchy
- Can obscure voice-leading relationships among dissonances

Cohn notes that deletion tacitly underlies many analyses in the book and is familiar to Anglophone readers through Schenkerian practice.

# Examples
**Schenkerian analysis generally**: Graphs represent nonadjacent consonant triads as register-specific pitches participating in linear strands, with dissonances either reduced to unstemmed notes or omitted entirely.

**Rameau's imputed dissonance**: Only tonic triads are truly consonant; all others bear acoustically suppressed sixths or sevenths. A triadic progression is thus coextensive with its progression of tonics.

**Modulatory analysis**: When analyzing a composition's key scheme, analysts typically list only the tonics, deleting the dominant seventh chords and other dissonances that connect them.

# Common Confusions
- Deletion is not denial; dissonances exist but are analytically suppressed
- Deletion differs from reduction; reduction assigns triadic identity to dissonances, deletion removes them
- Schenkerian deletion doesn't require local tonic status, unlike Rameau's approach
- The strategy is implicit in many analyses without being named
