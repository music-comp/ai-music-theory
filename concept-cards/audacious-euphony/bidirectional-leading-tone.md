# Quick Definition
In 19th-century German theory, the leading tone (Leitton) was understood as a bidirectional relationship: B leads up to C (as 7-8 in C major), but C also leads down to B (as 6-5 in e minor), creating reciprocal tonal attractions.

# Core Definition
"The modern conception of leading tone is restricted to the relationship between a tonic and its semitonal lower neighbor. For German theorists of the middle of the nineteenth century, this relationship captured only one half of a duality: Leitton applies equally to the relationship of dominant and its semitonal upper neighbor" (p. 64). When C major and e minor are juxtaposed (Leittonwechsel), "the attraction of C to B (as 6-5 in e minor) is as strong as that of B to C (as 7-8 in C major). The semitonal relation thus projects an unstable force field that pulls simultaneously in both directions" (p. 64).

# Prerequisites
- **Consonant triad**: Must understand major and minor triads and their tonal functions to grasp the bidirectional attraction

# Key Properties
1. The upward leading tone (B to C) represents the modern, restricted conception
2. The downward leading tone (C to B, as upper neighbor to dominant) was equally recognized in 19th-century German theory
3. When contexts are ambiguous, both directions are equally active
4. The bidirectional conception contributes to tonal indeterminacy in Leittonwechsel relations
5. The concept originates in dualist thinking but was adopted by non-dualists

# Construction / Recognition
- In C major context: B is active (leading tone up to tonic C)
- In e minor context: C is active (upper neighbor descending to dominant B)
- When contexts are ambiguous: both pitches are equally active, creating an "unstable force field"

# Context & Application
The bidirectional leading tone is one of Cohn's examples of reciprocity between consonant triads. It helps explain the tonal indeterminacy of L-related (Leittonwechsel) triads and the broader principle that semitonal relations project unstable force fields in chromatic music.

# Examples
**Theoretical basis** (p. 64): "For German theorists of the middle of the nineteenth century, this relationship captured only one half of a duality: Leitton applies equally to the relationship of dominant and its semitonal upper neighbor."

**Harrison 1994** (p. 64): "Harrison 1994 provides an excellent elaboration on these matters." The conception was taken up by "theorists with no commitment to dualism, such as Louis and Thuille [1913], Kurth 1923, and Lorenz 1933."

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the bidirectional conception as a universal acoustic truth
  **Correction**: This is a theoretical construct specific to 19th-century German theory, not a claim about acoustics

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The modern (unidirectional) and historical (bidirectional) conceptions are equivalent
  **Clarification**: Modern theory typically restricts "leading tone" to 7-8; the bidirectional conception is a distinct historical position
- **Confusion**: Bidirectionality determines tonal ambiguity
  **Clarification**: It contributes to but does not determine ambiguity; context can resolve the force field
