# Quick Definition
A method for calculating triadic distance based on the total semitonal displacement required to transform one triad into another under idealized voice leading.

# Formal Definition
The voice-leading approach measures triadic proximity by summing the absolute value of semitonal motion in each voice when triads are connected by idealized voice leading. This metric attends not only to the number of moving voices but also to the distance each voice travels. A unit of "voice-leading work" is defined as the motion of one voice by one semitone. The approach assumes octave equivalence and pairs chord tones one-to-one to minimize total displacement.

# Construction/Recognition
For triads X and Y with pitch classes {x1, x2, x3} and {y1, y2, y3}:
Voice-leading work = min(|x1-y1| + |x2-y2| + |x3-y3|) over all pairings

where distances are measured in semitones (mod 12, taking the smaller of the two possible directions).

Examples:
- C major to c minor: 1 unit (E to Eb)
- C major to Gb major: 2 units (C to Cb, E to Gb or C to Db, G to Gb)
- C major to f# minor: 3 units (C to C#, E to F#, G to A)

The maximum voice-leading work between consonant triads is 6 units; the minimum (excluding identity) is 1 unit.

# Musical Context/Application
The voice-leading approach:
- Has ancient roots: Marchettus of Padua (early 14th c.) articulated "closest approach" preference for semitonal voice leading
- Zarlino (1558): preferred minor third to unison because minor third "is closer"
- Capellen (c. 1900): proposed triadic connections based on common tones plus semitonal motions
- Provides the finest gradations of distance (6 distinct values vs. 3 for common-tone metric)

This approach underlies the pan-triadic model: triads related by single semitonal displacement form the closest connections in the triadic universe.

# Examples
- Schubert, Piano Sonata in Bb: total voice-leading work of 14 units across four triads positions the progression toward the lower end (more conjunct) of the range
- Gb major to f# minor: 1 unit of work (Bb to A) - enharmonic "conversion" costs only one semitone
- The voice-leading approach distinguishes between progressions that common-tone counting conflates (f# minor to Gb major: 1 unit vs. f# minor to A major: 2 units, though both share 2 common tones)

# Common Confusions
- Voice-leading work is measured in pitch-class space, not pitch space - it abstracts from actual registral motion
- Minimum voice-leading does not always mean maximum common-tone retention (C to g# minor: 3 units, 0 common tones vs. C to d: 4 units, 0 common tones)
- The metric assumes idealized voice leading regardless of how the music is actually voiced
- Some theorists (Tymoczko) argue voice leading should be measured along paths in circular space, distinguishing up from down
