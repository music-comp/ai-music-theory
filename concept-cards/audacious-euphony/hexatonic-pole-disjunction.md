# Quick Definition
A four-chord complex combining two zone-diametric triads with their hexatonic poles, producing four triads with no pitch-class overlap that exhaust the twelve-tone aggregate.

# Formal Definition
The hexatonic-pole disjunction complex pairs two zone-diametric triads (related by T2 or T6) with their respective hexatonic poles. The resulting four triads share no pitch classes and together use all twelve tones. This complex uniquely achieves aggregate completion through consonant triads while maintaining systematic voice-leading structure.

# Construction/Recognition
Structure:
- Two zone-diametric major (or minor) triads: e.g., C major and D major (T2)
- Their hexatonic poles: e minor and eb minor
- Combined: {C, D, e, eb} in triadic terms
- Zones rotate through positions like {1, 8, 7, 2}

Properties:
- No pitch-class intersection between any pair
- All twelve pitch classes used exactly once
- Each major triad paired with its H-related minor
- The pair of H-related pairs are zone-diametric to each other

J.S. Bach's version:
- Major triad followed by hexatonic pole, then transposed by T2
- Rotates through all twelve zones in two statements

# Musical Context
This complex became a trope of disjunction in the long nineteenth century, appearing in Mozart, Chopin, Schumann, Wagner, Debussy, Elgar, and others. It represents maximum triadic disjunction with aggregate completion. Under permutation, the same complex appears in different orderings across the repertoire.

# Examples
- Bach Prelude (Dahlhaus 1967b, p. 87): Major -> H-pole -> T2 transposition
- Mozart c minor Fantasy
- Chopin G major Nocturne (mm. 25-27 and mm. 1-12 permuted)
- Wagner Parsifal
- Debussy Prelude to the Afternoon of a Faun

# Common Confusions
- The aggregate completion comes from the combined structure, not coincidence
- Permutation of the four chords creates different compositional effects
- This exhausts (to within transposition) ways of distributing the aggregate among four consonant triads
