# Quick Definition
The principles governing how musical elements (particularly harmonies) combine, sequence, and relate to form coherent musical utterances, analogous to but distinct from linguistic syntax.

# Core Definition
Cohn uses "syntax" as "a problematic term whose chief virtue is that it is better than its alternatives" (p. 19, footnote 1). He employs it at three levels: (1) contrasted with phonology/lexicon, where phonology treats internal chord structure and syntax governs their combination; (2) sequential ordering, how triads progress moment-by-moment; and (3) most abstractly, following the Greek *harmonia*, as "the means of codifying the relationship between those notes that constituted the framework of the tonal system" — what Sessions called "relationships between tones and the organization which the ear deduces from those relationships" (synthesized from Ch. 1, pp. 35-39). It is at this third level that different "syntaxes" (diatonic, pan-triadic) can be distinguished.

# Key Properties
1. Musical syntax governs which sequences of chords are normative and which are aberrant
2. Different syntaxes generate different orderings, doublings, expectations about dissonance
3. "Syntactic" and "coherent" are relative to a community's expectations (p. 38)
4. Multiple syntaxes can coexist in the same piece, processed by the same listener
5. Calling something "asyntactic" relative to one system does not mean it lacks organization

# Construction / Recognition
Musical syntax encompasses:
- What sequences of chords are "grammatical" (normative, expected)
- What sequences are "ungrammatical" (aberrant, surprising)
- How distance and proximity are measured
- What constitutes conjunct vs. disjunct motion
- How patterns generate expectations

# Context & Application
The concept of multiple coexisting syntaxes helps explain how passages can be coherent by one criterion and incoherent by another, and why some progressions "go down easy" (p. 38). Cohn notes that Fetis's distinction between the "law of tonality" and the "law of uniformity" anticipates the idea that different syntactic principles can operate within the same musical practice.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 38): Cohn cites Feld and Fox's characterization of syntax as relating to what, "for individuals or communities or cultures, there are things that make sense and things that don't."

**Example 2** (pp. 35-39): Diatonic syntax (I-IV-V-I) and pan-triadic syntax (hexatonic cycles) are two distinct syntactic systems that can operate in the same piece.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating "syntactic" as synonymous with "rule-governed" in a prescriptive sense
  **Correction**: Syntax describes what a community perceives as coherent, not prescriptive rules

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Equating musical syntax with linguistic syntax
  **Clarification**: Music lacks the referential dimension of language; the analogy is structural, not semantic
- **Confusion**: Assuming a passage that is asyntactic in one system is incoherent
  **Clarification**: It may be syntactic relative to another system
