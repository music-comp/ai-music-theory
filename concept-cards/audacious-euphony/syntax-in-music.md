# Quick Definition
The principles governing how musical elements (particularly harmonies) combine, sequence, and relate to form coherent musical utterances, analogous to but distinct from linguistic syntax.

# Formal Definition
Cohn uses "syntax" in three related senses:
1. **Contrasted with phonology/lexicon**: While phonology treats the internal structure of atomistic units (chords) and lexicon lists available sounds, syntax governs their combination
2. **Sequential ordering**: How triads "progress" moment-by-moment and at deeper structural levels
3. **Systemic organization**: Following the Greek *harmonia*, "the means of codifying the relationship between those notes that constituted the framework of the tonal system" - what Sessions called "relationships between tones and the organization which the ear deduces from those relationships"

It is at this third, most abstract level that different "syntaxes" (diatonic, pan-triadic) can be distinguished.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Musical syntax encompasses:
- What sequences of chords are "grammatical" (normative, expected)
- What sequences are "ungrammatical" (aberrant, surprising)
- How distance and proximity are measured
- What constitutes conjunct vs. disjunct motion
- How patterns generate expectations

Different syntaxes (Fetis's "laws of tonality" vs. "uniformity"; diatonic vs. pan-triadic) generate:
- Different orderings of harmonies
- Different doublings of chords
- Different expectations about dissonance treatment
- Different modes of musical cognition

# Musical Context/Application
The concept of multiple coexisting syntaxes helps explain:
- How passages can be coherent by one criterion and incoherent by another
- Why some progressions "go down easy" and others "go down hard"
- How listeners navigate between different modes of organization
- Why 19th-century chromatic music was perceived as both audacious and euphonious

Cohn notes that "syntactic" and "coherent" are not absolute judgments but relative to a community's expectations: "for individuals or communities or cultures, there are things that make sense and things that don't."

# Examples
- Diatonic syntax: I-IV-V-I "goes down easy" for classically trained listeners
- Pan-triadic syntax: Hexatonic cycles are coherent by voice-leading criteria despite diatonic irregularity
- Fetis's "law of uniformity": Sequences suspend tonal syntax in favor of pattern-based expectations
- The distinction is analogous to linguists' "emic" (fits the worldview of a particular community)

# Common Confusions
- Musical syntax is not identical to linguistic syntax - music lacks the referential dimension of language
- "Syntactic" does not mean "rule-governed" in a prescriptive sense but describes what a community perceives as coherent
- Multiple syntaxes can coexist in the same piece, processed by the same listener
- Calling something "asyntactic" relative to one system does not mean it lacks organization - it may be syntactic relative to another system
