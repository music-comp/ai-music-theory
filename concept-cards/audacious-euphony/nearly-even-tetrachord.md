# Quick Definition
A nearly even tetrachord is a four-note chord minimally deviating from the perfectly even diminished seventh chord by single semitonal displacement, encompassing dominant seventh and half-diminished seventh chords -- the Tristan genus.

# Core Definition
**Nearly even tetrachords** are four-note chords formed by displacing one tone of a perfectly even diminished seventh chord by a single semitone. Two species result: **dominant seventh** (downward displacement, interval content [4,3,3,2]) and **half-diminished seventh** (upward displacement, interval content [3,3,4,2]). Both belong to set-class 4-27, prime form [0258], related by inversion. Together they constitute the Tristan genus, a tetrachordal system analogous to the system of consonant triads (nearly even trichords). The analogy was "initially reported in Cohn 1996" and "worked out in five papers presented at Buffalo conferences in 1993 and 1997" by Lewin, Callender, Childs, Gollin, and Douthett & Steinbach (p. 167 n. 5).

# Prerequisites
- **Nearly even chord**: The general concept that nearly even tetrachords instantiate for n=4
- **Diminished seventh chord**: The perfectly even core from which nearly even tetrachords derive

# Key Properties
1. Each diminished seventh generates 8 nearly even tetrachords (4 V7 + 4 ø7)
2. Three diminished sevenths yield 24 total Tristan-genus chords (12 V7 + 12 ø7)
3. This mirrors: each augmented triad generates 6 nearly even trichords (3 major + 3 minor)
4. Any two nearly even tetrachords in the same Boretz region are exactly 2 voice-leading units apart
5. The minor third is to tetrachords what the major third is to trichords: it divides the octave into as many equal parts as the chord has tones

# Construction / Recognition
- **Perfectly even tetrachord**: Diminished seventh [0369], e.g., B-D-F-G#
- **Downward displacement**: Lower one tone by semitone to create V7 (e.g., G# down to G yields G-B-D-F)
- **Upward displacement**: Raise one tone by semitone to create ø7 (e.g., D up to D# yields D#-F#-A-C#, enharmonically)
- Both types are "nearly even" because they minimally deviate from perfect symmetry

# Context & Application
The concept of nearly even tetrachords extends pan-triadic theory to seventh chords, demonstrating that voice-leading parsimony principles apply beyond triads. However, the triadic case retains special privilege: "from the standpoint of minimal voice leading, the ideal situation occurs when n = 3. It is only then that n - 2 = 1, that is, that a bridging motion involves only a single unit of voice-leading work" (p. 184). For n=4, bridging motions involve 2 units of work, making the tetrachordal case less parsimonious at the boundaries between regions.

# Examples
- **Comparison to triads**: Just as C major and C minor are both single semitonal displacements of the C-E-G# augmented triad, G7 and G#ø7 are both single semitonal displacements of a diminished seventh core
- **The Tristan Prelude opening**: All five initial chords are nearly even tetrachords displacing the B-D-F-G# diminished seventh (pp. 167-168)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Including minor seventh or major seventh chords as nearly even tetrachords
  **Correction**: Only V7 and ø7 are nearly even tetrachords; minor and major sevenths are combination-generated chords of different set classes

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking "nearly even" is defined relative to a diatonic scale
  **Clarification**: It is defined relative to the perfectly even division of the chromatic octave (the diminished seventh chord)
