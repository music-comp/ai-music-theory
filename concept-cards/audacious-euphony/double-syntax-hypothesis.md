# Quick Definition
The hypothesis that the human mind can organize musical patterns simultaneously or in immediate succession according to two distinct and incompatible syntactic systems.

# Formal Definition
The double syntax hypothesis proposes that listeners and analysts can process music according to multiple organizational principles operating at different structural levels or in rapid alternation. In the context of 19th-century triadic music, this means recognizing that classical diatonic syntax and pan-triadic (chromatic) syntax can coexist within a single composition, with the mind capable of switching between them "quasi-instantaneously" or "multitasking" between them.

# Construction/Recognition
The hypothesis addresses the cognitive challenge posed by music that:
- Is locally diatonic but globally chromatic (or vice versa)
- Contains passages organized by voice-leading proximity nested within diatonic frameworks
- Requires different parsing strategies at different structural levels
- Exhibits properties that are coherent under one system but paradoxical under the other

The analogy is to Fetis's model of his four "tonal species," where the best composers know how to combine distinct syntactic principles in a single work.

# Musical Context
The double syntax hypothesis responds to objections that:
- High-value music cannot partake of two systemic modes of organization
- Listeners cannot shift between syntaxes rapidly
- Multiple simultaneous syntaxes indicate analytical failure rather than compositional richness

Without accepting double syntax, analysis of 19th-century chromatic music is limited to:
- Forcing all progressions into diatonic categories (often unsuccessfully)
- Dismissing chromatic passages as random, irrational, or coloristic
- Treating voice-leading analysis as incompatible with functional analysis

# Examples
- Schubert Piano Sonata in Bb major, D. 960, recapitulation: locally diatonic spans (each key area internally coherent) within a globally chromatic framework (key areas related by voice-leading rather than diatonic function)
- Fetis's concept of diatonic sequences: when a sequence is recognized, the "law of tonality" is suspended and "law of uniformity" takes over
- Kurth's "absolute progressions": chromatic sequences governed by repetition logic rather than tonal function

# Common Confusions
- Double syntax does not mean music is disorganized or lacks coherence - it means coherence operates through multiple systems
- The hypothesis does not claim both systems are always active - they may alternate or one may dominate
- Accepting double syntax does not require abandoning traditional tonal analysis - it supplements it
- The hypothesis is about cognitive capacity, not about compositional intent (though composers may have consciously exploited these possibilities)
