# Quick Definition
The hypothesis that the mind is capable of organizing musical patterns, simultaneously or in immediate succession, in two distinct and incompatible ways: diatonic tonal syntax (based on acoustic consonance) and pan-triadic chromatic syntax (based on voice-leading parsimony).

# Core Definition
The double syntax hypothesis proposes that "the mind is capable of organizing musical patterns, simultaneously or in immediate succession, in two distinct and incompatible ways" (Glossary, p. 229). In the context of nineteenth-century triadic music, "these two incommensurate ways of measuring triadic distance emerge respectively from two independent properties of consonant triads" (p. 216): their acoustic consonance generates diatonic syntax, and their near-evenness generates pan-triadic chromatic syntax. The "central claim of this book" is that these dual properties lead to dual syntaxes (p. 216).

# Prerequisites
- **Pan-triadic syntax**: Must understand the chromatic alternative to diatonic tonality
- **Consonant triad**: Must understand the dual nature of the triad (acoustic and nearly even)
- **Near-evenness**: The property enabling the triad's second syntactic role

# Key Properties
1. Two syntaxes based on two independent properties of the consonant triad
2. Acoustic consonance generates diatonic syntax; near-evenness generates chromatic syntax
3. The two can operate simultaneously or in immediate succession
4. The triad is the pivot (homophonous diamorph) enabling smooth switching
5. Switching can be cognitively opaque -- occurring beneath conscious awareness

# Construction / Recognition
**Diatonic syntax**: chromatic (12+) -> diatonic (7) -> triad (3) -> tonic (1). Arrows indicate "takes its meaning from."

**Chromatic syntax**: chromatic (12) <- triad (3) <- tone (1). Arrows flip direction.

The reversal mechanism: "It is as if the arrow... is attached to the system at the point of the triad (3), where it rests on a spring-loaded pivot and is locked into position by the diatonic (7). When the diatonic lock is released, the spring uncoils and the direction of the arrow instantly reverses" (p. 222).

# Context & Application
The hypothesis is the central theoretical claim of the book and provides:
- A framework for analyzing 19th-century chromatic music without forcing all progressions into diatonic categories
- An explanation for how Webern's atonality emerged from Schubert's tonality
- A model for the listener's experience of passages that move between diatonic and chromatic organization
- The theoretical foundation for the convertible Tonnetz

The Schubert C major Symphony Scherzo provides the culminating demonstration: "Listening to the passage with reference to C major... we are hearing in terms of a system of distance relations governed by the degree to which harmonic roots are consonantly related to each other and to C major... Listening to the same passage attuned to voice leading, we hear an elimination of chordal dissonance and the elimination of whole steps" (p. 216).

# Examples
- **Schubert C major Symphony Scherzo** (mm. 93-153): The passage analyzed under both syntaxes in Figures 9.2(a) and 9.2(b) (pp. 215-216)
- **Liszt Consolation no. 3**: Each hexatonic station demonstrates local diatonic syntax within global chromatic syntax
- **Fetis's diatonic sequences**: The "law of uniformity" suspends the "law of tonality" -- an early instance of syntactic switching

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Double syntax means music is disorganized or lacks coherence
  **Correction**: Coherence operates through multiple systems, not a single one

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Both systems must always be active simultaneously
  **Clarification**: They may alternate or one may dominate; the hypothesis is about cognitive capacity, not constant co-activation

- **Confusion**: Accepting double syntax requires abandoning traditional tonal analysis
  **Clarification**: It supplements, not replaces, diatonic analysis
