# Quick Definition
Near evenness is the property of chords that are minimal perturbations of perfectly even divisions of the octave, enabling smooth voice-leading connections between chords of the same type.

# Formal Definition
A chord is nearly even if it can be formed by displacing one or more tones of a perfectly even chord by the smallest possible amount (one semitone). Consonant triads are nearly even because they are single-semitone displacements of augmented triads: major triads result from displacing one tone of an augmented triad downward by semitone; minor triads from displacing one tone upward. This property, named by Dmitri Tymoczko, underlies the capacity of triads to connect via minimal voice leading.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
The perfectly even augmented triad divides the 12-semitone octave into three equal parts (0, 4, 8). Near-even triads are formed by semitonal displacement:

- C-E-G# (augmented, perfectly even)
- C-E-G (major, G# displaced down to G) - nearly even
- C-Eb-G# (minor, but spelled as C-Eb-Ab: E displaced up to Eb... wait, actually: the TONE that was G# moved down to G to make major; a different tone of augmented moves up to make minor)

Correction per the text (p. 52-53):
- "Major triads are nearly even because they can be formed from an augmented triad by a single semitonal displacement downward"
- "Minor triads, conversely, are formed by a semitonal displacement upward"

# Musical Context/Application
Near evenness explains WHY consonant triads can voice-lead so smoothly to each other. Cohn argues: "Th e role of near evenness, with respect to the participation of triads in hexatonic progressions, is thus analogous to that of consonance, with respect to their participation in diatonic ones" (p. 56). The augmented triad functions as the "invisible axis about which pan-triadic progressions spin" (p. 52).

# Examples

Other examples of near evenness in music theory:
- Dominant and half-diminished seventh chords as minimal displacements of fully diminished seventh chords
- Mystic chords as minimal displacements of whole-tone collections
- Diatonic collections as nearly (but not perfectly) even seven-note collections
- Diatonic triads in seven-tone space are maximally but not perfectly even

# Common Confusions
- Near evenness is NOT the same as consonance, though the two properties happen to coincide for triads in 12-tone equal temperament
- Nearly even chords can be dissonant (e.g., dominant seventh chords are nearly even but contain tritones)
- The direction of displacement (up vs. down) determines whether the result is major or minor
- Near evenness is about internal structure; voice-leading efficiency is about external relations - near evenness causes voice-leading efficiency
