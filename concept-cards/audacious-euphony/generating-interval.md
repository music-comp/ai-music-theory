# Quick Definition
An interval that forms a chord or scale by recursive (repeated) application, such as the whole tone generating the whole-tone scale or the perfect fifth generating the diatonic scale.

# Core Definition
A generating interval is "an interval that forms a chord by recursive application. In a whole-tone scale, the whole tone is the generating interval; in the diatonic scale, the perfect fourth or fifth is the generating interval" (Glossary, p. 229). Starting from any pitch class and repeatedly adding the generating interval produces the complete collection. For example, the major third (4 semitones) generates the augmented triad by dividing the octave into 3 equal parts, a fact Cohn italicizes for emphasis: "the major third divides the octave into as many equal parts as a triad has notes" (p. 52). This principle underlies the special voice-leading properties of consonant triads.

# Prerequisites
- Basic interval theory and modular arithmetic

# Key Properties
1. Recursive application from any pitch class produces the complete collection
2. The number of distinct pitch classes generated depends on gcd(interval, 12)
3. Perfectly even collections result when the interval divides 12 evenly
4. The generating interval determines the collection's transpositional symmetry
5. For pan-triadic theory: the major third's role is fundamental because it divides the octave into as many parts as a triad has notes

# Construction / Recognition
For an interval i (measured in semitones):
- Cycle length = 12 / gcd(i, 12)
- Whole tone (i=2): 6-note whole-tone scale
- Minor third (i=3): 4-note diminished seventh chord
- Major third (i=4): 3-note augmented triad
- Perfect fifth (i=7): all 12 pitch classes (first 7 form diatonic scale)
- Tritone (i=6): 2-note tritone dyad
- Semitone (i=1): 12-note chromatic scale

# Context & Application
The generating interval concept explains the internal structure of perfectly even collections and connects to the book's central argument about why consonant triads have special voice-leading properties. The minor third "divides the octave into as many equal parts as a seventh chord has tones" (p. 167), paralleling the major third's relationship to triads. This parallel underlies the analogy between hexatonic systems (triadic) and Boretz regions (tetrachordal) in Chapter 7.

# Examples
- **Augmented triad**: generated by major third (interval 4), divides octave into 3
- **Diminished seventh**: generated by minor third (interval 3), divides octave into 4
- **Whole-tone scale**: generated by whole tone (interval 2), divides octave into 6
- **Diatonic scale**: generated by perfect fifth (interval 7), though presented in stepwise order
- **Chromatic scale**: generated by semitone (interval 1)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: The generating interval describes the intervals within the resulting collection
  **Correction**: It describes the interval used for recursive construction, not the internal step intervals

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Every interval generates a musically useful scale
  **Clarification**: Only intervals whose relationship with 12 produces useful cycle lengths create standard collections

- **Confusion**: The diatonic scale is generated by steps
  **Clarification**: It is generated by fifths but typically presented in stepwise order
