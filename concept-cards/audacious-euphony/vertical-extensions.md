# Quick Definition
Extensions of the diatonic Tonnetz along the vertical (third) axis, arising through semitonal substitution of scale degrees, most characteristically the raised leading tone in minor and the lowered sixth in major (nebenverwandt substitutions).

# Formal Definition
Vertical extensions occur when a semitone substitutes for a whole tone in the diatonic scale, a principle articulated by Marchettus of Padua in the early fourteenth century. The substitution of G-sharp for G-natural in A minor converts the minor dominant (e minor) to major dominant (E major), the nebenverwandt of the minor tonic. Dually, the substitution of A-flat for A-natural in C major converts the major subdominant (F major) to minor subdominant (f minor), the nebenverwandt of the major tonic. These substitutions are located on the Tonnetz directly above (sharpward) or below (flatward) the diatonic pitch they replace.

# Construction/Recognition
In A minor (Figure 8.8a):
- G-sharp replaces G-natural on dominant side
- e minor (east of tonic) becomes E major (northeast of tonic)
- E major is the nebenverwandt of a minor

In C major (Figure 8.8b):
- A-flat replaces A-natural on subdominant side
- F major (west of tonic) becomes f minor (southwest of tonic)
- f minor is the nebenverwandt of C major

Why these substitutions work:
- Voice-leading principle: A semitone may substitute for a whole tone
- Near-evenness: Single semitonal displacement preserves chord type
- The e minor to E major substitution reduces voice-leading work while maintaining harmonic consistency

# Musical Context/Application
Standard explanation (modal borrowing/mixture) has three limitations:
1. **Presumptuous**: Assumes parallel major/minor scales already exist
2. **Unparsimonious**: Borrows three tones to use only one
3. **Ahistorical**: The single-tone substitution arose before the full borrowed scales

The vertical extension is better explained by:
- Marchettus's semitone-for-whole-tone principle
- Near-evenness of the consonant triad enabling type-preserving semitonal displacement
- Fifteenth-century staging of pitch-class substitution as triadic substitution (e minor to E major)

The extension propagates: C-sharp and F-sharp arise to rectify G-sharp's dissonances with natural-scale tones, enabling encapsulation in A major region.

# Examples
- The raised leading tone in minor (G-sharp in a minor) creates E major dominant
- The lowered sixth in major (A-flat in C major) creates f minor subdominant
- Liszt Consolation no. 3 (Figure 8.7c): f minor's E-natural as raised leading tone creates vertical extension above the diatonic rank
- The tierce de Picardie and its nineteenth-century expansion (aspera to astra plot prototype) exploit acoustic gains of major-for-minor substitution

# Common Confusions
- Vertical extensions are not identical to modal mixture, though they produce similar results; the explanation differs
- The acoustic gain (major triad more purely tuned than minor) is offset by dissonance at scalar level; rectification drives further extensions
- Vertical extensions can exponentiate: The process that creates A major region from A minor can push ever northward across historical time
- e minor to E major arose earlier historically than F major to f minor, explicable only by acoustic bonus of the former
