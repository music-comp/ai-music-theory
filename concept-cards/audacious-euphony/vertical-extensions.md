# Quick Definition
Extensions of the diatonic Tonnetz along the vertical (third) axis through semitonal substitution of scale degrees, most characteristically the raised leading tone in minor and the lowered sixth in major (nebenverwandt substitutions).

# Core Definition
Vertical extensions occur when a semitone substitutes for a whole tone in the diatonic scale, a principle articulated by Marchettus of Padua in the early fourteenth century. In A minor, G-sharp replaces G-natural on the dominant side, converting e minor to E major (the nebenverwandt of the minor tonic). In C major, A-flat replaces A-natural on the subdominant side, converting F major to f minor (the nebenverwandt of the major tonic). These substitutions are located on the Tonnetz directly above (sharpward) or below (flatward) the diatonic pitch they replace (pp. 200-202).

# Prerequisites
- **Diatonic Tonnetz**: The space being vertically extended
- **Nebenverwandt**: The transformation that produces the substituted triad
- **Near-evenness**: Explains why single semitonal displacement preserves chord type

# Key Properties
1. Semitone-for-whole-tone substitution principle (Marchettus of Padua)
2. Near-evenness of the consonant triad enables type-preserving displacement
3. Reduces voice-leading work while maintaining harmonic consistency
4. Creates acoustic dissonance at the scalar level that drives further extensions
5. The process is susceptible to exponentiation: each rectification drives further northward/southward extension

# Construction / Recognition
Standard explanation (modal borrowing/mixture) has three limitations (p. 201):
1. **Presumptuous**: Assumes parallel major/minor scales already exist
2. **Unparsimonious**: Borrows three tones to discard two
3. **Ahistorical**: Single-tone substitution arose before borrowed scales

Better explanation: voice-leading principle (semitone may substitute for whole tone) combined with near-evenness (semitonal displacement preserves chord type in odd-cardinality nearly even chords). "It was only in the fifteenth century... that harmonic consistency became a compositional desideratum and that the G-natural to G-sharp substitution at the pitch-class level became staged as an e minor to E major substitution at the triadic level" (p. 202).

# Context & Application
The vertical extension propagates recursively: G-sharp enters into dissonant relations with natural-scale tones not directly connected on the Tonnetz. Rectifying this anomaly requires adding C-sharp and F-sharp above, enabling encapsulation in an A major region. This process "can push ever northward across historical time" (p. 202). An inverse procession southward is initiated with A to A-flat, triadically staged as F major to f minor.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Vertical extensions are identical to modal mixture
  **Correction**: They produce similar results but the explanatory mechanism differs (voice-leading vs. scale borrowing)

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The acoustic gain of major-for-minor substitution is the primary motivation
  **Clarification**: At the scalar level, the acoustic gain is "more than offset by an immense loss"; near-evenness and voice-leading parsimony provide the deeper explanation
