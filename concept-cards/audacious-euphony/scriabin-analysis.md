# Quick Definition
Cohn's analysis of Scriabin's Feuillet d'Album, Op. 58, demonstrates how the concept of generalized Weitzmann regions extends to six-note chords, specifically the mystic chord, showing voice-leading among nearly even hexachords.

# Formal Definition
Cohn's **Scriabin analysis** applies the generalized Weitzmann region (GWR) concept to six-note chords, specifically exploring:
- The mystic chord (Scriabin's characteristic sonority)
- The Wozzeck chord (related six-note structure)
- Both as nearly even hexachords minimally displacing whole-tone scales

This analysis demonstrates that voice-leading parsimony principles extend beyond triads and Tristan-genus chords to larger chord cardinalities, though with diminishing returns in efficiency.

# Construction/Recognition
Nearly even hexachords:
- Perfectly even six-note chord: whole-tone scale (two exist)
- Nearly even: single semitonal displacement of whole-tone scale
- Mystic chord: upward displacement (Forte class 6-34, prime form [013579])
- Wozzeck chord: downward displacement (same Forte class)

Voice-leading within a GWR:
- Any two chords are 2 voice-leading units apart (as with triads and tetrachords)
- Motion "in and out" of the whole-tone core

Bridging between GWRs:
- Would involve n-2 = 4 moving voices (far less parsimonious than triads or tetrachords)
- This explains why hexachordal GWRs are less musically useful than triadic ones

# Musical Context/Application
The analysis shows:
- How Scriabin's late harmonic language can be understood through voice-leading parsimony
- The limits of extending nearly even chord systems to larger cardinalities
- Why triads have uniquely privileged voice-leading properties

Scriabin's Feuillet d'Album demonstrates:
- Motion among three transpositions of the mystic chord
- Each chord differs from the whole-tone collection by one "spoiler" tone
- Voice-leading connections made salient through registral transfers and motivic play

# Examples
**Scriabin's Feuillet d'Album, Op. 58 (1910)**: The opening three phrases present three transpositions of the mystic chord:
1. First chord: whole-tone except D# substitutes for D
2. Second chord: D# recuperated to Ebb in bass, but E → F creates new spoiler
3. Third chord: F recuperated to E in bass, C# becomes new spoiler in alto

Scriabin makes the idealized voice leadings salient through:
- Registral transfers between spoiler and soon-to-be-spoiled tones
- Motivic voice exchanges at mm. 4, 8-9
- Direction of semitonal motion echoing direction of registral displacement

# Common Confusions
- Mystic chord analysis shows extension of the theory, not central application
- Hexachordal GWRs are less useful than triadic or tetrachordal ones
- Bridging between hexachordal GWRs would require 4 moving voices (very unparsimonious)
- The analysis demonstrates limits as much as possibilities
