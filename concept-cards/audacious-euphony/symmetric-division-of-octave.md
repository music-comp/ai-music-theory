# Quick Definition
Symmetric division of the octave refers to dividing the twelve semitones into equal parts, producing perfectly even collections like the augmented triad (3 parts), diminished seventh (4 parts), whole-tone scale (6 parts), or tritone (2 parts).

# Formal Definition
When the chromatic octave (12 semitones) is divided into n equal parts, where n is a divisor of 12, the result is a symmetric collection that is invariant under transposition by 12/n semitones. These collections have special voice-leading properties: because they lack intervallic variety, they cannot voice-lead to other instances of the same collection type via single semitonal displacement. They serve as the "axes" around which nearly even chords (like consonant triads) rotate.

# Construction/Recognition
Symmetric divisions of 12:
| Divisor | Interval | Collection | Transpositions |
|---------|----------|------------|----------------|
| 2 | Tritone (6) | Dyad | 6 |
| 3 | Major third (4) | Augmented triad | 4 |
| 4 | Minor third (3) | Diminished seventh | 3 |
| 6 | Whole step (2) | Whole-tone scale | 2 |
| 12 | Semitone (1) | Chromatic scale | 1 |

Key property: Each symmetric division produces collections that are:
- Invariant under transposition by their generating interval
- Unable to voice-lead smoothly to another instance of the same type
- Potentially ambiguous in root or tonal orientation

# Musical Context/Application
Symmetric divisions play a central role in late-Romantic and early-modern harmony. The augmented triad's symmetric division generates hexatonic systems; the diminished seventh's symmetry underlies octatonic collections and the "Weitzmann regions" discussed in Chapter 4. These symmetric structures provide frameworks for organizing non-diatonic chromatic progressions.

# Common Confusions
- Symmetric divisions are defined by equal-interval structure, not by any particular pitch class serving as "root"
- A symmetric collection cannot voice-lead to another instance of the same type via single semitone (but can via larger intervals)
- "Symmetric" here means transpositional symmetry, not necessarily inversional symmetry (though these often coincide)
- The number of distinct collections of each type equals 12 divided by the number of notes
