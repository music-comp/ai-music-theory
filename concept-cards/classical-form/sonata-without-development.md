# Quick Definition
A two-part sonata form consisting of an exposition followed directly by a recapitulation, with no development section and no exposition repeat, to which a coda may optionally be appended.

# Core Definition
Sonata without development consists of a sonata exposition followed immediately by a recapitulation, to which a coda may optionally be appended. The exposition is never repeated, for two reasons: (1) repetition would arouse expectations for a development; (2) the recapitulation would create a redundant third run-through of the same content. A retransition regularly appears between the exposition's closing section and the recapitulation. The recapitulation follows standard sonata norms, with a secondary development frequently appearing to compensate for the absent development. A significant deviation is the truncated recapitulation, in which only the main theme is restated. The form is employed most often by Mozart, with significant examples by Haydn and Beethoven.

# Prerequisites
- **Sonata form**: The fundamental structure being modified
- **Recapitulation**: Understanding standard recapitulatory procedures
- **Secondary development**: The compensatory device in the recapitulation

# Key Properties
1. Two-part form: exposition followed by recapitulation
2. Exposition is never repeated
3. A retransition regularly connects exposition to recapitulation
4. Recapitulation follows standard sonata norms
5. Secondary development often appears to compensate for absent development
6. Optional coda may be appended
7. Truncated recapitulation (main theme only) creates resemblance to large ternary
8. Most often used by Mozart

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify a sonata exposition that is not repeated
- Look for a retransition after the exposition's closing section
- Check whether a development follows (if not, this is sonata without development)
- Assess whether the recapitulation is complete or truncated
- Note any secondary development in the recapitulation

# Context & Application
This form is most commonly used in slow movements but occasionally appears in fast movements and single-movement overtures. On first hearing, the listener cannot distinguish this form from a regular sonata with a repeated exposition until the subordinate theme appears in the home key (confirming recapitulation rather than exposition repeat). A truncated recapitulation creates a form resembling large ternary, but the distinction rests on real-time listening experience.

# Examples
- Mozart: Symphony No. 39, K. 543, ii; String Quintet in C, K. 515, iii; Piano Sonata in F, K. 332/300k, ii; String Quartet in G, K. 387, ii
- Haydn: String Quartet in B-flat, Op. 33/4, iii; String Quartet in C, Op. 50/2, ii
- Beethoven: Piano Trio in G, Op. 1/2, ii; Piano Sonata in F Minor, Op. 2/1, ii; Piano Sonata in D Minor, Op. 31/2, ii
- Truncated recapitulation: Haydn, Op. 33/5, ii; Mozart, Clarinet Quintet K. 581, ii

# Common Errors
- Expecting the exposition to be repeated (it is never repeated in this form)
- Confusing this form with a sonata whose development is merely short
- Equating truncated recapitulation with large ternary without considering listening experience

# Common Confusions
- **First-hearing ambiguity**: On first hearing, the reappearance of the main theme after the exposition could be either a repeat of the exposition or the beginning of a recapitulation; only the subordinate theme in the home key confirms the recapitulation
- **Truncated recapitulation vs. large ternary**: Despite resemblance, the two forms are distinct based on real-time experience; the listener hears sonata until the truncation is perceived
