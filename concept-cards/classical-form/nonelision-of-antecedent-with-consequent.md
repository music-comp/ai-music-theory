# Quick Definition
Antecedent and consequent phrases in a period do not elide. Even when rhythmic motion continues through the boundary between them, the formal goal of the antecedent (its cadence) precedes the beginning of the consequent, keeping the formal boundary clear and preserving the period's symmetrical structure.

# Core Definition
"When the *end* of one formal unit directly coincides with the *beginning* of the subsequent unit, then we term this boundary process *elision*" (Ch. 3, p. 87). However, "an examination of the classical repertory reveals that *an antecedent phrase does not elide with a consequent phrase*" (Ch. 3, p. 87). Even when m. 4 maintains rhythmic drive into m. 5, "the progression of the harmonies may not truly give rise to an authentic cadence at m. 5" (Ch. 3, p. 87). Even if such a cadence were harmonically possible, "an elision would result in the antecedent becoming five bars in length compared to the four bars of the consequent. The symmetrical phrase structure, which seems to be an important component of the period structure, would be broken" (Ch. 3, p. 87).

# Prerequisites
- **Antecedent** -- the first phrase of the period
- **Consequent** -- the second phrase of the period
- **Period** -- the theme type requiring clear phrase boundaries

# Key Properties
1. Antecedent and consequent never elide in classical periods
2. Rhythmic continuity does not equal elision
3. The formal boundary exists even without a rhythmic gap
4. Elision would break the period's symmetrical phrase structure (e.g., 5+3 or 5+4)
5. Elision obscures the perception of repeating weak closure with stronger closure
6. Even when bass motion seems to create V-I at m. 5, harmonic details often undermine this reading

# Construction / Recognition
1. Look for a clear cadential goal *before* the beginning of the consequent
2. Even with continuous rhythmic motion, the antecedent's cadence is identifiable
3. Check harmonic details: often the bass motion between mm. 4-5 undermines any potential authentic cadence at m. 5
4. The restatement of the basic idea at m. 5 confirms the new beginning of the consequent
5. Do not confuse rhythmic stop with formal end -- a cadence can occur without a rhythmic gap

# Context & Application
This principle preserves the formal clarity essential to the period form. If antecedent and consequent elided, the perception of cadential differentiation -- weak then strong -- would be compromised. A lead-in (connecting melodic idea between the phrases) is acceptable because it "could easily be eliminated without upsetting the melodic, harmonic, and formal organization of the phrases" (Ch. 3, p. 86), unlike elision which would fundamentally alter phrase lengths.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 3, p. 87, ex. 3.16): Haydn, Piano Trio in E-flat, Hob. XV:30, iii, mm. 1-16 (R = 2N): despite continuous rhythmic motion, the bass note B-flat in m. 7 is filled in with stepwise motion leading up to E-flat in m. 9, and the resulting change to first inversion on the third beat of m. 8 "undermines the potential for the dominant to function as the 'penultimate' harmony of an authentic cadential progression." The dominant in root position at m. 7 must be construed as an "ultimate" dominant ending a half-cadential progression.

# Relationships

- Clear formal analysis of periods with rhythmic continuity
## Related
- Stop vs. end (rhythmic stop and formal end are different phenomena)
- Lead-in (an acceptable boundary process that maintains continuity without elision)
## Contrasts With
- Elision as used in other formal contexts (e.g., continuation eliding with cadential extension)

# Common Errors
- Hearing an authentic cadence at m. 5 when the harmonic details do not support it
- Confusing rhythmic continuity with formal elision
- Assuming that the absence of rests means the phrases must overlap

# Common Confusions
- Rhythmic continuity does not imply elision -- the formal boundary can exist without a rhythmic gap
- When the consequent's basic idea begins with the same harmony as a potential authentic cadence resolution, check whether the bass motion truly supports an authentic cadential progression or merely leads stepwise into the new phrase
- Elision is appropriate in other formal contexts but not within a period between antecedent and consequent
