# Quick Definition
Antecedent and consequent phrases in a period rarely, if ever, elide. Even when rhythmic motion continues through the boundary between them, the sense of the antecedent's formal goal (cadence) precedes the sense of the consequent's new beginning, keeping the formal boundary clear.

# Core Definition
When measure 4 of the antecedent maintains rhythmic continuity into measure 5, one might be tempted to say that the antecedent closes with an authentic cadence that elides with the beginning of the consequent — that is, measure 5 would function as both the last measure of the antecedent and the first measure of the consequent. Such an interpretation usually proves faulty. In most cases, there are specific harmonic reasons that the beginning of measure 5 cannot be considered a cadence. More importantly, the sense of a formal "goal" in an antecedent almost always precedes the sense of a new beginning for the consequent. The perception of repeating a unit of weak closure with one of stronger closure is obscured if the boundaries between the units are not clear.

# Construction / Recognition
1. Look for a clear cadential goal *before* the beginning of the consequent
2. Even with continuous rhythmic motion, the antecedent's cadence is identifiable
3. Check harmonic details: often the bass motion between mm. 4-5 undermines any potential authentic cadence at m. 5
4. The restatement of the basic idea at m. 5 confirms the new beginning of the consequent

# Context & Application
This principle preserves the formal clarity essential to the period form. If antecedent and consequent elided, the distinction between the two units — and thus the perception of cadential differentiation — would be compromised. The classical composers maintain clear phrase boundaries to ensure the listener perceives the weak-then-strong cadential pairing.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Trio in E-flat, Hob. XV:30, iii, mm. 1-16: despite continuous rhythmic motion, the dominant at m. 7 is the HC ending the antecedent, not part of an authentic cadence at m. 9; the bass motion (B-flat filled in stepwise to E-flat) undermines V-I cadential reading at m. 9

# Common Confusions
- Rhythmic continuity does not imply elision — the formal boundary can exist without a rhythmic gap
- When the consequent's basic idea begins with the same harmony as a potential authentic cadence resolution, check whether the bass motion truly supports an authentic cadential progression or merely leads stepwise into the new phrase
- An examination of the classical repertory reveals that antecedent-consequent elision is virtually nonexistent
