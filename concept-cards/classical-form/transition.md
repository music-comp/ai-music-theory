# Quick Definition
An interthematic formal function that stands between the main theme and subordinate theme, destabilizing the home key so that the subordinate key can emerge as a competing tonality, while loosening the form and imparting rhythmic momentum.

# Formal Definition
The transition serves to destabilize the home key so that the subordinate key can emerge as a competing tonality in the exposition. In addition, it loosens the form established by the tight-knit main theme, imparts greater rhythmic continuity and momentum, and liquidates characteristic melodic-motivic material to "clear the stage" for the subordinate theme. Most often, the transition destabilizes through modulation (modulating transition), but it can also simply lead to the home-key dominant (nonmodulating transition). The harmonic goal is normally dominant -- of the subordinate key (modulating) or the home key (nonmodulating). The transition is not considered a genuine theme but rather a themelike unit, since it need not end with a cadence. It is constructed from the same intrathematic functions as themes (presentation, continuation, cadential) but avoids a true consequent. A transition is more loosely organized than the main theme but uses fewer loosening techniques than subordinate themes.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Connects main theme to subordinate theme
2. Destabilizes the home key through modulation or dominant emphasis
3. Harmonic goal is normally dominant (either subordinate-key or home-key)
4. May end with HC, dominant arrival, or no concluding function at all
5. Constructed from sentential functions: presentation, continuation, cadential
6. No true consequent (which requires authentic cadence, contradicting the HC goal)
7. More loosely organized than the main theme
8. Often characterized by significant increase in dynamic intensity and forward drive
9. Often begins with structural elision with the end of the main theme
10. Can be as short as four measures
11. Seldom features strings of evaded cadences or begins with standing on the dominant

# Musical Context
The transition is often the moment when the movement seems to be "getting under way." It frequently features brilliant passage-work (arpeggiations, scale patterns projecting a "brilliant style"), Sturm und Drang character, forte dynamics, and continuous rhythmic accompaniment. Structural elision with the main theme and a sudden dynamic change from piano to forte create the impression of high energy. Transitions can begin with new material, main-theme material, a false closing section, or from a nontonic region.

# Examples
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in C, K. 403/385c, i, mm. 5-8 (nonmodulating transition: sentence repeated with HC ending)
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:21, ii, mm. 7-12 (new material with model-sequence technique)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C, Op. 2/3, iv, mm. 1-29 (begins like A' section of small ternary but becomes modulating transition)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C Minor, Op. 10/1, iii, mm. 8-18 (false closing section becomes transition presentation)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in F, K. 332/300k, i, mm. 23-40 (begins from nontonic region VI with Sturm und Drang style)

# Common Confusions
- The transition is NOT a theme -- it is a "themelike unit" that need not end with a cadence
- A transition's HC ending is distinct from a main theme's HC ending -- the transition's HC functions as the harmonic goal preparing the subordinate key
- Strings of evaded cadences are NOT typical of transitions (they relate to authentic cadences of subordinate themes)
- A transition can be very short (even 4 measures); massive transitions are exceptions
- The beginning of the transition is often determined retrospectively -- what seems like a continuation of the main theme may prove to be the transition's start
