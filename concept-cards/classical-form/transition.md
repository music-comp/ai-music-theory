# Quick Definition
An interthematic formal function standing between the main theme and subordinate theme, destabilizing the home key so that the subordinate key can emerge as a competing tonality, while loosening the form and imparting rhythmic momentum.

# Core Definition
The transition serves to destabilize the home key so that the subordinate key can emerge as a competing tonality in the exposition. In addition, it loosens the form established by the tight-knit main theme, imparts greater rhythmic continuity and momentum, and liquidates characteristic melodic-motivic material to "clear the stage" for the subordinate theme. The transition destabilizes through modulation (modulating transition) or by leading to the home-key dominant (nonmodulating transition). A third category combines both into a two-part transition. The harmonic goal is normally dominant. The transition is NOT a genuine theme but a themelike unit, since it need not end with a cadence. It is constructed from sentential functions (presentation, continuation, cadential) but avoids a true consequent. It is more loosely organized than the main theme but uses fewer loosening techniques than subordinate themes.

# Prerequisites
- **Main theme**: The preceding formal unit establishing the home key
- **Subordinate theme**: The following formal unit confirming the subordinate key
- **Subordinate key**: The tonal goal toward which the transition moves

# Key Properties
1. Destabilizes the home key through modulation or dominant emphasis
2. Harmonic goal is normally dominant (subordinate-key or home-key)
3. NOT a genuine theme -- a "themelike unit" that need not end with a cadence
4. May end with HC, dominant arrival, or no concluding function at all
5. No true consequent (which requires authentic cadence, contradicting HC goal)
6. More loosely organized than main theme but less than subordinate theme
7. Significant increase in dynamic intensity and forward drive
8. Can be as short as four measures
9. Seldom features strings of evaded cadences or begins with standing on dominant
10. Four main beginnings: new material, main-theme material, false closing section, nontonic region

# Construction / Recognition
- Connects main theme to subordinate theme
- Begins with home-key tonic (usually) or nontonic region
- Constructed from sentential functions (presentation, continuation)
- Features passage-work, brilliant style, increased dynamics
- Ends with dominant harmony (HC, dominant arrival, or unemphasized dominant)
- May include postcadential standing on the dominant
- Transition function often confirmed retrospectively

# Context & Application
The transition is often the moment when the movement seems to be "getting under way." It features brilliant passage-work, Sturm und Drang character, forte dynamics, and continuous rhythmic accompaniment. Structural elision with the main theme and sudden dynamic change create high energy. Transitions are often the first place in the movement where continuous rhythmic accompaniment (Alberti bass) is employed. They seldom contain melodic material that would be characterized as "tuneful."

# Examples
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in C, K. 403/385c, i, mm. 5-8: nonmodulating -- sentence repeated with HC ending
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:21, ii, mm. 7-12: new material with model-sequence technique
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C, Op. 2/3, iv, mm. 1-29: begins like A' section of small ternary, becomes modulating transition
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C Minor, Op. 10/1, iii, mm. 8-18: false closing section becomes transition presentation
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in F, K. 332/300k, i, mm. 23-40: begins from nontonic region VI with Sturm und Drang

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Main theme (transition destabilizes what the main theme establishes)
- Interthematic functions (transition as a mediating function)

## Enables
- Subordinate theme (transition prepares the subordinate key)
- Medial caesura (the break between transition and subordinate theme)
- Subordinate-key establishment (through modulation or dominant reinterpretation)

## Related
- Modulating transition (most common type)
- Nonmodulating transition (remains in home key)
- Two-part transition (combines both types)
- Standing on the dominant (often follows the transition's HC)

## Contrasts With
- Main theme (transition is looser, more dynamically driven)
- Subordinate theme (transition lacks authentic cadential goal; ST lacks HC goal)

# Common Errors
- Treating the transition as a theme (it is a "themelike unit" that need not end with a cadence)
- Locating the transition's start at the moment of modulation rather than at the return of material
- Expecting strings of evaded cadences (these relate to authentic cadences in subordinate themes)

# Common Confusions
- **Transition vs. theme**: The transition is a "themelike unit" that need not close with a cadence
- **Transition HC vs. main-theme HC**: The transition's HC functions as the harmonic goal preparing the subordinate key
- **Beginning determination**: The beginning of the transition is often determined retrospectively
- **Length variability**: Transitions can be very short (4 measures) or very long; massive transitions are exceptions, not the norm

# Verification Notes
The characterization as "themelike unit" is Caplin's own terminology. The four beginning types (new material, main-theme material, false closing section, nontonic region) are explicitly enumerated. The prohibition on consequent function is explicitly stated.
