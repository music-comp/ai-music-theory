# Quick Definition
The sentence's forward-striving, dynamic character makes it especially suited to initiate first-movement sonata forms, while the period's symmetrical balance and repose make it more appropriate for finales and rondos.

# Core Definition
As explained by Erwin Ratz, the period has "a certain 'repose in itself' due to the balance of its two halves, which are more or less equal." The sentence, however, "contains a certain forward-striving character due to the increased activity and compression in its continuation phrase, making it fundamentally different in construction from the symmetrical organization of the period." Since sonata form is inherently the most dynamic of the various full-movement forms, the sentence is especially well suited to initiate that formal type. Hybrids of the sentence and period are also suitable for main themes. Small ternary and small binary are used much less frequently, owing most likely to their formal complexity.

# Prerequisites
- **Sentence**: Theme type with presentation + continuation (dynamic, forward-striving)
- **Period**: Theme type with antecedent + consequent (balanced, symmetrical)
- **Main-theme function in sonata**: The formal context for this choice

# Key Properties
1. Sentence: forward-striving character, dynamic momentum pushing toward a single cadence
2. Period: symmetrical phrasing, balance, "repose in itself," emphasis on closure
3. First-movement sonata forms particularly favor the sentence (or sentential hybrids)
4. Finale movements more often feature periods (lighter, more relaxed affect)
5. Rondo forms typically feature periodic main themes
6. Slow-movement sonata forms show no particular preference
7. The choice correlates with position in the instrumental cycle
8. First-movement main themes are more likely to exhibit extensions and expansions than finales

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the main theme's type (sentence, period, hybrid, ternary, or nonconventional)
- Consider the movement's position in the cycle (first movement, slow, finale)
- Assess the aesthetic effect: dynamic forward drive (sentential) vs. balanced repose (periodic)
- Note that hybrids combine features of both

# Context & Application
The theoretical basis for sentence vs. period choice goes beyond mere statistical frequency to a fundamental aesthetic difference. The sentence's single-minded drive toward its cadence matches the dynamic energy required at the opening of a sonata-form movement, where the inherent tonal conflict must be set in motion. The period's balanced closure is better suited to movements emphasizing thematic return and playfulness (finales, rondos).

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F minor, Op. 2/1, i: model sentence -- the dynamic momentum is ideal for initiating this first-movement sonata form (Ch. 9, Ex. 9.1)
- Rondo finales typically feature periodic main themes, matching the lighter affect
- First-movement main themes by all three classical composers favor sentential organization

# Common Errors
- Assuming any sentence or period can serve equally well in any movement position
- Overlooking the aesthetic rationale for theme-type choice

# Common Confusions
- **Not a strict rule**: The sentence/period preference is a tendency, not a requirement
- **Hybrids**: Many main themes combine features of both sentence and period
- **Slow movements**: Neither sentence nor period is preferred in slow-movement sonata forms
