# Quick Definition
Two formal functions can frame the principal boundaries of a theme: a thematic introduction precedes the theme's beginning (before-the-beginning), and postcadential material follows its end (after-the-end). Postcadential function takes two forms: a closing section after an authentic cadence, or a standing on the dominant after a half cadence.

# Core Definition
The principal boundaries of a theme are marked by the beginning of the basic idea and the moment of cadential closure. Two additional formal functions frame these boundaries: a thematic introduction (before-the-beginning) and postcadential material (after-the-end). The thematic introduction is a short unit, rarely more than two measures, consisting of minimal motivic content (often just accompanimental figurations) that prolongs tonic harmony in root position. Postcadential function takes two forms depending on the cadence type: a closing section (after PAC) or a standing on the dominant (after HC). Framing functions are expressions of the general temporal functions "before-the-beginning" and "after-the-end."

# Prerequisites
- **Formal function**: The framework for understanding framing functions
- **Closing section**: One type of postcadential function
- **Standing on the dominant**: The other type of postcadential function

# Key Properties
1. **Thematic introduction**: Short unit (1-2 measures); minimal motivic content; often accompaniment figurations continuing into the theme; prolongs tonic harmony in root position; progressive dynamic (building up energy)
2. **Closing section**: Follows PAC; prolongs tonic harmony and tonic scale degree; series of codettas with fragmentation; may exhibit recessive dynamic (diminishing energy) or sustain/increase energy
3. **Standing on the dominant (postcadential)**: Follows HC; prolongs dominant harmony; reinforces energy of instability; antecedent of a period rarely followed by standing on dominant
4. Introductions exhibit progressive dynamic (crescendo, accumulating texture)
5. Closing sections often exhibit recessive dynamic (calando, diminuendo, dissolving texture)
6. Not to be confused with slow introduction (which precedes an entire exposition, Ch. 16)

# Construction / Recognition
1. **Thematic introduction**: Identify material before the first basic idea; check for minimal motivic content; look for accompanimental figuration; confirm tonic prolongation
2. **Closing section**: Identify material after PAC; check for tonic prolongation; look for series of codettas with fragmentation; note subdominant emphasis (characteristic of codettas)
3. **Standing on dominant (postcadential)**: Identify material after HC; confirm dominant prolongation; note that the antecedent of a period is rarely followed by this function

# Context & Application
Framing functions expand the actual length of a theme beyond its normative structure. They are expressions of temporal functions analogous to real-life experiences: the "before-the-beginning" time when you enter class and prepare, and the "after-the-end" time when you pack up and leave. These periods frame the actual class but do not belong within its literal boundaries. Similarly, framing functions "belong" to the theme they frame without being part of its core formal structure.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in B-flat, Op. 22, ii, mm. 1-12: 1-m. thematic introduction (left-hand "vamp" figure); theme begins at upbeat to m. 2; closing section after PAC at m. 9 with descending codettas
- Beethoven, String Quartet in F, Op. 135, iii, mm. 1-4: progressive dynamic in introduction (crescendo, accumulating texture, no melody)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in F, K. 332, iii, mm. 27-35: recessive dynamic in closing section (flowing eighth notes, tonic pedal, calando to pianissimo)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 330, iii, mm. 13-20: closing section sustains or increases energy (triplet sixteenth notes after PAC)

# Common Errors
- Confusing a thematic introduction with a slow introduction (the latter precedes an entire sonata exposition)
- Identifying postcadential codettas as additional "cadences" -- they are after-the-end material
- Assuming all closing sections are recessive in dynamic -- some sustain or increase energy

# Common Confusions
- Thematic introduction is NOT a slow introduction -- the latter is a much larger formal unit
- Codettas are NOT cadences, even when supported by cadential progressions
- The "plagal cadence" (I-IV-I) is almost always a codetta with subdominant emphasis, not a genuine cadence
- A standing on the dominant after an HC is a postcadential function, but only when the HC is not part of a period's antecedent
