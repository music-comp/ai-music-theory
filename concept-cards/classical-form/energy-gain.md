# Quick Definition
The characteristic increase in dynamic intensity, rhythmic activity, and forward drive at the beginning of a transition, often marking the moment when the movement seems to be "getting under way."

# Formal Definition
A transition is typically characterized by a significant increase in dynamic intensity and forward drive. The beginning of the transition is often the moment when the movement seems to be "getting under way." This energy gain is created through multiple simultaneous changes: structural elision with the end of the main theme, a sudden change from piano to forte, the introduction of continuous rhythmic accompaniment, brilliant passage-work, increased surface rhythmic activity, and forward-driving sequential progressions. The energy gain distinguishes the transition from both the preceding main theme (typically more contained in energy) and the following subordinate theme (which often begins in relative calm before building its own progressive dynamic).

# Construction/Recognition
1. Sudden increase in dynamic level (often piano to forte)
2. Structural elision with the end of the main theme (no gap between theme and transition)
3. Introduction of continuous rhythmic accompaniment (Alberti bass, repeated chords)
4. Brilliant passage-work (arpeggiations, scales)
5. Increased surface rhythmic activity
6. Forward-driving sequential progressions
7. Often the first use of forte dynamic in the movement
8. Change in articulation (legato to staccato, or addition of sforzandi)

# Musical Context
The energy gain at the start of the transition is one of the most audible formal markers in a sonata exposition. It signals that the stable, self-contained world of the main theme has ended and a more dynamically active process has begun. The energy gain serves the transition's formal function: destabilizing the home key requires momentum and forward drive. The listener's experience of increased intensity correlates with the harmonic process of tonal destabilization. In many movements, the transition's energy gain represents the first major dynamic event.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in F, K. 332/300k, i, m. 23 (abrupt shift to forte and Sturm und Drang style at the start of the transition from VI)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C Minor, Op. 10/1, iii, m. 12 (sudden acceleration of surface rhythm and change of material marks the transition's continuation)
- General principle observable throughout the classical repertory: most transitions begin with greater energy than the preceding main theme's conclusion

# Common Confusions
- Energy gain does not occur in ALL transitions -- slow-movement transitions may be more subtle
- The energy gain marks the TRANSITION, not the subordinate theme -- the subordinate theme typically begins with reduced energy (relative calm)
- Structural elision is not the same as energy gain, though they often coincide -- elision is a grouping phenomenon, energy gain is a dynamic one
- Do not assume the loudest moment of the exposition is in the transition; the subordinate theme's cadential climax may exceed the transition's energy
