# Quick Definition
The characteristic increase in dynamic intensity, rhythmic activity, and forward drive at the beginning of a transition, often marking the moment when the movement seems to be "getting under way."

# Core Definition
A transition is typically characterized by a significant increase in dynamic intensity and forward drive. The beginning of the transition is often the moment when the movement seems to be "getting under way." This energy gain is created through multiple simultaneous changes: structural elision with the end of the main theme, a sudden change from piano to forte, the introduction of continuous rhythmic accompaniment, brilliant passage-work, increased surface rhythmic activity, and forward-driving sequential progressions. The frequent use of structural elision with the end of the main theme and a sudden change from piano to forte helps create the impression of high energy at the beginning of the transition.

# Prerequisites
- **Transition**: The formal unit exhibiting energy gain
- **Main theme**: The preceding unit from which the energy increase is measured

# Key Properties
1. Sudden increase in dynamic level (often piano to forte)
2. Structural elision with end of main theme (no gap)
3. Introduction of continuous rhythmic accompaniment (Alberti bass, repeated chords)
4. Brilliant passage-work (arpeggiations, scales)
5. Increased surface rhythmic activity
6. Forward-driving sequential progressions
7. Often the first use of forte dynamic in the movement
8. Change in articulation (legato to staccato, sforzandi)

# Construction / Recognition
- Note the dynamic level change at the boundary between main theme and transition
- Observe whether structural elision occurs (no rhythmic gap)
- Listen for the onset of continuous rhythmic accompaniment
- Identify passage-work replacing thematic melody
- Assess the overall sense of increased momentum and forward drive

# Context & Application
The energy gain at the start of the transition is one of the most audible formal markers in a sonata exposition. It signals that the stable, self-contained world of the main theme has ended and a more dynamically active process has begun. The energy gain serves the transition's formal function: destabilizing the home key requires momentum and forward drive. Not all transitions exhibit dramatic energy gain; slow-movement transitions may be more subtle.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in F, K. 332/300k, i, m. 23: abrupt shift to forte and Sturm und Drang style at start of transition from VI
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C Minor, Op. 10/1, iii, m. 12: sudden acceleration of surface rhythm and change of material marks the transition's continuation

# Common Errors
- Assuming energy gain occurs in ALL transitions (slow-movement transitions may be subtle)
- Confusing transition energy with subordinate theme energy (ST typically begins with reduced energy)
- Equating structural elision with energy gain (they are different phenomena that often coincide)

# Common Confusions
- **Energy gain vs. structural elision**: Elision is a grouping phenomenon; energy gain is a dynamic one -- they often coincide but are distinct
- **Transition vs. subordinate theme energy**: The ST typically begins with reduced energy (relative calm) after the transition's high energy
- **Not universal**: Energy gain does not occur in ALL transitions -- slow-movement transitions may be more subtle
