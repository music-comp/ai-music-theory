# Quick Definition
A dramatic, emotionally intense musical character featuring forte dynamics, minor-mode inflections, agitated rhythmic patterns, and sudden dynamic contrasts, associated primarily with transitions and development cores where it serves the function of tonal destabilization and expressive intensification.

# Core Definition
Sturm und Drang character refers to a musical style of heightened dramatic intensity, named after the German literary movement emphasizing emotional extremity and dramatic conflict. In classical form, this character appears most prominently in two contexts: transitions, where its dramatic intensity serves the function of tonal destabilization, and development cores, where the core typically projects an emotional quality of instability, restlessness, and dramatic conflict. The style is marked by forte or fortissimo dynamics, minor-mode inflections (even in major-key movements), syncopation, sudden dynamic contrasts, and agitated rhythmic patterns. The dynamic level is usually forte, the general character often one of Sturm und Drang.

# Key Properties
1. Forte or fortissimo dynamics (often in contrast to preceding piano)
2. Minor-mode inflections or modal contrast
3. Agitated rhythmic patterns and syncopation
4. Sforzandi and sudden dynamic contrasts
5. Emotionally intense, dramatic character
6. Associated with transitions and development cores
7. Particularly effective when beginning from VI (submediant) in transitions
8. In the core: continuous rhythmic activity, thicker textures, polyphonic devices
9. Serves a functional purpose: tonal destabilization and dramatic intensification

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify sudden shifts to forte dynamics, especially after piano passages
- Look for minor-mode inflections in major-key movements
- Note agitated rhythmic patterns, syncopation, and sforzandi
- In transitions: marks the beginning of tonal destabilization
- In development cores: marks the onset of the core's "working out" of material
- Distinguish from brilliant style (which features virtuosic passage-work rather than dramatic intensity)

# Context & Application
The Sturm und Drang character serves formal functions rather than being merely decorative. In transitions, it creates a sense of dramatic urgency appropriate to tonal destabilization, contrasting with both the preceding main theme (typically more balanced) and the subsequent subordinate theme (often beginning in relative calm). In development cores, it represents the section's characteristic quality of instability, restlessness, and dramatic conflict. The pre-core, by contrast, is generally more relaxed and anticipatory -- "the calm before the storm." Polyphonic devices (imitation, canon, fugal entries) can contribute further to the complexity typical of Sturm und Drang passages.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in F, K. 332/300k, i, m. 23: abrupt shift to forte and Sturm und Drang style at transition beginning from VI; dramatic contrast with preceding codettas
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in A Minor, K. 310, i, mm. 58-73: core with Sturm und Drang character, forte dynamics, model-sequence-fragmentation
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Hob. XVI:49, i, mm. 84-103: core with model-sequence technique and dramatic intensity
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62: pervasive Sturm und Drang character throughout the development

# Common Errors
- Treating Sturm und Drang as a formal function rather than a style descriptor
- Assuming all transitions feature Sturm und Drang (many use brilliant style instead)
- Treating Sturm und Drang as merely decorative rather than functionally motivated

# Common Confusions
- **Style vs. function**: Sturm und Drang character is a STYLE descriptor, not a formal function; it describes the manner of expression, not the formal role
- **Not universal**: Not all transitions or development cores feature Sturm und Drang; brilliant style, learned style, and other textures are alternatives
- **Functional motivation**: Sturm und Drang in transitions and cores is functionally motivated (tonal destabilization, dramatic conflict), not merely decorative
- **Pre-core vs. core**: If the core often bursts out with Sturm und Drang, the pre-core can be likened to "the calm before the storm"

# Verification Notes
Caplin uses "Sturm und Drang" specifically to describe the character of certain transitional passages and development cores. The association with the core is particularly strong: "The dynamic level is usually forte, and the general character is often one of Sturm und Drang" (Ch. 10).
