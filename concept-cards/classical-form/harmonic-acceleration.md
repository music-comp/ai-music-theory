# Quick Definition
An increase in the rate of harmonic change in the continuation phrase compared to the presentation phrase, contributing to the destabilizing character of continuation function.

# Core Definition
Harmonic acceleration is "an increase in the rate of harmonic change" (Ch. 1, p. 10). In the presentation phrase, harmony typically changes every two measures (one harmony per basic idea). In the continuation, there is a "steady acceleration in the harmonic rhythm" (Ch. 1, p. 11). Fragmentation and harmonic acceleration "frequently go hand in hand" but are independent: "we will encounter examples in which the units decrease in size within a uniform harmonic rhythm or, conversely, in which an increase in the rate of harmonic change occurs without any corresponding fragmentation" (Ch. 1, p. 11).

# Prerequisites
- **Continuation function** -- the formal context where acceleration occurs

# Key Properties
1. Measured by comparing harmonic rhythm in continuation vs. presentation
2. Often congruent with fragmentation but independent from it
3. Analysis can be "problematic and requires careful judgment about the relative structural importance of the constituent chords" (Ch. 1, p. 12)
4. Surface harmonic activity may not correspond to deeper harmonic structure
5. A sequential progression in the continuation may create the perception of harmonic motion even without literal acceleration

# Construction / Recognition
- Compare the rate of chord change between presentation and continuation
- Consider whether foreground harmonies represent genuine harmonic changes or ornamental chords
- Assess whether sequential progression creates a "palpable sense of harmonic motion and increased activity" even without literal acceleration (Ch. 1, p. 12)

# Context & Application
Harmonic acceleration is one of the four characteristics of continuation function. It contributes to the forward-driving quality of the continuation phrase. Its assessment requires analytical judgment about which harmonic events are structural.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 1, p. 11, ex. 1.1): Beethoven, Op. 2/1, i: presentation has one harmony per two measures; continuation has steady acceleration through mm. 5-8.
**Example 2** (Ch. 1, p. 12, ex. 1.2): Beethoven, Op. 135, iii: foreground harmonies in the presentation progress at two chords per measure, but the dominant seventh chords "seem not to represent genuine harmonies in their own right," creating perceived stasis. The sequential progression of the continuation brings "a palpable sense of harmonic motion and increased activity" by comparison.

# Common Errors
- Measuring only surface harmonic rhythm without considering structural importance of chords
- Treating harmonic acceleration as a simple, mechanical metric

# Common Confusions
- Harmonic acceleration "can be problematic and requires careful judgment" -- not all surface chord changes represent genuine harmonic activity
- Perceived acceleration may result from a shift from prolongational to sequential harmony rather than literal increase in chord-change rate
