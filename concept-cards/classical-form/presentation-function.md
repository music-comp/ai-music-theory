# Quick Definition
The opening formal function of a sentence, in which a two-measure basic idea is repeated to form a four-measure phrase that establishes the theme's melodic material over tonic harmony, without cadential closure.

# Core Definition
Presentation function is defined by two criteria: (1) melodic-motivic: a basic idea is stated and then immediately repeated, and (2) harmonic: the phrase is grounded in tonic prolongation. The repetition has three significant effects: it helps the listener "learn and remember the principal melodic-motivic material"; it confirms "the actual boundaries of the idea"; and its strongly ongoing quality "generates demand for a continuation phrase" (Ch. 1, p. 10). Presentation, in principle, does not end with a cadence.

# Prerequisites
- **Basic idea** -- the unit being presented and repeated
- **Formal function** -- understanding function as role, not just position

# Key Properties
1. Four measures: two-measure basic idea + two-measure repetition
2. Supported by tonic prolongation (essential harmonic requirement)
3. Three types of repetition:
   - Statement-response: tonic version (statement) followed by dominant version (response)
   - Exact: no fundamental change of harmony
   - Sequential: transposed to different scale-degrees
4. Does not end with a cadence (the absence of cadential closure is a defining feature)
5. Creates an ongoing, open quality demanding continuation

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify a two-measure basic idea
2. Confirm immediate repetition in the following two measures
3. Verify tonic prolongation across all four measures (even in statement-response, the V resolves to I at the start of the continuation, making the overall progression prolongational)
4. Confirm no cadence at the end of the phrase
5. The presence of a cadence rules out a genuine presentation

# Context & Application
Presentation function occurs at the beginning of sentence themes and is characteristic of main themes in sonata forms. Its stable character makes it ideal for establishing the home key and introducing primary melodic material. Detailed treatment in Chapter 3.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 1, p. 10, ex. 1.1): Beethoven, Op. 2/1, i, mm. 1-4: statement (I, mm. 1-2) and response (V7, mm. 3-4); the V7 resolves to I on the downbeat of m. 5, creating a tonic prolongational progression overall.
**Example 2** (Ch. 1, p. 11, ex. 1.2): Beethoven, Op. 135, iii, mm. 3-6: exact repetition with no fundamental change of harmony; the repetition "brings no fundamental change of harmony."

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Basic idea (the unit being presented)
## Enables
- Sentence form (presentation + continuation = sentence)
- Continuation function (demanded by the ongoing quality of the presentation)
## Related
- Statement-response (a specific type of presentation repetition)
## Contrasts With
- Antecedent: begins with basic idea but follows with contrasting idea (not repetition) and ends with a cadence
- Continuation function: destabilizes the context established by the presentation

# Common Errors
- Identifying a presentation solely because an idea is repeated, without checking tonic prolongation
- Calling a phrase with a cadence at its end a "presentation"

# Common Confusions
- A presentation must have tonic prolongation, not just a repeated idea. Without tonic harmony, the phrase is not a genuine presentation
- Presentation does not end with a cadence. "The presence of a cadence at the end of this phrase rules out a presentation, since this function, in principle, does not end with a cadence" (Ch. 1, p. 14)
