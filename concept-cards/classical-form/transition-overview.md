# Quick Definition
A themelike unit standing between the main theme and subordinate theme that destabilizes the home key, usually modulates to the subordinate key, and features distinctly looser organization than the main theme.

# Core Definition
The transition's fundamental functions are "to destabilize the home key, to effect a modulation to the subordinate key, and to motivate the appearance of the subordinate theme, which will eventually confirm the new key" (Ch. 1, p. 19). The transition expresses looser organization through "harmonic and tonal instability," "asymmetrical grouping structure," and functional expression that is "not as efficiently expressed or as clearly defined as those in the main theme" (Ch. 1, p. 19).

# Prerequisites
- **Interthematic functions** -- the framework
- **Main theme** -- the preceding function
- **Tight-knit vs. loose organization** -- the dimension of comparison

# Key Properties
1. Destabilizes the home key
2. Usually modulates to the subordinate key
3. Often ends with HC in the subordinate key followed by standing on the dominant
4. Lacks full presentation phrase (basic idea not repeated = no solid beginning)
5. Continuation function is often extended beyond the minimum
6. Asymmetrical phrase groupings
7. Functional expression is less efficient, more ambiguous than in the main theme
8. Loosening techniques are appropriate, not deficiencies

# Construction / Recognition
- Begins after the main theme (often with the basic idea restated in new harmonic context)
- Features harmonic-tonal instability and/or modulation
- Ends with HC or dominant arrival in the subordinate key
- May include standing on the dominant

# Context & Application
The transition bridges main and subordinate themes in sonata-form expositions. Its loosening techniques are deliberate and functionally appropriate. Detailed treatment in Chapter 9.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 1, pp. 18-19, ex. 1.7): Beethoven, Op. 2/1, i, mm. 9-20: basic idea restated in C-minor harmony (disrupting F-minor home key), continuation with fragmentation and descending-fifth sequential progression (mm. 11-14), HC in Ab major (m. 16), standing on the dominant for four measures (mm. 17-20). Asymmetrical grouping: 2 + 6 + 4.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Interthematic functions, tight-knit vs. loose organization
## Enables
- Modulation to the subordinate key
- Appearance of the subordinate theme
## Related
- Standing on the dominant (common transition ending)
## Contrasts With
- Main theme: tight-knit vs. loose
- Subordinate theme: both are loose but use different loosening techniques (transition uses harmonic/tonal/cadential means; subordinate theme uses phrase-structural means)

# Common Errors
- Viewing the transition's looseness as a compositional weakness

# Common Confusions
- The absence of a presentation is not a weakness; the basic idea was already established in the main theme
- Loosening techniques in the transition are appropriate to its function of destabilization and modulation
