# Quick Definition
Standard modulatory procedures in transitions include pivot-chord modulation (I becomes IV of new key; vi pivots to become ii), sequential progressions that traverse key areas, and the prominent move to vi as a signal that modulation is about to take place.

# Core Definition
In major-mode movements, the subordinate key (almost always V) is usually reached by means of pivot-chord modulation. One simple pivot sees the home-key tonic becoming the subdominant of the subordinate key, which then can move easily to the dominant. Often, there is a prominent move to the submediant (vi), which pivots to become the pre-dominant ii in the subordinate key. Indeed, such a move to vi is so common that it is akin to "waving a flag" that the modulation is about to take place. Sequential progressions are also effective in creating a smooth modulation; in such cases, it is not always possible to identify just which specific harmony within the sequence is the actual pivot chord. In minor-mode movements, the modulation to the relative major (III) also tends to employ the home-key IV or vi, pivoting to become ii or IV respectively.

# Prerequisites
- **Modulating transition**: The formal function performing the modulation
- **Subordinate key**: The tonal goal of the modulation

# Key Properties
1. Major-mode pivot 1: I becomes IV of subordinate key (V)
2. Major-mode pivot 2: vi pivots to become ii of subordinate key -- "waving a flag"
3. Minor-mode pivot: IV or vi pivot to become ii or IV of III
4. Sequential progressions provide smooth modulation without clear single pivot
5. In sequences, multiple harmonies may function within both keys simultaneously
6. The vi as pivot is so common it practically announces the modulation

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the point where the home key is abandoned
- Look for the submediant (vi) as a signal of impending modulation
- Trace the pivot chord: does I become IV of the new key? Does vi become ii?
- If sequential, note that the pivot may be distributed across multiple harmonies
- Verify the modulation is confirmed by the HC or dominant arrival in the subordinate key

# Context & Application
Understanding modulation techniques helps predict where in the transition the key change occurs. The prominence of vi as a modulatory pivot is a reliable analytical marker: its appearance in a transition strongly suggests that modulation to the subordinate key is imminent. Sequential modulation, while less clear-cut harmonically, is equally common and creates a smoother, more gradual departure from the home key.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F minor, Op. 2/1, i, mm. 9-16 (Ch. 9, Ex. 9.2): C minor initially suggests new tonic; descending-fifth sequential progression leads to HC in A-flat major
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, H. 21, ii (Ch. 11, Ex. 11.15): pre-dominant II6 leads to cadential 6/4 and HC in subordinate key
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in E minor, K. 304, i (Ch. 11, Ex. 11.9): VI pivots to become IV of subordinate key

# Common Errors
- Trying to identify a single pivot chord in a sequential modulation
- Missing the vi-as-flag signal of impending modulation
- Confusing tonicization within the transition with the actual modulation

# Common Confusions
- **vi is not just a passing chord**: Its appearance signals imminent modulation
- **Sequential modulation**: Multiple harmonies may serve as the pivot simultaneously; do not insist on one
- **Major vs. minor procedures**: The specific pivot chords differ between major and minor modes but the principle is the same
