# Quick Definition
A framing function that precedes a theme's structural beginning, featuring a progressive dynamic of accumulating tension without distinct melodic-motivic identity, saving the genuine basic idea for the structural beginning.

# Core Definition
"An introduction to a theme (or thematic introduction, as it may more technically be called) is generally short, two to four measures at most" (Ch. 1, p. 15). Sometimes one or two chords alone suffice. "The melodic-motivic component of such an introduction is either weakly defined or entirely absent, so that the expression of a genuine basic idea can be saved for the structural beginning of the theme" (Ch. 1, p. 15). Introductions usually emphasize tonic harmony, occasionally dominant. They feature a "progressive dynamic -- one in which there is an increasing buildup of energy and anticipation" (Ch. 1, p. 16), functioning rhythmically as "an 'anacrusis,' an upbeat, whose corresponding 'thesis,' a downbeat, is the structural beginning of the theme" (Ch. 1, p. 16).

# Prerequisites
- **Basic idea** -- the thematic material that the introduction prepares
- **Formal function** -- understanding the "before-the-beginning" role
- **Progressive and recessive dynamic** -- the energy profile of the introduction

# Key Properties
1. Precedes the structural beginning of the theme (the basic idea)
2. Short: typically 1-4 measures, sometimes just one or two chords
3. Lacks distinctive melodic profile (saves genuine thematic material for the basic idea)
4. Emphasizes tonic harmony (occasionally dominant)
5. Features progressive dynamic: crescendo, accumulating texture, anacrustic quality
6. Relatively rare: most themes begin with their structural beginning

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify material preceding the basic idea that lacks distinctive melodic profile
- Check for progressive dynamic (buildup of tension)
- Verify that the material emphasizes tonic (or occasionally dominant) without cadential progression
- The arrival of a clear basic idea marks the structural beginning and the end of the introduction

# Context & Application
Introductions to themes are relatively rare, as most themes begin directly with their structural beginning. More common at movement beginnings (slow introductions, Chapter 13) than within movements. Do not confuse thematic introductions (short, local) with slow introductions to movements (larger scale).

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 1, p. 16, ex. 1.5): Beethoven, Op. 135, iii, mm. 1-2: staggered entrances of each of the four instruments gradually establishing the tonic triad with crescendo. When the downbeat at m. 3 arrives, Beethoven "suddenly pulls back the intensity level (sotto voce)" because "the moment of beginning, the downbeat, has sufficient structural weight not to require any additional emphasis."

- The introduction is not part of the theme proper; it frames the theme's boundaries
