# Quick Definition
A framing function that precedes a theme's structural beginning, featuring a progressive dynamic of accumulating tension without distinct melodic-motivic identity.

# Formal Definition
An introduction to a theme (thematic introduction) is a framing function standing outside the theme's structural boundaries as a "before-the-beginning." It is generally short (two to four measures), sometimes just one or two chords. The melodic-motivic component is weakly defined or absent, saving the genuine basic idea for the structural beginning. Introductions usually emphasize tonic harmony (occasionally dominant) and feature a progressive dynamic--systematic buildup of energy and anticipation, functioning as an anacrusis whose thesis is the theme's structural beginning.

# Construction/Recognition
- Precedes the structural beginning (the basic idea)
- Short: typically 1-4 measures
- Lacks distinctive melodic profile
- Emphasizes tonic (occasionally dominant) harmony
- Features progressive dynamic: crescendo, accumulating texture, anacrustic quality

# Musical Context
Introductions to themes are relatively rare, as most themes begin with their structural beginning. More common at movement beginnings (slow introductions, Chapter 13) than within movements.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 135, iii, mm. 1-2: staggered entrances of four instruments establishing tonic triad with crescendo before the basic idea at m. 3 (sudden sotto voce)

# Common Confusions
- The introduction is not part of the theme proper; it frames the theme's boundaries.
- Do not confuse thematic introductions (short, local) with slow introductions to movements (larger scale).
