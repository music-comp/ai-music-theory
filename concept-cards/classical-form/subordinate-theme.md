# Quick Definition
The thematic unit in a sonata exposition that confirms the subordinate key through a perfect authentic cadence, employing a distinctly looser organization than the main theme.

# Core Definition
A subordinate theme is an integral unit of form containing a syntactical succession of formal functions and a concluding perfect authentic cadence in the subordinate key. It is not merely a new melody but a complete formal entity. Subordinate-theme function is not tied to a specific grouping structure or formal type -- it appears in sonata, rondo, concerto, minuet, and various slow-movement forms. Subordinate themes are, with rare exceptions, more loosely organized than their preceding main themes, employing a broad range of loosening techniques across all intrathematic functions.

# Prerequisites
- **Main theme**: The preceding tight-knit theme that establishes the home key
- **Intrathematic functions**: Presentation, continuation, cadential functions that make up theme interiors
- **Subordinate key**: The tonal region confirmed by the subordinate theme's PAC

# Key Properties
1. Confirms the subordinate key with a perfect authentic cadence (the essential expositional closure)
2. More loosely organized than the main theme -- this is a defining characteristic, not a deficiency
3. Constituent phrases frequently express initiating function (antecedent, presentation, compound basic idea)
4. Continuation and cadential functions are typically extended and expanded
5. The ternary functions of exposition, contrasting middle, and recapitulation are NOT used
6. Almost always resides in the major mode, even in minor-mode movements (relative major)

# Construction / Recognition
- Begins after the transition, usually with a new basic idea in the subordinate key
- Built from the same intrathematic functions as main themes but in looser configurations
- Presentation may be loosened by additional repetition, weakened tonic prolongation, or dominant pedal
- Continuation typically extended through model-sequence technique or delayed fragmentation
- Cadential function typically expanded (ECP) and extended (evaded/deceptive/abandoned cadences)
- Must end with PAC in subordinate key to fulfill its confirmational function
- May include a postcadential closing section

# Context & Application
The subordinate theme is the primary cadential goal of the exposition. Its loose organization dramatizes the process of confirming the subordinate key -- the cadential goal is made more effective by being harder to achieve. The contrasting character between main and subordinate themes has long been discussed by theorists, though the nineteenth-century notion of melodic dualism (masculine vs. feminine themes) has been abandoned in favor of form-functional contrast.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F Minor, Op. 2/1, i, mm. 21-48: presentation (mm. 21-24, dominant pedal), extended continuation (mm. 25-32), expanded cadential phrases (mm. 33-41, with evasion), closing section (mm. 42-48). Asymmetrical 4+8+4+5+7
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, i, mm. 14-28: loosened presentation with I6 and V4/3 embellishment
- Haydn, String Quartet in B Minor, Op. 64/2, i: single subordinate theme with modal shift and evaded cadence

# Common Errors
- Identifying a subordinate theme solely by melodic character rather than formal function
- Assuming any theme in the subordinate key is a subordinate theme
- Treating the looseness of the subordinate theme as disorganization rather than a systematic deployment of specific loosening techniques

# Common Confusions
- **Subordinate theme vs. "second subject"**: The traditional term "second subject" often refers to a melody; subordinate theme is a complete formal unit with specific functional requirements
- **Looseness vs. weakness**: A loosened form is not a weakened form -- extensions and expansions intensify rather than diminish the moment of cadential arrival
- **Single theme vs. theme group**: An exposition may have one or multiple subordinate themes, each ending with PAC
