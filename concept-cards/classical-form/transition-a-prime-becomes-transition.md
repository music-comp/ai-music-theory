# Quick Definition
When a main theme is forming as a small ternary, the A' section may depart from the path of the prior A section and lead to dominant closure instead of a home-key PAC, so that the A' section is retrospectively understood as the beginning of a modulating transition rather than a genuine recapitulation.

# Core Definition
The main theme may be in the process of forming itself as a small ternary, but its A' section, rather than closing with a home-key PAC, may instead depart from the path of the prior A section and lead to dominant closure. We understand, in retrospect, that the A' section has "become" a transition. This situation can be difficult to distinguish from a two-part transition, where the main theme is a simple theme (ending with PAC) and what seems like a B section is actually the first part of the transition. Two factors help: (1) in a small ternary, the A and B sections must NOT elide (if they do, the B section is actually a transition's first part); (2) the B section of a ternary often emphasizes the home-key dominant at its beginning, whereas a transition tends to emphasize home-key tonic first.

# Prerequisites
- **Transition**: The formal function that the A' section becomes
- **Small ternary**: The theme type whose recapitulation is "hijacked"
- **Recapitulation (in small ternary)**: The A' section that departs from expectations

# Key Properties
1. Main theme begins as a small ternary (A section, B/contrasting middle)
2. The return of the basic idea at the A' section signals recapitulation
3. Instead of PAC in home key, the A' section modulates to the subordinate key
4. The departure is retrospectively understood as the transition's beginning
5. The two-part transition creates a similar structure but from a different analytical perspective
6. The elision test: A and B sections of ternary must not elide; if they do, prefer the two-part transition interpretation
7. The dominant-emphasis test: B sections of ternary often begin with dominant; transition first parts begin with tonic

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify a main theme that begins as a small ternary
- Look for the A' section (return of opening material)
- Check whether the A' section closes with PAC (genuine ternary) or departs toward the subordinate key (becomes transition)
- Apply the elision test: if A and B elide, it is not a ternary
- Apply the dominant-emphasis test: does the middle unit begin on dominant (= B section) or tonic (= transition part 1)?

# Context & Application
This technique creates a particularly organic connection between the main theme and transition, since the listener initially expects the A' section to complete the ternary form but is surprised when the music redirects toward the subordinate key. The retrospective reinterpretation creates a moment of formal "discovery" for the listener and analyst. The distinction between this technique and a two-part transition (Fig. 11.1 in the source) is one of the most subtle analytical judgments in sonata-form analysis.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C, Op. 2/3, iv, mm. 1-29 (Ch. 11, Ex. 11.18): A section (mm. 1-8), B section (mm. 9-18), A' section returns at m. 19 but melodic D# in m. 22 steers toward subordinate key; HC at m. 29; the A' section is retrospectively understood as a modulating transition
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in B-flat ("Hammerklavier"), Op. 106, i (Ch. 11, Ex. 11.19): the return of fanfare chords at m. 35 has a striking "recapitulatory" character, but the elision test and tonic-emphasis test favor a two-part transition interpretation

# Common Errors
- Insisting on a ternary interpretation when the A' section clearly modulates
- Confusing this with a two-part transition without applying the elision and dominant-emphasis tests
- Treating the A' section as both recapitulation and transition simultaneously (it is one or the other)

# Common Confusions
- **A' becomes transition vs. two-part transition**: Apply two tests: (1) do A and B elide? (2) does the middle unit begin on dominant? If A-B elision: two-part transition; if middle begins on dominant: ternary main theme
- **Retrospective**: The transition beginning is understood ONLY in retrospect, after the modulation becomes clear
- **Fig. 11.1**: The source provides a table comparing the two interpretations (ternary main theme vs. two-part transition) for the same three-unit structure
