# Quick Definition
Three conventionalized rhythmic-contour patterns developed in the classical period to accompany an upper-voice melody -- the Alberti bass, drum bass, and murky bass -- frequently first appearing at the transition, where they provide continuous rhythmic propulsion and forward drive.

# Core Definition
The classical period, with its newfound emphasis on a distinctive upper-voice melody, developed a number of conventionalized rhythmic-contour patterns to accompany that melody. Three particularly common patterns are the Alberti bass (arpeggiated chord in a specific contour), the drum bass (repeated notes), and the murky bass (alternating octaves or intervals). All three give rise to variants related to their basic shapes. These patterns can be found at any place within a classical movement, including the main theme, where they are typically associated with the medial phrase functions of continuation and contrasting middle. They are also prominently associated with the medial "thematic" functions of transition and core of the development. Transitions are often the first place in the movement where a conventional accompanimental pattern appears, bringing continuous rhythmic propulsion.

# Prerequisites
- **Transition**: The formal context where these patterns most prominently appear
- **Continuation function**: The intrathematic function with which patterns are associated

# Key Properties
1. Three main types: Alberti bass, drum bass, murky bass
2. All generate variants of their basic shapes
3. Prominently associated with medial functions (transition, continuation, development core)
4. Often first appear at the transition, signaling the movement is "getting under way"
5. Provide continuous rhythmic propulsion and forward drive
6. In main themes, associated with medial phrase functions (continuation, contrasting middle)
7. The onset of a new pattern can signal an accompanimental overlap at formal boundaries

# Construction / Recognition
- Alberti bass: arpeggiated chord pattern (typically lowest-highest-middle-highest)
- Drum bass: repeated notes or chords providing rhythmic regularity
- Murky bass: alternating octaves or intervals (typically root-octave alternation)
- Note where in the movement the pattern first appears (often the transition)
- Check whether the pattern coincides with a formal boundary (accompanimental overlap)

# Context & Application
The appearance of a conventional accompanimental pattern at the transition signals a fundamental change in the movement's character. The main theme's textural variety and rhythmic discontinuities give way to the continuous, driving momentum of the accompanimental pattern. This change supports the transition's function of generating forward motion toward the subordinate key. The pattern may continue into the subordinate theme or give way to a different accompanimental texture.

# Common Errors
- Assuming accompanimental patterns are exclusive to transitions (they appear throughout)
- Ignoring the formal significance of when a pattern first appears in the movement
- Treating accompanimental patterns as merely decorative rather than functionally significant

# Common Confusions
- **Not just keyboard**: Though most visible in piano works, these patterns appear in all genres
- **Medial association**: Patterns are associated with medial functions (continuation, transition, development) more than initiating or concluding functions
- **The "getting under way" effect**: The first appearance of a continuous pattern often marks the point where the movement's dynamic momentum truly begins
