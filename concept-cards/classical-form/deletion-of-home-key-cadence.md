# Quick Definition
The omission of the main theme's closing cadence in the recapitulation, justified by the ample opportunity to confirm the home key later in the subordinate theme.

# Formal Definition
Since there is ample opportunity to confirm the home key later in the recapitulation (in the subordinate theme), a cadence to end the main theme is dispensable. A subsequent closing section may also be omitted because the home key hardly requires reinforcement at this point in the form. This deletion reflects the main theme's changed function: home-key confirmation is no longer its primary role.

# Construction/Recognition
- The main theme in the exposition closes with a PAC, but in the recapitulation this cadence is omitted
- The main theme may end with a half cadence instead, or it may flow directly into the transition
- Associated closing sections may also be deleted
- Often occurs alongside other main-theme alterations (deletion of restatements, secondary development)
- The subordinate theme takes over the function of definitively confirming the home key

# Musical Context
The home key has already been reestablished at the end of the development. The recapitulation's main theme functions primarily as a signal of "return" and a local harmonic resolution of the development's final dominant. Extended home-key confirmation is postponed to the subordinate theme area.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i (ex. 11.1): main theme's cadence eliminated in recapitulation alongside deletion of restatement
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in G, K. 283, i (ex. 11.2): new continuation ends on I6 with no cadential closure; transition follows immediately

# Common Confusions
- Deletion of the cadence is not a compositional weakness — it reflects the changed formal needs of the recapitulation
- The cadence is not simply forgotten; it is deliberately omitted because the subordinate theme will provide home-key confirmation
- This deletion sometimes enables fusion of the main theme and transition
