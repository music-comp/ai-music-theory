# Quick Definition
A sonata form adapted for slow movements through various compression techniques that prevent the movement from becoming excessively long at a slower tempo.

# Formal Definition
Slow-movement sonata form retains the fundamental structure of sonata form (exposition, development, recapitulation) but employs characteristic modifications to reduce its scope: transition/subordinate-theme fusion, elimination of the transition, reduction of the development section, and use of a single subordinate theme. These compressions prevent the movement from becoming disproportionately long relative to surrounding fast movements. The exposition is subject to phrase-structural procedures that produce compressions or inhibit expansions, and standings on the dominant are generally held in check.

# Construction/Recognition
- Look for a conventional sonata layout but with noticeably compressed sections
- The exposition typically contains a single subordinate theme (often relatively tight knit)
- Transition and subordinate theme are frequently fused into a single grouping unit
- The development section is often much shorter than in fast-movement sonatas
- Form-functional fusion is regularly employed
- The exposition may lack a distinct transition entirely
- Dominant prolongations are kept shorter than in fast movements

# Musical Context
This form appears in slow interior movements (Adagio, Largo, Andante) of instrumental cycles. It maintains the sonata's tonal drama while accommodating the slower pacing of events. Many slow movements end on dominant harmony rather than tonic, with resolution occurring only at the start of the following movement, forging a stronger bond between movements.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in D, Op. 10/3, ii (transition/subordinate-theme fusion, mm. 9-17)
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E, Hob. XVI:31, ii (elimination of transition)
- Haydn, Piano Trio in A, Hob. XV:9, i (reduced development, approximately one-third the length of the exposition)

# Common Confusions
- Students may expect slow-movement sonatas to follow the same proportions as fast-movement sonatas. The development section in a Haydn slow-movement sonata can be less than one-third the length of the exposition, whereas in fast movements the development is roughly equal in length to the exposition and recapitulation.
- The ending on dominant harmony (common especially in Haydn) should not be mistaken for an incomplete movement; the resolution to tonic at the start of the next movement is an intentional formal link.
