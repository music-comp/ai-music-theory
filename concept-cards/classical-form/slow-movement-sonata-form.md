# Quick Definition
A sonata form adapted for slow movements through compression techniques -- transition/subordinate-theme fusion, elimination of the transition, reduced development, and use of a single subordinate theme -- that prevent the movement from becoming excessively long at slower tempos.

# Core Definition
Slow-movement sonata form retains the fundamental structure of sonata form (exposition, development, recapitulation) but employs characteristic modifications to reduce its scope. Because the slower pacing of events would make a fully elaborated sonata disproportionately long relative to surrounding fast movements, composers adopt phrase-structural procedures that produce compressions or inhibit expansions. The exposition typically contains a single subordinate theme that is relatively tight knit (though still looser than the main theme). Standings on the dominant are generally held in check, and form-functional fusion is regularly employed. The three principal compression techniques are: fusing the transition and subordinate theme into a single grouping unit, eliminating the transition entirely, and reducing the development section (sometimes to the point where it resembles a contrasting middle).

# Prerequisites
- **Sonata form**: The fundamental three-part structure being compressed
- **Transition**: The interthematic function most subject to elimination or fusion
- **Subordinate theme**: Understanding its normal construction to recognize compression
- **Development section**: Knowing standard development to recognize reduction

# Key Properties
1. Exposition contains a single subordinate theme (often relatively tight knit)
2. Transition/subordinate-theme fusion occurs with greater frequency than in fast movements
3. Transition may be eliminated entirely, with main theme followed directly by subordinate theme
4. Development section is often reduced to one-third or less of the exposition's length
5. Standings on the dominant are generally held in check
6. Form-functional fusion is regularly employed
7. Many slow movements end on dominant harmony, resolving at the start of the next movement

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for a conventional sonata layout but with noticeably compressed sections
- Check whether the transition and subordinate theme are fused (no clear boundary between them)
- Note if the exposition lacks a distinct transition entirely
- Compare the development's length to the exposition -- in slow movements it may be drastically shorter
- Assess whether the development resembles a contrasting middle of a small ternary
- Check for dominant ending rather than tonic closure

# Context & Application
This form appears in slow interior movements (Adagio, Largo, Andante) of instrumental cycles. It maintains the sonata's tonal drama while accommodating slower pacing. Transition/subordinate-theme fusion is favored by Mozart; reduced development is favored by Haydn. A number of slow movements, particularly by Haydn, end on dominant harmony as a remnant of baroque practice, with tonic resolution occurring only at the beginning of the following movement.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in D, Op. 10/3, ii, mm. 9-17: Transition/subordinate-theme fusion with model-sequence technique transitioning into cadential units confirming the subordinate key
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E, Hob. XVI:31, ii, mm. 1-8, 33-49: Elimination of transition; main theme's HC followed directly by subordinate theme in the new key; recapitulation eliminates entire subordinate theme
- Haydn, Piano Trio in A, Hob. XV:9, i, mm. 26-36: Reduced development, less than one-third the length of the exposition, touching three tonal regions via descending third sequence

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Sonata form (the fundamental structure being adapted)
- Transition/subordinate-theme fusion (the primary compression technique)

## Enables
- Understanding of formal proportion across movement types
- Recognition of inter-movement connections through dominant endings

## Related
- Sonata without development (another compressed sonata variant for slow movements)
- Large ternary form (alternative slow-movement form)
- Theme and variations (alternative slow-movement form)

## Contrasts With
- Fast-movement sonata form (fully elaborated proportions, development roughly equal to exposition)
- Large ternary form (different formal logic despite surface similarities)

# Common Errors
- Expecting slow-movement sonatas to follow the same proportions as fast-movement sonatas
- Failing to recognize that a reduced development may resemble a contrasting middle without being one
- Overlooking fusion boundaries where transition function blends into subordinate-theme function

# Common Confusions
- **Dominant ending vs. incomplete movement**: Ending on dominant harmony (especially in Haydn) is intentional, creating inter-movement connection, not an incomplete form
- **Reduced development vs. contrasting middle**: A very short development may resemble a contrasting middle, but the overall form is still sonata, not small ternary
- **Monothematic subordinate theme**: When Haydn uses main-theme material for the subordinate theme, the recapitulation must be substantially rewritten to avoid redundancy
