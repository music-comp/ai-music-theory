# Quick Definition
A formal section's conclusion that lacks a genuine cadence, ending instead with a dominant arrival or premature dominant arrival, representing one of the strongest markers of loose formal organization.

# Core Definition
A noncadential ending occurs when a formal section concludes without a genuine cadence. The section may reach its harmonic goal (typically the dominant), but the manner of arrival prevents it from functioning as a true cadential goal. This can happen when the dominant is inverted, contains a dissonant seventh, arrives without a proper cadential progression, or appears prematurely before the phrase-structural end. Noncadential endings are characteristic of transitions and contrasting middles. In the subordinate theme, the concept applies when an internal dominant arrival functions as a structural midpoint rather than a true closing gesture.

# Prerequisites
- **Half cadence**: The cadential type that a noncadential ending fails to achieve
- **Dominant arrival**: The noncadential analogue to a half cadence
- **Cadential function**: The formal function that is absent or unfulfilled

# Key Properties
1. No authentic cadential progression concludes the section
2. Final dominant may be inverted, contain a seventh, or arrive prematurely
3. Often marked by fermata to compensate for lack of cadential articulation
4. Results in significantly looser organization on the tight-knit/loose continuum
5. Common in contrasting middles, transitions, and development cores
6. May be followed by standing on the dominant to reinforce the ending quality
7. Distinguished from a half cadence by the absence of a proper cadential progression

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the final dominant of a section
- Check whether a cadential progression leads to it (if not, it is noncadential)
- Check whether the dominant is in root position without a dissonant seventh
- Determine whether the harmonic and phrase-structural endpoints coincide
- A fermata often signals a noncadential ending but does not convert it into a cadence

# Context & Application
In the tight-knit/loose continuum, noncadential closure represents the loosest possibility for ending a formal section. It is entirely appropriate for transitions (which Caplin calls "themelike units" precisely because they need not end with a cadence) and for contrasting middles. In development sections, cores frequently end with dominant arrivals rather than genuine half cadences, especially when the dominant arrives through sequential processes rather than cadential progressions.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in G Minor, Op. 49/1, ii, m. 12: V4/3 with fermata -- dominant is inverted and dissonant
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 7, Op. 92, i, mm. 81-88: premature arrival; harmonic goal precedes phrase-structural end
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 2, Op. 36, i, m. 61: transition ends with dominant in first inversion (noncadential)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 31/3, ii, mm. 10-19: premature dominant arrival, no cadence

# Common Errors
- Calling every dominant arrival a half cadence
- Assuming a fermata creates cadential status
- Treating noncadential endings as analytical failures rather than compositional choices

# Common Confusions
- **Noncadential ending vs. half cadence**: A dominant arrival is NOT a half cadence; cadences carry greater structural weight and require proper cadential progressions
- **Fermata function**: A fermata emphasizes the ending quality but does not convert a noncadential dominant into a genuine cadence
- **Harmonic goal**: A noncadential ending does not mean the section lacks a harmonic goal; the dominant IS the goal, but it is reached noncadentially
