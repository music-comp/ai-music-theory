# Quick Definition
Transitions employ four principal opening strategies: beginning with new material (after PAC), beginning with main-theme material (especially after HC), beginning with a false closing section (codettas that become the transition), and beginning from a nontonic region (especially vi) -- the choice correlates with the main theme's cadential ending.

# Core Definition
The four principal opening strategies for transitions are: (1) New material -- commonly used after the main theme has closed with a PAC, introducing brilliant-style passage work or other contrasting material; (2) Main-theme material -- typically employed when the main theme ends with an HC, where the return of the basic idea gives the impression of a consequent before the music departs; (3) False closing section -- codettas following a PAC that are retrospectively understood as the transition's presentation; and (4) Nontonic region -- the transition begins directly in a nontonic area (usually vi) when the main theme has already strongly emphasized tonic through a closing section or multiple themes. The transition's beginning is often confirmed only retrospectively, when the music's subsequent behavior reveals its transitional function.

# Prerequisites
- **Transition**: The formal function being initiated
- **Main-theme function in sonata**: The preceding unit whose ending influences the choice

# Key Properties
1. New material: most common after PAC; brilliant style, fortissimo, passage work
2. Main-theme material: most common after HC; initially sounds like consequent beginning
3. False closing section: after PAC; codettas "become" the transition retrospectively
4. Nontonic region: after strong tonic emphasis; vi is the most common starting harmony
5. The choice correlates with the main theme's cadential ending (PAC, HC, or PAC + closing section)
6. The transition's beginning is often confirmed retrospectively
7. Three unusual strategies also exist: later portions of main theme, B section material, A' section that becomes transition

# Construction / Recognition
- Note the main theme's cadence type: PAC, HC, or IAC
- If PAC + new material: strategy 1
- If HC + return of basic idea: strategy 2 (initially sounds like consequent)
- If PAC + codetta material that departs from tonic: strategy 3 (false closing section)
- If PAC + closing section + nontonic opening: strategy 4
- In all cases, the transition's beginning may be confirmed only retrospectively

# Context & Application
The correlation between the main theme's ending and the transition's opening strategy reflects the composer's sensitivity to formal logic. After an HC, returning the basic idea creates the strongest sense of continuation; after a PAC, new material or a false closing section provides contrast; after extensive tonic emphasis, a nontonic opening accelerates the destabilization process. Three additional, less common strategies involve using later portions of the main theme (continuation or cadential idea), the B section of a ternary main theme, or the A' section that departs from the home key.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C, Op. 2/3, i, m. 13 (Ch. 11, Ex. 11.6): new material -- brilliant-style arpeggios and scales in fortissimo after PAC, with structural elision
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in C, K. 403, i, mm. 5-8 (Ch. 11, Ex. 11.7): main-theme material -- sentence repeated with HC ending (nonmodulating transition)
- Haydn, Piano Trio in E-flat, H. 30, i, mm. 16-26 (Ch. 11, Ex. 11.8): false closing section -- codettas become transition presentation when music moves to VI
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in E minor, K. 304, i, mm. 29-36 (Ch. 11, Ex. 11.9): nontonic region -- begins on VI (C major) after extensive tonic emphasis

# Common Errors
- Assuming the transition always begins with obviously new material
- Missing the retrospective nature of the transition's beginning
- Not considering the main theme's cadence type when predicting the transition's opening

# Common Confusions
- **Retrospective determination**: The transition's beginning is often not obvious at first hearing; subsequent events confirm it
- **Strategy 2 vs. second main theme**: When main-theme material returns after an HC, it may sound like a consequent (and potentially a second main theme) until the music modulates
- **Strategy 3 vs. genuine closing section**: A genuine closing section stays on tonic and ends with a clear break; a false closing section departs to nontonic harmony
