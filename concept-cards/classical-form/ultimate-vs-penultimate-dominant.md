# Quick Definition
A penultimate dominant groups with the following tonic as part of an authentic cadential progression, while an ultimate dominant stands as the final harmony of a half-cadential progression. The distinction is not about harmony per se but about how the dominant groups with its surrounding harmonies.

# Core Definition
"Whether a dominant can be characterized as one or the other has nothing to do with harmony per se. Rather, it is a matter of how the dominant *groups* with its surrounding harmonies" (Ch. 1, p. 17). "In the case of a penultimate dominant (as found in an authentic cadential progression), the dominant *groups* with the following tonic as part of the progression" (Ch. 1, p. 17). "In the case of an ultimate dominant (as found in a half-cadential progression), the dominant does not group with the following tonic, since the latter signals the start of a new progression" (Ch. 1, p. 17). The ultimate dominant must be a root-position consonant triad -- "adding a dissonant seventh, so appropriate for the penultimate position within an authentic cadential progression, would overly destabilize the ultimate dominant of a half-cadential progression" (Ch. 1, p. 17).

# Prerequisites
- **Authentic cadence** -- the progression containing a penultimate dominant
- **Half cadence** -- the progression containing an ultimate dominant
- **Dominant function** -- the harmonic function being classified

# Key Properties
1. Penultimate dominant: V within an authentic cadential progression, grouping with following I
2. Ultimate dominant: V as the goal of a half-cadential progression, not grouping with following I
3. The distinction is about *grouping*, not about harmony
4. The ultimate dominant must be a root-position consonant triad (no seventh)
5. The penultimate dominant may contain a seventh (V7)
6. The ultimate dominant may be embellished by the cadential six-four
7. Both must be in root position for cadential status

# Construction / Recognition
- Determine whether V is the final harmony of its progression (ultimate) or precedes a tonic (penultimate)
- If V is the goal harmony and the following I starts a new progression: ultimate (HC)
- If V resolves to I within the same progression: penultimate (authentic cadence)
- Check: does the dominant contain a seventh? If so, it is penultimate (not suitable as ultimate)
- The cadential six-four may embellish either type

# Context & Application
This distinction is essential for correctly identifying cadence types. Many students confuse HC and PAC because both involve V and I in succession. The key question is whether V and I group together (authentic cadence) or whether V is itself the goal and I begins a new formal unit (half cadence). This grouping perspective also applies to the analysis of formal boundaries -- the half cadence marks the end of a unit, while the authentic cadence creates closure within it.

# Common Errors
- Adding a seventh to the ultimate dominant of a half cadence
- Confusing all V-I motions as authentic cadences (some are prolongational)
- Treating the distinction as one of harmony rather than grouping

# Common Confusions
- The difference between ultimate and penultimate has nothing to do with the dominant's harmonic content -- it is entirely about how V groups with surrounding harmonies
- A V-I motion is not automatically an authentic cadence -- the V may be the ultimate harmony of a half-cadential progression, with I starting a new unit
- The ultimate dominant's restriction to a consonant triad is a stability requirement: the goal harmony of a half cadence must possess sufficient stability
