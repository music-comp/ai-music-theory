# Quick Definition
The compositional devices--such as repetition, fragmentation, liquidation, extension, and expansion--that operate within and across formal functions to shape a theme's structure.

# Formal Definition
Formal processes are the compositional techniques that articulate and differentiate formal functions. Key processes include: repetition (exact, statement-response, sequential), fragmentation (reduction in unit size), liquidation (elimination of characteristic motives), extension (adding material after a function is expressed), and expansion (internal lengthening of components). These processes interact with harmonic progression to create the specific character of each formal function.

# Construction/Recognition
- Repetition: immediate restatement of a unit (exact, transposed, or sequential)
- Fragmentation: reduction of unit size (e.g., two-measure to one-measure units)
- Liquidation: stripping characteristic motivic features, leaving conventional material
- Extension: adding material after a function is already established
- Expansion: stretching internal components beyond normative length

# Musical Context
These processes are essential to the identity of formal functions. Presentation relies on repetition; continuation depends on fragmentation and harmonic acceleration; cadential function involves liquidation.

# Examples
- Fragmentation: Beethoven, Op. 2/1, i, mm. 5-6 reduce the two-measure basic idea to one-measure units
- Liquidation: Op. 2/1, i, mm. 5-8, where motive "a" is eliminated and conventional cadential figures replace characteristic motives
- Sequential repetition: Beethoven, Op. 135, iii, mm. 7-9 (model-sequence technique)

# Common Confusions
- Fragmentation concerns unit length, not motivic content. A fragment may or may not derive motivically from the basic idea.
- Extension and expansion are different: extension adds on after; expansion stretches from within.
