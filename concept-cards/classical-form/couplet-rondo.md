# Quick Definition
A contrasting passage in rondo form that alternates with the refrain, set in a different tonal region and organized as either a subordinate-theme complex, an interior theme, or a development-like section.

# Core Definition
Couplets (also called "episodes" or "digressions") are the contrasting passages in rondo form that alternate with the refrain. Each couplet is numbered according to its position. The various couplets usually (though not always) contrast with one another as well as with the refrain. In a five-part rondo, couplet 1 may be a subordinate-theme complex or an interior theme; couplet 2 is most often an interior theme or a development-like unit. In a sonata-rondo, couplet 1 is a sonata-exposition subordinate-theme complex; couplet 2 is a development or interior theme; couplet 3 recapitulates couplet 1's subordinate-theme complex in the home key.

# Construction / Recognition
- Contrasts with the refrain in material, tonal region, and often form
- Each couplet is numbered (couplet 1, couplet 2, couplet 3)
- Standard organizational types:
  - Subordinate-theme complex: transition, subordinate theme(s), closing section, retransition
  - Interior theme: typically minore, IV, or vi; often modeled on small ternary
  - Development-like: model-sequence technique, tonal exploration
- Each couplet typically ends with a retransition preparing the return of the refrain
- Set in contrasting tonal regions (V, minore, IV, vi)

# Context & Application
Couplets provide the contrasting material that defines the rondo's alternation pattern. Their organizational type determines much about the character of the rondo: couplets organized as subordinate-theme complexes create a more sonata-like effect, while interior-theme couplets create a more lyrical, ternary-like effect.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, iii: Couplet 1 as compressed subordinate-theme complex in V
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in G, Hob. XVI:39, i: Couplet 1 as minore interior theme
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 3, Op. 55, ii: Couplet 2 as development-like fugal passage

# Common Confusions
- "Couplet," "episode," and "digression" are all used for these contrasting passages. Caplin prefers "couplet" as part of a systematic terminology paired with "refrain."
- Not all couplets are organized the same way. Students should identify the specific organizational type (subordinate-theme complex, interior theme, or development-like) for each couplet.
