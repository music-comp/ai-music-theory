# Quick Definition
The noncadential appearance of the final dominant at the end of a transition, where a half-cadential progression may be present but the dominant fails to create a genuine half cadence due to inversion, dissonance, or noncongruence with phrase structure.

# Formal Definition
Unlike a main theme or subordinate theme, a transition need not necessarily end with a cadence. In some cases, a half-cadential progression is present, but various factors obscure or destroy true cadential closure: the dominant may be inverted, may contain a dissonant seventh, may arrive before the phrase-structural end (premature dominant arrival), or may result from a sequential chain rather than a cadential progression. In such cases, the term "dominant arrival" distinguishes the noncadential ending from a genuine HC. The dominant may still function as an ending harmony through other means: marking what sounds like the beginning of a standing on the dominant, featuring liquidation and textural reduction, or being elongated relative to preceding harmonies.

# Construction/Recognition
1. **Presence of cadential progression but noncadential arrival**: Final dominant inverted, with seventh, or premature
2. **Absence of cadential progression**: Final dominant appears as last link of a sequential chain or without recognizable cadential preparation
3. The dominant may still SOUND like an ending through:
   - Beginning of a standing on the dominant
   - Liquidation and textural reduction
   - Elongation relative to preceding harmonies
4. Noncongruence between harmonic arrival and melodic-motivic change is a loosening trait
5. Sometimes the dominant resolves to I6 (premature) with continuation, then later achieves proper cadential status

# Musical Context
Dominant arrivals at the end of transitions are more common and more formally acceptable than in themes, because the transition is not a genuine theme and does not require cadential closure. The noncadential quality of the dominant arrival contributes to the transition's characteristic looseness. The discrepancy between harmonic arrival and melodic-motivic change is itself a loosening trait typical of transition sections, creating a noncongruence that would be unusual in tight-knit themes.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 2, Op. 36, i, m. 61 (dominant arrives in first inversion -- noncadential; immediately followed by root-position dominant in standing on dominant)
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 4, Op. 60, i, m. 92 (final dominant as last link of sequential progression; harmonic goal at m. 92 precedes melodic goal at m. 95 -- premature dominant arrival)
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:21, ii, mm. 10-12 (cadential 6/4 prolonged for over two measures before resolving; noncongruence between harmonic and melodic arrival)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C Minor, Op. 10/1, iii, mm. 14-16 (I-V progressions in series; no single dominant has clear cadential status; standing on dominant established)

# Common Confusions
- A dominant arrival is NOT a half cadence -- the distinction matters because cadences carry greater structural weight
- Noncongruence between harmonic arrival and melodic change is a TYPICAL feature of transition endings, not an analytical error
- A dominant that sounds like an ending (through elongation, liquidation, or standing on dominant) can serve the transition's formal needs even without cadential status
- Multiple I-V progressions near the end of a transition do not each create a HC -- they may prolong tonic or dominant without any achieving cadential status
