# Quick Definition
The noncadential appearance of the final dominant at the end of a transition, where a half-cadential progression may be present but the dominant fails to create a genuine half cadence due to inversion, dissonance, or noncongruence with phrase structure.

# Core Definition
Unlike a main or subordinate theme, a transition need not end with a cadence. In some cases, a half-cadential progression is present but various factors obscure or destroy true cadential closure: the dominant may be inverted, may contain a dissonant seventh, may arrive before the phrase-structural end (premature dominant arrival), or may result from a sequential chain rather than a cadential progression. The term "dominant arrival" distinguishes such situations from genuine half cadences. The dominant may still function as an ending harmony through other means: marking the beginning of a standing on the dominant, featuring liquidation and textural reduction, or being elongated relative to preceding harmonies.

# Prerequisites
- **Transition**: The formal context where noncadential endings are acceptable
- **Half cadence**: The genuine cadential type from which dominant arrival differs
- **Dominant arrival**: The general concept of noncadential dominant emphasis

# Key Properties
1. Final dominant does not create genuine half cadence
2. Factors preventing HC: inversion, dissonant seventh, premature arrival, sequential origin
3. The dominant may still SOUND like an ending through elongation, liquidation, or standing on dominant
4. Noncongruence between harmonic arrival and melodic-motivic change is a typical loosening trait
5. Acceptable because transitions are themelike units, not genuine themes
6. The standing on the dominant (when present) follows the dominant arrival
7. The discrepancy between harmonic and melodic-motivic closure is characteristic of transition endings

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the final dominant and check for cadential qualities (root position, triad, phrase-structural alignment)
- Note any factors preventing genuine HC (inversion, seventh, sequential origin, premature arrival)
- Check whether the dominant functions as an ending through other means
- Observe the standing on the dominant that typically follows
- Assess the noncongruence between harmonic and melodic-motivic arrival

# Context & Application
Dominant arrivals are more common and formally acceptable in transitions than in themes, because the transition does not require cadential closure. The noncadential quality contributes to the transition's looseness. The discrepancy between harmonic arrival and melodic-motivic change is a loosening trait typical of transition sections.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 2, Op. 36, i, m. 61: dominant arrives in first inversion (noncadential); immediately followed by root-position dominant in standing on dominant
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 4, Op. 60, i, m. 92: final dominant as last link of sequential progression; harmonic goal precedes melodic goal at m. 95 (premature dominant arrival)
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:21, ii, mm. 10-12: cadential 6/4 prolonged for over two measures before resolving; noncongruence between harmonic and melodic arrival
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C Minor, Op. 10/1, iii, mm. 14-16: multiple I-V progressions; no single dominant has clear cadential status

# Common Errors
- Treating every dominant at the end of a transition as a genuine half cadence
- Ignoring factors that prevent cadential status (inversion, seventh, sequential origin)
- Confusing the standing on the dominant with the dominant arrival itself

# Common Confusions
- **Dominant arrival vs. half cadence**: The distinction matters because cadences carry greater structural weight
- **Noncongruence as feature**: Discrepancy between harmonic and melodic arrival is a TYPICAL transition feature, not an analytical error
- **Multiple I-V progressions**: Near a transition's end, multiple I-V progressions do not each create HCs -- they may prolong tonic or dominant without cadential status
