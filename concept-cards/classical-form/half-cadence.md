# Quick Definition
A cadential progression in which the dominant itself becomes the goal harmony (root-position consonant triad, no seventh), providing weaker tonal confirmation than an authentic cadence and creating structural incompleteness.

# Core Definition
"In the half-cadential progression, the dominant itself becomes the goal harmony and so occupies the ultimate position" (Ch. 2, p. 28). "To acquire the requisite stability for an ending harmony, the dominant of the half-cadential progression must take the form of a root-position triad" without "a dissonant seventh" which "would overly destabilize" it (Ch. 2, p. 29). Half-cadential progressions "can contain the same harmonies as authentic cadential ones" with adjustments: the final chord is V (consonant triad) rather than continuing to tonic resolution. "Several other paradigms, in which the ultimate dominant is approached by descending motion from the sixth degree (usually lowered), are especially associated with that progression" (Ch. 2, p. 29).

# Prerequisites
- **Cadential progression** -- the broader category
- **Dominant function** -- the harmony that serves as goal

# Key Properties
1. Ends on root-position V (consonant triad, no seventh)
2. Complete: I(6) - PD - V; incomplete: omits initial T or PD
3. Weaker tonal confirmation than authentic cadence
4. Creates structural incompleteness demanding formal continuation
5. All authentic cadential paradigms apply (adjusted to end on V)
6. Special approach: b6 descending to scale-degree 5 in bass
7. Often followed by standing on the dominant (postcadential)

# Construction / Recognition
- Progression ends on root-position V triad (no seventh)
- Check for proper cadential approach (initial tonic and/or pre-dominant preceding V)
- Note: V is the GOAL harmony, not the penultimate harmony

# Context & Application
HC closes antecedent phrases in periods, ends some sentence themes (weaker closure than PAC), and concludes contrasting middles in small ternaries. Its structural incompleteness demands a consequent (in periods), continuation (in sentences), or recapitulation (in small ternaries).

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 1): Beethoven, Op. 2/1, i, m. 8: HC closes the main theme sentence.
**Example 2** (Ch. 1): Beethoven, Op. 15, ii, m. 12: HC closes the contrasting middle.
**Example 3** (Ch. 1): Mozart, K. 525, ii, m. 4: HC closes the antecedent of a period.
**Example 4** (Ch. 2, ex. 2.10): Special approach with b6 descending to scale-degree 5 in bass.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Cadential progression and dominant function
## Enables
- Antecedent function (HC creates structural incompleteness)
- Standing on the dominant (postcadential prolongation)
- Contrasting middles (HC is typical ending)
## Related
- Authentic cadence (stronger confirmation)
## Contrasts With
- Authentic cadence: AC resolves V to I; HC ends on V as goal

# Common Errors
- Adding a seventh to the HC dominant
- Confusing a HC goal dominant with a penultimate dominant

# Common Confusions
- The dominant in a HC must be a consonant triad (no seventh) -- a seventh would make it unstable and seeking resolution
- HC is a genuine cadence (it confirms tonality, albeit weakly), not merely an interrupted progression
