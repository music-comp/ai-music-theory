# Quick Definition
The solo recapitulation must synthesize elements from both the opening ritornello and the solo exposition into a single section, often resulting in an organization significantly different from either prior exposition, with ritornello material reintroduced at new formal positions.

# Core Definition
The concerto recapitulation fulfills the same basic functions as a sonata recapitulation (projecting large-scale return and resolving tonal conflict), but it has an additional function: elements of both the opening ritornello and the solo exposition must be recapitulated in a single section. Sometimes the recapitulation, especially at its start, resembles the opening ritornello more than the solo exposition. More often, however, the recapitulation is organized differently from either earlier section. In particular, the recapitulation is likely to reintroduce ideas from the opening ritornello that were not used in the solo exposition and that found no place in the subordinate-key ritornello or development. As a result, the formal placement of various passages is significantly altered compared to their original positions (Caplin, pp. 714-716).

# Key Properties
1. Must synthesize material from both the ritornello and the solo exposition
2. Often organized differently from either earlier section
3. May begin with ritornello main-theme material rather than solo main-theme material
4. Reintroduces ritornello ideas not used in the solo exposition
5. Alternative main theme rarely begins the recapitulation
6. The first lyrical subordinate theme of the ritornello often returns here for the first time
7. Formal placement of passages significantly altered from original positions

# Construction / Recognition
1. Compare the recapitulation with both the ritornello and the solo exposition
2. Identify material from the ritornello appearing for the first time in the solo
3. Note where the formal placement of ideas differs from both previous sections
4. Track how alternative main-theme material is handled (if applicable)

# Context & Application
This synthesis principle is the strongest argument for the "double exposition" concept: the recapitulation's complexity makes sense only if one understands it as recapitulating material from two different sources. Understanding both earlier sections is essential for analyzing the recapitulation.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 716): Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i -- ritornello's first subordinate theme (m. 51, quiet and peaceful) eliminated in solo exposition, returns at beginning of recapitulation's subordinate-theme group (m. 312) played by solo piano.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Expecting the recapitulation to follow the solo exposition's plan. **Correction**: The recapitulation synthesizes material from both the ritornello and the solo exposition, often in a new arrangement.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Why the recapitulation contains material not heard in the solo exposition. **Clarification**: Ritornello material that was deleted from the solo exposition must be recapitulated somewhere.
