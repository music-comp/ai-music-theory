# Quick Definition
A view of sonata form emphasizing the dramatic conflict between two contrasting tonal regions (home key and subordinate key), their intensification in the development, and resolution in the recapitulation.

# Formal Definition
The tonal-polarity model sees the exposition as establishing two contrasting tonal regions — the home key and the subordinate key. The subordinate key creates a "structural dissonance" in relation to the home key, one that is intensified throughout the development and eventually resolved (in favor of the home key) in the recapitulation. This view has dominated recent sonata-form scholarship but is deficient in several respects: the notion of structural dissonance is most evident in major-mode movements (where V provides genuine contrast) but less palpable in minor-mode movements (where III shares the same basic scale); and it says little about the development's tonal plan.

# Construction/Recognition
- Exposition: two keys established — home key (I) and subordinate key (V in major, III in minor)
- Subordinate key as "structural dissonance" requiring resolution
- Development: intensifies the tonal conflict
- Recapitulation: resolves the conflict by restoring subordinate-theme material to the home key
- Most clearly applicable to major-mode movements

# Musical Context
The tonal-polarity model provides the most widely used framework for understanding sonata form's large-scale tonal plan. It is complemented by the tour-of-keys model, which accounts for aspects the polarity model overlooks.

# Examples
- Any major-mode sonata with V as the subordinate key provides the clearest illustration of tonal polarity

# Common Confusions
- The polarity model is less convincing for minor-mode movements, where the subordinate key (III) is closely related to the home key
- The model does not adequately explain the development section's tonal plan (why specific keys are explored)
- Tonal polarity is one of two complementary models, not the sole explanation of sonata form's tonal design
