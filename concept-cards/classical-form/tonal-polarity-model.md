# Quick Definition
A view of sonata form emphasizing the dramatic conflict between two contrasting tonal regions (home key and subordinate key), their intensification in the development, and resolution in the recapitulation.

# Core Definition
The tonal-polarity model sees the exposition as establishing two contrasting tonal regions -- the home key and the subordinate key. The subordinate key creates a "structural dissonance" in relation to the home key, one that is intensified throughout the development and eventually resolved (in favor of the home key) in the recapitulation. This view has dominated recent sonata-form scholarship but is deficient in several respects: the notion of structural dissonance is most evident in major-mode movements (V provides genuine contrast) but less palpable in minor-mode movements (III shares the same basic scale); and the model says little about the development's tonal plan, treating its keys merely as prolonging the exposition's dominant (following Schenker).

# Prerequisites
- **Sonata form**: The formal framework
- **Subordinate key**: The second tonal region creating structural dissonance

# Key Properties
1. Exposition establishes two keys: home key (I) and subordinate key (V in major, III in minor)
2. The subordinate key creates "structural dissonance" requiring resolution
3. Development intensifies the tonal conflict
4. Recapitulation resolves the conflict by restoring subordinate-theme material to the home key
5. Most clearly applicable to major-mode movements
6. Less convincing for minor-mode movements (III shares the same basic scale)
7. Does not adequately explain the development section's tonal plan
8. Complemented by the tour-of-keys model

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the two primary keys established in the exposition
- Trace the intensification of tonal conflict in the development
- Note the resolution when the subordinate theme returns in the home key in the recapitulation

# Context & Application
The tonal-polarity model provides the most widely used framework for understanding sonata form's large-scale tonal plan. However, it must be complemented by the tour-of-keys model, which accounts for the development's internal tonal design and the systematic exploration of keys throughout the movement. Together, the two models provide a "fairly complete picture."

# Examples
- Any major-mode sonata with V as the subordinate key provides the clearest illustration
- Minor-mode sonatas with III as subordinate key illustrate the model's limitations

# Common Errors
- Treating the polarity model as the sole explanation of sonata form's tonal design
- Applying it uncritically to minor-mode movements

# Common Confusions
- **Minor-mode limitation**: The polarity model is less convincing for minor-mode movements, where III is closely related to the home key
- **Development omission**: The model does not adequately explain why specific keys are explored in the development
- **Complementary, not exhaustive**: Tonal polarity is one of two complementary models; the tour-of-keys model addresses its gaps
