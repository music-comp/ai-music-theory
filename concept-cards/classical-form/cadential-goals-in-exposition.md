# Quick Definition
The succession of cadences in a sonata exposition that articulates two primary keys: a tonal curve from partial home-key confirmation (HC), through full confirmation (PAC), to destabilization (HC), partial subordinate-key confirmation (HC), and full confirmation (PAC in SK).

# Formal Definition
In a sonata exposition, the establishment of two primary keys is articulated by a succession of cadential goals. In its complete form, this series projects a tonal curve: (1) partial confirmation of the home key by HC, (2) full confirmation by PAC, (3) destabilization of the home key by HC or dominant arrival, (4) partial confirmation of the subordinate key by HC or dominant arrival, and (5) full confirmation by PAC. Any of the first four cadences can be omitted, but the PAC in the subordinate key must always appear. The exposition must also contain at least one cadence confirming the home key.

# Construction/Recognition
Five potential cadential goals in the complete form:
1. HC in home key (optional) — usually at end of antecedent or main theme
2. PAC in home key (optional but common) — at end of main theme
3. HC in home key or dominant arrival (optional) — at end of transition
4. HC or dominant arrival in subordinate key (optional) — internal to subordinate theme
5. PAC in subordinate key (required) — at end of subordinate theme

# Musical Context
These cadential goals map onto the eight patterns in Caplin's Table 13.1, which shows how different combinations of cadences create different exposition designs. The PAC in the subordinate key is the only indispensable cadence.

# Common Confusions
- The complete five-cadence series is an idealized form; most expositions omit one or more cadences
- Only the final PAC in the subordinate key is truly required
- The order of cadences is fixed — they cannot be rearranged
