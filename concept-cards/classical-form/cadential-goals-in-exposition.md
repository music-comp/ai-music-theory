# Quick Definition
The succession of cadences in a sonata exposition that articulates two primary keys: a tonal curve from partial home-key confirmation (HC), through full confirmation (PAC), to destabilization (HC), partial subordinate-key confirmation (HC), and full confirmation (PAC in SK).

# Core Definition
In a sonata exposition, the establishment of two primary keys is articulated by a succession of cadential goals. In its complete form, this series projects a tonal curve that (1) partially confirms the home key by means of a half cadence, (2) fully confirms that key by a perfect authentic cadence, (3) destabilizes the home key by a half cadence or dominant arrival, (4) partially confirms the subordinate key by a half cadence or dominant arrival, and (5) fully confirms the new key by a perfect authentic cadence. Any one of the first four cadences can be omitted, but the PAC of the subordinate key must always appear. The exposition must also contain at least one cadence confirming the home key. These cadential goals relate to the exposition's constituent interthematic functions in a variety of ways, as shown in the eight cadential patterns of Table 13.1.

# Key Properties
1. Five potential cadential goals in the complete tonal curve
2. The PAC in the subordinate key is always required
3. At least one home-key cadence is also required
4. Any of the first four cadences can be individually omitted
5. The eight cadential patterns (Table 13.1) show how these goals relate to interthematic functions
6. The complete five-cadence series (Pattern 5) is relatively rare
7. The order of cadences is fixed -- they cannot be rearranged

# Construction / Recognition
1. HC in home key (optional) -- usually at end of antecedent or main theme
2. PAC in home key (optional but common) -- at end of main theme
3. HC in home key or dominant arrival (optional) -- at end of transition
4. HC or dominant arrival in subordinate key (optional) -- internal to subordinate theme or transition pt 2
5. PAC in subordinate key (required) -- at end of subordinate theme

# Context & Application
These cadential goals provide a framework for understanding the exposition's tonal design. They map onto the eight patterns in Table 13.1, showing how different combinations create different exposition designs. Understanding the cadential goals helps the analyst identify which pattern is at work.

# Examples
- Pattern 5 (all five cadences): the most complete tonal curve, with periodic main theme, nonmodulating transition, and two-part subordinate theme
- Pattern 1 (most common): sentential main theme ends PAC; modulating transition; subordinate theme ends PAC in SK

# Common Errors
- Assuming all five cadences must appear (most expositions omit one or more)
- Forgetting that the PAC in SK is the only indispensable cadence

# Common Confusions
- **Idealized vs. actual**: The complete five-cadence series is an idealized form; most expositions omit one or more
- **Single requirement**: Only the final PAC in the subordinate key is truly required
- **Home-key minimum**: At least one cadence confirming the home key must also be present
- **Fixed order**: The cadences occur in a fixed order corresponding to the tonal curve
