# Quick Definition
The first large-scale section of sonata form, establishing two contrasting tonal regions through a succession of cadential goals articulated by the main theme, transition, and subordinate theme.

# Formal Definition
The sonata exposition modulates to a subordinate key, giving rise to the central tonal characteristic of the form: the dramatic establishment of two contrasting tonal regions. The exposition contains three interthematic functions — main theme (establishing the home key), transition (destabilizing the home key and leading to the subordinate key), and subordinate theme (confirming the subordinate key) — arranged in a specific cadential sequence. The exposition is normally repeated.

# Construction/Recognition
- **Main theme**: confirms the home key (PAC or HC); tight-knit organization
- **Transition**: destabilizes the home key; modulates to the subordinate key; ends with HC or dominant arrival in the new key
- **Subordinate theme**: confirms the subordinate key with PAC; loose organization
- **Closing section**: codettas following the final PAC; may include a retransition
- The minimum requirement: at least one HK cadence and a PAC in SK
- Eight standard cadential patterns (Table 13.1) describe the possible arrangements

# Musical Context
The exposition presents the movement's melodic-motivic material in its original form and establishes the tonal conflict that drives the rest of the movement. Its repeat ensures the listener absorbs both the material and the tonal plan.

# Common Confusions
- The exposition is not simply "the themes" — it is a tonal process articulated by cadences
- The main theme does not always end with PAC; HC is also possible
- The boundary between transition and subordinate theme may be obscured (see separate concept card)
