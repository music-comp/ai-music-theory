# Quick Definition
Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven each approach the development section differently: Haydn typically omits the core in favor of rotation; Mozart uses pre-core/core technique with tight-knit pre-cores and new material; Beethoven extends the tonal scope with modal borrowing and favors dominant arrivals over half cadences.

# Core Definition
Haydn, as a general rule, does not employ the pre-core/core technique, though some prominent examples do arise. He tends to write transition-like or subordinate-themelike units in place of a core. Moreover, Haydn frequently runs through exposition material in roughly the same order, exemplifying "rotational" form (Hepokoski and Darcy). Mozart employs pre-core/core technique regularly but tends to restrict the overall scope of his developments, which are usually quite shorter than the exposition or recapitulation. More than the others, Mozart begins developments with a tight-knit pre-core and likes to introduce new material. Beethoven extends the tonal scope to include regions from modal borrowing (bVI, bIII, bII). Like Mozart, he uses pre-core/core technique and introduces new ideas, but like Haydn, his development sections are comparable in size to the exposition and recapitulation. More than his mentors, Beethoven employs dominant arrivals in place of half cadences.

# Prerequisites
- **Development section**: The formal context
- **Core technique**: The organizing principle that distinguishes the approaches
- **Pre-core**: Present in Mozart and Beethoven, adapted in Haydn

# Key Properties
1. **Haydn**: no core (uses transition-like/subordinate-themelike units); rotational ordering; roughly equal in size to exposition
2. **Mozart**: regular core; shorter developments; tight-knit pre-cores; new material common
3. **Beethoven**: regular core; extended tonal scope (modal borrowing); dominant arrivals; comparable in size to exposition
4. Haydn's rotational form recycles exposition material in roughly the same order
5. Mozart rarely introduces transition or subordinate-theme material at the start of the pre-core
6. Beethoven's modal borrowing creates development keys from the parallel minor (bVI, bIII, bII)
7. Half cadences are the norm for core endings; Beethoven more often replaces them with dominant arrivals

# Construction / Recognition
- Check for pre-core/core technique: if absent, likely Haydn
- If present with a compact, short development, likely Mozart
- If present with extended tonal scope and dominant arrivals, likely Beethoven
- Note whether exposition material returns in the same order (Haydn's rotational tendency)
- Check for new, unrelated material (Mozart's characteristic)

# Context & Application
These differences represent genuine stylistic distinctions, not merely individual variation. Understanding them helps the analyst set appropriate expectations when approaching a development section.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in B-flat, H. 41, i (ex. 13.16): four-part development without a core; material runs through exposition order (rotational)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in B-flat, K. 333, i (ex. 13.10): tight-knit sentential pre-core; pseudo-core replaces genuine core
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in G, Op. 14/2, i (exs. 13.7-8): development keys from parallel minor (B-flat = bIII)

# Common Errors
- Assuming all developments must have a core (Haydn frequently omits it)
- Expecting Haydn-scale developments in Mozart (Mozart's are typically shorter)
- Missing Beethoven's modal borrowing because the borrowed keys are major-mode triads

# Common Confusions
- **Rotational form**: Haydn's tendency to recycle exposition material in order is described by Hepokoski/Darcy as "rotational" -- this is different from pre-core/core technique
- **Mozart's brevity**: Mozart's developments are relatively short, not because they are simpler, but because he restricts their scope
- **Beethoven's "major-mode" development keys**: bVI and bIII are major triads but belong to the parallel minor, providing modal contrast
