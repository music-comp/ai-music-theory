# Quick Definition
The systematic comparison of formal organization across the three sections of a small ternary, using seven criteria (tonality, cadence, harmony, grouping, functional efficiency, motivic uniformity, thematic conventionality) to evaluate relative degrees of tight-knit and loose expression.

# Core Definition
Caplin identifies seven criteria for evaluating tight-knit vs. loose organization, first observed by Arnold Schoenberg: (1) Tonality -- home key tightest, modulation loosest; (2) Cadence -- PAC tightest, no cadence loosest; (3) Harmony -- cadential/tonic prolongational tightest, sequential loosest; (4) Grouping structure -- symmetrical (powers of 2) tightest, asymmetrical loosest; (5) Functional efficiency -- efficient tighter, redundant (via extensions, etc.) looser; (6) Motivic uniformity -- uniform tighter, diverse looser; (7) Thematic conventionality -- period tightest, sentence less tight, nonconventional loosest. As a general rule, the A section is tight-knit, the B section is loose, and the A' section falls between depending on its modifications.

# Key Properties
1. Seven criteria: tonality, cadence, harmony, grouping structure, functional efficiency, motivic uniformity, thematic conventionality
2. A section: always the most tightly knit (tight-knit is a relative term)
3. B section: always looser than A (dominant prolongation, non-conventional design, lack of cadence)
4. A' section: varies -- may be tighter (tonally adjusted to stay in home key) or looser (expanded, new continuation, sequences)
5. Criteria can conflict, making comparison difficult
6. The distinction is always RELATIVE, never absolute
7. Period is tightest conventional type; sentence is least tight among conventional types

# Construction / Recognition
1. Evaluate each section against the seven criteria
2. A vs. B: A is relatively tight knit; B is distinctly looser (in virtually all small ternaries)
3. A vs. A': If identical, same tightness; if altered, A' usually looser (exception: tonally adjusted A' staying in home key may be tighter than modulatory A)
4. B vs. A': If A' closely models A, it retains tightness relative to B; if A' is heavily altered, comparison with B becomes problematic
5. Criteria can conflict -- some factors may make a passage tighter while others make it looser

# Context & Application
This distinction, introduced here in the context of the small ternary, becomes a central analytical concept throughout the study of classical form. The tight-knit/loose spectrum applies to all formal contexts: subordinate themes are looser than main themes; development sections are looser than expositions; transitions are looser than main themes. Analyzing specific loosening techniques is often more productive than ranking overall looseness.

# Examples
- Mozart, Eine kleine Nachtmusik, K. 525, ii: A very tight knit (period, home key, PAC); B distinctly loose (dominant prolongation, no cadence, nonconventional); A' intermediate (consequent only, slight chromatic inflection)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 7, ii: A tightest (sentence, home key); B loose (dominant prolongation); A' markedly loose (sequences, chromatic enrichment, cadential deception) -- B may actually be tighter than A'
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Hob. XVI:49, iii: A modulatory (loosening factor); A' tonally adjusted and tighter; B loose (sequential sentence)

# Common Errors
- Treating tight-knit or loose as absolute properties -- the distinction is always relative to context
- Assuming the B section is always the loosest part -- in some cases (Beethoven, Op. 7, ii), A' is actually looser
- Assuming the period is always "better" because it is tighter -- the sentence's relative looseness serves different expressive purposes

# Common Confusions
- "Loose" is NOT "weak" or "deficient" -- it is a descriptive category for structural instability
- The period is the tightest conventional type, NOT the sentence
- A tonally adjusted A' (staying in home key) may be tighter than the modulatory A it models
- The distinction is always relative to the compositional context -- the same sentence may be tighter or looser depending on what it is compared with
