# Quick Definition
The central contrasting section of a large ternary form, typically set in the minor mode (minore) of the home key and occupying a position between two statements of the main theme.

# Formal Definition
An interior theme occupies the central position in a large ternary form, standing between statements of a main theme. It contrasts through changes in melodic-motivic material, texture, accompanimental figurations, and most strikingly through an immediate change in modality (usually to minor). The interior theme generally resides in the same tonality as the main theme but in the opposite mode, distinguishing it fundamentally from a subordinate theme. Its formal organization is usually related to the small ternary or small binary, but it is often altered, truncated, or left incomplete. The final harmony is usually the dominant of the home key, motivating the main theme's return.

# Construction/Recognition
- Located between two statements of the main theme
- Usually shifts immediately to the minor mode (minore)
- May reside in related tonal regions: vi, bVI, or IV
- Formal organization based on a distorted small ternary:
  - A' section initially closed, then reopened
  - A' section lacks cadential closure
  - A' section eliminated (truncated small ternary)
  - Both B and A' sections eliminated
- Often brings Sturm und Drang affect

# Musical Context
The interior theme appears in the second part of large ternary slow movements and in similar positions in rondo forms. Its prominence of minor modality recalls development sections, but its primary characteristics make it an entirely different formal entity.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Trio in A-flat, Hob. XV:14, ii, mm. 17-34: Small binary; A' initially closed but reopened
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Hob. XVI:52, ii, mm. 28-33: A' lacks cadential closure
- Haydn, String Quartet in B-flat, Op. 64/3, ii: Truncated small ternary (A + B only)

# Common Confusions
- An interior theme is fundamentally different from a subordinate theme: it never resides in the standard subordinate key of the movement.
- The distinction between a contrasting middle and a retransition at the end of an interior theme is subtle: a retransition typically elides with the end of the preceding section.
