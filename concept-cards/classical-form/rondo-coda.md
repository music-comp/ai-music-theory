# Quick Definition
The closing section of a rondo, which in sonata-rondos is a required element that embraces the final return of the refrain.

# Core Definition
In a five-part rondo, the coda is an optional section following refrain 3 that may be quite extensive, often displaying strong developmental qualities, introducing new material, making reference to earlier couplets, or bringing additional statements of the refrain. In a sonata-rondo, the coda is a required element. Its relationship to the final refrain is somewhat fluid: the coda is best understood as starting where the recapitulation stops corresponding to the exposition, and the final refrain always appears somewhere within the coda -- often at its very beginning but sometimes only after the coda is under way. Most theorists treat the coda as separate from the final refrain, but the two are intertwined in practice.

# Construction / Recognition
- In five-part rondo: optional, follows refrain 3
- In sonata-rondo: required element
- May have strong developmental qualities
- May introduce new material or reference earlier couplets
- Embraces the final refrain (refrain 4 in sonata-rondo)
- Starts where recapitulation stops corresponding to exposition
- The final refrain may begin the coda or appear later within it
- Mozart often reduces refrain 4 to its opening motives rather than a full statement

# Context & Application
The coda in rondo form has a particularly important role because it provides the final return of the refrain, which is the culminating event in the rondo's characteristic pattern of returns. In sonata-rondos, it also provides final tonal resolution after the recapitulation.

# Examples
- Various five-part rondos with extensive codas that bring developmental qualities and references to couplet material
- Mozart's sonata-rondos: if refrain 3 is retained, refrain 4 is often eliminated (with only opening motives pervading the coda texture)

# Common Confusions
- In sonata-rondo form, the coda embraces the final refrain rather than following it. Students should not expect a clean separation between the final refrain and the coda.
- The coda is optional in five-part rondos but required in sonata-rondos. This distinction reflects the sonata-rondo's greater formal complexity and its need for final tonal resolution.
