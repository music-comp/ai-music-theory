# Quick Definition
A group of codettas following the PAC of the final subordinate theme, serving to dissipate or sustain the energy built up in achieving the cadential goal of the exposition.

# Core Definition
A postcadential closing section almost always follows the PAC ending the subordinate theme (or the last theme of a group). The closing section usually contains several different codettas, each typically shorter than the previous (creating fragmentation). The first codetta is repeated, followed by shorter second and possibly third codettas. A general sense of compression is expressed (expansions are rare). The melodic content usually consists of conventionalized scalar or arpeggiated patterns, though codettas can establish significant motivic references, especially when ideas from the main theme return to "round out" the exposition. Traditional theories refer to a "closing theme," but this concept is not adopted; what is traditionally called a closing theme can most often be identified as either a true subordinate theme (last of a group) or a collection of codettas (closing section).

# Prerequisites
- **Subordinate theme**: The theme whose PAC the closing section follows
- **Codetta**: The individual units composing the closing section

# Key Properties
1. Follows PAC ending the final subordinate theme
2. Multiple codettas with progressive shortening (fragmentation)
3. First codetta usually repeated, then shorter second/third codettas
4. Tonic pedal and tonic scale-degree prolongation in soprano are typical
5. Conventionalized melodic material (scales, arpeggios) or motivic references to main theme
6. General compression (expansions rare)
7. Rarely omitted from an exposition
8. May be followed by a retransition for exposition repeat
9. Subordinate themes require closing sections more than main themes due to greater energy buildup

# Construction / Recognition
- Locate the PAC ending the final subordinate theme
- Identify codetta material (tonic pedal, conventional patterns, recessive character)
- Note progressive shortening of codettas
- Observe dynamic state: recessive (energy dissipation), sustained forte (decisive conclusion), or mixed
- Check for motivic references to main theme ("rounding out")
- Distinguish from a false closing section (which retrospectively begins a new theme)

# Context & Application
Subordinate themes differ from main themes in almost always requiring a closing section. This results from the greater energy built up in achieving the cadential goal of a subordinate theme. The closing section either dissipates this energy (recessive dynamic) or sustains it to mark a decisive conclusion. In movements with multiple subordinate themes, a true closing section appears only after the final theme; false closing sections may appear after earlier themes.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:35, i, mm. 63-67: codettas derived from main-theme basic idea; two 2-measure codettas followed by 1-measure fragment
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i, mm. 104-116: recessive dynamic dissipates energy
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 31/3, ii, mm. 50-59: long closing section emphasizes root-position tonic (compensating for modulating ST)
- Mozart, Clarinet Quintet in A, K. 581, i, mm. 78-79: sudden return to forte for decisive conclusion

# Common Errors
- Using the term "closing theme" (Caplin rejects this as imprecise)
- Identifying a closing section after the first theme in a group rather than the last
- Confusing a closing section with a new subordinate theme

# Common Confusions
- **"Closing theme" vs. closing section**: What is traditionally called a closing theme is usually either a true subordinate theme (last of group) or a collection of codettas (closing section)
- **Closing section vs. theme**: A closing section consists of CODETTAS, not a theme; it lacks the formal functions of a true theme
- **True vs. false closing section**: A true closing section follows the LAST theme in the group; earlier PACs may generate false closing sections
