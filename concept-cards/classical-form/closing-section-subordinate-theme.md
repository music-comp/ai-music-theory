# Quick Definition
A group of codettas that follows the PAC ending the final subordinate theme, serving to dissipate or sustain the energy built up in achieving the cadential goal of the exposition.

# Formal Definition
A postcadential closing section almost always follows the PAC ending the subordinate theme (or the last theme of a group). The closing section usually contains several different codettas, each typically shorter than the previous (creating fragmentation). The first codetta is repeated and then followed by a second codetta, which may or may not be repeated; a third may also appear. A general sense of compression is expressed. The melodic content usually consists of conventionalized scalar or arpeggiated patterns, though codettas can establish significant motivic references (especially ideas from the main theme, "rounding out" the exposition). Rarely is a closing section omitted from an exposition.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Follows PAC ending the final subordinate theme
2. Multiple codettas, typically with progressive shortening (fragmentation)
3. First codetta usually repeated, then shorter second codetta, possibly third
4. Tonic pedal and tonic scale-degree prolongation in soprano are typical
5. Conventionalized melodic material (scales, arpeggios) or motivic references to main theme
6. General compression of material (expansions are rare)
7. Dynamic state varies: recessive (energy dissipation), continued forte (decisive conclusion), or sustained intensity
8. May be followed by a retransition leading back to the home key

# Musical Context
Subordinate themes differ from main themes in almost always requiring a closing section. This difference results from the greater energy built up in achieving the cadential goal of a subordinate theme compared to that of a main theme. The closing section either dissipates this energy (recessive dynamic) or sustains it to mark a decisive exposition close. In movements with multiple subordinate themes, a true closing section appears only after the final theme of the group. A false closing section may appear after earlier themes in the group.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:35, i, mm. 63-67 (closing section with codettas derived from main-theme basic idea; two 2-measure codettas followed by 1-measure fragmented codetta)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i, mm. 104-116 (recessive dynamic dissipates energy from cadential arrival)
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62, mm. 114-118 (sustained intensity to end of exposition)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 31/3, ii, mm. 50-59 (long closing section emphasizing root-position tonic to compensate for its absence in the modulating subordinate theme)

# Common Confusions
- The traditional concept of "closing theme" is rejected by Caplin -- what is traditionally called a closing theme is usually either a true subordinate theme (last of a group) or a collection of codettas (closing section)
- A closing section consists of CODETTAS, not a theme; it lacks the formal functions (presentation, continuation, cadential) of a true theme
- In a subordinate-theme group, a true closing section appears only after the LAST theme -- earlier PACs may be followed by false closing sections
- A closing section's material may reference the main theme, but this does not make it a "recapitulation"
