# Quick Definition
The final orchestral section of concerto form, completing the textural frame initiated by the opening ritornello, typically interrupted by a solo cadenza, and ending with the same closing section as the opening ritornello.

# Core Definition
The closing ritornello, performed by the orchestra alone (except for the interrupting cadenza), elides with the cadential trill and PAC that end the solo recapitulation, completing the textural frame initiated by the opening ritornello. Like the subordinate-key ritornello, it gives the orchestra a final opportunity to sound its own music (now rooted in the home key), sustains or intensifies the dynamic climax, and provides a last chance to bring back ideas from the opening ritornello. In most of Mozart's concertos and all of Beethoven's, the closing ritornello brings a cadenza for the solo. The ritornello thus divides into two parts: a first part before the cadenza (ending with a cadential six-four embellishment of the dominant) and a second part after the cadenza (ending with a closing section, usually the same as that of the opening ritornello).

# Construction / Recognition
- Orchestra alone (except for cadenza)
- Elides with the cadential trill ending the solo recapitulation
- Completes the textural frame
- First part: material from opening ritornello leading to cadential 6/4
- Cadenza: large-scale prolongation of dominant, completing the cadential progression
- Second part: closing section, usually matching the opening ritornello's closing section
- Ends the movement with a sense of formal rhyme (matching closing sections)
- Should not be confused with a genuine coda

# Context & Application
The closing ritornello provides formal closure by matching the opening ritornello, creating a textural frame for the entire movement. In Haydn's practice, the cadenza marks the end of the recapitulation (before the closing ritornello), while in Mozart and Beethoven, the cadenza is placed within the closing ritornello itself.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i, mm. 357-67: Closing ritornello drawn from the second subordinate theme of the opening ritornello; presentation followed by new continuation leading to cadential 6/4; after cadenza, the rest of the opening ritornello's subordinate theme returns; movement concludes with the same closing section as the earlier ritornellos

# Common Confusions
- The closing ritornello should not be confused with a genuine coda. It brings back material in much the same way it appeared earlier (unlike a coda, which recontextualizes ideas); it lacks loosening devices typical of a coda; and it creates formal closure through matching closing sections rather than main-theme circularity.
- Beethoven's Emperor Concerto (Op. 73, i) is a notable exception that includes a genuine coda after the closing ritornello, demonstrating how unlike the two are.
