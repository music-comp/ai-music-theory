# Quick Definition
In a compound sentence, the continuation and cadential functions can be fully expressed in fewer than the normative eight measures, resulting in a compressed continuation of only four measures. The composer typically restores the sixteen-measure length through repetition, postcadential extension, or standing on the dominant.

# Core Definition
The sentence, with its forward-striving character, has less need to preserve symmetrical phrase structure than the period. The formal functions of continuation and cadence can often be fully expressed in fewer than the eight measures offered by the model form. Many compound sentences compress their continuation and cadential functions into a single four-measure continuation or continuation => cadential phrase. Even when compressed, the composer typically restores symmetry by repeating the phrase or adding a postcadential extension. The use of an expanded cadential progression to support a continuation => cadential phrase is also common.

# Prerequisites
- **Compound sentence**: The theme type in which this deviation occurs
- **Compression**: The general deviation technique of shortening formal functions

# Key Properties
1. Continuation occupies only four measures (mm. 9-12) instead of the normative eight
2. Fragmentation may jump from four-measure units directly to one-measure units (4:1 ratio instead of normative 2:1)
3. Total theme length typically remains sixteen measures despite the compression
4. Symmetry restored by repeating the continuation phrase, adding standing on the dominant, or postcadential extension
5. Compressed continuation often supported by ECP (continuation => cadential)
6. When first attempt ends with DC, repetition achieves PAC
7. When ending with HC, standing on the dominant reinforces the cadential goal

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the compound presentation (mm. 1-8)
2. Check whether the continuation achieves cadential closure by m. 12 (rather than m. 16)
3. Look for restoration techniques in mm. 13-16: repeated phrase, standing on dominant, postcadential extension
4. Note fragmentation ratios: 4:1 (four-measure to one-measure) is more aggressive than the normative 2:1

# Context & Application
The compressed continuation is the deviation technique most characteristic of the sixteen-measure sentence. It reflects the sentence's essential character: the ability to achieve its goals (fragmentation, acceleration, cadence) efficiently. The various methods of restoring sixteen-measure length (repetition after cadential failure, standing on the dominant) are themselves formally significant events that create additional expressive possibilities.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in G, Op. 77/1, i, mm. 1-14: continuation compressed to six measures with 4:1 fragmentation ratio (four-measure units directly to one-measure); descending-third sequence
- Mozart, Clarinet Trio in E-flat, K. 498, i, mm. 1-16: four-measure continuation => cadential ending with DC at m. 12; phrase repeated to achieve PAC, restoring symmetry
- Haydn, Symphony No. 83 ("The Hen"), i, mm. 1-16: compressed continuation to HC at m. 12; four-measure standing on the dominant extends passage to sixteen measures

# Common Errors
- Assuming compressed continuation means the compound sentence is shorter than sixteen measures -- restoration techniques bring it back to normative length
- Calling the compressed continuation a deficiency rather than a characteristic feature of the sentence's efficiency
- Failing to recognize the 4:1 fragmentation ratio as a compressed version of the normative two-stage process (4:2:1)

# Common Confusions
- Compressed continuation does not mean the compound sentence is shorter than sixteen measures -- restoration techniques typically bring it back to the normative length
- A compressed continuation is not a deficiency -- it reflects the sentence's inherent efficiency and forward drive
- The 4:1 fragmentation ratio (four-measure to one-measure) is more aggressive than the normative 2:1 ratio but is entirely natural in the compressed context
