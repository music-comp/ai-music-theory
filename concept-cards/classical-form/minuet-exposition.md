# Quick Definition
The A section of minuet form, resembling a small ternary exposition but functioning at the interthematic level, expressing main-theme, transition, and subordinate-theme functions in varying degrees depending on whether it modulates.

# Core Definition
The minuet exposition (A section) is usually constructed as a relatively tight-knit, conventional form (sentence, period, or hybrid) closing with a PAC. It may remain entirely in the home key (expressing only main-theme function) or modulate to a subordinate key (expressing main-theme, transition, and subordinate-theme functions). When the exposition does not modulate, strong expectations arise for the B section to bring tonal contrast. When it does modulate, the presence of a subordinate key represents the principal tonal contrast of the minuet. Given the relatively small scale compared to sonata form, form-functional fusion is widespread, especially of transition and subordinate-theme functions (Caplin, pp. 635-641).

# Key Properties
1. Tight-knit, conventional form (sentence, period, or hybrid)
2. Closes with a PAC (either in home key or subordinate key)
3. May be nonmodulating (main-theme function only) or modulating (multiple functions)
4. Form-functional fusion of transition and subordinate-theme is widespread
5. May contain an early authentic cadence when modulating
6. At times expanded to resemble a miniature sonata exposition
7. May include a closing section with new content

# Construction / Recognition
1. Determine whether the A section modulates
2. If nonmodulating: identify main-theme function exclusively
3. If modulating: identify main-theme, transition, and subordinate-theme functions
4. Look for early authentic cadences as markers of main-theme function
5. Assess degree of form-functional fusion
6. In the most complex cases, the A section resembles a miniature sonata exposition

# Context & Application
Whether the A section modulates is of particular importance because it determines how the rest of the minuet is organized. A nonmodulating A section transfers tonal contrast to the B section, while a modulating A section frees the B section to explore other tonal regions or simply emphasize the home-key dominant.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 632): Haydn, String Quartet in G, Op. 54, No. 1, iii -- nonmodulating A section expressing only main-theme function.
**Example 2** (p. 638): Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 7, iii, mm. 1-24 -- modulating A section with fused transition/subordinate-theme functions.
**Example 3** (p. 642): Mozart, String Quartet in A, K. 464, ii, mm. 1-28 -- A section with distinct main theme, transition, and subordinate theme resembling a miniature sonata exposition.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Analyzing a minuet A section as if it were a small ternary A section. **Correction**: The minuet A section operates at the interthematic level, expressing thematic functions like main theme, transition, and subordinate theme.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Whether the subordinate key in the A section matters. **Clarification**: In a small ternary, modulation is of minor concern; in a minuet, it may represent the principal tonal conflict of the entire form.
