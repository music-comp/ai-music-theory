# Quick Definition
An orchestral passage following the solo exposition's final cadence, performed in the subordinate key, functioning as the formal analogue of a closing section while sustaining or intensifying the dynamic climax.

# Formal Definition
The subordinate-key ritornello follows the final PAC of the solo exposition and is performed by the orchestra alone, with the solo remaining silent until the development begins. Although located where a closing section might be expected, it displays few characteristics of closing-section function. Instead, it serves as an internal ritornello that begins in the subordinate key and consists of material taken from the opening ritornello (though rarely from the main theme). It fulfills four functions: (1) giving the orchestra another opportunity to sound its own music in the context of the subordinate key; (2) bringing back ideas from the opening ritornello that were eliminated from the solo exposition; (3) sustaining or intensifying the dynamic level attained by the solo; and (4) providing the solo a chance to rest before the development.

# Construction/Recognition
- Orchestra alone, solo silent
- Follows the final PAC (cadential trill) of the solo exposition
- Begins in the subordinate key
- Material drawn from the opening ritornello (usually not the main theme)
- Often uses the opening ritornello's transition material (forte, brilliant style)
- Usually structured as a complete thematic unit ending with PAC in subordinate key
- Form is often nonconventional but tight knit
- May include a brief closing section that modulates to start the development
- Sometimes fails to close cadentially before the development

# Musical Context
The subordinate-key ritornello is a distinctly concerto-form element with no exact parallel in sonata form. It allows the orchestra to participate as more than a mere accompaniment in the tonal conflict. The dynamic intensity reached by the solo's climax is typically sustained or surpassed during this section.

# Examples
- Haydn, Trumpet Concerto in E-flat, Hob. VIIe:1, i, mm. 81-94: Subordinate-key ritornello elides with the cadential trill; content from the opening ritornello's subordinate theme; descending-stepwise sequence leads to I6, which does not resolve cadentially but continues to modulate to the development key (vi)
- Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i: Subordinate-key ritornello uses the second subordinate theme from the opening ritornello (mm. 58-76), material eliminated from the solo exposition

# Common Confusions
- The subordinate-key ritornello is not a closing section, despite its location. It does not bring postcadential, recessive material; instead, it sustains or intensifies the dynamic climax.
- Material from the opening ritornello that appears in the subordinate-key ritornello often comes from the transition (forte, brilliant style), not the main theme. Students should trace the origins of this material back to the opening ritornello.
