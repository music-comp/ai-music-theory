# Quick Definition
The formal functions distinguishing the various themes and themelike units within a movement -- main theme, transition, subordinate theme -- defined fundamentally by their relationship to tonality and occurring in a conventionalized order.

# Core Definition
"Just as the component parts of themes are functionally differentiated, so, too, are the various themes (or themelike units) in movements. Moreover, these interthematic functions also occur in a conventionalized order (depending on the full-movement form) and thus give rise to a higher-level formal syntax" (Ch. 1, p. 17). Their definition depends "fundamentally on their relationship to tonality" (Ch. 1, p. 18): "the main theme expresses the home key through cadential closure. Likewise, a later-occurring subordinate theme confirms the subordinate key. Standing between these two functions is the transition, a themelike unit that destabilizes the home key and (usually) modulates to the subordinate key" (Ch. 1, p. 18).

# Prerequisites
- **Intrathematic functions** -- understanding the within-theme level before the between-theme level
- **Formal function** -- the broader concept

# Key Properties
1. Defined by tonal relationships: which key is confirmed, destabilized, or established
2. Occur in conventionalized order (higher-level syntax)
3. Main theme: confirms home key (HK) through cadential closure; most tight-knit
4. Transition: destabilizes HK, usually modulates to subordinate key (SK)
5. Subordinate theme: confirms SK; distinctly looser than main theme
6. Tight-knit/loose distinction characterizes the differing phrase-structural designs
7. Tonal considerations alone do not account for differences -- phrase-structural organization also matters

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the key confirmed by each thematic unit
- Assess the degree of tight-knit vs. loose organization
- Verify the conventionalized syntactic order for the given movement type

# Context & Application
Interthematic functions are most prominent in sonata-form expositions (main theme -> transition -> subordinate theme -> closing section). Other movement types have their own interthematic syntaxes.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 1, pp. 18-21): Beethoven, Op. 2/1, i: main theme (mm. 1-8, F minor, tight-knit sentence), transition (mm. 9-20, modulatory, loose), subordinate theme (mm. 21-48, Ab major, distinctly loose).

# Common Errors
- Defining interthematic functions solely by motivic content or position

# Common Confusions
- Interthematic functions are defined primarily by tonal relationships, while intrathematic functions are defined by local harmonic progressions
- Tonal considerations are central but not sufficient -- phrase-structural organization (tight-knit vs. loose) also differentiates interthematic functions
