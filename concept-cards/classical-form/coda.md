# Quick Definition
An optional section following the recapitulation that expresses the temporal quality of "after-the-end," fulfilling compensatory functions that could not be addressed in the recapitulation.

# Core Definition
A coda is an optional section that follows and is fully distinct from the recapitulation. Since a movement's structural close is fulfilled by the recapitulation, the primary function of a coda is to express the temporal quality of "after-the-end." A coda is analogous to a closing section but operates at a higher hierarchical level -- comparable to the exposition, development, and recapitulation. It is a relatively large unit containing one or more complete coda themes, each ending with a PAC, and it closes with a series of codettas. The coda avoids introducing new material calling for further development and does not initiate new processes that cannot be completed. In addition to its after-the-end function, the coda serves five compensatory functions: recollection of main-theme ideas, restoration of deleted material, development reference, shaping a dynamic curve, and realization of unrealized implications. As Schoenberg noted, "the composer wants to say something more."

# Prerequisites
- **Recapitulation**: The section the coda follows
- **Closing section**: The analogous smaller-scale structure
- **Codetta**: The constituent unit of closing sections (and of the coda's own closing section)

# Key Properties
1. Optional section -- many movements have no coda
2. Expresses "after-the-end" -- fundamental processes are already closed
3. Hierarchically comparable to exposition, development, recapitulation
4. Contains one or more coda themes, each ending with PAC
5. Ends with a closing section of codettas
6. Remains primarily in the home key (brief tonicizations and sequences may occur)
7. Avoids introducing genuinely new developmental material
8. Does not initiate processes that cannot be completed
9. Governed by the aesthetic ideal: "once the music finally stops, the listener should have no desire to hear anything more"
10. Serves five compensatory functions

# Construction / Recognition
- Follows the recapitulation; begins where the recapitulation's music no longer corresponds to the exposition
- Contains one or more coda themes (usually loose, like subordinate themes)
- Ends with a closing section of codettas
- Remains primarily in the home key
- Start may be difficult to locate (see coda-start-identification)
- Look for compensatory functions (main-theme recollection, restored material, development reference, dynamic shaping, implication realization)

# Context & Application
Many movements end with the recapitulation and have no coda. When present, the coda completes secondary processes left unresolved. The coda rarely initiates tonal changes that might undermine its after-the-end expression, though prominent tonicizations and sequential progressions may occur early to provide harmonic contrast. Coda themes are usually loose in organization (like subordinate themes), though tight-knit types appear in sonata-rondo form.

# Examples
- Haydn, Symphony No. 104 ("London"), iv (ex. 12.7): two coda themes; first with expanded cadential progression (PAC m. 287); second with modal shift to minor, internal dominant arrival, powerful closure (PAC m. 309)
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62 (ex. 12.6): begins in major with subordinate-theme material; restores main-theme material to home key (m. 276); shapes dramatic recessive dynamic curve; realizes unrealized cadential implications
- Haydn, Symphony No. 97 in C, i (ex. 12.3): begins with sequential repetition of cadential idea; transition-like unit leads to HC; main-theme fanfare returns as closing section (m. 279)
- Mozart, Symphony No. 39, K. 543, ii (ex. 12.10): realizes implication of modal disturbance in exposition; restores deleted PAC

# Common Errors
- Treating the coda as a "large codetta" (they differ hierarchically)
- Treating the coda as a "terminal development" (their tonal organizations are fundamentally different)
- Introducing genuinely new material in a coda (violates the after-the-end function)

# Common Confusions
- **Coda vs. codetta**: They differ in hierarchical level and complexity, not just size
- **After-the-end vs. conclusion**: Not contradictory; the coda comes after fundamental processes close but completes secondary processes
- **Not a second development**: The coda may use development-like techniques (sequences, model-sequence) but remains tonally stable
- **Five compensatory functions**: Recollection of MT ideas, restoration of deleted material, development reference, dynamic curve, realization of unrealized implications
