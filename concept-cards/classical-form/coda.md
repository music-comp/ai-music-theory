# Quick Definition
An optional section following the recapitulation that expresses the temporal quality of "after-the-end," fulfilling compensatory functions that could not be addressed in the recapitulation itself.

# Formal Definition
A coda is an optional section that follows and is fully distinct from the recapitulation. Since a movement's structural close is fulfilled by the recapitulation, the primary function of a coda is to express the temporal quality of "after-the-end." A coda is analogous to a closing section (made up of codettas) that follows a PAC ending a theme, but operates at a higher hierarchical level — comparable to the exposition, development, and recapitulation. A coda is a relatively large unit containing one or more complete coda themes, each ending with a PAC, and it closes with a series of codettas.

# Construction/Recognition
- Follows the recapitulation; begins where the recapitulation's music stops corresponding to the exposition
- Contains one or more coda themes, each ending with PAC
- Ends with a closing section of codettas
- Remains primarily in the home key, though brief tonicizations and sequential progressions may occur
- Themes usually organized along the lines of a subordinate theme (loose structure)
- Avoids introducing new material calling for further development
- Does not initiate new processes that cannot be completed

# Musical Context
Many movements end with the recapitulation and have no coda. When present, the coda allows the composer to recall main-theme ideas, restore deleted material, reference the development, shape a new dynamic curve, and realize unrealized implications. It is governed by the aesthetic ideal that "once the music finally stops, the listener should have no desire to hear anything more."

# Examples
- Haydn, Symphony No. 104 ("London"), iv (ex. 12.7): two coda themes; first with modal shift to minor; powerful closure
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62 (ex. 12.6): coda begins in major mode, restores main-theme material to the home key, shapes a dramatic recessive dynamic curve
- Haydn, Symphony No. 97 in C, i (ex. 12.3): coda begins with sequential repetition of cadential idea

# Common Confusions
- A coda is not a "large codetta" — they differ in hierarchical level and formal complexity
- The coda is not a second development section, though it may briefly employ similar techniques
- "After-the-end" and "genuine conclusion" are not contradictory: the coda comes after the fundamental processes close but completes secondary processes
