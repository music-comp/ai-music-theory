# Quick Definition
A compound basic idea (c.b.i.) is a four-measure phrase consisting of a basic idea and a contrasting idea that does not close with a cadence. By its melodic content it resembles an antecedent; by its tonic prolongational harmony it resembles a presentation. It is itself a hybrid of antecedent and presentation functions.

# Core Definition
By definition, an antecedent contains a basic idea followed by a contrasting idea leading to a weak cadence. If a cadence does not appear at the end of the phrase, an essential component of antecedent function is lost. The lack of cadential closure creates open-endedness, and the phrase takes on the character and function of a higher-level basic idea. Thus the term "compound basic idea" applies to a phrase consisting of a simple basic idea and a contrasting idea that does not close with a cadence. By virtue of its melodic-motivic content, the compound basic idea resembles an antecedent. In light of its underlying harmony, which is usually (but not always) tonic prolongational, it resembles a presentation. The four-measure phrase itself represents a hybrid of antecedent and presentation functions. The term gains special appropriateness in compound themes, where the c.b.i. occupies the same hierarchical position as a simple basic idea within an eight-measure structure.

# Prerequisites
- **Basic idea**: The two-measure initiating unit within the c.b.i.
- **Contrasting idea**: The two-measure responding unit within the c.b.i.
- **Cadential progression**: Must be understood to recognize the absence of cadence that distinguishes c.b.i. from antecedent
- **Tonic prolongation**: The typical harmonic support for the c.b.i.

# Key Properties
1. Contains a two-measure basic idea + two-measure contrasting idea
2. **No cadence** at the end of the phrase -- the crucial distinguishing feature from an antecedent
3. Underlying harmony is typically tonic prolongational (I prolonged, possibly reaching V without forming a cadential progression)
4. A dominant chord at the end does not automatically create an HC -- check whether there is a cadential *progression* or merely a tonic *prolongation*
5. The tonic prolongation may extend into the following phrase
6. In compound themes, the c.b.i. occupies the same hierarchical position as a simple basic idea does in a simple theme

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify a four-measure phrase with basic idea + contrasting idea
2. Check whether the phrase ends with a genuine cadence (cadential progression leading to cadential goal)
3. If no cadence: compound basic idea; if cadence: antecedent
4. Examine the supporting harmony -- tonic prolongation confirms c.b.i. interpretation
5. A V chord at m. 4 that supports the contrasting idea from m. 3 is part of a tonic prolongation (I-V7), not a cadential progression
6. In compound sentences, the c.b.i. is immediately repeated (statement-response) to form the compound presentation

# Context & Application
The compound basic idea appears in hybrid themes (types 3 and 4) and as a building block of compound (sixteen-measure) themes, particularly compound sentences (where it is repeated to form the presentation) and compound periods (where it can form part of the large-scale antecedent). In compound themes, the four-measure c.b.i. stands in the same relation to the sixteen-measure structure as a two-measure basic idea does to an eight-measure structure. The c.b.i. is also important in compound periods because it states the basic idea only once, saving its return for the higher-level consequent at m. 9.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:35, i, mm. 1-4: dominant at m. 4 supports the entire contrasting idea from m. 3, creating I-V7 tonic prolongation with no cadential progression
- Haydn, Symphony No. 95 in C Minor, iii, mm. 1-4: tonic pedal throughout destroys any cadential potential
- Beethoven, String Quartet in G, Op. 18/2, iv, mm. 21-24: no harmonic motion into m. 24 prohibits any cadence
- Beethoven, Piano Concerto No. 3 in C Minor, Op. 37, i, mm. 1-4: c.b.i. with statement-response repetition forming compound presentation

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Basic idea (the two-measure initiating unit within the c.b.i.)
- Contrasting idea (the two-measure responding unit within the c.b.i.)
- Tonic prolongation (the typical harmonic support)

## Enables
- Compound sentence (c.b.i. is repeated to form compound presentation)
- Compound period (c.b.i. can structure the compound antecedent)
- Hybrid theme types 3 and 4 (c.b.i. serves as first phrase)

## Related
- Antecedent (same melodic-motivic content but WITH cadence)
- Presentation function (same harmonic character -- tonic prolongation)

## Contrasts With
- Antecedent: the distinction is solely the presence or absence of a cadence; if there is a cadence, it is an antecedent; if not, it is a c.b.i.

# Common Errors
- Automatically labeling any V at the end of a four-measure phrase as an HC -- check for a cadential *progression*, not just a dominant harmony
- Treating the c.b.i. as a "defective antecedent" rather than a distinct formal function with its own characteristics
- Confusing the "compound" in compound basic idea with compound theme -- the compound refers to internal complexity (b.i. + c.i.), not to sixteen-measure scope

# Common Confusions
- The difference between a c.b.i. and an antecedent is **solely** the presence or absence of a cadence -- if there is a cadence, it is an antecedent; if not, it is a c.b.i.
- A dominant harmony at the end of m. 4 is not automatically an HC -- it must be the goal of a cadential *progression*, not part of a tonic *prolongation*
- The "compound" in compound basic idea refers to its internal complexity (basic idea + contrasting idea), not to its size relative to a compound theme
- An apparent "plagal cadence" (IV-I) at the end of a c.b.i. is usually just a subdominant embellishing a tonic prolongation

# Verification Notes
The absence-of-cadence criterion is Caplin's central distinction between c.b.i. and antecedent. The hierarchical analogy (c.b.i. in 16-measure theme = b.i. in 8-measure theme) is explicitly stated in both Chapter 4 and Chapter 6. All examples cited appear in the source text.
