# Quick Definition
When the exposition uses the same basic idea for both main and subordinate themes, the recapitulation must substantially alter or eliminate the subordinate theme's opening to avoid redundant home-key statements of the same idea.

# Formal Definition
A monothematic exposition uses the same basic idea for both the main theme and the subordinate theme. If the original structure of the subordinate theme is retained in the recapitulation, the basic idea would occur in the home key twice — first in the main theme and second in the tonally adjusted subordinate theme. To preclude this potential tautology, the composer normally alters the subordinate theme more substantially or even eliminates its opening entirely. The first appearance is needed to articulate the beginning of the recapitulation; the second must be changed. Haydn, who writes many monothematic expositions, typically uses these procedures, but striking examples are found in Mozart as well.

# Construction/Recognition
- Identify whether the exposition uses the same basic idea for main and subordinate themes
- In the recapitulation, look for: deletion of the subordinate theme's opening basic idea, substantial recomposition, or substitution with material from a different subordinate theme
- The first statement (main theme) must be retained to mark the recapitulation's beginning
- The composer may rearrange subordinate themes within a group, bringing a later theme first

# Musical Context
Monothematic expositions are especially characteristic of Haydn and require the most creative adjustments in the recapitulation. The composer must balance the need for recognizable thematic return with the avoidance of redundancy.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 576, i (ex. 11.12): begins recapitulation's subordinate theme with material from the second subordinate theme to avoid redundant home-key statement of the basic idea; the first subordinate theme is interpolated later, transposed to VI and converted from tonic prolongation to sequential passage
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C Minor, K. 457, i (ex. 11.6): first part of subordinate theme deleted in recapitulation; second part serves as the theme; justified by the first part's appearance in the development's pre-core

# Common Confusions
- Simply omitting the basic idea at the start of the subordinate theme is not sufficient — the theme cannot logically begin with continuation function
- The alterations are not random but follow systematic principles of avoiding tautology while maintaining formal coherence
- Haydn's monothematic expositions produce the most drastically altered recapitulations
