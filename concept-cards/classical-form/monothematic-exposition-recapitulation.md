# Quick Definition
When the exposition uses the same basic idea for both main and subordinate themes, the recapitulation must substantially alter or eliminate the subordinate theme's opening to avoid redundant home-key statements of the same idea.

# Core Definition
A monothematic exposition uses the same basic idea for both the main theme and the subordinate theme. If the original structure of the subordinate theme is retained in the recapitulation, the basic idea would occur in the home key twice: first in the main theme and second in the tonally adjusted subordinate theme. To preclude this potential tautology, the composer normally alters the subordinate theme more substantially or even eliminates it altogether. The first appearance (main theme) is needed to articulate the beginning of the recapitulation. Simply omitting the basic idea at the start of the subordinate theme does not suffice, for a theme cannot necessarily begin logically with the continuation function that formerly followed it. Haydn, who writes many monothematic expositions, typically uses these procedures, but striking examples are found in Mozart as well.

# Key Properties
1. The basic idea would occur in the home key twice if the subordinate theme were unchanged
2. Simply omitting the basic idea is insufficient -- the theme cannot logically begin with continuation function
3. The composer must usually alter the subordinate theme more substantially
4. The first statement (main theme) must be retained to mark the recapitulation's beginning
5. Haydn writes many monothematic expositions; these produce the most drastically altered recapitulations
6. Material from a second subordinate theme may be substituted
7. Material deleted from the recapitulation may have appeared in the development, justifying its omission

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify whether the exposition uses the same basic idea for main and subordinate themes
- In the recapitulation, look for deletion of the subordinate theme's opening, substantial recomposition, or substitution with material from a different subordinate theme
- Check whether the deleted material appeared in the development (justifying its omission)

# Context & Application
Monothematic expositions are especially characteristic of Haydn and require the most creative adjustments in the recapitulation. The composer must balance recognizable thematic return with avoidance of redundancy. In some cases, deleted material can be justified by its appearance in the development section's pre-core.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 576, i (ex. 11.12): begins subordinate theme with material from the second subordinate theme to avoid redundant home-key statement; first subordinate theme interpolated later, transposed to VI and converted from tonic prolongation to sequential passage; resulting cadential area more expansive than either exposition theme
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C Minor, K. 457, i (ex. 11.6): first part of two-part subordinate theme deleted; second part serves as the recapitulation's subordinate theme; Rosen notes the deleted passage appeared in the development at the subdominant

# Common Errors
- Assuming simply omitting the basic idea solves the problem (a theme cannot logically begin with continuation)
- Treating the alterations as arbitrary rather than systematically motivated

# Common Confusions
- **Insufficiency of omission**: Simply removing the basic idea does not work; the theme must be more substantially recomposed
- **Haydn vs. Mozart/Beethoven**: Haydn writes many monothematic expositions and thus produces the most altered recapitulations; Mozart and Beethoven encounter this situation less frequently
