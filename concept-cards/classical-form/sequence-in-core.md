# Quick Definition
The sequential repetition of the model in a development core, transposing it one or more times to create modulatory or tonicizing progressions.

# Formal Definition
Following its initial statement, the model is sequenced one or more times. Although a single sequential pattern may be used throughout (such as descending fifth or ascending second), the pattern often changes in the course of the core. The supporting sequential progressions either create a true modulation or introduce a variety of tonicized regions. The structure of the model is normally retained in its sequenced version, though subsequent phrases may be altered or eliminated.

# Construction/Recognition
- The model is transposed according to a sequential pattern (descending fifth, ascending second, descending third, etc.)
- The sequential pattern may change partway through the core
- At minimum, one sequential repetition is needed; two or more are common
- The sequence may be incomplete -- the model may be truncated in its repetition
- Sequential progressions generate the tonal exploration characteristic of the development

# Musical Context
The sequence provides the engine of tonal motion in the development core, moving away from the subordinate key toward the development key or home-key dominant.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in A Minor, K. 310, i (ex. 10.3): model sequenced twice down a perfect fifth
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Hob. XVI:49, i (ex. 10.4): model sequenced twice by descending thirds
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C Minor, Op. 10/1 (ex. 10.5): four-measure model in ascending stepwise sequence

# Common Confusions
- The large-scale sequence of the full model should be distinguished from smaller sequential patterns within the model itself
- An incomplete sequence often marks the onset of fragmentation
