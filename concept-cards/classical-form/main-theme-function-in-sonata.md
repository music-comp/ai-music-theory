# Quick Definition
The main theme of a sonata exposition establishes the home key, introduces the principal melodic-motivic ideas, and defines tight-knit organization as a baseline for comparison with looser units.

# Core Definition
The main theme of a sonata exposition is most often constructed as a conventional theme-type (sentence, period, hybrid, small ternary, or small binary). A significant minority are nonconventional. It must reside primarily in the home key. Most end with a PAC, rarely IAC. A main theme may end with a HC as long as there has been no internal modulation. Minor-mode main themes often close with HC, matching the unsettled emotional quality. Unlike subordinate themes, main themes normally remain in a single mode. Many main themes exhibit hesitancy or uncertainty (sudden textural changes, rhythmic discontinuities), yet retain sufficient cadential and phrase-structural solidity to function as the true formal beginning. If ending with PAC, a closing section of codettas may be appended. If ending with HC, a postcadential standing on the dominant may follow. Two main themes may form a main-theme group (both ending with PAC in the home key).

# Key Properties
1. Usually a conventional theme-type (sentence, period, hybrid, small ternary, small binary)
2. A significant minority are nonconventional
3. Must reside primarily in the home key (transient modulations allowed)
4. Ends with PAC (most common), IAC (rare), or HC (common in minor)
5. Normally remains in a single mode (unlike subordinate themes)
6. Many exhibit hesitancy and textural discontinuities ("introductory" character)
7. Not to be confused with an actual introduction despite introductory character
8. May be followed by closing section (after PAC) or standing on the dominant (after HC)
9. Two main themes may form a main-theme group (both ending PAC in HK)
10. If a second theme ends HC, it functions as a transition, not a second main theme

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the theme type (sentence, period, hybrid, etc. or nonconventional)
- Confirm it resides in the home key
- Note the cadential ending (PAC, IAC, or HC)
- Check whether it is followed by a closing section, standing on the dominant, or a second main theme
- Compare its tight-knit organization with the subsequent subordinate theme

# Context & Application
The main theme's tight-knit organization provides the standard against which the subordinate theme's looser organization is measured. Its home-key establishment provides the tonal stability that the transition will subsequently destabilize. An "introductory" character (hesitancy, textural gaps) should not be confused with an actual slow introduction.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in G Minor ("Rider"), Op. 74/3, iv (ex. 13.1): eight-measure nonconventional theme (2+3+3); more sentential than periodic but not a genuine sentence
- Mozart, Symphony No. 38 ("Prague"), K. 504, i (ex. 13.2): nonconventional -- moves from introduction to continuation, bypassing initiation; codetta with fanfare motives
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E Minor, Hob. XVI:34, i (ex. 13.3): 3+3+2 asymmetry; related to sentence but insufficiently expressed
- Haydn, Symphony No. 97 in C, i (ex. 13.4): tonic-pedal fanfare theme; general sentential quality but presentation and continuation cannot be distinguished
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i (ex. 13.6): loosely organized with repetitions and extensions, yet tighter than its subordinate theme

# Common Errors
- Confusing an "introductory" main theme with an actual introduction
- Treating a HC-ending theme as tonally unconfirmed (partial confirmation is acceptable)

# Common Confusions
- **Not an introduction**: A main theme with introductory character has cadential substance
- **HC ending**: Partial home-key confirmation is sufficient in the exposition
- **Second main theme vs. transition**: If a second theme ends HC, it is a transition, not a main theme
- **Mode consistency**: Main themes normally remain in one mode, unlike subordinate themes
