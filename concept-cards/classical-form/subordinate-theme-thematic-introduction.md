# Quick Definition
A brief introductory passage preceding the structural beginning of a subordinate theme, typically supported by dominant harmony prolonged from the transition's end that resolves to tonic at the theme's true beginning, or by tonic harmony with a new accompaniment pattern appearing before the basic idea enters.

# Core Definition
The structural beginning of a subordinate theme may be preceded by a thematic introduction. Two types are common. First, the underlying harmony may be the dominant, prolonged from the end of the transition and resolved to the tonic at the beginning of the subordinate theme proper. Second, tonic harmony (of the subordinate key) and a new accompanimental pattern start on the downbeat of the measure following the end of the transition, but the basic idea then begins later in that measure, so that the downbeat of the next measure must be seen as the actual "first" bar of the theme. The first type is similar to a main theme's thematic introduction (though over dominant rather than tonic). The second type is rarely encountered with main themes, with Mozart's Symphony No. 40 being a notable exception.

# Prerequisites
- **Subordinate theme**: The formal unit being introduced
- **Introduction function**: The general concept of thematic introduction
- **Dominant arrival in transition**: The harmonic predecessor

# Key Properties
1. Type 1: dominant harmony prolonged from transition, resolving to tonic at theme's start
2. Type 2: tonic harmony + new accompaniment pattern, basic idea enters mid-measure
3. The introductory passage is NOT part of the subordinate theme proper
4. The theme's "first bar" is determined by when the basic idea structurally begins
5. Type 2 is rarely found with main themes
6. Both types create a smoother boundary between transition and subordinate theme

# Construction / Recognition
- Note whether there is dominant harmony between the transition's end and the subordinate theme's clear beginning
- Check whether a new accompaniment pattern appears before the melodic basic idea
- Determine the theme's "first bar" based on when the basic idea begins, not when the accompaniment starts
- Distinguish from standing on the dominant (which belongs to the transition)

# Context & Application
Thematic introductions to subordinate themes are a subtle framing device that can be overlooked. They smooth the transition-subordinate theme boundary and provide a moment of harmonic and textural preparation for the new thematic material. The distinction between the introductory downbeat and the theme's structural "first bar" is important for accurate measure counting and formal analysis.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in B minor, Op. 64/2, i (Ch. 12, Ex. 12.7): tonic harmony and new accompaniment begin on the downbeat; basic idea enters later in the measure; the following downbeat is the theme's true "first bar"
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i (Ch. 12, Ex. 12.8): similar technique with tonic and accompaniment preceding the basic idea
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in A, K. 331 (Ch. 12, Ex. 12.11): tonic and accompaniment before the basic idea

# Common Errors
- Counting the introductory downbeat as the theme's "first bar"
- Confusing the thematic introduction with the transition's standing on the dominant
- Treating the new accompaniment pattern as the beginning of the subordinate theme

# Common Confusions
- **Introduction vs. standing on dominant**: The introduction is brief and leads into the theme; the standing on the dominant prolongs the transition's ending
- **First bar determination**: The theme's "first bar" is where the basic idea structurally begins, which may be later than the first appearance of the new accompaniment
- **Rarity with main themes**: Type 2 introductions are common with subordinate themes but rare with main themes
