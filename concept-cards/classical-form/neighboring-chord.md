# Quick Definition
A subordinate chord that prolongs a harmony by moving away from and returning to the same position of the prolonged chord, typically featuring neighbor-tone motion in one or more voices.

# Formal Definition
An individual harmony is prolonged by one or more neighboring chords when the prolonged harmony remains in the same position (root position or inversions) from the beginning to the end of the progression. A melodic neighbor-tone motion is usually (but not necessarily) present in one or more of the voices. The neighboring chord is subordinate to the prolonged harmony and has a weaker structural status.

# Construction/Recognition
- The prolonged harmony appears in the same position at the beginning and end
- One or more voices move by step to a neighboring tone and return
- The most common example: I-V-I, where V functions as a neighboring chord to I
- Also found: I-IV-I, or more complex neighboring progressions
- Distinguished from a cadential I-V-I by context: neighboring V does not function as a cadential dominant

# Musical Context
Neighboring-chord prolongations are common in presentation phrases (prolonging tonic), in postcadential areas, and in various accompanimental patterns. The simple I-V-I progression, often taught as a cadential formula, is in actual compositional practice more often prolongational (with V as a neighbor to I).

# Examples
- I-V-I where V is a neighboring chord (ex. 2.2a): resembles the cadence formula described by textbooks, but in practice is often better understood as prolongational
- Standing on the dominant using V/V as a neighboring chord to V (Beethoven, Op. 15, ii, mm. 12-14)

# Common Confusions
- The I-V-I progression can be either prolongational (V as neighbor) or cadential (V as penultimate dominant). Context determines which interpretation is correct.
- Neighbor-tone motion in the voices is usual but not required for a neighboring-chord prolongation.
