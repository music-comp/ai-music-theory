# Quick Definition
A subordinate chord that prolongs a harmony by moving away from and returning to the same position of the prolonged chord, typically featuring neighbor-tone motion in one or more voices.

# Core Definition
"An individual harmony is prolonged by one or more neighboring chords when the prolonged harmony remains in the same position (root position or inversions) from the beginning to the end of the progression" (Ch. 2, p. 25). "A melodic neighbor-tone motion is usually (but not necessarily) present in one or more of the voices" (Ch. 2, p. 25). The simple I-V-I progression "can indeed be classified as cadential" but "in actual compositional practice" is "often better understood as prolongational" (Ch. 2, p. 25).

# Prerequisites
- **Prolongational progression** -- the category to which neighboring chords belong

# Key Properties
1. Prolonged harmony in the same position at beginning and end
2. Neighbor-tone motion usually present (but not required)
3. I-V-I is often prolongational (V as neighbor) rather than cadential
4. Context determines whether V is a neighbor (prolongational) or cadential dominant

# Construction / Recognition
- Same harmony in same position at beginning and end of the progression
- Subordinate harmony between them
- Check for neighbor-tone motion in voices

# Context & Application
Neighboring-chord prolongations are common in presentation phrases (prolonging tonic), in postcadential areas, and in standings on the dominant (where V/V neighbors V).

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 2, ex. 2.2a): I-V-I where V is a neighboring chord -- resembles textbook cadence formula but in practice is often prolongational.
**Example 2** (Ch. 1, p. 14): Beethoven, Op. 15, ii, mm. 12-14: standing on the dominant using V/V as neighboring chord to V.

# Common Errors
- Automatically analyzing I-V-I as cadential when it may be prolongational

# Common Confusions
- The I-V-I progression can be either prolongational (V as neighbor) or cadential (V as penultimate dominant). Context determines which interpretation is correct
- Neighbor-tone motion in voices is usual but not required
