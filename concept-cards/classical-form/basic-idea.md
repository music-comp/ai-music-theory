# Quick Definition
A normatively two-measure unit that introduces the fundamental melodic-motivic material of a theme, serving as the building block from which the theme develops -- not the individual motive.

# Core Definition
The basic idea is normatively a two-measure unit that "brings in the fundamental melodic material of the theme" (Ch. 1, p. 9). It frequently contains several distinct motives that are developed in the course of the theme. The basic idea, not the individual motive, is "the fundamental building block of the theme" (Ch. 1, p. 10). Its characteristic motives define the uniqueness of the theme: a basic idea is immediately identifiable as belonging to a specific work. The basic idea stands opposed to the cadential idea, whose conventional (non-thematic) character could close any number of themes.

# Prerequisites
- **Formal function** -- understanding the role-based approach to form

# Key Properties
1. Normatively two measures long (in real measures)
2. Contains one or more distinct motives
3. Supported by tonic harmony (in presentation and antecedent contexts)
4. Its boundaries are confirmed through repetition
5. Distinguished from the contrasting idea by its role as primary melodic-motivic identity
6. Distinguished from the cadential idea by its characteristic (vs. conventional) content
7. The basic idea, not the motive, is the fundamental building block

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the opening two-measure unit of a theme
- Note the characteristic motives within it
- Confirm its boundaries by observing what follows (repetition = presentation; contrast = antecedent)
- Check for tonic harmonic support

# Context & Application
The basic idea opens sentences (where it is repeated to form a presentation) and periods (where it is followed by a contrasting idea in the antecedent). It returns at the start of consequent phrases and at the beginning of recapitulations. Detailed treatment in Chapter 3.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 1, p. 9, ex. 1.1): Beethoven, Op. 2/1, i, mm. 1-2: contains motives "a" (leaping grace note, arpeggiation) and "b" (stepwise descent with sixteenth-note triplet) within a single gesture.
**Example 2** (Ch. 1, p. 11, ex. 1.2): Beethoven, Op. 135, iii, mm. 3-4: the basic idea proper, preceded by a two-measure introduction.
**Example 3** (Ch. 1, p. 12, ex. 1.3): Mozart, K. 525, ii, mm. 1-2: basic idea of the antecedent phrase in a period.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Formal function (the basic idea is the smallest unit with clear functional identity)
## Enables
- Presentation function (basic idea + repetition)
- Antecedent function (basic idea + contrasting idea)
- Liquidation (stripping the basic idea of its characteristic features)
## Related
- Motive (components within the basic idea)
- Contrasting idea (the complementary idea in antecedent/consequent phrases)
## Contrasts With
- Contrasting idea: "not-a-repetition" that follows the basic idea
- Cadential idea: conventional (non-thematic) melody for cadences, opposed to the basic idea's characteristic content

# Common Errors
- Treating individual motives as the fundamental building block instead of the basic idea
- Assuming a basic idea indicates sentence form before seeing what follows

# Common Confusions
- The basic idea, not the motive, is the fundamental building block of the theme. Motives are components within the basic idea
- A basic idea by itself does not indicate sentence vs. period; what follows (repetition vs. contrast) determines the theme type
