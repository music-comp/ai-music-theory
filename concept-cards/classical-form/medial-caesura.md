# Quick Definition
A break in rhythmic activity at the end of the transition that separates the transition from the subordinate theme, creating a moment of silence or reduced texture that marks the boundary between these two formal functions.

# Formal Definition
The close of the transition is often marked by a liquidation of melodic-motivic material, a reduction in texture, and sometimes (but not always) a break in rhythmic activity to set off the entrance of the subordinate theme. When present, this break constitutes a medial caesura -- a gap in the musical fabric that articulates the formal boundary between transition and subordinate theme. However, the medial caesura is not always present. In some transitions, the final dominant resolves directly to the tonic of the subordinate theme with little or no rhythmic break, and the transition may lack a specific concluding function altogether. The standing on the dominant, when present, typically precedes the medial caesura.

# Construction/Recognition
1. A break or gap in rhythmic activity between the transition and the subordinate theme
2. Follows the standing on the dominant (when present)
3. Not always present -- some transitions flow directly into the subordinate theme
4. May be marked by rests, fermata, or dramatic reduction in texture
5. The final dominant is usually given temporal emphasis to arouse expectation for tonic resolution
6. Liquidation and textural reduction typically precede the caesura
7. The strength of the caesura varies along a continuum from very clear to completely absent

# Musical Context
The medial caesura is an important formal articulation point in sonata expositions. It marks the moment of maximum expectation: the transition has achieved its dominant goal, all melodic-motivic material has been liquidated, and the listener awaits the tonic resolution that will begin the subordinate theme. The presence or absence of a medial caesura significantly affects the perceived relationship between transition and subordinate theme. When absent, the boundary between these functions may be blurred, requiring retrospective determination of where one ends and the other begins.

# Examples
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in C, K. 403/385c, i, m. 8 (clear break after HC; subordinate theme begins immediately in new key)
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 2, Op. 36, i, mm. 61-73 (dominant arrival followed by standing on dominant; clear break before subordinate theme)
- Haydn, Symphony No. 90, iv, mm. 28-30 (dominant resolves directly to subordinate-theme tonic with no break -- absent medial caesura)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C Minor, K. 457, i, mm. 22-23 (transition flows directly into subordinate theme with no sense of an ending)

# Common Confusions
- The medial caesura is NOT always present -- its absence does not mean the transition has failed
- The medial caesura is the BREAK itself, not the dominant harmony that precedes it
- A standing on the dominant usually precedes the medial caesura but is distinct from it
- The term comes from Hepokoski and Darcy's "sonata theory" but Caplin addresses the same phenomenon under "ending the transition" -- the concept of a break between transition and subordinate theme
- Even without a medial caesura, the subordinate theme's beginning can usually be identified through new melodic material and tonic support
