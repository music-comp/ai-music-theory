# Quick Definition
A break in rhythmic activity at the end of the transition that separates the transition from the subordinate theme, creating a moment of silence or reduced texture marking the formal boundary.

# Core Definition
The close of the transition is often marked by a liquidation of melodic-motivic material, a reduction in texture, and sometimes (but not always) a break in rhythmic activity to set off the entrance of the subordinate theme. When present, this break constitutes a medial caesura -- a gap in the musical fabric articulating the formal boundary between transition and subordinate theme. The medial caesura is not always present. In some transitions, the final dominant resolves directly to tonic at the beginning of the subordinate theme with little or no rhythmic break. The standing on the dominant, when present, typically precedes the medial caesura. The strength of the caesura varies along a continuum from very clear to completely absent.

# Prerequisites
- **Transition**: The formal unit preceding the caesura
- **Standing on the dominant**: Often precedes the caesura
- **Subordinate theme**: The formal unit following the caesura

# Key Properties
1. A break or gap in rhythmic activity between transition and subordinate theme
2. Follows the standing on the dominant (when present)
3. Not always present -- some transitions flow directly into the subordinate theme
4. May be marked by rests, fermata, or dramatic textural reduction
5. The final dominant is usually given temporal emphasis to arouse expectation
6. Liquidation and textural reduction typically precede the caesura
7. Strength varies along a continuum from clear to absent
8. The moment of maximum expectation in the exposition

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for a rhythmic gap or textural break at the transition-subordinate theme boundary
- Note whether the standing on the dominant precedes the break
- Assess the strength of the caesura (clear break, reduced texture, or absent)
- When absent, identify the subordinate theme's beginning through other means (new material, tonic support)
- Distinguish the caesura itself (the break) from the dominant harmony that precedes it

# Context & Application
The medial caesura marks the moment of maximum expectation: the transition has achieved its dominant goal, all material has been liquidated, and the listener awaits the tonic resolution beginning the subordinate theme. Its presence or absence significantly affects the perceived relationship between transition and subordinate theme. When absent, the boundary may be blurred, requiring retrospective determination.

# Examples
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in C, K. 403/385c, i, m. 8: clear break after HC; subordinate theme begins immediately in new key
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 2, Op. 36, i, mm. 61-73: dominant arrival followed by standing on dominant; clear break before subordinate theme
- Haydn, Symphony No. 90, iv, mm. 28-30: dominant resolves directly to ST tonic with no break (absent medial caesura)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C Minor, K. 457, i, mm. 22-23: transition flows directly into ST with no ending function

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Transition (the formal unit ending at the caesura)
- Standing on the dominant (typically precedes the caesura)

## Enables
- Subordinate theme entrance (the formal unit following the caesura)
- Formal articulation (marking the exposition's midpoint)

## Related
- Dominant arrival (the harmonic event preceding the caesura)
- Half cadence (the cadential event preceding the caesura)
- Liquidation (the process leading to the caesura)

## Contrasts With
- No direct contrast; the medial caesura is an optional formal articulation

# Common Errors
- Assuming the medial caesura is always present (some transitions lack it entirely)
- Confusing the caesura (the break) with the dominant harmony preceding it
- Treating the standing on the dominant as the caesura itself

# Common Confusions
- **Not always present**: The medial caesura's absence does not mean the transition has failed
- **Caesura vs. dominant**: The medial caesura is the BREAK itself, not the dominant harmony
- **Standing on dominant vs. caesura**: The standing on dominant precedes but is distinct from the caesura
- **Terminology note**: The term comes from Hepokoski and Darcy's "sonata theory"; Caplin addresses the same phenomenon under "ending the transition"

# Verification Notes
The continuum of caesura strength is described through the systematic progression from clear HC to absent concluding function. Caplin does not use the term "medial caesura" himself but the concept is present.
