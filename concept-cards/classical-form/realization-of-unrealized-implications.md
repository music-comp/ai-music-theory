# Quick Definition
The coda's function of resolving compositional processes or "problems" initiated earlier in the movement that could not be appropriately resolved in the recapitulation.

# Formal Definition
Many compositional processes initiated in a work imply particular modes of continuation. Some implications are realized immediately; others are realized only much later; and still others may never be realized at all. The coda gives the composer the last opportunity to realize an earlier implication, often one that would not have found an appropriate realization earlier in the movement. These implications usually arise in relation to the main theme, particularly a "problem" or "disturbance" that is not resolved until the coda.

# Construction/Recognition
- Identify a compositional "problem" or anomaly in the exposition (unusual harmonic progression, modal disturbance, cadential ambiguity)
- Trace whether this problem is addressed in the development or recapitulation
- Look for its resolution in the coda
- The resolution often involves the main theme material being presented in a way that corrects the original disturbance
- Implications may involve tonality (key adjustment), mode (modal correction), cadential closure, or melodic continuation

# Musical Context
The realization of implications is the most sophisticated of the coda's compensatory functions. It requires the analyst to trace compositional processes across the entire movement and understand how the coda provides their final closure.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62 (ex. 12.6): (1) the main theme's recapitulation in the subdominant (m. 152) implies a later home-key restatement, realized at m. 276; (2) a cadential ambiguity at the close of the exposition's main theme (HC at m. 13 or elided PAC at m. 15?) implies later unequivocal cadential closure, realized at m. 296
- Mozart, Symphony No. 39 in E-flat, K. 543, ii (ex. 12.10): an unusual modal shift to minor in the exposition's main theme (mm. 22-25) implies the theme may return without this disturbance; the coda version (m. 144) eliminates the explicit modal shift; the coda also restores the PAC deleted from the recapitulation
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C Minor, Op. 10/1, ii (ex. 12.11): the exposition's continuation with intense rhythmic and melodic activity implies a simpler continuation; the coda (mm. 95-102) provides the "closing down" version

# Common Confusions
- Not every compositional anomaly generates an implication that must be realized — some remain permanently unrealized
- The realization is not always a literal correction; it may involve a transformed version of the original material
- This function requires careful analytical attention; it is not always obvious on first hearing
