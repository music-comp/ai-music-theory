# Quick Definition
A loosening technique in which a subordinate theme begins with a medial function (continuation) or concluding function (cadential) instead of a standard initiating function (presentation, compound basic idea), creating a sense of starting in medias res.

# Formal Definition
A subordinate theme can acquire formal loosening by beginning with continuation or cadential function instead of a standard initiating function. This creates a kind of formal "dissonance" -- a middle or end appearing at a beginning. Classical instrumental music has the capacity for a passage to express the sense of beginning, middle, or end independent of its actual temporal location, because formal functions are so conventionalized and well-defined by specific characteristics. Model-sequence technique alone can signal continuation function; a cadential progression can signal cadential function. If carefully controlled, this formal dissonance creates a suitable loose organization. Another possibility is replacing the initiating function with a standing on the dominant from the end of the transition.

# Construction/Recognition
1. **Beginning with continuation**: Model-sequence technique (most effective signal); harmonic sequence replaces tonic prolongation; no presentation or compound basic idea
2. **Beginning with cadential**: Expanded cadential progression from the very start; I6 onset suggests cadential function despite being the theme's opening
3. **Beginning with standing on dominant**: Dominant from transition sustained into theme; new melodic material over dominant pedal; followed by continuation or cadential (not initiating) function
4. In all cases, the theme bypasses the conventional "beginning" function
5. Often found in second or later subordinate themes of a group

# Musical Context
Beginning with a medial or concluding function is one of the boldest loosening techniques, appropriate to subordinate themes but rarely found in main themes. When the first subordinate theme of a group begins with continuation, the theme group as a whole lacks a sense of structural initiation -- often compensated by a later theme emphasizing its own presentation function. Beginning with cadential function is rarer and more radical, though precedents exist in Mozart and Haydn. Beginning with standing on the dominant blurs the transition-theme boundary.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i, mm. 58-74 (first subordinate theme begins with 3-measure model sequenced by ascending minor thirds -- pure continuation function; no presentation)
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:35, i, mm. 36-45 (first subordinate theme supported by single expanded cadential progression -- cadential function from the start)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 31/3, i, mm. 46-53 (subordinate theme starts with I6 and progresses to cadential function; same procedure used for main theme, mm. 1-8)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A-flat, Op. 26, iv, mm. 33-48 (subordinate theme begins with standing on dominant, 9 measures, before cadential function)

# Common Confusions
- A theme "beginning" with continuation or cadential function is a formal dissonance, not a formal error -- it is a carefully controlled loosening device
- Beginning with a standing on the dominant creates ambiguity about where the transition ends and the subordinate theme begins -- the boundary is determined retrospectively
- The first subordinate theme beginning with continuation may leave the entire theme group without a sense of initiation, which a later tight-knit theme may compensate for
- Beginning a MAIN theme with cadential function (as Beethoven does in Op. 31/3) is a radical procedure looking forward to the Romantic style
