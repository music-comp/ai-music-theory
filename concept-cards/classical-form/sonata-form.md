# Quick Definition
The most important large-scale formal type in classical instrumental music, consisting of three principal functions -- exposition, development, and recapitulation -- with optional slow introduction and coda.

# Core Definition
Sonata form consists of three large-scale functions: exposition, development, and recapitulation. Two additional functions are sometimes included: a slow introduction before the exposition and a coda after the recapitulation. In its large-scale tonal and form-functional organization, sonata form is analogous to the small ternary form -- specifically the rounded binary version, since the exposition is normally repeated and the development-recapitulation are sometimes repeated together. The exposition necessarily modulates to a subordinate key, creating a "structural dissonance" (tonal-polarity model) that is intensified in the development and resolved in the recapitulation. The form exhibits both ternary characteristics (three functional sections) and binary characteristics (two repeated halves), and the debate over which is primary is a "false dichotomy." Almost every multimovement work (except the concerto) contains at least one sonata-form movement.

# Prerequisites
- **Sonata exposition**: The section establishing two contrasting tonal regions
- **Development section**: The section generating maximum tonal instability
- **Recapitulation**: The section resolving the tonal conflict

# Key Properties
1. Three principal functions: exposition, development, recapitulation
2. Two optional functions: slow introduction, coda
3. Analogous to small ternary form (specifically rounded binary version)
4. Exposition necessarily modulates to a subordinate key
5. The subordinate key creates structural dissonance resolved in the recapitulation
6. Exhibits both binary and ternary characteristics
7. Exposition normally repeated; development-recapitulation sometimes repeated (especially pre-1780)
8. Almost every multimovement work contains at least one sonata-form movement
9. The period's most highly developed and complex compositional design
10. Continued to influence later styles through the 20th century

# Construction / Recognition
- **Exposition**: establishes home key and subordinate key; contains MT, Tr, ST with closing section; normally repeated
- **Development**: explores various tonal regions; features pre-core/core or alternatives; ends on home-key dominant
- **Recapitulation**: restates exposition in the home key; tonally adjusts transition and subordinate theme
- **Slow introduction** (optional): precedes exposition; usually slow tempo; ends on dominant
- **Coda** (optional): follows recapitulation; after-the-end function; compensatory functions
- Eight cadential patterns describe the exposition's form-functional design (Table 13.1)
- Two tonal models: tonal-polarity (two-key conflict) and tour-of-keys (systematic exploration)

# Context & Application
Sonata form is the period's most highly developed and complex compositional design, revealing composers' greatest technical skill and expressive potential. It continued to exert enormous influence through at least the mid-20th century. The form's principles are reflected in allied forms: sonata-rondo, concerto, and sonata without development. Caplin's approach focuses on formal functionality, emphasizing the ternary aspects without devaluing the binary traits.

# Common Errors
- Treating sonata form as a rigid template rather than a set of functional principles
- Confusing "sonata form" (a formal type within movements) with "sonata" (a multi-movement genre)

# Common Confusions
- **Binary vs. ternary**: The debate is a "false dichotomy"; sonata form exhibits both characteristics
- **"Sonata form" vs. "sonata"**: Sonata form is a formal type used within individual movements; a sonata is a multi-movement genre
- **Not a rigid template**: The form is a set of functional principles that composers realize in individual ways
- **Two tonal models**: The tonal-polarity model (two-key conflict) is complemented by the tour-of-keys model (systematic exploration); neither alone suffices
