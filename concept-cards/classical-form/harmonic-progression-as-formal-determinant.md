# Quick Definition
In Caplin's theory, local harmonic progression--not melodic-motivic content--is the most important factor in defining formal functions at the theme level and below.

# Formal Definition
Caplin emphasizes local harmonic progression as the primary determinant of formal functions in themes and themelike units. While most discussions focus on how tonal regions create high-level formal designs, Caplin examines how specific progressions within a given tonal center define formal functionality at lower levels. The formal function of an individual group does not depend on its motivic content. A single motive can saturate a composition without obscuring the form, precisely because motives carry little in the way of functional implications.

# Construction/Recognition
- Presentation function: identified by tonic prolongation
- Continuation function: identified by sequential or destabilizing progressions
- Cadential function: identified by cadential progressions
- The appearance of a particular motive, leaving aside its implied harmony, rarely determines formal function

# Musical Context
This principle is foundational to Caplin's analytical method. One of the earliest tasks in formal analysis is to determine the underlying harmony of a passage. Only after establishing the harmonic context can formal functions be accurately identified.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 15, ii, mm. 9-14: cannot be a genuine sentence despite its motivic layout, because the opening four measures do not prolong tonic harmony
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1, i, mm. 1-4: identified as presentation not merely by the repeated idea but by the tonic prolongation

- Caplin deliberately minimizes motivic analysis as a criterion of formal function, setting aside Schoenberg's own preoccupation with Grundgestalt and "developing variation."
