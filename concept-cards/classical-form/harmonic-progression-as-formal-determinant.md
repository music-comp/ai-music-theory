# Quick Definition
In Caplin's theory, local harmonic progression -- not melodic-motivic content, tonal regions, or durational proportions -- is the most important factor in defining formal functions at the theme level and below.

# Core Definition
Caplin emphasizes local harmonic progression as the primary determinant of formal functions in themes and themelike units. While most discussions focus on how tonal regions create high-level formal designs, Caplin examines "how formal functionality at a movement's lower levels is defined by specific progressions of harmonies in a given tonal center" (Introduction, p. 4). The formal function of an individual group does not depend on its motivic content: "the appearance of a particular melodic motive -- leaving aside its implied harmony -- rarely determines its formal expression. In fact, a single motive can saturate a musical composition without obscuring the form, precisely because motives carry little in the way of functional implications" (Introduction, p. 5).

# Prerequisites
- **Formal function** -- understanding what is being determined

# Key Properties
1. Local harmonic progression is the most important factor in expressing formal functions in themes
2. One of the earliest tasks in formal analysis is to determine the underlying harmony of a passage
3. Motivic content is deliberately minimized as a criterion of formal function
4. A single motive can permeate a work without obscuring the form
5. This approach sets aside Schoenberg's own preoccupation with Grundgestalt and developing variation

# Construction / Recognition
- Presentation function: identified by tonic prolongation
- Continuation function: identified by sequential or destabilizing progressions
- Cadential function: identified by cadential progressions (I-PD-V-I)
- Determine the underlying harmony before assigning formal labels

# Context & Application
This principle is foundational to Caplin's analytical method. It explains why a passage that "looks like" a sentence based on motivic layout may not function as one if the harmonic progressions do not support it (see the Beethoven Op. 15 contrasting middle example). It also explains why motivically saturated themes remain formally clear.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 1, pp. 14-15): Beethoven, Op. 15, ii, mm. 9-14: cannot be a genuine sentence despite motivic layout (2-measure idea, repetition, fragmentation), because the opening four measures do not prolong tonic harmony and the cadence precedes rather than follows the fragmentation.
**Example 2** (Ch. 1, p. 10): Beethoven, Op. 2/1, i, mm. 1-4: identified as presentation not merely by the repeated idea but by the tonic prolongation.

# Common Errors
- Assigning formal function based on motivic layout without checking harmonic support
- Identifying a presentation solely because an idea is repeated, without confirming tonic prolongation

# Common Confusions
- Caplin deliberately minimizes motivic analysis as a criterion of formal function, setting aside Schoenberg's own preoccupation with Grundgestalt and "developing variation"
- This does not mean motives are unimportant to musical experience, only that they do not determine formal function
