# Quick Definition
The closing section of the subordinate theme in the recapitulation, which usually reappears similar to the exposition but may be altered or extended depending on whether a coda follows.

# Core Definition
The closing section of the subordinate theme (group) usually reappears in the recapitulation in much the same way as it did in the exposition. If the recapitulation is followed by a coda, the final codettas may be altered or eliminated. In the absence of a genuine coda, the closing section may be extended to impart a more decisive sense of conclusion to the movement as a whole. If the exposition closes with a retransition leading back to the home key, the recapitulation may bring a similar passage -- but this "retransition" now leads to the subdominant region (or occasionally the supertonic) for the beginning of the coda. It no longer fulfills its nominal function of returning to the home key; instead it functions more as a "transition" to the coda.

# Prerequisites
- **Recapitulation**: The formal context
- **Closing section in subordinate theme**: The original section

# Key Properties
1. Usually reappears much as in the exposition
2. May be altered or shortened when a coda follows
3. May be extended when no coda follows (providing final closure)
4. Exposition retransitions become "transitions" to the coda in the recapitulation
5. The "retransition" typically leads to IV for the coda, not back to I

# Construction / Recognition
- Compare closing section in exposition and recapitulation
- Check whether codettas have been altered, extended, or eliminated
- If the exposition had a retransition, check whether the recapitulation's version leads to IV or II instead of I
- Note whether the closing section is extended in movements without codas

# Context & Application
The closing section's treatment depends on the larger formal context. In movements with codas, the closing section may be abbreviated since the coda will provide additional closure. In movements without codas, the closing section bears the burden of providing the movement's final sense of conclusion.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F Minor, Op. 2/1, i (ex. 11.15): closing section extended relative to exposition to form a complete sentence with eight-measure stretched continuation
- Mozart, String Quintet in G Minor, K. 516, i (ex. 12.1, mm. 230-31): "retransition" at end of recapitulation leads to subdominant for the coda
- Mozart, Symphony No. 36 ("Linz"), K. 425, i (ex. 12.2, mm. 265-68): "retransition" leads to supertonic for the coda

# Common Errors
- Assuming the "retransition" at the end of the recapitulation functions the same way as in the exposition

# Common Confusions
- **"Retransition" misnomer**: The passage at the end of the recapitulation that resembles the exposition's retransition does not actually return to the home key; it modulates to another region for the coda
- **Extended vs. shortened**: Movements without codas typically extend the closing section; movements with codas may shorten it
