# Quick Definition
The orchestral framing of a concerto movement created by the opening and closing ritornellos, in which the orchestra performs alone to establish and complete the movement's formal boundaries.

# Core Definition

# Prerequisites
See front matter.

# Key Properties
See Core Definition.
The textural frame is a defining structural feature of concerto form, created by the opening ritornello (which initiates the frame) and the closing ritornello (which completes it). In both sections, the orchestra performs alone, asserting its own identity apart from the soloist. The frame ensures that the orchestra is not reduced to a merely accompanimental role and provides the concerto with its distinctive textural architecture. The closing ritornello completes the frame by ending with the same closing section that ended the opening ritornello, creating a formal "rhyme." The orchestra is also given at least one other opportunity to appear alone (the subordinate-key ritornello), further reinforcing its independent identity.

# Construction / Recognition
- Opening ritornello: orchestra alone, initiating the frame
- Closing ritornello: orchestra alone (except cadenza), completing the frame
- Both ritornellos share closing-section material, creating a formal rhyme
- The subordinate-key ritornello provides an additional orchestral-alone passage
- The frame ensures the orchestra maintains an independent identity throughout the movement

# Context & Application
The textural frame is a vestige of baroque ritornello form adapted to the classical concerto. It addresses the concerto's fundamental challenge: ensuring that the orchestra maintains an independent identity despite the soloist's prominence. The frame bookends the movement with the orchestra's own material, creating a structural symmetry.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i: Opening ritornello establishes the frame; closing ritornello completes it with matching closing-section material

# Common Confusions
- The textural frame is not merely a conventional opening and closing; it serves the essential formal function of giving the orchestra an independent identity within a form dominated by the soloist.
- The matching closing sections between opening and closing ritornellos create a structural rhyme, not a recapitulation in the sonata sense.
