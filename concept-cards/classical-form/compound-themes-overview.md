# Quick Definition
Compound themes are sixteen-measure structures that extend the principles of the simple sentence and period to a larger scale. The compound period has an eight-measure antecedent and eight-measure consequent; the compound sentence has an eight-measure presentation and eight-measure continuation.

# Core Definition
The simple themes (sentence, period, and hybrids) are defined as eight measures in length, though the use of various phrase and cadential deviations as well as the addition of framing functions can alter the actual size of the theme. Two compound themes -- the compound period and the compound sentence -- are constructed out of sixteen real measures. The basic functional components of the period and sentence are present in the compound versions, but their internal structure is more complex. Like simple themes, compound themes may be subjected to deviation techniques. The "ideas" within compound themes are four-measure phrases (vs. two-measure ideas in simple themes).

# Prerequisites
- **Sentence**: The fundamental theme type whose principles extend to the compound sentence
- **Period**: The fundamental theme type whose principles extend to the compound period
- **Hybrid themes**: The theme types that serve as building blocks for compound antecedents and consequents

# Key Properties
1. Constructed from sixteen real measures (not notated measures with R = 2N)
2. Compound period: 8 (antecedent) + 8 (consequent), where each unit is built as a sentence or hybrid
3. Compound sentence: 8 (presentation: c.b.i. + repetition) + 8 (continuation: fragmentation, acceleration, cadence)
4. The four-measure "ideas" stand in the same hierarchical relation to the sixteen-measure structure as two-measure ideas do to eight-measure structures
5. Compound themes combine form-functional traits of both sentence and period at different hierarchical levels
6. Subject to the same deviation techniques as simple themes (extension, expansion, compression, interpolation)

# Construction / Recognition
1. Determine whether the theme consists of sixteen real measures
2. **Compound period**: Identify whether the first eight measures end with a weak cadence and the second eight repeat the opening material with stronger cadential closure (PAC)
3. **Compound sentence**: Identify whether the first eight measures consist of a repeated c.b.i. over tonic prolongation (no cadence) and the second eight bring fragmentation, acceleration, and cadential closure
4. Check for the return of the basic idea at m. 9 -- this signals consequent function (compound period) or is absent in the compound sentence's continuation
5. Beware of cases where R = 2N (notated as 16 measures but sounding as 8)

# Context & Application
Compound themes represent the interaction between sentence and period principles at the sixteen-measure level. They are common in the classical repertory and appear in all formal positions (main themes, subordinate themes, slow movements). The compound sentence is especially associated with large public genres (symphonies, concertos), while the compound period appears across all genres. The compound period is particularly important because its construction rules (avoiding lower-level consequents) reveal how classical composers manage hierarchical relationships between form-functional levels.

# Common Errors
- Confusing a simple theme with R = 2N for a compound theme -- check the relationship between real and notated measures
- Assuming compound themes must be exactly sixteen measures -- deviation techniques can alter the length
- Labeling a compound period a "double period" -- this term implies period-within-a-period, which is avoided in classical practice

# Common Confusions
- "Compound" refers to the sixteen-measure scope and hierarchical complexity, not to anything being "combined" or "added together"
- Do not confuse a compound period with two adjacent simple periods -- the compound period is a single theme with hierarchically nested formal functions
- Compound themes use the same formal functions as simple themes (presentation, continuation, antecedent, consequent, cadential) but at different hierarchical levels
- There is no essential difference in formal functions between simple and compound themes; only the hierarchical level differs
