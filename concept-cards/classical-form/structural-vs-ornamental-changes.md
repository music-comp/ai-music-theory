# Quick Definition
The two categories of changes between an exposition and its recapitulation: structural changes affect harmony, tonality, grouping, and formal functions; ornamental changes affect dynamics, instrumentation, register, texture, and embellishments.

# Core Definition
When comparing a recapitulation bar by bar with its exposition, two basic types of changes may occur. Structural changes involve harmonic-tonal organization, melodic-motivic material, grouping structure, and formal functions. These changes are motivated primarily by fundamental differences in the function of the recapitulation compared with the exposition, and may also be prompted by the content of the development section. Ornamental changes involve dynamics, instrumentation, register, texture, accompanimental figuration, melodic embellishments, and the like. These changes are motivated by general aesthetic concerns for variety and by particular expressive values the composer wishes to convey. The methodology most appropriate for analyzing a recapitulation is one of comparison -- identifying changes and asking why they are made.

# Prerequisites
- **Recapitulation**: The section being analyzed
- **Sonata exposition**: The section against which the recapitulation is compared

# Key Properties
1. Structural changes affect harmony, tonality, grouping structure, and formal functions
2. Ornamental changes affect dynamics, instrumentation, register, texture, and embellishments
3. Structural changes are motivated by fundamentally different formal functions
4. Ornamental changes are motivated by aesthetic variety and expressive goals
5. Structural changes may also be prompted by the development section's content
6. The analytical methodology is one of bar-by-bar comparison
7. The analyst should not only identify changes but ask WHY they are made
8. Using the same labels (main theme, transition, subordinate theme) for both sections obscures their different functions

# Construction / Recognition
- Compare the recapitulation bar by bar with the exposition
- Classify each change as structural or ornamental
- For structural changes: identify the functional motivation (tonal adjustment, deletion of redundancy, development influence)
- For ornamental changes: note aesthetic or expressive motivation
- Ask "why?" for each change identified

# Context & Application
This distinction is Caplin's primary analytical tool for understanding recapitulations. The emphasis on structural changes reflects his view that the recapitulation serves fundamentally different formal functions from the exposition -- despite carrying the same conventional labels. Even the most apparently minor structural change may reveal important functional shifts.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i (ex. 14.1): secondary development added (structural), restatement deleted (structural), final cadence eliminated (structural)
- Mozart, Clarinet Trio in E-flat, K. 498, i, mm. 74-83 (ex. 14.12): rhythmic gaps filled with imitative entries (ornamental change with structural implications -- greater rhythmic continuity)

# Common Errors
- Treating all changes as equally significant (structural changes reveal functional recomposition; ornamental changes provide variety)
- Failing to recognize that exposition labels obscure different formal functions in the recapitulation
- Assuming structural changes are composer whims rather than functionally motivated

# Common Confusions
- **Labels vs. functions**: The same labels (main theme, transition, subordinate theme) are used for both exposition and recapitulation, but their formal functions differ
- **Ornamental with structural implications**: Some ornamental changes (like filling rhythmic gaps) serve structural purposes (greater continuity)
- **Causal vs. appropriateness**: Speaking of the development "influencing" the recapitulation does not imply a causal connection -- it is a question of appropriateness of occurrence
