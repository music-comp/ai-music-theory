# Quick Definition
A chord constructed over the fifth scale-degree (^5 in the bass) that embellishes the dominant in a cadential progression, resolving its upper voices downward to form the dominant chord proper.

# Formal Definition
The principal embellishment of dominant harmony in a cadential progression occurs through the use of a "cadential six-four" chord constructed over the fifth scale-degree. The cadential six-four helps clarify why II6 is preferred to IV as the main pre-dominant: II6 creates a more active and directed melodic line in which the motion from ^2 (the second scale-degree) to the leading-tone can be filled in by a passing tone supported by the cadential six-four. When IV is used instead, the melody tends to be static, and adding a six-four embellishment would further emphasize the tonic scale-degree redundantly.

# Construction/Recognition
- Built over ^5 in the bass voice
- Upper voices contain ^1 and ^3 (which resolve down to ^7 and ^2 respectively)
- Functions as an embellishment of V, not as a tonic chord in second inversion
- Typically placed between the pre-dominant and the root-position V
- Creates a suspension-like resolution: 6-5 and 4-3 above the bass
- The passing tone between ^2 and ^7 fills the melodic line when preceded by II6

# Musical Context
The cadential six-four is one of the most frequently used embellishments in classical cadential progressions. Its use is so common that it is almost a standard component of the cadential formula. It bridges the pre-dominant and the dominant, creating a smooth voice-leading connection and a sense of harmonic weight on the dominant arrival.

# Examples
- II6-I6/4-V-I: the standard cadential progression with six-four embellishment (ex. 2.6)
- The cadential six-four is frequently combined with a preceding II6, creating the melodic line ^2-^1-^7 over the bass ^4-^5-^5

# Common Confusions
- The cadential six-four is NOT a tonic chord in second inversion. It functions as a dominant embellishment. Analyzing it as "I6/4" can be misleading; it is better understood as a suspension or appoggiatura chord over ^5.
- Many textbooks confusingly analyze this as a tonic chord, but Caplin (following the German functional tradition) treats it as a dominant embellishment.
