# Quick Definition
Transitions close with dominant harmony through three mechanisms of decreasing cadential strength: a genuine half cadence (strongest), a noncadential dominant arrival (weaker), or no concluding function at all (weakest, merging directly into the subordinate theme) -- each typically accompanied by liquidation of melodic material and reduction of texture.

# Core Definition
The close of the transition is typically marked by a liquidation of melodic-motivic material, a reduction in texture, and sometimes a break in rhythmic activity -- a medial caesura -- to set off the entrance of the subordinate theme. The final harmony is a dominant, of either the subordinate key or the home key. This dominant may be articulated as a genuine half cadence (root-position triad approached by a cadential progression), a noncadential dominant arrival (the dominant appears but lacks a genuine cadential progression or arrives "too early"), or in some cases there is no concluding function at all and the transition merges directly into the subordinate theme. A characteristic pre-dominant signal approaching the transition's end is a harmony featuring the raised 4th scale degree (the "leading tone" of the dominant), commonly realized as vii6/V or an augmented-sixth chord.

# Prerequisites
- **Transition**: The formal function being concluded
- **Half cadence**: The strongest transition closure
- **Dominant arrival**: The alternative, weaker closure
- **Standing on the dominant**: Often follows the HC or dominant arrival

# Key Properties
1. Three closure types: HC (strongest), dominant arrival (weaker), no concluding function (weakest)
2. HC requires root-position dominant triad in the context of a genuine cadential progression
3. Dominant arrival: the dominant appears as ending harmony but without a full cadential progression
4. Premature dominant arrival: the harmonic goal precedes the melodic-motivic goal
5. Characteristic pre-dominant: raised 4th scale degree in the bass (vii6/V, augmented-sixth chord)
6. Liquidation of melodic material and textural reduction typically accompany closure
7. A fermata on the final sonority creates a medial caesura
8. Standing on the dominant may follow any type of closure
9. When no concluding function exists, the subordinate theme must provide clear initiating function

# Construction / Recognition
- Locate the final dominant of the transition
- Determine if it is a genuine HC (root-position V triad, cadential progression)
- If not HC: is it a dominant arrival (ending-quality dominant without full cadential support)?
- If premature: does the harmonic arrival precede the melodic-motivic completion?
- If no clear dominant ending: does the transition merge directly into the subordinate theme?
- Note liquidation, textural reduction, fermata, medial caesura
- Check for the characteristic raised-4th pre-dominant (vii6/V, augmented 6th)

# Context & Application
The transition's closure is analytically critical because it marks the structural end of the transition. The specific type of closure (HC, dominant arrival, or omission) has consequences for the subordinate theme: after a strong HC, the subordinate theme can begin with a conventional initiation; after a premature dominant arrival, the boundary may be less clear; without any transition closure, the subordinate theme must provide its own strong initiation or the functions may fuse.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F minor, Op. 2/1, i, m. 16 (Ch. 9, Ex. 9.2): HC in A-flat major; standing on dominant mm. 17-20
- Haydn, String Quartet in E-flat, Op. 50/3, i, m. 17 (Ch. 11, Ex. 11.13): dominant arrival -- V65 (inverted, with dissonant 7th) plus fermata creating medial caesura
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 4, Op. 60, i, m. 92 (Ch. 11, Ex. 11.14): premature dominant arrival -- harmonic goal (m. 92) precedes melodic goal (m. 95)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C minor, K. 457, i, mm. 19-22 (Ch. 11, Ex. 11.20): no concluding function -- dominant receives no emphasis, resolves directly to subordinate theme

# Common Errors
- Treating all dominant arrivals as half cadences (HC has specific requirements)
- Identifying the dominant resolution to tonic at the subordinate theme's start as the transition's end
- Missing the premature dominant arrival when the harmonic and melodic goals are non-simultaneous

# Common Confusions
- **HC vs. dominant arrival**: An HC requires root-position V triad in a genuine cadential progression; a dominant arrival lacks one or both requirements
- **Sequence ending ≠ cadence**: When a sequential progression ends on V-I, the final unit is a repetition of prior material and should not be heard as a cadential idea
- **Premature = non-simultaneous**: The dominant is "premature" because it arrives before the melodic-motivic process concludes, not because it is "wrong"
