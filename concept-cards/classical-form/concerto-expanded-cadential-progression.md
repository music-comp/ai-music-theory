# Quick Definition
Enormous expanded cadential progressions are a hallmark of the concerto, with the rhythmic and dynamic climax of the exposition normally reached during the expansion of the final cadential dominant, culminating in the conventional "cadential trill" that signals the close of the solo exposition.

# Core Definition
Enormous expanded cadential progressions (ECPs) are a hallmark of the concerto. The rhythmic and dynamic climax of the exposition is normally reached during the expansion of the final cadential dominant, in which the shortest durational values culminate in the "cadential trill," the conventional sign for the close of the solo exposition. This stylistic element permeated other genres of the period, such as the solo sonata and string quartet; a broad ECP leading to a cadential trill immediately brings the idea of concerto to mind regardless of the genre. Extended sequences and expanded cadential progressions create structural space for the soloist to display virtuosic abilities, filled with figurational passage work exploring extreme ranges and showcasing technique (Caplin, pp. 710-711).

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the final cadential progression in the solo exposition
2. Look for massive expansion of the cadential dominant
3. Note accelerating rhythmic values culminating in a trill
4. Assess the virtuosic figurational work during the expansion

# Context & Application
The ECP is where the concerto's dual aesthetic goals converge most powerfully: the soloist achieves maximum virtuosic display while the harmonic structure drives toward the strongest cadential closure. The subsequent subordinate-key ritornello then sustains or surpasses this climax with the full orchestral mass.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the ECP in a concerto as identical to ECPs in other genres. **Correction**: Concerto ECPs are characteristically more enormous and serve the additional function of virtuosic display.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Whether the cadential trill marks the end of the movement. **Clarification**: It marks the end of the solo exposition (or recapitulation), not the movement; the subordinate-key ritornello (or closing ritornello) follows.
