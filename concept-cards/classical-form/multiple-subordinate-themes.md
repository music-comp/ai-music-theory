# Quick Definition
A group of two or more subordinate themes in the exposition, each ending with a PAC in the subordinate key, that collectively fulfills the subordinate-theme function through a broader range of loosening techniques than any single theme could contain.

# Formal Definition
The exposition of a large-scale movement often features a group of subordinate themes, each ending with a PAC in the subordinate key. The use of multiple subordinate themes is one of the principal means for enlarging the formal dimensions of the composition, since a greater number of loosening techniques can be employed across multiple themes. The way loosening techniques are dispersed among the themes is of significant analytical interest. The functional relationship among the themes is difficult to define precisely; they are identified neutrally by order of appearance (first subordinate theme, second subordinate theme). The themes are linked by elision (final tonic of one functioning as initial tonic of the next) or by accompanimental overlap.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Each theme in the group ends with a PAC in the subordinate key
2. Themes may be connected by elision (cadential tonic = initial tonic of next theme) or accompanimental overlap
3. One theme is often distinctly more tight knit than the others (often the first, especially in Mozart)
4. The overall group (not any single theme) should be compared to the main theme for looseness
5. If the first theme is tight knit, later themes typically introduce more loosening techniques
6. If the first theme is very loose (e.g., beginning with continuation), a later tight-knit theme may compensate
7. A true closing section appears only after the final theme in the group

# Musical Context
Multiple subordinate themes are typical of large-scale movements in sonata, rondo, and concerto forms. The group as a whole provides the loosening contrast to the main theme. Individual themes within the group can range from relatively tight knit (simple sentence or period) to extremely loose (beginning with model-sequence, featuring extensive cadential evasion). The interplay between tighter and looser themes within the group is a key analytical feature. False closing sections may intervene between themes in the group.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:35, i (two subordinate themes: first is entirely cadential with expanded I6; second begins with continuation and features "one more time" technique)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i (two themes: first begins with model-sequence continuation [very loose]; second begins with repeated presentation [compensatory tightness])
- Mozart, Clarinet Quintet in A, K. 581, i (two themes: first is tight-knit compressed sentence; second features modal shift, tonicization of bVI, and expanded dominant)
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62 (two themes: first is modulating, very loose; second is tight-knit sentence with IAC-to-PAC extension)

# Common Confusions
- Each subordinate theme in a group must end with PAC -- a theme ending with HC is reinterpreted as the first part of a two-part subordinate theme
- The overall group (not individual themes) should be compared with the main theme for relative looseness
- A tight-knit subordinate theme within a loose group does NOT violate the loosening principle
- A false closing section between themes may initially suggest the group has ended, but retrospectively it is understood as the beginning of the next theme
