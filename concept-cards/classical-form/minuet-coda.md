# Quick Definition
A coda appended to the minuet form, appearing after the A' section and included within the repeat of the B and A' sections, serving to expand the form and provide cadential weight.

# Formal Definition
A coda may appear following the recapitulation (A' section) of a minuet form, usually when the recapitulation is modeled closely on the exposition and the B section has introduced subordinate-theme function that requires compensatory recapitulation. Unlike most other forms, in which the coda is entirely separate from the recapitulation, the coda in minuet form is more intimately linked to it, since it is included in the repetition of the B and A' sections (i.e., the coda precedes the double bar and repeat signs). For the most part, codas in minuet form display the same basic features and functions as codas in other forms. They provide cadential weight, expand the form, and help establish tonal stability in the home key.

# Construction/Recognition
- Located after the A' section but before the double bar and repeat signs
- Included in the repetition of B + A' sections
- May consist of new material or develop prior ideas
- Provides cadential reinforcement of the home key
- Appears when the A' section needs additional support to overcome the subordinate-key confirmation in an earlier section

# Musical Context
Codas are most likely to appear in minuet form when a nonmodulating exposition has been followed by a B section that confirms a subordinate key. In such cases, the A' section's simple restatement of the exposition may not provide sufficient home-key weight, and the coda supplies the needed cadential expansion.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in G, Op. 54/1, iii: Coda following the A' section, consisting of a new six-measure unit repeated identically, providing cadential weight via an expanded cadential progression

# Common Confusions
- The minuet coda is included within the repeat of the B + A' sections (before the double bar), unlike codas in other forms which typically follow the final section without being repeated.
- A closing section and a coda are distinct: a closing section follows a cadence within a section, while a coda is a separate formal unit requiring its own cadential closure.
