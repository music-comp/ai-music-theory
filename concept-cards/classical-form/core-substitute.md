# Quick Definition
A thematic unit in a development section that occupies the structural position of the core but is organized along different formal lines, such as a transition-like or subordinate-themelike unit.

# Core Definition
Many classical development sections are not organized by pre-core/core technique. In place of a core, a variety of phrase-structural options may be employed: (1) a pseudo-core (dramatic character without model-sequence technique); (2) a transition-like unit beginning in one key with initiating function, modulating, and closing with HC or dominant arrival; or (3) a subordinate-themelike unit whose harmonic goal is an authentic cadence in a development key, featuring loosening devices such as extended continuation, expanded cadential progression, and evaded/deceptive/abandoned cadences. Haydn generally constructs developments without a core, whereas Mozart and Beethoven omit the core only now and then. Some developments model the organization of an exposition: pre-core as main theme, transition-like unit, and subordinate-themelike unit.

# Prerequisites
- **Development section**: The formal context
- **Core technique**: The organizational method being substituted

# Key Properties
1. Occupies the structural position of the core without genuine model-sequence technique
2. Three main types: pseudo-core, transition-like unit, subordinate-themelike unit
3. **Transition-like**: begins with initiating function, modulates, closes with HC or dominant arrival
4. **Subordinate-themelike**: goals authentic cadence in development key; features loosening devices; may promise PAC but never achieve it
5. Model-sequence technique of limited scope may appear but not at core scale
6. Haydn frequently uses core substitutes; Mozart and Beethoven less often
7. Some developments model exposition organization (pre-core = MT, transition-like = TR, subordinate-themelike = ST)

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify a development passage in the core's expected position that lacks genuine model-sequence technique
- Determine which substitute type is present (pseudo-core, transition-like, subordinate-themelike)
- For transition-like: check for initiating function, modulation, and HC/dominant arrival ending
- For subordinate-themelike: check for authentic cadential goal, loosening devices, and possible PAC evasion
- Note whether the development as a whole models an exposition

# Context & Application
Core substitutes are especially characteristic of Haydn's developments. The transition-like core substitute resembles a transition in organization but appears within the development. The subordinate-themelike unit resembles a subordinate theme but with PAC in a minor-mode development key rather than the subordinate key. Some developments create an entire exposition-like organization using these substitute types.

# Examples
- Haydn, Symphony No. 102 in B-flat, ii, mm. 36-46 (ex. 10.17): transition-like unit with presentation and continuation; modulates back to home key; premature dominant arrival
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Hob. XVI:49, i (ex. 10.18): four-part development -- pre-core (tight-knit, like MT), transition-like unit (modulating, like TR), subordinate-themelike unit (loosened, like ST with abandoned cadence and PAC in development key), retransition

# Common Errors
- Assuming the absence of a core means the development lacks organization
- Confusing a transition-like core substitute with a transition-like pre-core (different structural position)
- Treating a subordinate-themelike unit as a genuine subordinate theme

# Common Confusions
- **No core does not mean no organization**: Core substitutes provide legitimate alternative organizational strategies
- **Transition-like substitute vs. transition-like pre-core**: They differ in structural position (core position vs. pre-core position)
- **Subordinate-themelike unit vs. subordinate theme**: The former operates in a development key and may never achieve PAC; the latter confirms the subordinate key with PAC
