# Quick Definition
A passage within the contrasting middle of a small ternary that modulates back to the home key after the B section has confirmed a subordinate key through authentic cadential closure.

# Core Definition
When the contrasting middle modulates to a subordinate key and confirms it with an authentic cadence, the composer may add a retransition to return to the home-key dominant for the recapitulation. This can range from a single chord (adding a seventh to the new "tonic" to convert it into the dominant of the home key) to a more extended passage featuring model-sequence technique leading to the home-key dominant. A modulating B section typically follows upon an exposition that has remained in the home key.

# Prerequisites
- **Contrasting middle**: The section containing the retransition
- **Subordinate key in small ternary**: The key from which the retransition departs

# Key Properties
1. Follows an authentic cadence in a subordinate key within the B section
2. Returns the music to the home-key dominant
3. Can be as minimal as a single chord (adding a seventh converts tonic of subordinate key to dominant of home key)
4. Can be an extended passage with model-sequence technique
5. Leads to home-key HC or dominant arrival
6. A modulating B section usually follows a nonmodulatory A section

# Construction / Recognition
1. Locate the cadential confirmation of the subordinate key within the B section
2. Identify the passage that moves from the confirmed key back toward the home-key dominant
3. Note the size: single chord, brief gesture, or extended phrase
4. Check for model-sequence technique in longer retransitions
5. The passage ends when the home-key dominant is reached, preparing A'

# Context & Application
Retransitions are needed when the B section confirms a subordinate key through cadential closure. The concept relates directly to the retransition function in sonata form (discussed in later chapters), where it serves the analogous purpose of returning from the development section to the home key for the recapitulation.

# Examples
- Haydn, Symphony No. 101 ("The Clock"), iv, mm. 17-20: model-sequence in mm. 17-18 leading to home-key HC at m. 20; recapitulation starts immediately thereafter
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in B-flat, K. 570, ii, mm. 17-20: model-sequence with fragmentation leading to HC

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Contrasting middle (the section in which the retransition appears)
- Subordinate key (the confirmed key from which the retransition departs)

## Enables
- Recapitulation (by restoring the home-key dominant)

## Related
- Standing on the dominant (a different means of preparing the recapitulation -- prolongs an already-present dominant rather than modulating back to it)
- Retransition in sonata form (the analogous function at a larger scale)

## Contrasts With
- Standing on the dominant: a retransition actively MODULATES back; a standing on the dominant simply PROLONGS an already-present dominant

# Common Errors
- Confusing a retransition with a postcadential standing on the dominant -- the retransition modulates, the standing on the dominant prolongs
- Failing to recognize a single-chord retransition (adding a seventh to the subordinate tonic)
- Assuming every passage leading to the dominant is a retransition -- only after cadential confirmation of a subordinate key

# Common Confusions
- A retransition actively MODULATES back; a standing on the dominant simply PROLONGS an already-present dominant
- A single chord can serve as an entire retransition
- The retransition is not always labeled as such in the score -- it is identified by its function
