# Quick Definition
The large-scale tonal design of a sonata movement, understood through two complementary models: the tonal-polarity model (two-key conflict and resolution) and the tour-of-keys model (systematic exploration of related keys).

# Formal Definition
Taken together, the tonal-polarity model and the tour-of-keys model provide a fairly complete picture of the classical sonata's large-scale tonal design. The tonal-polarity model emphasizes the oppositional nature of two primary keys (home key and subordinate key); the tour-of-keys model emphasizes the diversity of tonal expression arising throughout the movement. Neither model alone is sufficient: the polarity model does not account for the development's internal tonal plan, and the tour-of-keys model does not capture the dramatic tension between two primary keys.

# Construction/Recognition
- **Exposition**: home key (I) → subordinate key (V in major, III in minor)
- **Development**: explores minor-mode regions (VI, III, II in major; IV, V in minor); may confirm one or more development keys; ends on home-key dominant
- **Recapitulation**: home key throughout — subordinate theme transposed to I
- **Coda**: home key with possible brief tonicizations
- The subdominant may be emphasized in the recapitulation as a vestige of the tour-of-keys model

# Musical Context
The tonal plan is the backbone of sonata form. Understanding the tonal plan allows the analyst to predict general tonal tendencies (though not specific keys) for each section of the movement.

# Examples
- A typical major-mode sonata: I → V (exposition) → vi → iii → V/I (development) → I throughout (recapitulation)
- A typical minor-mode sonata: i → III (exposition) → iv → v → V/i (development) → i throughout (recapitulation)

# Common Confusions
- No single model explains the entire tonal plan — both tonal-polarity and tour-of-keys perspectives are needed
- The development is not "free" tonally; it follows the conventions of development key selection
- Subdominant emphasis in the recapitulation is not a failure of tonal adjustment — it is a legitimate stylistic feature
