# Quick Definition
The large-scale tonal design of a sonata movement, understood through two complementary models: the tonal-polarity model (two-key conflict and resolution) and the tour-of-keys model (systematic exploration of related keys).

# Core Definition
Taken together, the tonal-polarity model and the tour-of-keys model provide a fairly complete picture of the classical sonata's large-scale tonal design. The tonal-polarity model emphasizes the oppositional nature of two primary keys (home key and subordinate key); the tour-of-keys model emphasizes the diversity of tonal expression arising throughout the movement. Neither model alone is sufficient: the polarity model does not account for the development's internal tonal plan, and the tour-of-keys model does not capture the dramatic tension between two primary keys.

# Prerequisites
- **Sonata form**: The formal framework
- **Tonal-polarity model**: One of the two tonal perspectives
- **Tour-of-keys model**: The complementary tonal perspective

# Key Properties
1. Two complementary models are needed for a complete picture
2. Exposition: I to V (major) or i to III (minor)
3. Development: explores minor-mode regions; may confirm one or more keys by authentic cadence
4. Recapitulation: I throughout; subordinate theme transposed to home key
5. Coda: home key with possible brief tonicizations
6. Subdominant emphasis in recapitulation is a vestige of the tour-of-keys model
7. Polarity model explains the dramatic conflict; tour-of-keys model explains tonal diversity

# Construction / Recognition
- Map the primary keys of each section
- Exposition: identify home key and subordinate key
- Development: identify explored regions and any cadentially confirmed keys
- Recapitulation: confirm all material returns in the home key
- Note any subdominant emphasis as a tour-of-keys vestige

# Context & Application
The tonal plan is the backbone of sonata form. Understanding it allows prediction of general tonal tendencies for each section, though not specific keys. The development is not "free" tonally; it follows conventions of development key selection.

# Examples
- Typical major-mode: I to V (exposition) to vi/iii/ii (development) to I throughout (recapitulation)
- Typical minor-mode: i to III (exposition) to iv/v (development) to i throughout (recapitulation)

# Common Errors
- Treating the development as tonally "free" (it follows clear conventions)
- Relying on only one tonal model

# Common Confusions
- **Two models needed**: No single model explains the entire tonal plan
- **Subdominant in recapitulation**: Not a failure of tonal adjustment but a legitimate stylistic feature (tour-of-keys vestige)
- **Development not random**: The development follows conventions of key selection, not haphazard modulation
