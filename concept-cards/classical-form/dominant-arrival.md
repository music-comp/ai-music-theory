# Quick Definition
A noncadential articulation of dominant harmony at the end of a formal section, distinguished from a genuine half cadence by the absence of a proper cadential progression or by the instability of the dominant chord itself.

# Formal Definition
A dominant arrival occurs when the final dominant of a section cannot be construed as a genuine HC because: (1) the dominant is inverted, (2) it contains a dissonant seventh, (3) no independent harmonic progression leads to it, or (4) the dominant appears before the phrase-structural end (premature dominant arrival). The term distinguishes such noncadential situations from actual half cadences.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Final dominant is inverted (e.g., V^4/3) -- too unstable for cadential goal
2. Final dominant contains a dissonant seventh
3. No cadential progression leads to the dominant (entire section is a prolongation)
4. Dominant appears before phrase-structural end (premature dominant arrival)
5. Often marked by a fermata to emphasize ending quality

# Musical Context
Dominant arrivals occur most frequently in contrasting middle sections of small ternaries and at the end of transitions. The concept is essential for distinguishing cadential from noncadential endings in formal analysis.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in G Minor, Op. 49/1, ii, m. 12 (V^4/3 with fermata)
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 7, Op. 92, i, m. 81 (premature: harmonic goal precedes phrase-structural end)
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 2, Op. 36, i, m. 61 (transition: dominant in first inversion)

# Common Confusions
- A dominant arrival is NOT a half cadence -- cadences carry greater structural weight
- A fermata does not convert a noncadential dominant into a cadence
