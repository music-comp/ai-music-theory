# Quick Definition
A noncadential articulation of dominant harmony at the end of a formal section, distinguished from a genuine half cadence by the absence of a proper cadential progression, the instability of the dominant chord, or the timing of its arrival.

# Core Definition
A dominant arrival occurs when the final dominant of a section cannot be construed as a genuine half cadence. The term distinguishes such noncadential situations from actual half cadences. Unlike a half cadence, which requires a proper cadential progression culminating in a root-position dominant triad, a dominant arrival may involve: (1) a dominant that is inverted, (2) a dominant containing a dissonant seventh, (3) a dominant that appears without an independent harmonic progression leading to it, or (4) a dominant that appears before the phrase-structural end (premature dominant arrival). The concept is essential for formal analysis because a transition need not end with a cadence, and therefore many transition endings are classified as dominant arrivals rather than half cadences.

# Prerequisites
- **Half cadence**: The genuine cadential type from which dominant arrival is distinguished
- **Cadential progression**: The harmonic progression required for a genuine cadence
- **Dominant function**: The harmonic function articulated in both cases

# Key Properties
1. The final dominant is inverted (e.g., V4/3) -- too unstable for cadential goal
2. The final dominant contains a dissonant seventh
3. No independent cadential progression leads to the dominant
4. The dominant appears before the phrase-structural end (premature)
5. Often marked by a fermata to emphasize ending quality
6. A transition may end with a dominant arrival rather than a half cadence
7. A core in the development may conclude with a dominant arrival
8. May be followed by a standing on the dominant

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the final dominant of the section
- Check whether a cadential progression (with at least I-V or IV-V) leads to it
- Verify the dominant is in root position (first inversion disqualifies it as a HC)
- Verify the dominant is a triad without a seventh
- Check whether the harmonic arrival coincides with the phrase-structural end
- If any condition fails, the ending is a dominant arrival, not a half cadence

# Context & Application
Dominant arrivals occur most frequently in transitions and development cores. In transitions, Caplin explicitly states that a transition need not end with a cadence, making dominant arrival a perfectly normal ending. In development cores, the dominant may arrive through sequential processes rather than cadential progressions, yielding a dominant arrival. The concept is essential for the analyst who must distinguish between the greater structural weight of a half cadence and the lesser weight of a dominant arrival.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in G Minor, Op. 49/1, ii, m. 12: V4/3 with fermata (inverted and dissonant)
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 7, Op. 92, i, m. 81: premature dominant arrival -- harmonic goal precedes phrase-structural end
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 2, Op. 36, i, m. 61: transition ends with dominant in first inversion
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 4, Op. 60, i, m. 92: transition -- harmonic goal precedes melodic goal at m. 95
- Haydn, Symphony No. 102, ii, m. 41: premature dominant arrival in transition-like development unit

# Common Errors
- Calling every dominant ending a half cadence regardless of the progression
- Ignoring chord quality (inversion, seventh) when evaluating cadential status
- Treating dominant arrivals and half cadences as functionally identical

# Common Confusions
- **Dominant arrival vs. half cadence**: A dominant arrival is NOT a half cadence; cadences carry greater structural weight and require a proper cadential progression with root-position triad
- **Fermata function**: A fermata emphasizes the ending quality but does not upgrade a dominant arrival to a half cadence
- **Premature dominant arrival**: When the harmonic goal precedes the phrase-structural end, the noncongruence prevents cadential status even if the chord quality is correct
- **Postcadential standing**: Do not confuse the postcadential standing on the dominant (which follows a genuine HC) with the prolongation of a noncadential dominant arrival
