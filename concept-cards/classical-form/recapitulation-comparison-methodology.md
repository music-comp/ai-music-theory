# Quick Definition
The primary analytical method for the recapitulation: compare it bar by bar with the exposition, identify every change (structural or ornamental), and ask why each change was made.

# Core Definition
Because the recapitulation is largely modeled on the exposition, the methodology most appropriate for analyzing it is one of comparison. The analyst is encouraged to begin by comparing the recapitulation bar by bar with the exposition and identifying any changes that the recapitulation makes in relation to the earlier section. When making this comparison, it is useful to recognize two basic types of changes: structural and ornamental. The analyst is also encouraged to ask (and try to answer) why these changes are made. Structural changes are motivated primarily by fundamental differences in the formal functions of the recapitulation compared with the exposition. They may also be prompted by the content and organization of the development section. Ornamental changes are motivated by general aesthetic concerns for variety and by particular expressive values.

# Prerequisites
- **Recapitulation**: The section being analyzed
- **Sonata exposition**: The section used as comparison point
- **Structural vs. ornamental changes**: The two categories for classifying differences

# Key Properties
1. The analytical method is comparison, not independent analysis
2. Comparison should be bar by bar
3. Every change should be identified and classified as structural or ornamental
4. The analyst should ask WHY each change was made
5. Structural changes are motivated by different formal functions
6. Ornamental changes are motivated by variety and expression
7. Development content may also motivate structural changes
8. The recapitulation is defined by its DIFFERENCES from the exposition

# Construction / Recognition
Step-by-step:
1. Align the recapitulation with the exposition (score comparison)
2. Mark every point of divergence
3. Classify each change as structural or ornamental
4. For structural changes: identify the functional motivation
5. For ornamental changes: note aesthetic or expressive motivation
6. Check for development-influenced changes
7. Note the crux (point of tonal divergence)

# Context & Application
This methodology reflects a fundamental insight: the recapitulation is defined by its differences from the exposition. Using the same labels for both sections obscures their "significantly different formal functions." The bar-by-bar approach ensures that no change is overlooked, and the "why" question moves the analysis from description to interpretation.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i (ex. 14.1): systematic comparison reveals secondary development added, restatement deleted, final cadence eliminated
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in G, K. 283, i (ex. 14.5): comparison reveals presentation sequenced up a step, new continuation, compensation for lack of core in development

# Common Errors
- Analyzing the recapitulation independently without comparing to the exposition
- Noting changes without asking why they were made
- Overlooking ornamental changes as unimportant (they reveal expressive intentions)

# Common Confusions
- **Labels vs. functions**: Same labels, different functions -- the comparison reveals this
- **Causal vs. appropriateness**: Changes prompted by the development are not causally determined, just "appropriate"
