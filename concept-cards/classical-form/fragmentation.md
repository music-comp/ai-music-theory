# Quick Definition
A reduction in the size of the constituent units in a theme, typically from two-measure basic ideas to one-measure (or smaller) units, characteristic of continuation function.

# Core Definition
Fragmentation is "a reduction in the size of the units" (Ch. 1, p. 10). In the presentation phrase, the constituent units (the basic idea) are two measures long. At the beginning of the continuation phrase, the units are reduced by half to one measure. "The concept of fragmentation exclusively concerns the length of the musical units, not necessarily their motivic content" (Ch. 1, p. 11). Fragments may derive motivically from the basic idea (as in Op. 2/1, where motive "b" is detached) or bring "entirely different melodic material" (as in Op. 135, where the fragments have no motivic connection to the basic idea).

# Prerequisites
- **Basic idea** -- establishes the normative two-measure unit size
- **Continuation function** -- the formal context where fragmentation occurs

# Key Properties
1. Concerns unit length, not motivic content
2. Normative reduction: two-measure units to one-measure units
3. May involve further reduction to half-measure units
4. Fragments may or may not derive motivically from the basic idea
5. Repetition of fragments helps clarify their reduced size
6. Independent from liquidation (which concerns motivic content)
7. Fragmentation and liquidation frequently work together but are distinct processes

# Construction / Recognition
- Compare unit sizes: presentation units (2mm) vs. continuation units (1mm or less)
- Look for immediate repetition of the shorter units to confirm their boundaries
- Do not require motivic derivation from the basic idea

# Context & Application
Fragmentation is a primary characteristic of continuation function in sentences. It also appears in closing sections (where codettas fragment), contrasting middles, transitions, and development sections.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 1, p. 10, ex. 1.1): Beethoven, Op. 2/1, i, mm. 5-6: motive "b" is detached from the basic idea, creating one-measure fragments that derive motivically from the original.
**Example 2** (Ch. 1, p. 12, ex. 1.2): Beethoven, Op. 135, iii, mm. 7-8: one-measure fragments bring entirely different melodic material from the basic idea, demonstrating that "structural fragmentation can still be identified there despite the lack of motivic connection."

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Basic idea (establishes the two-measure norm from which units are reduced)
## Enables
- Continuation function (fragmentation is one of its most common traits)
- Closing sections (codettas fragment in sentence-like patterns)
## Related
- Harmonic acceleration (often accompanies fragmentation but is independent)
## Contrasts With
- Liquidation: fragmentation concerns unit length; liquidation concerns motivic content. They frequently coincide but are "different compositional processes" (Ch. 1, p. 11)

# Common Errors
- Requiring motivic derivation from the basic idea for fragmentation to occur
- Conflating fragmentation with liquidation

# Common Confusions
- Fragmentation is about LENGTH, liquidation is about CONTENT. A fragment may contain entirely new motives
- The immediate repetition of shorter units helps confirm their boundaries but is not strictly required
