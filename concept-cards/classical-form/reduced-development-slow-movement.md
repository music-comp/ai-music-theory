# Quick Definition
A significantly shortened development section in a slow-movement sonata, often lacking a full core and sometimes resembling a contrasting middle of a small ternary.

# Formal Definition
Reduction of the development is a primary technique for limiting the length of a slow-movement sonata. The composer forgoes a full-fledged core, although brief model-sequence activity may appear, and normally does not allow a development key to be cadentially confirmed. In extreme cases, the development may be so simple as to resemble a contrasting middle of a small ternary form. This technique is particularly favored by Haydn.

# Construction/Recognition
- The development section is noticeably shorter than the exposition
- No development key receives cadential confirmation
- A full-fledged core with extensive model-sequence technique is absent
- Brief sequential patterns may touch on several tonal regions without establishing any
- The final dominant may be so weakly articulated as to be barely perceptible

# Musical Context
This is one of the principal methods for maintaining proportional balance in slow-movement sonatas. Haydn particularly favors this approach.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Trio in A, Hob. XV:9, i, mm. 26-36: The development is less than one-third as long as the exposition. In eight measures, Haydn touches on three tonal regions (vi, IV, ii) via a descending-third sequential pattern.

# Common Confusions
- A reduced development should not be confused with the complete elimination of the development (sonata without development). A reduced development still maintains the three-part sonata structure.
