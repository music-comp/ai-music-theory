# Quick Definition
A significantly shortened development section in a slow-movement sonata, often lacking a full core and sometimes resembling a contrasting middle of a small ternary form.

# Core Definition
Reduction of the development is a primary technique for limiting the length of a slow-movement sonata, particularly favored by Haydn. The composer forgoes a full-fledged core (although brief model-sequence activity may appear) and normally does not allow a development key to be cadentially confirmed. In extreme cases, the development may be so simple as to resemble a contrasting middle of a small ternary form. Despite this resemblance, the overall three-part form remains a sonata.

# Prerequisites
- **Slow-movement sonata form**: The formal context requiring length management
- **Development section**: Understanding the standard development to recognize reduction
- **Core technique**: Knowing what a full core entails to recognize its absence

# Key Properties
1. Development section is noticeably shorter than the exposition (can be less than one-third)
2. No development key receives cadential confirmation
3. A full-fledged core with extensive model-sequence technique is absent
4. Brief sequential patterns may touch on several tonal regions without establishing any
5. The final dominant may be so weakly articulated as to be barely perceptible as an ending
6. Particularly favored by Haydn

# Construction / Recognition
- Compare the development's length to the exposition -- drastically shorter suggests reduction
- Check for the absence of a cadentially confirmed development key
- Note if model-sequence activity is brief rather than sustained
- Assess whether the section resembles a contrasting middle rather than a development
- Look for a weakly articulated final dominant

# Context & Application
In fast-movement sonata form by Haydn, the development tends to be roughly the same length as the exposition and recapitulation. The reduced development of slow movements contrasts sharply with this norm. This technique works alongside transition/subordinate-theme fusion and transition elimination to maintain proportional balance in slow movements.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Trio in A, Hob. XV:9, i, mm. 26-36: Development is less than one-third as long as the exposition; in eight measures, Haydn touches on three tonal regions (vi, IV, ii) via a descending-third sequential pattern; the final dominant at m. 34 is so weakly articulated it is unlikely to be perceived as an ending

# Common Errors
- Confusing a reduced development with a complete absence of development (sonata without development)
- Expecting the reduced development to follow the same proportional norms as fast movements
- Treating a weakly articulated final dominant as a formal anomaly rather than a standard practice

# Common Confusions
- **Reduced development vs. contrasting middle**: A reduced development may resemble a contrasting middle, but the overall form is still sonata, not small ternary
- **Reduced vs. absent**: A reduced development maintains the three-part sonata structure; sonata without development is a two-part form
