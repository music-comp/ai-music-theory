# Quick Definition
The classical concerto must balance two competing aesthetic demands: highlighting the soloist's individuality and virtuosity while ensuring the orchestra maintains an independent identity rather than merely accompanying.

# Core Definition
The classical concerto pits a single instrumentalist against a full orchestra. Because of this inherent inequality, compositional devices are developed to ensure both that the soloist can compete effectively and that the orchestra is not reduced to mere accompaniment. Techniques for highlighting the solo include: assigning musical ideas not previously sounded by the orchestra, permitting the solo part to have the principal modulatory action, and interpolating an unaccompanied cadenza. To ensure orchestral independence, the orchestra provides a textural frame for the opening and closing of the movement and appears by itself at least one other time (the subordinate-key ritornello) to assert its identity. This summary owes much to the writings of Tovey and Rosen (Caplin, pp. 695-696).

# Key Properties
1. Inherent inequality between soloist and orchestra must be managed
2. Solo is highlighted by new ideas, principal modulatory action, and cadenza
3. Orchestra maintains independence through textural frame and ritornellos
4. Orchestra appears alone at least three times: opening, subordinate-key, and closing ritornellos
5. The balance of forces drives the formal organization

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify passages where the solo introduces ideas not heard in the ritornello
2. Note that the solo exposition accomplishes the principal modulation
3. Locate the cadenza (usually in the closing ritornello)
4. Identify purely orchestral sections that assert the orchestra's identity

# Context & Application
These aesthetic goals explain the structural features of concerto form at every level. The opening ritornello stays in the home key so the soloist can claim the modulation. The subordinate-key ritornello exists so the orchestra can participate in the tonal conflict. The cadenza gives the soloist an unaccompanied display.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the orchestra in a concerto as mere accompaniment. **Correction**: The orchestra has an independent identity maintained through ritornellos and the textural frame.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Whether the concerto's structural features are arbitrary. **Clarification**: Every structural feature can be explained by the aesthetic need to balance soloist and orchestra.
