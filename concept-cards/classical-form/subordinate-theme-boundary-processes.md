# Quick Definition
To maintain rhythmic continuity among multiple subordinate themes, the end of one theme may elide with the beginning of the next -- the final tonic of the PAC functions simultaneously as the initial tonic of the subsequent theme, reinvigorating rhythmic activity after the cadence.

# Core Definition
To maintain rhythmic continuity among multiple subordinate themes, the end of one theme may elide with the beginning of the next. That is, the final tonic of the PAC functions simultaneously as the initial tonic of the subsequent theme. The elided cadence is particularly effective in helping to reinvigorate rhythmic activity for the next subordinate theme following the long note values of the pre-dominant and dominant harmonies of the ECP closing the prior theme. The alternative to elision is a clear break between themes, which may be appropriate when the next theme needs a fresh start (especially if it begins with new material in a different character).

# Prerequisites
- **Subordinate theme**: The formal units being connected
- **Multiple subordinate themes**: The context of a subordinate-theme group
- **Authentic cadence**: The PAC that may elide with the next theme

# Key Properties
1. PAC's final tonic = initial tonic of next theme (simultaneous ending and beginning)
2. Maintains rhythmic continuity across the theme boundary
3. Reinvigorates rhythmic activity after long cadential note values
4. Especially effective after ECP (which features long pre-dominant and dominant values)
5. The alternative is a clear break (appropriate for fresh starts)
6. The elided tonic belongs to both themes simultaneously

# Construction / Recognition
- Locate a PAC closing a subordinate theme
- Check whether new melodic-motivic material begins at the exact moment of cadential arrival
- If yes: structural elision between subordinate themes
- If no (clear break, rest, textural change): separate themes without elision
- Note the rhythmic effect: does the elision reinvigorate the momentum?

# Context & Application
Elision between subordinate themes is one of the key techniques for maintaining the forward momentum of the exposition's concluding section. After the long note values and gradual harmonic motion of an expanded cadential progression, the sudden onset of new thematic material at the moment of cadential resolution creates a burst of renewed energy. This is particularly important in subordinate-theme groups with multiple themes, where the exposition might otherwise lose momentum between successive PACs.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i, m. 92 (Ch. 12, Ex. 12.8): the elided cadence reinvigorates rhythmic activity for the second subordinate theme after the long note values of the ECP closing the first theme

# Common Errors
- Treating the elided tonic as belonging only to one theme (it belongs to both simultaneously)
- Missing the rhythmic reinvigoration effect of the elision
- Confusing elision between subordinate themes with elision between main theme and transition

# Common Confusions
- **Elision vs. separate themes**: Elision is appropriate for maintaining momentum; separate themes are appropriate when contrast is desired
- **Which theme owns the tonic?**: The tonic belongs to both themes simultaneously -- it is the final harmony of one and the initial harmony of the next
- **Not always present**: Not all subordinate-theme groups use elision; some have clear breaks between themes
