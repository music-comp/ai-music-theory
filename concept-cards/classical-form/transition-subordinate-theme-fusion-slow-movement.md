# Quick Definition
The merging of transition and subordinate-theme functions into a single thematic unit, occurring with particular frequency in slow-movement sonata forms as a compression technique.

# Formal Definition
Transition/subordinate-theme fusion in slow movements compresses the exposition by eliminating several phrase-functional elements: the half cadence and subsequent standing on the dominant that would normally end the transition, and the initiating unit that would normally begin the subordinate theme. The result is a single grouping unit in which the opening basic idea and model-sequence activity express transition function, while the cadential units express subordinate-theme function. The formal boundary between the two functions is recognizable only after the fact, lying "in the middle" without a clear sense of either ending (of the transition) or beginning (of the subordinate theme).

# Construction/Recognition
- Identify a single thematic unit following the main theme that both modulates and cadentially confirms the subordinate key
- The opening portion typically features model-sequence technique (transition function)
- The cadential portion confirms the new key with IAC and/or PAC (subordinate-theme function)
- No clear boundary exists between the end of transition function and the beginning of subordinate-theme function
- The half cadence that would normally end a transition is eliminated

# Musical Context
This technique appears more frequently in slow movements than in fast movements, where it also occurs. Mozart particularly favors this procedure. The fusion permits the interthematic functions of transition and subordinate theme to be traversed more rapidly.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in D, Op. 10/3, ii, mm. 9-17: The transition begins after the main theme closes at m. 9. Model-sequence technique starts in the second half of m. 11, modulating to C major. The new key is partially confirmed by an IAC at m. 15 and fully confirmed by a PAC two measures later. The middle of m. 13 can be identified as the formal boundary, but only after the fact.

# Common Confusions
- Students may try to locate a specific measure where the transition "ends" and the subordinate theme "begins." In a true fusion, no such decisive boundary exists; the formal boundary is apparent only retrospectively.
- Transition/subordinate-theme fusion also occurs in fast-movement sonatas, but it is especially characteristic of slow movements.
