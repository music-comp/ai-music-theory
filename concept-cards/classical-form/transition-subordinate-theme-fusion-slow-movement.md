# Quick Definition
The compression of transition and subordinate-theme functions into a single thematic unit in slow-movement sonata form, eliminating the half cadence ending the transition and the initiating unit of the subordinate theme.

# Core Definition
In slow-movement sonata form, the transition and subordinate theme are frequently fused into a single grouping unit. This technique, which occurs with greater frequency in slow movements than in fast movements, compresses the form by eliminating several phrase-functional elements: the half cadence (and subsequent standing on the dominant) for the transition and an initiating unit for the subordinate theme. The opening basic idea of the fused unit can be identified with transition function (especially model-sequence activity), and the cadential units with subordinate-theme function, but the formal boundary between the two is recognizable only after the fact, lying "in the middle" without a clear sense of either ending or beginning.

# Prerequisites
- **Transition/subordinate-theme fusion**: The general technique as it appears in fast movements (see ch. 13)
- **Slow-movement sonata form**: The formal context requiring compression
- **Model-sequence technique**: Often used in the transitional portion of the fused unit

# Key Properties
1. Eliminates the half cadence that would normally end the transition
2. Eliminates the standing on the dominant between transition and subordinate theme
3. Eliminates the initiating unit of the subordinate theme
4. The formal boundary lies "in the middle" and fails to project either an ending or a beginning
5. Model-sequence activity typically signals transition function
6. Cadential units confirming the subordinate key signal subordinate-theme function
7. More frequent in slow movements than fast movements

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for a single thematic unit following the main theme's close that both modulates and confirms the new key
- Identify model-sequence activity (transition function) flowing directly into cadential confirmation (subordinate-theme function)
- Note the absence of a half cadence or dominant arrival separating the two functions
- The boundary is recognizable only retrospectively

# Context & Application
This fusion technique is favored by Mozart and permits the interthematic functions to be traversed more rapidly than when they occupy distinct groups. It is the most common compression technique in slow-movement sonata form. The technique also appears in fast-movement sonatas (see exs. 13.8 and 13.9) but is especially characteristic of slow movements.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in D, Op. 10/3, ii, mm. 9-17: Transition begins after main theme closes (m. 9); model-sequence technique in second half of m. 11 modulates to C major; IAC at m. 15 partially confirms, PAC at m. 17 fully confirms the new key; the middle of m. 13 can be identified as the boundary only retrospectively

## Contrasts With

# Common Errors
- Searching for a precise measure where transition "ends" and subordinate theme "begins" in a fused unit
- Expecting a half cadence or standing on the dominant to separate the functions
- Confusing fusion with elimination (in fusion, transition function is present but merged)

# Common Confusions
- **Fusion vs. elimination**: Fusion retains elements of both functions in a single unit; elimination removes transition function entirely
- **Retrospective boundary**: The midpoint of a fused unit is identifiable only after the cadence in the new key has been heard, not in real time
