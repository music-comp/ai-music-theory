# Quick Definition
A transition whose final dominant gives no sense of being an ending harmony, resolving directly to tonic at the beginning of the subordinate theme with little or no rhythmic break, making the formal boundary difficult to determine.

# Core Definition
Now and then, the final dominant of a transition gives no sense of being an ending harmony whatsoever. The dominant does not appear to be the goal of the progression; it receives no emphasis; and it resolves directly to tonic at the beginning of the subordinate theme with little or no rhythmic break. With a minimal sense of functional end for the transition, it can be difficult sometimes to determine just where the subordinate theme begins. Usually, however, the composer provides some means of expressing a beginning, such as the appearance of a new basic idea in the context of a presentation phrase. The lack of a concluding function for a transition may produce a subordinate theme containing an internal half cadence and standing on the dominant -- the dominant emphasis missing from the end of the transition is thus regained in the subordinate theme itself.

# Prerequisites
- **Transition**: The formal unit that normally ends with a half cadence or dominant arrival
- **Half cadence / dominant arrival**: The concluding functions that are absent
- **Subordinate theme**: The following unit whose beginning is obscured

# Key Properties
1. The final dominant receives no emphasis -- it is not the goal of the progression
2. The dominant resolves directly to tonic at the subordinate theme's start
3. Little or no rhythmic break separates transition from subordinate theme
4. The boundary between transition and subordinate theme is difficult to locate
5. The composer usually provides a new basic idea to signal the subordinate theme's beginning
6. The subordinate theme often compensates with an internal half cadence and standing on the dominant
7. This is the extreme end of a continuum from clear HC to no concluding function

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for a dominant that passes without emphasis into the next section
- Identify the subordinate theme's beginning by the appearance of a new basic idea
- Check for root-position tonic prolongation in the subordinate key
- Note whether the subordinate theme contains an internal HC (compensating for the transition's lack)
- Compare with transitions that have a clear HC -- if no cadential articulation or dominant emphasis can be found, the transition lacks concluding function
- Harmonic criteria (root-position tonic in the new key) take priority over melodic/textural criteria

# Context & Application
This situation is at the extreme end of a continuum of transition endings, from the clearest half-cadential articulation to the complete absence of concluding function. It challenges the analyst to look beyond surface cues (melody, rhythm, texture) and attend to harmonic structure. The compensatory relationship between transition and subordinate theme is key: when the transition fails to emphasize the subordinate-key dominant, the subordinate theme often provides this emphasis through an internal half cadence.

# Examples
- Haydn, Symphony No. 90 in C, iv, mm. 28-35: transition modulates; dominant at m. 29 moves directly to tonic; main-theme basic idea returns in subordinate key as clear beginning marker
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C Minor, K. 457, i, mm. 19-22: transition repeats main-theme idea over subordinate-key dominant, but dominant receives no cadential articulation; subordinate theme begins at m. 23 with root-position tonic and presentation phrase; subsequent internal HC at m. 30 compensates

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Transition (the formal unit whose concluding function is absent)
- Half cadence (the expected ending that does not materialize)

## Enables
- Internal half cadence (the subordinate theme compensates for the missing dominant emphasis)
- Standing on the dominant (within the subordinate theme)

## Related
- Obscured transition/subordinate-theme boundary (a related analytical difficulty)
- Transition/subordinate-theme fusion (the more extreme case where no boundary exists)
- Omission of concluding function in transition (the same concept from a different analytical angle)

## Contrasts With
- Half cadence ending (the norm for transitions)
- Dominant arrival in transition (still provides a sense of ending, even if noncadential)

# Common Errors
- Assuming every transition must end with a cadence or dominant arrival
- Placing the subordinate-theme boundary at the moment of melodic change rather than harmonic change
- Failing to recognize the compensatory internal HC in the subordinate theme

# Common Confusions
- **Not incomplete**: The absence of a cadential conclusion does not mean the transition is "incomplete"; it simply makes the boundary less clear
- **Harmonic priority**: When harmonic and melodic/textural criteria conflict for locating the boundary, preference should normally be given to harmony
- **Vs. transition/ST fusion**: This situation differs from fusion, where no subordinate-theme initiation can be found even in retrospect
- **Compensatory relationship**: The subordinate theme's internal HC is not coincidental but compensates for the transition's missing dominant emphasis
