# Quick Definition
A situation in some expositions where the boundary between transition and subordinate theme is unclear, either because the transition lacks a concluding function or because the subordinate theme lacks an initiating function.

# Core Definition
In most late-18th-century sonata expositions, the boundary between transition and subordinate theme is demarcated clearly through form-functional devices (a distinct ending for the transition and a clear initiation for the subordinate theme) and through marked changes in melody, rhythm, and texture. In some cases, however, the music flows without interruption. If sufficient form-functional cues are present (a distinct HC and standing on the dominant followed by new material in a firm tonic prolongation), the boundary can still be identified. In a few works, such cues are considerably weaker or missing, and the listener only gradually becomes aware that the subordinate theme is under way. Two conditions promote this confusion: (1) the transition lacks a concluding function (no HC or dominant arrival), and (2) the subordinate theme lacks an initiating function and fuses with the transition. When harmonic and melodic/textural criteria conflict, "preference should normally be given to the powerful, form-defining role of harmony."

# Prerequisites
- **Transition**: The section whose ending may be unclear
- **Subordinate theme**: The section whose beginning may be unclear

# Key Properties
1. In most expositions, the boundary is clear through form-functional and surface cues
2. In some works, the music flows without interruption
3. Two conditions cause obscurity: transition lacking concluding function; subordinate theme lacking initiating function
4. Harmonic criteria (root-position tonic prolongation) take priority over melodic/textural criteria
5. The boundary may only become clear in retrospect
6. This is not a compositional flaw but a deliberate formal choice

# Construction / Recognition
- Check whether the transition has a clear HC or dominant arrival
- Check whether the subordinate theme has a clear initiating function
- If both are missing, the passage may be a transition/subordinate-theme fusion
- When criteria conflict, look for root-position tonic prolongation in the subordinate key
- The boundary often emerges only in retrospect

# Context & Application
These ambiguous boundaries challenge the analyst and reveal that interthematic functions are not always discretely articulated. They are more common in movements with long, through-composed textures. The analyst must look beyond surface cues to harmonic structure.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F Minor ("Appassionata"), Op. 57, iii (ex. 13.7): transition begins at m. 64 with main-theme basic idea; presentation repeated in dominant of C minor; root-position tonic at m. 72 marks the probable subordinate-theme beginning (retroactively), though the melodic change occurs at m. 76; harmonic considerations (root-position tonic prolongation at m. 72) take priority over melodic/textural considerations (new material at m. 76)

# Common Errors
- Treating an obscured boundary as a compositional flaw
- Placing the boundary at the melodic change rather than the harmonic change

# Common Confusions
- **Harmonic priority**: When harmonic and melodic/textural cues disagree, harmony takes precedence
- **Retrospective identification**: The boundary may only become clear in retrospect, not in real-time listening
- **Obscured vs. fused**: In obscured boundaries, a subordinate theme can be identified (in retrospect); in fusion, it cannot
- **Not a flaw**: The obscured boundary is a deliberate formal choice, not a compositional oversight
