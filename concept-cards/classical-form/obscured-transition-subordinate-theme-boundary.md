# Quick Definition
A situation in some expositions where the boundary between transition and subordinate theme is unclear, either because the transition lacks a concluding function or because the subordinate theme lacks an initiating function.

# Formal Definition
In most sonata expositions, the boundary between transition and subordinate theme is demarcated clearly through form-functional devices (a distinct ending for the transition and a clear initiation for the subordinate theme) and through marked changes in melody, rhythm, and texture. In some cases, however, the music flows without interruption, and the boundary becomes obscured. Two conditions promote this confusion: (1) the transition lacks a concluding function (no HC or dominant arrival), and (2) the subordinate theme lacks an initiating function and fuses with the transition.

# Construction/Recognition
- **Transition lacking concluding function**: no HC or dominant arrival ends the transition; if the subordinate theme begins with a strong initiation and tonic prolongation in the new key, the boundary can still be identified (in retrospect); harmonic criteria (root-position tonic prolongation) take priority over melodic/textural criteria
- **Transition/subordinate-theme fusion**: a single thematic unit that both modulates and confirms the subordinate key with PAC; no possible initiating function for a discrete subordinate theme
- When harmonic and melodic criteria conflict, preference should be given to the "powerful, form-defining role of harmony"

# Musical Context
These ambiguous boundaries challenge the analyst and reveal that the interthematic functions of the exposition are not always discretely articulated. They are more common in movements with long, through-composed textures.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F Minor ("Appassionata"), Op. 57, iii (ex. 13.7): transition begins at m. 64 with main-theme's basic idea; presentation repeated in dominant of subordinate key (C minor); tonic prolongation at m. 72 marks the probable beginning of the subordinate theme (retroactively), though the melodic change does not occur until m. 76

# Common Confusions
- When harmonic and melodic/textural cues point to different boundaries, harmony takes precedence
- The boundary may only become clear in retrospect, not in real-time listening
- An obscured boundary is not a compositional flaw — it is a deliberate formal choice
