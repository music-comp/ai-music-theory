# Quick Definition
An optional section in slow tempo preceding the exposition, functioning as "before-the-beginning" and combining solemnity with uncertainty and anticipation.

# Formal Definition
The exposition of a fast sonata movement is sometimes preceded by a slow introduction. This section functions as a "before-the-beginning" and is optional to the form. It resides on a hierarchical level comparable to the exposition, development, recapitulation, and coda — unlike a thematic introduction, which is comparable to a basic idea or codetta. A slow introduction must be distinguished from a thematic introduction that precedes a theme: they differ in hierarchical level and complexity of formal organization.

# Construction/Recognition
- Slow tempo contrasting with the fast exposition
- Typically invokes a solemn, serious tone combined with uncertainty and instability
- **Solemn qualities**: fanfare gestures, tutti textures, dotted rhythms, initial forte dynamic
- **Anticipatory qualities**: melodic/rhythmic/textural discontinuities, harmonic instability, minor modality, chromaticism
- Usually begins on tonic of the home key
- Harmonic goal is typically the dominant (HC or dominant arrival + standing on the dominant)
- Sometimes ends with PAC eliding with the exposition's beginning
- Loose in formal organization; may be bipartite, tripartite, or multi-part
- May hint at melodic ideas realized in the exposition (especially Beethoven)
- Some introductions are very short; others (Beethoven's 2nd and 7th Symphonies) are extensive

# Musical Context
The slow introduction is a hallmark of the classical symphony and appears in some sonatas and chamber works. It sets the movement's tone and builds anticipation for the allegro.

# Examples
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in B-flat, K. 454, i (ex. 13.10): majestic fanfare opening; compound basic idea creates presentation; continuation leads to IAC at m. 7; repeated phrase leads to PAC at m. 9; standing on dominant resembles a contrasting middle but becomes a truncated ternary
- Haydn, Symphony No. 104 ("London"), i (ex. 13.11): fortissimo fanfare with fermatas; modulates to III then back; highly deviated small binary (mm. 1-14) + closing section (mm. 14-17); final dominant seventh resolves as elided cadence at exposition start
- Beethoven, Violin Sonata in A Minor ("Kreutzer"), Op. 47, i (ex. 13.12): violin alone; modulates to III then IV; ends unusually on minor triad (IV) rather than dominant; this IV becomes the pre-dominant at the start of the main theme

# Common Confusions
- A slow introduction is not a first movement in slow tempo — it is a section within the movement
- It differs from a thematic introduction in hierarchical level and complexity
- Not all introductions end on the dominant — some end with PAC or even on a non-dominant harmony (e.g., IV)
- The introduction's melodic material may seem unrelated to the movement or may subtly anticipate later ideas
