# Quick Definition
An optional section in slow tempo preceding the exposition, functioning as "before-the-beginning" and combining solemnity with uncertainty and anticipation.

# Core Definition
The exposition of a fast sonata movement is sometimes preceded by a slow introduction. This section functions as a "before-the-beginning" and is optional to the form. It resides on a hierarchical level comparable to the exposition, development, recapitulation, and coda -- unlike a thematic introduction, which is comparable to a basic idea or codetta. A slow introduction typically invokes a solemn, serious tone, yet also arouses a strong sense of anticipation. It unites two seemingly incompatible affective worlds: the realm of the stately, heraldic, and solemn (slow tempo, fanfare gestures, tutti textures, dotted rhythms, forte dynamics) with the realm of the anticipatory, uncertain, and unstable (melodic/rhythmic/textural discontinuities, harmonic instability, minor modality, chromaticism). Slow introductions are the least predictable of all large-scale units in their internal organization.

# Key Properties
1. Optional to the form
2. Functions as "before-the-beginning"
3. Hierarchically comparable to exposition, development, recapitulation, coda
4. Differs from a thematic introduction in hierarchical level and complexity
5. Combines solemnity (slow tempo, fanfare, tutti, dotted rhythms, forte) with uncertainty (discontinuities, instability, minor, chromaticism)
6. Usually begins on tonic of the home key
7. Harmonic goal typically the dominant (HC or dominant arrival + standing on the dominant)
8. Some end with PAC eliding with the exposition's beginning
9. Usually loose in formal organization; may be bipartite, tripartite, or multi-part
10. May hint at ideas more fully realized later (especially Beethoven)
11. Can range from short to extensive (Beethoven's 2nd and 7th Symphonies)
12. Can itself open with a thematic introduction

# Construction / Recognition
- Slow tempo contrasting with the fast exposition
- Identify solemn qualities (fanfare gestures, dotted rhythms, forte dynamics)
- Identify anticipatory qualities (discontinuities, instability, chromaticism)
- Note the harmonic goal (usually dominant; sometimes PAC eliding with exposition)
- Determine the internal organization (bipartite, tripartite, etc.)
- Check for motivic anticipations of later material

# Context & Application
The slow introduction is a hallmark of the classical symphony and appears in some sonatas and chamber works. It sets the movement's tone and builds anticipation for the allegro. Many Haydn and Mozart introductions present material unrelated to the rest of the movement; in some late works and many by Beethoven, motivic anticipations appear. The internal formal organization is unpredictable -- some introductions are tight-knit (rarely), some bipartite, some tripartite, some have four or more distinct parts.

# Examples
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in B-flat, K. 454, i (ex. 13.10): majestic fanfare basic idea; compound basic idea repeated to form presentation; continuation leads to IAC at m. 7; repeated phrase closes with PAC at m. 9; standing on dominant resembles a contrasting middle; the implied small ternary becomes truncated when resolution to tonic at m. 14 initiates the exposition
- Haydn, Symphony No. 104 ("London"), i (ex. 13.11): fortissimo fanfare with fermatas; modulates to III then back; highly deviated small binary (mm. 1-14) + closing section (mm. 14-17); final dominant seventh resolves as elided cadence at exposition start; overall form: part 1 (mm. 1-7), part 2 (mm. 7-14), closing section (mm. 14-17)
- Beethoven, Violin Sonata in A Minor ("Kreutzer"), Op. 47, i (ex. 13.12): violin alone; modulates to III then IV (D minor); ends unusually on a minor triad (IV) rather than dominant; this IV becomes the pre-dominant at the start of the main theme; anticipates the main theme's opening motive (E-F) from m. 16 onward

# Common Errors
- Confusing a slow introduction with a first movement in slow tempo
- Assuming all introductions end on the dominant

# Common Confusions
- **Not a slow movement**: A slow introduction is a section within the movement, not a separate movement
- **Differs from thematic introduction**: Hierarchical level and complexity differ
- **Harmonic ending variety**: Not all introductions end on the dominant; some end with PAC or even a non-dominant harmony (e.g., IV in the Kreutzer)
- **Material relationship**: Introduction material may seem unrelated to the movement (Haydn, Mozart) or may subtly anticipate later ideas (Beethoven)
- **Least predictable**: Slow introductions are the least predictable in their internal organization of all large-scale classical formal units
