# Quick Definition
The restatement of the minuet proper following the trio, indicated by "Menuet da capo" rather than being written out -- a literal restatement continuing baroque suite practice, not a recapitulation.

# Core Definition
The da capo is the third part of minuet/trio form, continuing baroque practice by performing the minuet proper again after the trio. The restatement is not usually written out (unless the composer wishes to introduce ornamental variations). Instead, "Menuet da capo" or a variant is indicated in the score. The da capo represents a literal restatement, not a recapitulation in the sonata-form sense, since no tonal adjustment or structural alteration occurs. In a few cases, the da capo is followed by a coda to conclude the movement as a whole.

# Prerequisites
- **Minuet/trio form**: The full-movement context

# Key Properties
1. Indicated by "Menuet da capo" (or variant) in the score
2. Not usually written out
3. A literal restatement, not a recapitulation
4. No tonal adjustment or structural alteration
5. May be followed by a coda
6. Continues baroque suite convention

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for "Menuet da capo" or "D.C." indication in the score
- The minuet proper is performed again exactly as before
- Check for a coda following the restated minuet

# Context & Application
The da capo derives from baroque suite convention and is the standard concluding element of minuet/trio form. The literal restatement distinguishes it from a sonata recapitulation.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in C, Op. 20/2, iii: Truncated trio leads directly to "Menuetto D.C."

# Common Confusions
- **Da capo vs. recapitulation**: The da capo is a literal restatement; a recapitulation adjusts tonality and resolves tonal conflict
