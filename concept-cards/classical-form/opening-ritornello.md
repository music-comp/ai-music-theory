# Quick Definition
The first major section of concerto form, played by the orchestra alone, presenting the movement's principal melodic-motivic material in the home key while building expectations for the solo entrance.

# Formal Definition
The opening ritornello, played by the orchestra alone, initiates the textural frame for the concerto and brings much (but rarely all) of the fundamental melodic-motivic material of the movement. It distributes ideas as discrete thematic units suggesting the interthematic functions of a sonata exposition: a tight-knit main theme closing with a PAC, a loosely organized transition-like unit ending with a home-key HC, and one or more subordinate-theme-like units. However, the ritornello differs from a sonata exposition in two critical ways: (1) it almost always remains entirely in the home key (reserving modulation for the solo), and (2) the "subordinate themes" remain relatively tight knit, since formal loosening (sequences, cadential expansions) is reserved for the solo part's virtuosic display.

# Construction/Recognition
- Performed by orchestra alone
- Begins with a tight-knit main theme closing with PAC
- Followed by a transition-like unit (usually new material, not derived from main theme) ending with a home-key HC
- One or more subordinate-theme-like units in the home key
- Ends with a closing section of codettas
- Almost always remains entirely in the home key
- Subordinate themes are more tight knit than in a typical sonata exposition
- Transition begins with new material (unlike sonata transitions, which may begin with main-theme material)
- Establishes initial formal associations between ideas and their functions

# Musical Context
The opening ritornello allows the orchestra to express its own material and build expectations for the solo entrance. By remaining in the home key, it generates strong expectations for the solo to accomplish the establishment of the subordinate key. An additional role is forging initial associations between musical ideas and their form-functional expressions, which may be reassigned to different functions in subsequent sections.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i, mm. 50-76: Transition ends on dominant of home key; first subordinate theme (mm. 51-57) is a tight-knit sentence; second subordinate theme (mm. 58-72) is more extensive but still relatively compressed compared to symphonic standards

# Common Confusions
- The opening ritornello is not simply a sonata exposition played by the orchestra (the "double exposition" model). It lacks genuine tonal conflict and keeps its subordinate themes relatively tight knit.
- Students should not expect the subordinate themes of the opening ritornello to match the scope and looseness of subordinate themes in a symphonic sonata exposition.
- Occasionally, a unit following the transition begins briefly in the subordinate key but returns quickly to the home key without cadencing there. Only rarely does the ritornello genuinely confirm a subordinate key.
