# Quick Definition
An interior theme whose underlying small ternary (or binary) form is left structurally incomplete through the elimination, truncation, or distortion of its component sections -- more common than complete versions.

# Core Definition
Interior themes in large ternary form are frequently based on a small ternary (or small binary) that is left structurally incomplete. Four standard techniques of increasing distortion occur: (1) the A' section is initially closed but then reopened on repeat, often via deceptive cadence; (2) the A' section lacks cadential closure, with a premature dominant arrival; (3) the A' section is eliminated entirely, creating a truncated small ternary (A + B only); (4) both B and A' sections are eliminated, leaving only the modulating A section followed by a retransition. The final harmony is typically the dominant of the home key, motivating the main theme's return. Incomplete forms are more common than complete ones.

# Prerequisites
- **Interior theme (large ternary)**: The formal context being distorted
- **Small ternary**: The form being left incomplete

# Key Properties
1. Four standard techniques of incompletion, in order of increasing distortion
2. Incomplete forms are more common than complete ones
3. The dominant of the home key at the end signals the main theme's return
4. The degree of distortion varies on a continuum
5. Similar incomplete techniques appear in rondo interior themes

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the interior theme's underlying small ternary model
- Determine which component(s) have been eliminated or distorted
- Note whether the theme achieves cadential closure or ends on dominant
- Distinguish between a retransition (elides with preceding section) and a contrasting middle (independent section)

# Context & Application
Incomplete interior themes create formal instability that heightens the need for the main theme's return. The more extreme distortions can make it difficult to recognize the underlying small ternary model, but Caplin demonstrates that even the most distorted forms can be traced back to this model.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Trio in A-flat, Hob. XV:14, ii: A' closed in first ending, reopened by deceptive cadence in second ending (mm. 31-34)
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Hob. XVI:52, ii: A' gets "stuck" on the dominant -- premature dominant arrival (mm. 28-33)
- Haydn, String Quartet in B-flat, Op. 64/3, ii: Truncated small ternary, A + B only; exposition repeated, B section followed directly by main theme return (mm. 47-58)

## Contrasts With
- Complete interior themes that follow a standard small ternary plan

# Common Errors
- Treating incomplete forms as anomalies rather than the norm
- Expecting all interior themes to follow a complete small ternary plan
- Misidentifying a retransition as a contrasting middle

# Common Confusions
- **Norm vs. exception**: Incomplete forms are the standard practice, not anomalies; complete forms are the minority
- **Retransition vs. contrasting middle**: A retransition elides with the preceding section; a contrasting middle is independent

# Verification Notes
Caplin presents the four techniques in order of increasing distortion, with specific examples for each. He states explicitly that the boundary between contrasting middle and retransition can be "subtle" but is "experientially significant."
