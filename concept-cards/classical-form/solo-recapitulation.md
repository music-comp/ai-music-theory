# Quick Definition
The recapitulation in concerto form, which must reconcile elements from both the opening ritornello and the solo exposition into a single section, often reintroducing ritornello ideas not used in the solo exposition.

# Core Definition
The solo recapitulation fulfills the basic functions of a sonata recapitulation: projecting a large-scale return and resolving the tonal conflict of the exposition. However, because the concerto brings two different "expositions" (the opening ritornello and the solo exposition), the recapitulation has an additional function: elements of both must somehow be recapitulated in a single section. The recapitulation usually begins with main-theme material from the opening ritornello, since music from the very beginning of the movement most effectively projects large-scale return. It is organized differently from either earlier "exposition," frequently reintroducing ideas from the opening ritornello that were not used in the solo exposition, and thus significantly altering the formal placement of various passages.

# Construction / Recognition
- Projects large-scale return and resolves tonal conflict
- Usually begins with the opening ritornello's main theme (not the alternative main theme)
- Must reconcile material from both the opening ritornello and the solo exposition
- Often reintroduces ideas from the opening ritornello that were eliminated in the solo exposition
- The formal placement of ideas frequently differs from both earlier expositions
- The first subordinate theme from the ritornello is especially likely to return here
- Ends with a cadential trill and PAC, leading to the closing ritornello
- Alternative main themes, if used, may appear elsewhere (development, theme group) rather than at the start

# Context & Application
The solo recapitulation is the section where the concerto's unique "double exposition" structure most fully asserts itself. The composer must find a way to bring back material from both the ritornello and the solo exposition, creating a synthesis that is more than a simple transposition of either earlier section.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i: The ritornello's first subordinate theme (m. 51), eliminated in the solo exposition, returns at the start of the recapitulation's subordinate-theme group (m. 312), played by solo piano. The lyrical theme from the solo exposition (m. 153) returns at m. 328 as a second subordinate theme.

# Common Confusions
- The solo recapitulation is not simply a transposition of the solo exposition. It typically draws on material from both the opening ritornello and the solo exposition, creating a new formal arrangement.
- If an alternative main theme was used in the solo exposition, students should not expect it to begin the recapitulation. The recapitulation typically begins with the ritornello's main theme to project the strongest sense of return.
