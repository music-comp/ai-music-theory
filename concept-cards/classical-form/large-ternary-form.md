# Quick Definition
A full-movement tripartite form used almost exclusively in slow movements, consisting of a main theme (small ternary or binary), an interior theme (usually in the opposite mode), and a return of the main theme -- fundamentally different from the small ternary despite superficial similarities.

# Core Definition
The large ternary form is a three-part full-movement structure whose parts are: (1) a main theme that begins and ends in the home key with a PAC, usually constructed as a small ternary or small binary; (2) an interior theme that contrasts through a change in modality (typically to minore), residing in the home key but in the opposite mode; and (3) a return of the main theme, essentially restating the first part with possible ornamental changes. Unlike the small ternary, the functional labels of exposition, contrasting middle, and recapitulation do not apply: the first part always closes in the home key (never modulating to a subordinate key for its structural cadence), the second part frequently closes with tonic harmony (not dominant), and the third part requires no tonal adjustment. The form is used almost exclusively in slow movements, most often by Haydn.

# Prerequisites
- **Small ternary**: Understanding the form on which each main theme is typically built
- **Small binary**: Alternative form for the main theme
- **Interior theme**: The central contrasting element unique to this form

# Key Properties
1. First part: main theme in small ternary or small binary, always closes in home key with PAC
2. Second part: interior theme, usually in minore of the home key
3. Third part: return of main theme, no tonal adjustment needed
4. Interior theme never resides in the standard subordinate key (distinguishing it from a subordinate theme)
5. The form is often labeled with minore/maggiore designations
6. An optional coda may follow, often referring to interior-theme material
7. The third part is a "return" rather than a genuine "recapitulation"

# Construction / Recognition
- Check that the first part begins and ends in the home key with a PAC
- Identify the interior theme by its immediate shift to the opposite mode
- Confirm that the interior theme does not reside in the standard subordinate key
- Note that the third part follows the same plan as the first, often with ornamental embellishment
- Look for a coda that references interior-theme material

# Context & Application
This form is used almost exclusively in slow movements and appears most often in Haydn, with examples in Mozart and Beethoven. Ratz identifies the outer parts as main themes and considers the second part a subordinate theme, but Caplin challenges this view because the interior theme is not organized as a subordinate theme and does not reside in the subordinate key. The large ternary has a stronger relationship to minuet/trio form than to sonata form, since both feature outer sections modeled on the small ternary.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Trio in A-flat, Hob. XV:14, ii: Interior theme as small binary with reopened closure (mm. 17-34)
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Hob. XVI:52, ii: Interior theme with A' section lacking cadential closure (mm. 28-33)
- Haydn, String Quartet in B-flat, Op. 64/3, ii: Interior theme as truncated small ternary (A + B only)

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Small ternary form (the model for each main theme)
- Small binary form (alternative model for main theme)

## Enables
- Understanding of interior-theme concept (unique to this form and rondo)

## Related
- Minuet/trio form (analogous tripartite structure with outer sections based on small ternary)
- Sonata without development with truncated recapitulation (resembles large ternary)

## Contrasts With
- Small ternary (fundamentally different: first part may modulate; B section ends on dominant; recapitulation adjusts tonality)
- Sonata form (different formal functions, different tonal plan)

# Common Errors
- Applying the labels exposition/contrasting middle/recapitulation from small ternary
- Treating the interior theme as a subordinate theme
- Expecting the third part to make tonal adjustments

# Common Confusions
- **Large ternary vs. small ternary**: Fundamentally different forms. In small ternary, the A section may modulate; in large ternary, the first part always closes in home key. Small ternary B ends on dominant; interior theme often closes with tonic (of minore)
- **Return vs. recapitulation**: The third part is better described as a "return" since no tonal conflict needs resolution

# Verification Notes
Caplin explicitly distinguishes the large ternary from both the small ternary and the sonata, rejecting Ratz's "subordinate theme" label for the interior theme. The form is "used almost exclusively in slow movements."
