# Quick Definition
A passage at the beginning of the development (or pre-core) that begins immediately in a new key with dominant harmony, creating the character of an extended upbeat while simultaneously effecting a tonal change -- hence both "transitional" and "introductory."

# Core Definition
Sometimes the pre-core begins immediately in a new key (or region). In such cases, the opening harmony is either a dominant or a harmonic progression that leads to dominant harmony, and the music takes on the character of an extended upbeat to the start of the next unit (which may still be part of the pre-core or the beginning of the core). The formal function of this upbeat gesture is complex: it gives the impression of appearing "before the beginning" (introductory), yet its harmonic basis, with emphasis on dominant harmony of a new region, imparts a sense of transition function. The expression "transitional introduction" describes both aspects.

# Prerequisites
- **Development section**: The formal context containing the transitional introduction
- **Pre-core**: The larger unit to which the transitional introduction often belongs
- **Dominant function**: The harmonic basis of the passage

# Key Properties
1. Begins immediately in a new key or tonal region
2. Based on dominant harmony (or progression leading to dominant)
3. Creates a "before-the-beginning" character (introductory)
4. Effects a tonal change from the end of the exposition (transitional)
5. Functions as an extended upbeat to the next unit
6. May occur at the very start of the development or within the pre-core
7. Typically short and harmonically focused
8. Resolution of the dominant initiates the next formal unit

# Construction / Recognition
- Locate a passage at the start of the development that emphasizes dominant harmony of a new key
- Note the "upbeat" character and before-the-beginning quality
- Observe the tonal change from the subordinate key (end of exposition) to a development key
- The resolution of the dominant typically initiates the pre-core proper or the core
- Distinguish from a genuine transition (which is a larger unit) and from a thematic introduction (which prolongs tonic)

# Context & Application
Transitional introductions appear most commonly at the very beginning of the development but can also occur early in the coda (see Ch. 15). The passage links the end of the exposition to the new tonal region of the development. It may pick up motivic material from the exposition's closing section. The concept also appears in the coda, where a similar passage based on dominant harmony links the end of the recapitulation to the coda proper.

# Examples
- Mozart, String Quintet in E-flat, K. 614, i, mm. 87-89 (ex. 13.5): pre-core begins directly with dominant of C minor; resolves deceptively to initiate core
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in B-flat, H. 41, i, mm. 56-59 (ex. 13.16): opening motive from closing section, supported by bVI of subordinate key, shifts to dominant of E-flat
- Mozart, Symphony No. 36 ("Linz"), K. 425, i, mm. 123-24 (ex. 13.6): prolonged dominant effects modulation to development key A minor
- Beethoven, String Quartet in F, Op. 18/1, i, mm. 274-81 (ex. 15.1): transitional introduction at the start of the coda, with fermatas and ascending scale patterns

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Pre-core (the transitional introduction is often the opening portion)
- Dominant function (harmonic basis)

## Enables
- Core technique (the transitional introduction prepares the core's entry)
- Pre-core proper (when the transitional introduction is followed by more pre-core material)

## Related
- Introduction function (shares before-the-beginning quality)
- Development key (the key toward which the passage moves)

## Contrasts With
- Core technique (the transitional introduction is harmonically static; the core is sequentially dynamic)
- Standing on the dominant (the transitional introduction precedes structural closure; the standing follows it)

# Common Errors
- Confusing a transitional introduction with the start of the core (the model-sequence process has not yet begun)
- Treating it as a genuine transition (it is too short and lacks the full formal apparatus of a transition)
- Missing it entirely because it does not sound like a "beginning"

# Common Confusions
- **Transitional introduction vs. thematic introduction**: A thematic introduction prolongs tonic and has no melodic profile; a transitional introduction emphasizes dominant of a new key
- **Part of pre-core or separate?**: The transitional introduction belongs to the pre-core by definition (it precedes the core), but it may feel distinct from the pre-core proper
- **In the coda**: The same technique appears at the start of codas, linking the recapitulation's closing material to the coda proper

# Verification Notes
The term "transitional introduction" is Caplin's own coinage, explicitly described as resolving a "terminological dilemma." Examples from Mozart, Haydn, and Beethoven are cited. The concept also applies to codas (Ch. 15).
