# Quick Definition
The tendency for recapitulations to fill in or remove rhythmic breaks (rests, fermatas) that appeared in the exposition, fostering greater momentum following the emotionally charged development.

# Formal Definition
The exposition often contains prominent interruptions in rhythmic activity — rests and fermatas — particularly in the main theme and at the end of the transition. The recapitulation often removes such discontinuities in order to foster greater rhythmic momentum. Following an emotionally charged development section, the flow of events may seem too inhibited if the music is continually checked by stops and starts.

# Construction/Recognition
- Rests or fermatas from the exposition are filled in or eliminated
- Gaps in the presentation may be filled with imitative entries
- Compression of two-part transitions eliminates pauses between parts
- Results in more continuous musical flow
- Most noticeable in the main theme and transition areas

# Musical Context
This alteration reflects the different expressive needs of the recapitulation. At the beginning of the movement, rhythmic breaks can create a sense of hesitancy appropriate to an "opening." After the development, continuous momentum is more appropriate to the recapitulation's function as resolution and return.

# Examples
- Mozart, Clarinet Trio in E-flat, K. 498, i (ex. 11.16): rhythmic gaps in every even-numbered measure of the exposition's presentation are filled in the recapitulation with newly added imitations of the basic idea
- Haydn, Piano Trio in E-flat, Hob. XV:30, i (ex. 11.7): compression of two-part transition eliminates a pronounced pause

# Common Confusions
- Greater continuity is not simply about adding notes — it reflects the changed expressive function of the recapitulation
- Not all rhythmic breaks are eliminated; the principle applies to prominent discontinuities that would impede momentum
