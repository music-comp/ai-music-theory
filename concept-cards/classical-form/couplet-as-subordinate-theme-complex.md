# Quick Definition
A rondo couplet organized as a complete subordinate-theme complex (transition, subordinate theme, closing section, retransition), making the refrain and couplet together resemble a sonata exposition.

# Core Definition
When a rondo couplet is organized as a subordinate-theme complex, the end of the refrain is followed by a transition (modulating or nonmodulating) and one or more subordinate themes, then a closing section leading directly into a retransition. This option, preferred by Mozart, makes the first refrain and couplet constitute a sonata exposition. However, the establishment and confirmation of the subordinate key in a rondo are often less emphatic than in a sonata: the rondo frequently eliminates a distinct transition or fuses it with the subordinate theme; the subordinate theme may lack PAC closure (especially in Beethoven); and the subordinate theme can be relatively compressed and simple. The tonal conflict of home and subordinate keys is tempered compared with sonata form.

# Construction / Recognition
- Contains standard interthematic functions: transition, subordinate theme(s), closing section, retransition
- The transition may be eliminated or fused with the subordinate theme
- Subordinate-theme confirmation may be weaker than in sonata form
- The subordinate theme may be relatively compressed (unlike the expansive subordinate themes of sonata form)
- A retransition follows, leading back to the refrain; this retransition is typically longer and more elaborate than at the end of a sonata exposition
- The retransition often anticipates the refrain's basic idea

# Context & Application
This organizational type is favored by Mozart for couplet 1 in both five-part rondos and sonata-rondos. It creates a sonata-like tonal conflict within the rondo framework while maintaining the characteristic emphasis on the refrain's return.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, iii, mm. 9-22: Compressed subordinate-theme complex with short transition, brief subordinate theme (based on main theme) with PAC at m. 16, and four-measure retransition

# Common Confusions
- A couplet organized as a subordinate-theme complex does not make the movement a sonata. The rondo form is distinguished by the regular return of the refrain and the presence of additional couplets.
- The subordinate-theme complex in a rondo is typically more compressed than in a sonata exposition. Students should not expect the same degree of formal elaboration.
