# Quick Definition
The second primary key of the sonata exposition — typically V in major-mode movements and III in minor-mode movements — confirmed by a PAC in the subordinate theme and creating "structural dissonance" with the home key.

# Formal Definition
The subordinate key is the second tonal region established in the exposition, creating a structural dissonance in relation to the home key that drives the movement's large-scale tonal plan. It must ultimately be confirmed by an authentic cadence. The subordinate key is primarily associated with the subordinate theme and the last part of the transition. It may appear briefly in the main theme (in small ternary or binary main themes) and is frequently extended into the beginning of the development. Once abandoned after the development's opening, the subordinate key rarely returns.

# Construction/Recognition
- In major: typically V (dominant region)
- In minor: typically III (relative major)
- Must be confirmed by PAC in the subordinate theme
- May also receive HC confirmation (internal to the subordinate theme or at the end of transition)
- Represents the "tonal antagonist" of the home key
- Resolved in the recapitulation when the subordinate theme is transposed to the home key

# Musical Context
The establishment of the subordinate key is what distinguishes a sonata exposition from a simple ternary A section. The tonal-polarity model views this key as creating a dramatic conflict that is intensified in the development and resolved in the recapitulation.

# Examples
- In any standard major-mode sonata: V is the subordinate key
- In any standard minor-mode sonata: III is the subordinate key
- Three-key expositions (discussed in Chapter 13) add an intermediate key before the final subordinate key

# Common Confusions
- The subordinate key is not simply "a different key" — it is the specific key that creates the fundamental tonal opposition
- In minor-mode movements, the sense of "structural dissonance" is less palpable because III shares the same basic scale
- The subordinate key is confirmed by cadence, not merely tonicized
