# Quick Definition
The establishment of a contrasting tonal region within minuet form, which may occur in the A section, the B section, or both, and whose location critically determines the formal organization of the minuet as a whole.

# Core Definition
In the small ternary theme type, whether the exposition modulates is of relatively minor concern because the form normally serves as a main theme within a larger context. In minuet form, however, the appearance of a subordinate key may represent the principal tonal contrast of the minuet, making its presence or absence of particular importance. If the A section modulates, the subordinate key is established there, freeing the B section to explore other regions. If the entire minuet remains nonmodulatory, the companion minuet (trio or minuet proper) usually expresses subordinate-key function. Rarely is a subordinate key not established somewhere in the complete minuet/trio form (Caplin, pp. 635-636).

- **Minuet form** -- The formal context

# Key Properties
1. May represent the principal tonal contrast of the entire minuet
2. Can be established in the A section, B section, or both
3. Its location determines the formal organization of the rest of the minuet
4. If A section is nonmodulating, B section usually provides the subordinate key
5. Rarely absent from the complete minuet/trio form
6. More significant than in a small ternary

# Construction / Recognition
1. Check the A section for modulation to a subordinate key
2. If A is nonmodulating, examine the B section for transition and subordinate-theme functions
3. If neither section provides a subordinate key, check the companion minuet (trio)
4. Assess the rhetorical weight of the subordinate-key confirmation

# Context & Application
The question of where the subordinate key is established is the central analytical concern in minuet form. Unlike sonata form, where the subordinate key is always established in the exposition, minuet form offers flexibility in distributing this tonal function across different sections.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 632): Haydn, String Quartet in G, Op. 54, No. 1, iii -- nonmodulating A section; subordinate key confirmed in B section.
**Example 2** (p. 638): Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 7, iii, mm. 1-24 -- modulating A section with subordinate-key confirmation.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the subordinate key as unimportant in a minuet (as in a small ternary). **Correction**: In minuet form, subordinate-key establishment may represent the principal tonal conflict.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Expecting the subordinate key always to appear in the A section. **Clarification**: It may appear in the A section, B section, or even only in the companion minuet.
