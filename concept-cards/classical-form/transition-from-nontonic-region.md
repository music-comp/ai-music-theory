# Quick Definition
A transition that begins directly in a nontonic region of the home key (most often VI), used when the preceding main-theme area has strongly emphasized tonic harmony through closing sections or multiple themes.

# Formal Definition
Most transitions begin with the home-key tonic to provide a firm footing for later tonal destabilization. In some contexts, however, harmonic stability at the opening is unnecessary or redundant. When the main-theme area strongly emphasizes tonic harmony -- through the use of a closing section, multiple main themes, or both -- the transition may begin directly in a nontonic region to advance the process of tonal destabilization. The nontonic region used in the majority of cases is the submediant (VI). This harmony is particularly effective because it creates immediate modal contrast, can easily function as a pivot to the new key, and continues to function as a tonic substitute, making it appropriate for projecting a sense of formal initiation.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Transition begins directly on VI (or other nontonic harmony) of the home key
2. Typically follows an extended main-theme area with strong tonic emphasis
3. The preceding PAC, closing section, or multiple main themes make opening tonic redundant
4. VI creates immediate modal contrast (major to minor inflection or vice versa)
5. VI serves as both a tonic substitute (for initiation) and a pivot (for modulation)
6. A distinct change in dynamic (often to forte) and character (often Sturm und Drang) marks the new beginning
7. The shift from small codetta units to a larger compound basic idea signals the transition's start

# Musical Context
Beginning from a nontonic region is a less common but dramatically effective way to open a transition. It is particularly appropriate when the main-theme area has provided extensive tonic emphasis (through closing sections, codettas, or multiple main themes), making further tonic at the start of the transition harmonically redundant. The submediant's dual function as tonic substitute and modulatory pivot makes it ideally suited for this purpose. The abrupt harmonic shift, combined with changes in dynamics and character, creates a powerful moment of formal articulation.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in F, K. 332/300k, i, mm. 23-40 (main-theme group consists of two themes, each ending with PAC, plus two codettas; transition starts with VI at m. 23; abrupt shift to forte dynamic and Sturm und Drang style; change from 1-measure codettas to 4-measure compound basic idea)

# Common Confusions
- Beginning from VI does not mean the transition has modulated -- VI still functions within the home key as a tonic substitute
- The nontonic opening is appropriate only when the preceding main-theme area has strongly emphasized tonic; it would be unusual without such preparation
- The submediant is chosen not merely for contrast but for its functional versatility as both a substitute tonic and a pivot chord
- Do not confuse with a modal shift (which changes the mode of the home key); the move to VI introduces a different tonal color while remaining in the home key's orbit
