# Quick Definition
A transition that begins directly in a nontonic region of the home key (most often VI), used when the preceding main-theme area has strongly emphasized tonic harmony through closing sections or multiple themes.

# Core Definition
Most transitions begin with the home-key tonic to provide a firm footing for later tonal destabilization. In some contexts, however, harmonic stability at the opening is unnecessary or redundant. When the main-theme area strongly emphasizes tonic harmony -- through a closing section, multiple main themes, or both -- the transition may begin directly in a nontonic region. The submediant (VI) is used in the majority of cases because it creates immediate modal contrast, can easily function as a pivot to the new key, and continues to function as a tonic substitute, making it appropriate for projecting formal initiation.

# Prerequisites
- **Transition**: The broader formal function
- **Main-theme group**: Must provide strong tonic emphasis to justify nontonic opening

# Key Properties
1. Transition begins on VI (or other nontonic harmony) of the home key
2. Follows an extended main-theme area with strong tonic emphasis
3. Preceding PAC, closing section, or multiple themes make opening tonic redundant
4. VI creates immediate modal contrast
5. VI serves as both tonic substitute (initiation) and pivot (modulation)
6. Distinct change in dynamic (often to forte) and character (often Sturm und Drang)
7. Shift from small codetta units to larger compound basic idea signals the start

# Construction / Recognition
- Check whether the main-theme area provides extensive tonic emphasis
- Identify the nontonic harmony at the transition's start (usually VI)
- Note the dramatic change in dynamics and character
- Observe the grouping-structure change from small units to a larger idea
- Confirm that VI functions within the home key's orbit (not a modulation)

# Context & Application
Beginning from a nontonic region is less common but dramatically effective. It advances the process of tonal destabilization by starting from a position already away from the tonic. The submediant's dual function makes it ideally suited for this purpose. The abrupt harmonic shift, combined with forte dynamics and Sturm und Drang style, creates a powerful formal articulation.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in F, K. 332/300k, i, mm. 23-40: main-theme group has two themes each with PAC plus two codettas; transition starts with VI at m. 23; abrupt shift to forte and Sturm und Drang; change from 1-measure codettas to 4-measure compound basic idea

# Common Errors
- Treating the move to VI as a modulation (VI still functions within the home key)
- Using this analysis when the main-theme area has not provided extensive tonic emphasis
- Confusing the nontonic opening with a modal shift

# Common Confusions
- **VI vs. modulation**: Beginning from VI does not mean the transition has modulated -- VI functions as a tonic substitute within the home key
- **Contextual appropriateness**: The nontonic opening is appropriate only when the main-theme area has strongly emphasized tonic
- **VI's dual function**: VI is chosen for its functional versatility as both substitute tonic and pivot chord
