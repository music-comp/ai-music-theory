# Quick Definition
The comparative aesthetic effects of different cadential extension techniques, ranging from the least effective (repeated PAC) to the most dramatic (evaded cadence), each serving the classical goal of dramatizing cadential confirmation.

# Core Definition
Caplin establishes a hierarchy of aesthetic effects among cadential extension techniques. A repeated PAC is the least effective and least used means, creating cadential redundancy. An IAC followed by PAC is somewhat redundant but motivates the latter through melodic incompleteness. A deceptive cadence provides greater motivation because lack of harmonic closure is more palpable than melodic incompleteness alone. The evaded cadence is unquestionably the most dramatic: imminent closure is thwarted at the last moment, arousing powerful expectation. The abandoned cadence is less dramatic but promotes significant formal loosening as the music "wanders off." This hierarchy relates directly to the different formal needs of main themes (moderate techniques) versus subordinate themes (dramatic techniques).

# Prerequisites
- **Cadential extension**: The broader technique encompassing all variants
- **Individual cadential failure types**: IAC, DC, evaded, abandoned

# Key Properties
1. Hierarchy from least to most dramatic:
   - Repeated PAC (cadential redundancy; least effective)
   - IAC then PAC (melodic incompleteness motivates repetition)
   - DC then PAC (harmonic non-closure more palpable than melodic)
   - Evaded cadence (most dramatic; imminent closure thwarted; no goal reached)
   - Abandoned cadence (less dramatic but significant formal loosening)
2. Main themes favor moderate techniques (IAC, DC)
3. Subordinate themes favor dramatic techniques (evaded cadence)
4. The choice of technique reflects the passage's formal function

# Construction / Recognition
- Assess which cadential extension technique is used
- Determine whether the technique is appropriate to the formal context (main theme vs. subordinate theme)
- Note the aesthetic effect: partial closure (IAC, DC), no closure (evaded), lost direction (abandoned)
- Consider the dramatic intensity in relation to the theme's cadential needs

# Context & Application
The aesthetic hierarchy directly relates to the different formal needs of main themes versus subordinate themes. Main themes do not require dramatic cadential articulation and thus typically use moderate techniques (IAC, DC). Subordinate themes must dramatically confirm the subordinate key and thus favor the evaded cadence for its unparalleled dramatic effect. The choice of technique is not arbitrary but reflects the formal function of the passage.

# Common Errors
- Treating the hierarchy as rigid (composers may use any technique in any context)
- Confusing dramatic intensity with structural importance
- Assuming abandoned cadence is always less effective than evaded cadence (they serve different purposes)

# Common Confusions
- **Dramatic effect vs. structural importance**: All techniques serve valid formal purposes; the hierarchy describes aesthetic impact, not value
- **Main theme vs. subordinate theme conventions**: Main themes' preference for IAC/DC and subordinate themes' preference for evaded cadence reflects different formal roles
- **Abandoned cadence's unique role**: Less dramatic than evaded but creates MORE formal loosening because the music loses cadential direction entirely
