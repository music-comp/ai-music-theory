# Quick Definition
The internal structure of slow introductions, which are usually loose in organization and may be bipartite, tripartite, or contain four or more parts, combining solemn and uncertain qualities.

# Formal Definition
Of all the large-scale units of classical form, slow introductions are the least predictable in their organization, making it difficult to generalize about internal phrase structure and formal functionality. They typically begin with tonic harmony and generally remain in the home key throughout, with prominent tonicizations and minor-mode emphasis. The harmonic goal is usually the dominant (HC or dominant arrival + standing on the dominant), though some close with PAC eliding with the exposition. Many introductions are bipartite or tripartite (as articulated by cadences), and some larger ones contain four or more distinct parts. Tight-knit theme-types appear now and then, but loose organization is the norm.

# Construction/Recognition
- Begins on home-key tonic; stays mostly in the home key
- Minor modality emphasized (even in major-key movements)
- Prominent tonicizations and chromaticism
- Harmonic goal usually V (HC or dominant arrival)
- Sometimes ends with PAC eliding with exposition
- May contain its own thematic introduction
- Bipartite, tripartite, or multi-part internal structure
- Later introductions (Beethoven) may hint at motivic ideas from the allegro

# Musical Context
The slow introduction's lack of formal predictability reflects its function as "before-the-beginning" — a liminal space between silence and the formal beginning of the exposition. Its combination of solemnity and uncertainty creates the unique affective world that prepares the listener for the movement's drama.

# Examples
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in B-flat, K. 454, i (ex. 13.10): compound sentence structure leading to truncated small ternary
- Haydn, Symphony No. 104 ("London"), i (ex. 13.11): highly deviated small binary (mm. 1-14) + closing section (mm. 14-17)
- Beethoven, Violin Sonata in A Minor ("Kreutzer"), Op. 47, i (ex. 13.12): sentential structure modulating to III then IV

# Common Confusions
- Slow introductions are not governed by the same formal expectations as expositions; their organization is deliberately unpredictable
- What appears as a small ternary may become truncated when the expected A' section becomes the exposition
- Internal cadences in the introduction do not carry the same structural weight as cadences in the exposition
