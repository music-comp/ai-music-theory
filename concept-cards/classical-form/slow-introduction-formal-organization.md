# Quick Definition
The internal structure of slow introductions, which are the least predictable of all classical formal units -- usually loose in organization, bipartite or tripartite, beginning on tonic and typically ending on dominant.

# Core Definition
Of all the large-scale units of classical form, slow introductions are the least predictable in their organization, and so it is difficult to generalize about their internal phrase structure and formal functionality. They typically begin with tonic harmony of the home key and generally remain in that key throughout. Prominent tonicizations and an emphasis on minor modality (in major-key movements) are common. The harmonic goal is usually the dominant, articulated by a HC or dominant arrival followed by standing on the dominant. Some introductions close with PAC eliding with the exposition. Many are bipartite or tripartite (as articulated by cadences), and some larger ones contain four or more distinct parts. Tight-knit theme-types appear now and then, but loose organization is the norm. Many Haydn and Mozart introductions present material unrelated to the rest of the movement; in some late works and many by Beethoven, the slow introduction hints at ideas more fully realized later.

# Key Properties
1. The least predictable of all classical large-scale formal units
2. Usually begins on home-key tonic
3. Generally remains in the home key with prominent tonicizations
4. Minor modality emphasized (even in major-key movements)
5. Harmonic goal usually dominant (HC or dominant arrival)
6. Some end with PAC eliding with the exposition
7. Some end on unusual harmonies (e.g., IV in the "Kreutzer")
8. Usually loose in organization
9. Bipartite, tripartite, or multi-part internal structure
10. May contain its own thematic introduction
11. May hint at later melodic ideas (especially Beethoven)
12. Haydn and Mozart often present unrelated material

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the harmonic goal (usually dominant)
- Determine the internal partitioning (bipartite, tripartite, etc.)
- Note prominent tonicizations and minor-mode emphasis
- Check for motivic anticipations of later material
- Note any unusual ending harmonies

# Context & Application
The lack of formal predictability reflects the introduction's function as "before-the-beginning" -- a liminal space between silence and the formal beginning of the exposition. Internal cadences do not carry the same structural weight as cadences in the exposition. What appears as a small ternary may become truncated when the expected A' section becomes the exposition instead.

# Examples
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in B-flat, K. 454, i (ex. 13.10): compound sentence structure; standing on dominant resembles contrasting middle; truncated small ternary when resolution to tonic at m. 14 initiates the exposition
- Haydn, Symphony No. 104 ("London"), i (ex. 13.11): highly deviated small binary (mm. 1-14) + closing section (mm. 14-17); parts elided (unusual for binary); closing section has stronger closure than part 2
- Beethoven, Violin Sonata in A Minor ("Kreutzer"), Op. 47, i (ex. 13.12): sentential structure modulating to III then IV (D minor); ends unusually on IV, which becomes the pre-dominant at the start of the main theme; anticipates the main theme's opening motive (E-F)

# Common Errors
- Expecting introductions to follow the same formal rules as expositions
- Assuming all introductions end on the dominant

# Common Confusions
- **Not governed by exposition rules**: Internal cadences do not carry the same structural weight
- **Truncated ternary**: An implied small ternary may be truncated when the expected A' becomes the exposition
- **Material relationship**: Some introductions hint at later material (especially Beethoven); many do not (Haydn, Mozart)
- **Unusual endings**: Not all introductions end on the dominant; some end with PAC or even non-dominant harmony
