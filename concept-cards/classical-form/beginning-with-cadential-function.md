# Quick Definition
A subordinate theme that begins directly with an expanded cadential progression, projecting cadential function from its very start despite the absence of any preceding material that the cadence would bring to a close -- a particularly strong form of formal dissonance creating a highly loosened expression.

# Core Definition
A subordinate theme occasionally begins directly with an expanded cadential progression and thus projects a sense of cadential function, despite the lack of any previous material that the cadence would bring to a close. When the opening tonic of the ECP is in first inversion (I6), the passage cannot be identified as a conventional initiating function. The expanded I6 at the theme's opening possesses a degree of harmonic stability and thus projects some sense of structural beginning. Nonetheless, the grouping structure more resembles a continuation than either a presentation or compound basic idea. In such cases, a single functional label -- cadential -- can be assigned to the theme as a whole.

# Prerequisites
- **Cadential function**: The function being expressed at the theme's opening
- **Subordinate theme**: The formal context
- **Expanded cadential progression**: The harmonic means
- **Intrinsic vs. contextual functionality**: The theoretical framework

# Key Properties
1. Theme begins with expanded cadential progression (ECP)
2. Opening harmony is typically I6 (first inversion tonic), not root-position I
3. I6 provides some sense of stability/beginning but is not a conventional initiation
4. The grouping structure resembles continuation rather than presentation
5. A single label -- cadential -- may apply to the entire theme
6. Formal dissonance: concluding function in contextually initiating position
7. The ECP progresses through pre-dominant and dominant to cadential closure
8. May relate to main-theme design if that also begins with cadential function

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for I6 at the opening of the subordinate theme
- Check whether the harmonic progression constitutes an ECP from the very start
- Note the absence of tonic prolongation in root position (which would suggest presentation)
- Verify that the grouping resembles continuation (not presentation or CBI)
- Track the ECP through to its cadential resolution

# Context & Application
Beginning with cadential function is relatively rare and creates the strongest formal dissonance among the omission-of-initiation techniques. It works best when there is a compositional rationale, such as when the main theme employs a similar procedure. The resulting subordinate theme is essentially one large cadential gesture, confirming the subordinate key through a single, extended progression.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in B minor, Op. 64/2, i (Ch. 12, Ex. 12.7): first subordinate theme is supported by a single ECP from its start; I6 is prolonged by conventional neighboring harmonies (mm. 36-39), then further expanded before cadential closure at m. 45
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 31/3, i, mm. 46-53 (Ch. 12, Ex. 12.12): subordinate theme starts with I6, progresses quickly to IV two measures later; the entire theme is supported by an ECP, with a deceptive cadence at m. 52 before final closure

# Common Errors
- Labeling I6 as a presentation because it opens the theme
- Missing the ECP structure because it begins at the theme's very opening
- Treating the theme as having separate presentation and cadential sections when it is really all cadential

# Common Confusions
- **I6 vs. root-position I**: Root-position I supports presentation; I6 at a theme's start more likely signals cadential function
- **Stability vs. initiation**: I6 provides some stability, but the overall progression is cadential, not prolongational
- **Rarity**: This technique is less common than beginning with continuation and represents a stronger formal dissonance
