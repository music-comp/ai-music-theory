# Quick Definition
The process of modifying the harmonic-tonal content of exposition material in the recapitulation so that the subordinate theme remains in the home key instead of modulating to the subordinate key.

# Core Definition
If the original transition is modulatory, the one in the recapitulation must be tonally adjusted to remain in the home key. The adjustment can be accomplished by any number of harmonic and phrase-structural means and can occur at any place in the transition. Frequently, the subdominant region is tonicized, promoting (as a pre-dominant) a logical succession to the dominant to end the transition. The subordinate theme is also tonally adjusted -- transposed into the home key, sometimes requiring register changes and altered cadential progressions. If the original transition is nonmodulating, tonal adjustment may not be necessary in that section. Modulating subordinate themes require additional, more complex adjustment.

# Prerequisites
- **Recapitulation**: The formal context requiring tonal adjustment
- **Modulating transition**: The section most likely requiring adjustment
- **Subordinate theme**: The section that must end up in the home key

# Key Properties
1. The essential technical procedure of the recapitulation
2. Can be accomplished by any number of harmonic and phrase-structural means
3. Can occur at any point in the transition (early, middle, or late crux)
4. Subdominant tonicization frequently facilitates the adjustment (IV as pre-dominant to V)
5. Subordinate theme transposed to home key with register adjustments as needed
6. Nonmodulating transitions may not require adjustment
7. Modulating subordinate themes require more complex reorganization
8. The crux marks the specific point where the tonal path diverges from the exposition

# Construction / Recognition
- **In the transition**: Identify where modulatory passages are replaced with home-key progressions
- **In the subordinate theme**: Identify the transposition to the home key and any register changes
- **Point of adjustment (crux)**: Locate the specific moment where the recapitulation's tonal path diverges
- Note any tonicization of the subdominant as pre-dominant
- For modulating subordinate themes, trace the harmonic reorganization needed to remain in or return to the home key

# Context & Application
Tonal adjustment is the fundamental requirement of the recapitulation. Every sonata recapitulation must resolve the tonal conflict of the exposition by keeping the subordinate theme in the home key. The manner of adjustment varies greatly between works and constitutes a significant arena for compositional creativity.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, i (ex. 11.10): subordinate theme transposed to C major; melody initially down a fifth, then up a fourth to maintain register
- Haydn, Piano Trio in E-flat, Hob. XV:30, i (ex. 11.7): two-part transition compressed into one-part nonmodulating structure, eliminating redundant modulating passages
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62 (ex. 11.14): modulating subordinate theme requires complex harmonic reorganization; Beethoven reorganizes harmonies to arrive on home-key dominant
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 31/3, ii (ex. 11.13): subordinate theme begins "off tonic" in bVII region, eventually "modulates" back to tonic

# Common Errors
- Treating tonal adjustment as simple transposition down a fifth
- Assuming the point of adjustment is fixed at a standard location

# Common Confusions
- **Not simple transposition**: Tonal adjustment involves creative recomposition of harmonic progressions, not mechanical transposition
- **Variable crux location**: The point of adjustment may occur at different places in different movements
- **Register changes**: Practical necessities resulting from transposition, not necessarily expressive choices (though they may have expressive consequences)
- **Modulating subordinate themes**: These require the most complex adjustments, sometimes beginning off-tonic
