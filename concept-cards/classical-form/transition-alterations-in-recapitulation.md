# Quick Definition
The transition is the section most likely to be altered in the recapitulation, with changes including tonal adjustment, deletions and compressions, additional model-sequence technique, and emphasis on flat regions.

# Formal Definition
The transition is the section of the recapitulation most likely to be altered in relation to the exposition. If the original is modulatory, the recapitulation's transition must be tonally adjusted to remain in the home key. In addition to tonal adjustment, three other regular alterations occur: (1) deletions and compressions of material, especially from the beginning where tonic prolongation is no longer needed; (2) additional model-sequence technique (secondary development); and (3) emphasis on flat regions (IV, bVI, bIII, Neapolitan).

# Construction/Recognition
- **Tonal adjustment**: modulatory passages rewritten to stay in or return to the home key; subdominant often tonicized as pre-dominant
- **Deletions and compressions**: tonic-prolongation passages from the beginning are dispensable; two-part transitions compressed into one
- **Secondary development**: new model-sequence passages extending or replacing the original continuation
- **Flat-region emphasis**: best opportunity for harmonic contrast in the recapitulation; prepares for "sharp-side" return at subordinate theme
- If originally nonmodulating, the transition may retain its structure

# Musical Context
Since the subordinate theme will confirm the home key in the recapitulation, the transition no longer needs to modulate to a new key. But it still functions to provide harmonic contrast and bridge the gap between the main and subordinate themes. Flat-region emphasis replaces the sharp-side modulation of the exposition.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 7, i (ex. 11.4): enormous tonic-pedal passage deleted; new extended continuation with secondary development
- Haydn, Piano Trio in E-flat, Hob. XV:30, i (ex. 11.7): two-part transition compressed into single unit; mode change initiates new passage
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C Minor, K. 457, i (ex. 11.6): canonic variation of basic idea, quick move to bIII, Neapolitan as expanded pre-dominant

# Common Confusions
- The transition is not simply shortened in the recapitulation — it may be shortened in some areas but expanded in others (through secondary development)
- Nonmodulating transitions may remain unchanged; alterations are primarily motivated by the need for tonal adjustment
- Deletions target tonic-prolongation material at the start (no longer needed), not the ending (still needed for the dominant goal)
