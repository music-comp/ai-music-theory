# Quick Definition
The opening passage of a development section that precedes and sets up the core, typically more relaxed and quiet than the dramatic core that follows.

# Formal Definition
A development section does not normally start with a core, as the dramatic character projected by a core usually requires it to be set up by material of lesser emotional intensity. Ratz refers to this passage as an "introduction" (Einleitung), but Caplin uses the neutral term "pre-core" instead. The pre-core typically begins with tonic of the subordinate key, retaining the harmony from the end of the exposition.

# Construction/Recognition
- Generally more relaxed, hesitant, and anticipatory than the core
- Dynamic level tends to be soft; rhythmic motion frequently discontinuous
- "The calm before the storm"
- Begins with material from: (1) the main theme's basic idea, (2) closing section material, or (3) new material (especially Mozart)
- May be organized as: a complete tight-knit theme, a transition-like unit, an incomplete thematic unit, or multiple units
- Often begins in the subordinate key and modulates to a development key

# Musical Context
The pre-core establishes continuity from the end of the exposition into the development. Pre-core/core technique is pervasive in Mozart and Beethoven.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in G, Op. 14/2, i (ex. 10.11): two-part pre-core
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Hob. XVI:49, i (ex. 10.12): transition-like pre-core with polyphonic texture
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in B-flat, K. 333, i (ex. 10.13): tight-knit sentential pre-core
- Mozart, String Quintet in E-flat, K. 614, i (ex. 10.6): incomplete pre-core as transitional introduction

# Common Confusions
- The pre-core is not an "introduction" in the strict form-functional sense
- Not all pre-cores begin with main-theme material
- A pre-core that modulates and ends on a dominant resembles a transition but differs in its formal position
