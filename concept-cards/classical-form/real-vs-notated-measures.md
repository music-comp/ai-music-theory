# Quick Definition
The distinction between measures as printed in the score (notated) and the actual perceived measure lengths based on harmonic rhythm and hypermeter (real measures), which is essential for applying Caplin's normative measure counts.

# Core Definition
Notated measures are the bar lines in the score. Real measures reflect the actual perceived unit of metric organization based on harmonic rhythm, melodic patterning, and hypermetric grouping. Two notated measures may function as a single real measure (common in fast tempos), while a single notated measure may contain two real measures (common in slow tempos). The normative eight-measure sentence or period refers to real measures, not necessarily notated ones.

# Prerequisites
- **Grouping structure** -- understanding how time spans are organized hierarchically

# Key Properties
1. Normative measure counts for formal types (8-measure sentence, 8-measure period) refer to real measures
2. In fast 3/4 (minuets, scherzos), two notated measures often equal one real measure
3. In slow movements, one notated measure may contain two real measures
4. The rate of harmonic change is a primary indicator of real measure length
5. Melodic patterning and hypermetric grouping also contribute

# Construction / Recognition
- Compare the rate of harmonic change to the notated bar lines
- If harmonies change every two bars in fast tempo, each pair = one real measure
- If two distinct harmonies occupy a single bar in slow tempo, each bar = two real measures
- Apply normative counts to real measures, not notated ones

# Context & Application
This distinction is critical for applying Caplin's formal categories. A minuet notated in sixteen measures of 3/4 may actually be an eight-real-measure sentence if the harmonic rhythm moves in two-bar units. A slow movement in 4/4 with two harmonies per bar may have themes that are eight real measures long within four notated measures.

# Examples
**Example 1**: A minuet in 3/4 where harmonies change every two bars: each pair of notated measures = one real measure; sixteen notated measures = an eight-real-measure theme.
**Example 2**: A slow movement in 4/4 with two harmonies per bar: each notated measure = two real measures; four notated measures = an eight-real-measure theme.

# Common Errors
- Counting notated measures literally and becoming confused when a theme has "too many" or "too few" measures for its formal type

# Common Confusions
- The solution to apparent measure-count discrepancies is to identify real measures based on harmonic rhythm, not to force the music into the notated bar-line count
- This distinction is especially important in minuet/scherzo movements and slow movements
