# Quick Definition
Weak cadential closure at the end of an antecedent phrase is achieved by either a half cadence (HC) or an imperfect authentic cadence (IAC). The HC is weaker because it leaves both harmonic and melodic processes open; the IAC leaves only the melodic process open (on ^3). A PAC cannot close an antecedent because it achieves complete closure.

# Formal Definition
Essential to antecedent function is the presence of a weak cadence that effects partial closure of the phrase. When defining antecedent function, both the HC and the IAC can be considered weak because each leaves unclosed some harmonic or melodic process. The HC, with its combination of harmonic and melodic incompleteness, is decidedly weaker than the IAC, which results in melodic incompleteness alone (the melody remains on ^3). The vast majority of antecedent phrases end with a HC, no doubt to magnify the sense of cadential differentiation. A PAC cannot be used to close an antecedent phrase since this strong cadence achieves complete harmonic and melodic closure.

# Construction/Recognition
1. **Half cadence (HC)**: harmonic goal is the dominant; both harmonic and melodic processes remain open — decidedly weaker
2. **Imperfect authentic cadence (IAC)**: harmonic goal is tonic but melody remains on ^3 (or rarely ^5); only melodic incompleteness
3. PAC is excluded from antecedent closure
4. HC is far more common as antecedent cadence than IAC
5. "Stop" (rhythmic cessation) and "end" (structural closure) are independent — a cadence may occur without a rhythmic stop, and a rhythmic stop may occur without a cadence

# Musical Context
Weak cadential closure is the mechanism that creates the antecedent's sense of incompleteness, which in turn motivates the consequent's repetition and stronger closure. The weaker the antecedent's cadence, the stronger the drive toward the consequent. This is why HC is preferred — it maximizes the sense of openness and the need for resolution.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Trio in C, Hob. XV:27, iii, m. 4: HC
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in B-flat, K. 281/189f, i, m. 4: IAC (less common)
- Haydn, Piano Trio in E-flat, Hob. XV:30, iii, m. 7: HC with continuous rhythmic motion — phrase "ends" but does not "stop"
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 311/284c, ii, m. 3: rhythmic stop in middle of phrase that is not a cadence (most unstable moment)

# Common Confusions
- A rhythmic stop is not the same as a cadence — rhythmic motion can halt at noncadential points, and cadences can occur with continuous rhythmic motion
- An apparent PAC at the end of m. 4 with rhythmic continuity into m. 5 does not mean the antecedent elides with the consequent — careful harmonic analysis usually reveals the cadence is actually a HC
- In ornamented melodies, determining whether the final note is on ^1 (PAC) or ^3 (IAC) can be ambiguous — consider the broader formal context
