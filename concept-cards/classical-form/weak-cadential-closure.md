# Quick Definition
The partial cadential closure required at the end of an antecedent phrase, achieved by either a half cadence (harmonically and melodically incomplete) or an imperfect authentic cadence (melodically incomplete alone), which creates the demand for a consequent with stronger closure.

# Core Definition
Essential to antecedent function is the presence of a weak cadence that effects partial closure of the phrase. Both the HC and IAC can be considered "weak" because each leaves unclosed some harmonic or melodic process. The HC, with its combination of harmonic and melodic incompleteness, is decidedly weaker than the IAC, which results in melodic incompleteness alone (the melody remains on the third scale-degree). The vast majority of antecedent phrases end with an HC, no doubt to magnify the sense of cadential differentiation. The PAC cannot close an antecedent because this strong cadence achieves complete harmonic and melodic closure, eliminating the structural need for a consequent.

# Prerequisites
- Half Cadence (most common weak closure)
- Authentic Cadence (IAC as alternative weak closure; PAC as the strong closure it contrasts with)

# Key Properties
- HC: harmonically AND melodically incomplete (weakest)
- IAC: harmonically complete but melodically incomplete on the third degree (less weak)
- PAC: harmonically AND melodically complete (strong; cannot close an antecedent)
- The overwhelming majority of antecedents end with HC
- Cadential differentiation: weak (ant.) -> strong (cons.) defines the period
- "Stop" (rhythmic cessation) and "end" (structural closure) are independent phenomena

# Construction / Recognition
1. At the end of the antecedent phrase, identify the cadence type
2. HC: phrase ends on dominant harmony
3. IAC: phrase ends with tonic in the bass but the melody on the third degree
4. PAC: phrase ends with tonic in bass AND melody on tonic -- this CANNOT close an antecedent
5. Confirm that the cadential type creates a demand for further closure
6. In ornamented melodies, determining whether the final note is on scale-degree 1 (PAC) or 3 (IAC) can be ambiguous

# Context & Application
Weak cadential closure is the defining feature that distinguishes an antecedent from a complete self-standing phrase. Without weak closure, there is no structural demand for a consequent and hence no period. The hierarchy of cadential strength (HC < IAC < PAC) governs the relationship between antecedent and consequent throughout the repertory.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Trio in C, Hob. XV:27, iii, m. 4: HC closing the antecedent
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in B-flat, K. 281/189f, i, m. 4: IAC closing the antecedent (less common)
- Haydn, Piano Trio in E-flat, Hob. XV:30, iii, m. 7: HC with continuous rhythmic motion -- phrase "ends" but does not "stop"
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 311/284c, ii, m. 3: rhythmic stop in middle of phrase that is not a cadence (most unstable moment)

# Common Errors
- Allowing a PAC to close an antecedent; it would eliminate the period structure
- Treating all dominant arrivals as half cadences; a true HC requires a cadential progression
- Confusing rhythmic cessation with cadential closure

# Common Confusions
- **HC vs. dominant arrival**: a half cadence requires a genuine cadential progression to the dominant; a dominant arrival (as in some B sections) is noncadential
- **Weak closure vs. no closure**: weak closure is still closure--it effects partial formal ending. No closure (as in a compound basic idea) means no cadential progression at all
- **Melodic ambiguity**: in ornamented melodies, the final note may appear to be on scale-degree 1 (PAC) when the more fundamental melodic note is on 3 (IAC); broader formal context helps resolve the ambiguity
