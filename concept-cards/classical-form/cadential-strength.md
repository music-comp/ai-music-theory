# Quick Definition
The hierarchy of cadential closure (HC < IAC < PAC) that governs formal relationships, especially the antecedent-consequent pairing of the period, where the principle of cadential differentiation requires a weaker cadence to precede a stronger one.

# Core Definition
Cadential strength is measured by the degree of harmonic-melodic closure achieved within a key. The HC is weakest (harmonically and melodically incomplete), the IAC intermediate (harmonically complete but melodically open on the third degree), and the PAC strongest (both harmonically and melodically complete). The principle of **cadential differentiation** requires that the antecedent's weak cadence be followed by the consequent's stronger cadence. When the consequent modulates, cadential strength is still assessed by harmonic-melodic closure within the key--a subordinate-key PAC is "stronger" than a home-key HC, even though the modulation creates greater tonal openness at a higher structural level.

# Prerequisites
- Half Cadence (weakest cadence type)
- Authentic Cadence (IAC and PAC)

# Key Properties
- Hierarchy: HC < IAC < PAC
- HC: harmonically and melodically incomplete
- IAC: harmonically complete, melodically incomplete (melody on third degree)
- PAC: harmonically and melodically complete
- Cadential differentiation: weak -> strong defines the period
- Cadential strength is assessed within a key, independent of tonal distance from the home key
- Two types of pitch closure: (1) harmonic-melodic closure within a key; (2) tonal closure in relation to home key

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the cadence type at the end of each phrase
2. Compare: is the second cadence stronger than the first?
3. For modulating consequents: assess strength by harmonic-melodic closure in the new key
4. Tonal openness (distance from home key) is a separate dimension from cadential strength

# Context & Application
Cadential differentiation is the defining principle of the period. It also operates at larger structural levels: the antecedent-consequent relationship can appear between parts of a small binary, between parts of a sonata without development, and between large-scale formal units.

# Examples
- Any standard period: antecedent (HC) -> consequent (PAC) = cadential differentiation
- A modulating period: antecedent (HC in home key) -> consequent (PAC in dominant) = the PAC is still "stronger" despite the modulation

# Common Errors
- Treating a subordinate-key PAC as "weaker" because it is not in the home key
- Confusing cadential strength (within a key) with tonal closure (relation to home key)

# Common Confusions
- **Cadential strength vs. tonal closure**: cadential strength concerns harmonic-melodic completeness within a key; tonal closure concerns the relationship of keys to the home key. A modulating consequent is cadentially strong but tonally open.
