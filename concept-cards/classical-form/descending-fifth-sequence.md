# Quick Definition
The most commonly used sequential progression, in which chord roots are organized into a series of descending fifths (or ascending fourths), creating the "circle of fifths" pattern with the strongest harmonic-functional expression of any sequence type.

# Formal Definition
The descending-fifth sequential progression features chords whose roots are organized into a series of descending fifths (or ascending fourths). This "circle-of-fifths" progression can be varied through chord inversion, chromatic alteration, and added dissonances. Compared to other sequential categories, the descending-fifth pattern features the strongest harmonic-functional expression, since the root motion of a descending fifth lies at the basis of every dominant-to-tonic progression. This functional relation is implied, by analogy, at each link in the sequential chain (e.g., VI-II or II-V), even when the "dominant" does not actually contain the leading-tone of the "tonic."

# Construction/Recognition
- Root motion: consistently descending fifths (or ascending fourths)
- Basic pattern: I-IV-VII-III-VI-II-V-I (or any portion)
- Each link implies a local dominant-to-tonic relationship by analogy
- Can be varied by: chord inversion, chromaticism, added sevenths, altered chords
- Normally concludes with tonic harmony as the conventional ending point
- Despite local functional implications, the overall direction weakens the harmonic-tonal environment

# Musical Context
The descending-fifth sequence is characteristic of continuation function, where its harmonic mobility creates forward motion toward the cadential goal. It also appears prominently in development sections and transitions. Despite being the most "functional" sequence, it still weakens tonal clarity because each link temporarily implies a different "tonic."

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 135, iii, mm. 7-9: roots progress F-Bb-Eb-Ab-Db in a continuation phrase
- Basic form: ex. 2.11a-b; varied forms with inversions and chromaticism: ex. 2.11c-g

# Common Confusions
- Despite each link implying a dominant-tonic relationship, the overall progression weakens tonal stability rather than confirming a key. The direction remains in doubt until the sequence concludes.
- The descending-fifth sequence is the most common and most functionally charged sequence, but it is still fundamentally different from a cadential progression.
