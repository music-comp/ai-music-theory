# Quick Definition
A lead-in is a brief melodic idea that links the end of one phrase to the beginning of the next, generating rhythmic continuity without creating formal elision. It can be removed without disrupting the formal organization.

# Core Definition
"A *lead-in* (a very useful term, introduced by William Rothstein) consists of a brief melodic idea that links the end of the antecedent with the beginning of the consequent" (Ch. 3, p. 86). "The lead-in functions largely to generate rhythmic continuity and flow from one phrase to the next" (Ch. 3, p. 86). Crucially, "a lead-in could easily be eliminated without upsetting the melodic, harmonic, and formal organization of the phrases" (Ch. 3, p. 86). Lead-ins are "not confined to periods; they may be used in other formal contexts as well" (Ch. 3, p. 86).

# Prerequisites
- **Antecedent** -- the typical phrase before a lead-in in a period
- **Consequent** -- the typical phrase after a lead-in in a period
- **Period** -- the primary formal context for lead-ins

# Key Properties
1. Brief melodic idea connecting two phrases
2. Generates rhythmic continuity and flow
3. Belongs to neither the preceding cadence nor the following phrase beginning
4. Can be removed without disrupting formal structure
5. Not confined to periods -- used in other formal contexts
6. Distinguished from elision: lead-in preserves formal boundaries; elision overlaps them
7. The accompanimental figuration continuing through a cadence can produce a similar continuity effect even without a melodic lead-in

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for melodic material between the cadential goal of one phrase and the beginning of the next
- Check: does this material belong to the cadence? To the new phrase? To neither?
- If it belongs to neither and can be removed: it is a lead-in
- If removing it would disturb the formal structure: it may be extension, not a lead-in

# Context & Application
Lead-ins are a standard boundary process in classical music. They prevent phrases from sounding overly disconnected by filling the gap between formal units with melodic motion. The accompanimental figuration continuing steadily through a half cadence can also create rhythmic continuity between antecedent and consequent, even without a melodic lead-in.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 3, p. 84, ex. 3.13): Mozart, Piano Concerto in D minor, K. 466, ii, m. 4: a lead-in connects the antecedent's HC to the consequent's return of the basic idea.
**Example 2** (Ch. 3, p. 82, ex. 3.10): Mozart, Piano Sonata in B-flat, K. 333, ii, m. 4: "the lead-in is extraordinarily elaborate, which undoubtedly helps set up the embellished form of the basic idea" at the start of the consequent.
**Example 3** (Ch. 3, p. 85, ex. 3.15): Mozart, Piano Concerto in F, K. 459, i, mm. 72-79: no lead-in, but "the steady eighth-note accompaniment of the antecedent phrase continues without pause directly into the consequent."

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Period form (the primary context)
- Cadence types (the lead-in follows a cadence)
## Enables
- Rhythmic continuity between phrases without formal elision
## Related
- Nonelision of antecedent with consequent (the principle that lead-ins respect)
- Stop vs. end (a cadence can end without stopping)
## Contrasts With
- Elision (which overlaps formal boundaries rather than bridging them)
- Extension (which stretches a formal function rather than connecting two)

# Common Errors
- Treating a lead-in as part of the antecedent (it is formally separate)
- Treating a lead-in as the beginning of the consequent (it precedes the return of the basic idea)
- Confusing accompaniment continuity with a melodic lead-in

# Common Confusions
- A lead-in is not the same as a bridge or transition -- it is brief and connective, not a formal unit in its own right
- The term comes from William Rothstein, not from Caplin's original theory
- Rhythmic continuity (from accompaniment or lead-in) does not imply formal elision
