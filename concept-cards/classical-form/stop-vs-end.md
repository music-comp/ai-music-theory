# Quick Definition
A "stop" is the cessation of rhythmic activity at the musical surface; an "end" is the structural cadential closure marking a formal boundary. These are entirely different phenomena: they often coincide but are conceptually and perceptually distinct.

# Formal Definition
The notion of cadence has often been associated with the cessation of musical activity — for many musicians, cadence refers to a stop in the rhythmical motion of the phrase. However, this is not invariably so: many cadences see the rhythmic motion continuing through to the next formal unit, sometimes with a melodic lead-in maintaining rhythmic continuity. Although a rhythmic stop may be associated with a given cadence, a cessation of activity is not essential to the concept of cadence. Conversely, rhythmic motion can come to a halt at places that are clearly noncadential. If cadence does not necessarily entail stopping, it nonetheless embodies the notion of structural closure — the marking of a formal end.

# Construction/Recognition
1. A **stop**: rhythmic motion ceases — silence or sustained note
2. An **end**: a cadential arrival marking structural closure of a formal unit
3. They may coincide (cadence with rhythmic stop) — common but not necessary
4. A stop without an end: rhythmic cessation at a noncadential, possibly unstable point
5. An end without a stop: cadential closure with continuous rhythmic motion into the next unit

# Musical Context
This distinction is crucial for analyzing antecedent-consequent relationships. When rhythmic motion continues from the antecedent through to the consequent, students may be tempted to interpret the cadence differently (e.g., hearing an authentic cadence at m. 5 instead of a HC at m. 4). Understanding that a cadence can occur without a rhythmic stop prevents such misidentification and clarifies formal boundaries.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Trio in E-flat, Hob. XV:30, iii, mm. 1-16: continuous eighth-note runs carry momentum through the HC (m. 7) into the consequent (m. 9) — the phrase "ends" with a HC but the motion does not "stop"
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 311/284c, ii, m. 3: the only complete stop in the phrase occurs at the most unstable moment — a stop without an end

# Common Confusions
- Do not equate rhythmic cessation with cadential closure — a stop is not necessarily a cadence
- Do not deny a cadence just because rhythmic motion continues — an end does not require a stop
- The most common error: hearing continuous motion from m. 4 to m. 5 and concluding that the antecedent elides with the consequent via an authentic cadence at m. 5
