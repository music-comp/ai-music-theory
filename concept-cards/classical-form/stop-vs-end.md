# Quick Definition
The conceptual distinction between a "stop" (cessation of rhythmic activity) and an "end" (structural formal closure marked by a cadence), which are entirely different phenomena that may or may not coincide.

# Core Definition
The notion of cadence has often been associated with the "cessation of musical activity." However, this is not invariably so: many cadences see the rhythmic motion continuing through to the next formal unit, and sometimes a melodic lead-in helps maintain rhythmic continuity. Although a rhythmic stop may be associated with a given cadence, a cessation of activity is not essential to the concept of cadence. Conversely, rhythmic motion can come to a halt at places that are clearly noncadential. A rhythmic stop and a formal end are "entirely different musical phenomena: although they often operate together, they are conceptually (and perceptually) distinct."

# Prerequisites
- Half Cadence / Authentic Cadence (the types of formal endings)

# Key Properties
- A cadence marks a formal "end" but does not require a rhythmic "stop"
- Rhythmic continuity through a cadence is common and does not negate the cadence
- Rhythmic stops can occur at noncadential, unstable moments
- The distinction is critical for avoiding false interpretations of phrase boundaries
- An end without a stop: cadential closure with continuous rhythmic motion
- A stop without an end: rhythmic cessation at a noncadential, possibly unstable point

# Construction / Recognition
1. Locate the harmonic moment of cadential arrival (the formal "end")
2. Check whether rhythmic activity continues through that point
3. If rhythmic activity continues, the cadence is still valid--it is an "end" without a "stop"
4. If rhythmic activity stops at a non-cadential point, it is a "stop" without an "end"

# Context & Application
This distinction is particularly important when analyzing antecedent-consequent boundaries. When rhythmic motion continues from the antecedent through to the consequent, one might be tempted to hear elision or to deny the cadence. But the cadence (formal end) is still present even without a rhythmic stop. Conversely, a dramatic mid-phrase silence does not indicate a cadence.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Trio in E-flat, Hob. XV:30, iii, mm. 1-16: continuous eighth-note runs carry momentum through the HC (downbeat of m. 7) into the consequent at m. 9; the phrase "ends" but the motion does not "stop"
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 311/284c, ii, m. 3: the only complete stop in the phrase occurs at the most unstable moment--a "stop" without an "end"

# Common Errors
- Denying a cadence because rhythmic activity continues
- Interpreting a mid-phrase silence as a cadence
- Hearing elision between antecedent and consequent when motion is continuous

# Common Confusions
- **Rhythmic continuity does not equal elision**: when rhythm continues from one formal unit to the next, the cadence still marks the formal boundary; the units do not elide
- **Silence does not equal cadence**: a dramatic pause in the middle of a phrase does not create a cadence if there is no cadential harmonic progression
