# Quick Definition
A contrasting middle may conclude on dominant harmony without achieving a genuine half cadence, either because there is no distinct half-cadential progression or because the dominant is rendered unstable (inverted, containing a dissonant seventh, or both). The resulting noncadential ending is termed a dominant arrival.

# Core Definition
If the contrasting middle consists entirely of a standing on the dominant, no cadential closure occurs because there has been no harmonic change and hence no cadential progression. Even when the B section includes greater harmonic activity, two situations can lead to a noncadential ending: (1) the absence of any distinct half-cadential progression, and (2) the presence of a potential half-cadential progression whose final dominant is rendered unstable by inversion or a dissonant seventh. In either case, the arrival on dominant harmony is termed a dominant arrival, a cadential deviation of the half cadence. A premature dominant arrival occurs when the dominant arrives at a point that precedes the phrase-structural end of the section, getting "stuck" early.

# Prerequisites
- **Contrasting middle**: The section ending without a cadence
- **Dominant arrival**: The noncadential concept

# Key Properties
1. Standing on the dominant = no cadence possible (no harmonic progression)
2. Dominant arrival: dominant without a genuine half-cadential progression
3. Unstable dominant: inverted (V6), dissonant seventh (V7), or both (V6/5)
4. The dominant may receive a fermata to create a "stop" despite noncadential status
5. Premature dominant arrival: harmonic goal (dominant) arrives before the phrase-structural goal
6. Premature dominant arrival: the section "plays itself out" on the dominant after it arrives early
7. A fermata does not convert a noncadential ending into a cadence

# Construction / Recognition
1. Check whether the B section ends with a genuine half-cadential progression (pre-dominant to root-position dominant)
2. If the dominant is inverted or contains a seventh: dominant arrival, not HC
3. If the entire section is a standing on the dominant: no cadence
4. Check whether the dominant arrives before the phrase-structural end (premature dominant arrival)
5. Note any fermata -- it creates a "stop" but not a "cadence"

# Context & Application
The noncadential ending of a B section is analytically significant because it affects how the recapitulation is prepared. Without a genuine HC, the postcadential standing on the dominant cannot technically occur. The distinction between HC and dominant arrival may seem subtle but has important implications for understanding the formal structure. A premature dominant arrival creates a particularly interesting temporal effect: the harmonic and phrase-structural goals are misaligned.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in G Minor, Op. 49/1, ii, mm. 7-12: tonic prolongation with dominant emphasis; final V6/5 marks a dominant arrival (inverted, dissonant); fermata adds emphasis but not cadential status
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 31/3, ii, mm. 10-19: premature dominant arrival at m. 16 (Db = seventh of home-key dominant); section plays out to m. 19 with fermata; harmonic goal precedes phrase-structural goal

# Common Errors
- Automatically labeling the end of every B section an "HC" -- check for a genuine cadential progression
- Treating a fermata as proof of cadential status -- a fermata creates a "stop" but not a "cadence"
- Confusing dominant emphasis (prolongation) with dominant arrival (noncadential ending)

# Common Confusions
- A fermata over the final chord does NOT make it a cadence
- Standing on the dominant = no cadence (no harmonic change = no cadential progression)
- An inverted or seventh-containing dominant cannot support a genuine HC
- Premature dominant arrival: the dominant "arrives" before the section "ends"
