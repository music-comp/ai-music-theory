# Quick Definition
All rondo forms feature a principal thematic idea (the refrain) that alternates regularly with two or more contrasting passages (couplets); the two main classical types are the five-part rondo (ABACA) and the sonata-rondo (ABACABA).

# Core Definition
All rondo forms in Western art music display the same basic pattern: a principal thematic idea -- the "rondo refrain" or "theme" -- alternates regularly with two or more contrasting passages, variously termed "couplets," "episodes," or "digressions." Letters have traditionally been used to identify rondo forms (ABACA, ABACADA, ABACABA), where A stands for the theme or refrain and other letters refer to the couplets. In the classical era, most rondos can be analyzed in relation to two general types: the five-part rondo (ABACA) and the sonata-rondo (ABACABA). The five-part rondo is used most often for slow movements, while the sonata-rondo is used almost exclusively for fast finales. The refrain always appears in the home key, while couplets are set in contrasting keys or the minore of the home key (Caplin, p. 664).

# Prerequisites
- **Sonata form** -- The contrasting full-movement form
- **Small ternary** -- A common theme type for rondo refrains

# Key Properties
1. Alternation of refrain and couplets
2. Refrain always in the home key, closing with PAC
3. Couplets in contrasting keys or minore
4. Two main classical types: five-part rondo (ABACA) and sonata-rondo (ABACABA)
5. Five-part rondo: slow movements and some fast finales
6. Sonata-rondo: fast finales (rarely slow movements, never opening movements)
7. Lighter, more relaxed character than sonata form

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify a recurring theme (refrain) that always returns in the home key
2. Count the number of refrain returns and contrasting sections
3. Determine whether the form is five-part (ABACA) or sonata-rondo (ABACABA)
4. Check for the absence of internal repeat signs (which suggest sonata rather than rondo)
5. Label sections as numbered refrains and couplets with formal-function designations

# Context & Application
Rondo forms represent one of the principal full-movement formal types in the classical era, alongside sonata form and large ternary. Understanding the basic rondo pattern is essential for analyzing slow movements and finales.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 667): Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, iii -- five-part rondo with subordinate-theme complex as first couplet.
**Example 2** (p. 669): Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C minor ("Pathetique"), Op. 13, iii -- sonata-rondo with interior theme as second couplet.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Identifying a movement as a rondo after hearing only the first refrain and couplet. **Correction**: Rondo form is confirmed only with the appearance of the second couplet.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Whether the sonata-rondo is a sonata or a rondo. **Clarification**: It combines features of both, with rondo aspects (refrain alternation) and sonata aspects (exposition-development-recapitulation).
