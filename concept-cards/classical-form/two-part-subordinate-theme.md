# Quick Definition
A subordinate theme divided into two distinct parts: the first part ends with an internal half cadence (possibly followed by a standing on the dominant), and the second part begins with a new basic idea in an initiating function and eventually achieves the PAC that closes the entire theme.

# Formal Definition
In a two-part subordinate theme, the first part ends with a HC, which may be followed by a standing on the dominant. The second part begins with a new basic idea, usually incorporated into a presentation or compound basic idea, and eventually leads to PAC for the entire theme. The cadential endings of the two parts create a certain "antecedent-consequent" expression (HC in part 1, PAC in part 2), but the overall structure should not be considered a period because too many other characteristics of periodic organization are lacking -- especially the notion that the consequent "repeats" the antecedent by beginning with the same basic idea.

# Construction/Recognition
1. First part ends with HC (possibly followed by standing on the dominant)
2. Second part begins with a NEW basic idea (different from the first part's)
3. The new basic idea appears in an initiating function (presentation, compound basic idea)
4. Second part eventually achieves PAC to close the entire theme
5. The HC-PAC cadential pattern creates antecedent-consequent expression
6. But NOT a true period: the second part does not "repeat" the first part's material
7. The two-part structure maintains the principle that subordinate themes must end with PAC

# Musical Context
The two-part subordinate theme represents Caplin's solution to situations where a subordinate theme leads to a HC followed by a new basic idea. Rather than interpreting this as two separate subordinate themes (the first ending with HC, which violates the PAC requirement), Caplin proposes a single two-part theme. This interpretation preserves the analytical principle that subordinate themes end with PAC while acknowledging the internal structural complexity. The first part typically provides the dominant emphasis missing from the end of a nonmodulating transition.

# Examples
- Mozart, Violin Concerto in A, K. 219, i, mm. 74-98 (first part: presentation + compressed continuation ending HC at m. 80; second part: new basic idea at m. 81, organized as 16-measure sentence with compressed continuation and PAC at mm. 94/98)

# Common Confusions
- The two-part subordinate theme is NOT a period, despite the HC-PAC cadential pattern -- the second part begins with a DIFFERENT basic idea, violating the periodic requirement of repetition
- An alternative interpretation (two separate subordinate themes) is reasonable but conflicts with the principle that subordinate themes must end with PAC
- The two-part structure is distinct from multiple subordinate themes: in the latter, EACH theme ends with its own PAC
- Not every subordinate theme with an internal HC is two-part -- when continuation (not a new basic idea) follows the HC, the theme is a single unit with an internal HC
