# Quick Definition
A brief passage preceding the subordinate theme proper, typically consisting of tonic harmony and new accompanimental patterns that begin on the downbeat following the transition, with the basic idea entering later in the measure.

# Core Definition
The beginning of a subordinate theme can be preceded by a short introduction, analogous to the introduction that sometimes precedes a main theme. The underlying harmony is typically dominant, prolonged from the end of the transition, which resolves to tonic at the beginning of the subordinate theme. One type of introduction specific to subordinate themes is created when tonic harmony and new accompanimental patterns start on the downbeat following the transition, while the basic idea begins later in that measure. The downbeat of the NEXT measure must then be seen as the actual "first" measure of the theme. This type of introduction is rarely encountered with main themes (a notable exception being Mozart's Symphony No. 40, K. 550).

# Prerequisites
- **Subordinate theme**: The theme being introduced
- **Transition**: The preceding formal unit whose ending is separated from the theme
- **Standing on the dominant**: Often the harmonic basis for the introduction

# Key Properties
1. Brief passage preceding the subordinate theme's basic idea
2. Typically features dominant harmony (from transition) resolving to tonic
3. New accompanimental patterns begin on the downbeat; basic idea enters later
4. The "real" first measure of the theme is the NEXT downbeat after the introduction
5. The composer separates transition end from introduction through rests or dynamic/textural changes
6. More common with subordinate themes than with main themes
7. Usually very short (one measure or less)

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for tonic harmony and new accompaniment beginning on a downbeat after the transition
- Note that the basic idea enters later (upbeat to the next downbeat)
- The introduction belongs to the subordinate theme, not the transition
- Distinguish from standing on the dominant (introduction resolves TO tonic; standing PROLONGS dominant)
- Compare with main-theme introductions (rare in main themes but analogous in function)

# Context & Application
The introduction establishes the new key's tonic and sets up the accompanimental texture before the thematic material begins. It creates a "buffer zone" between transition and theme, providing a moment of harmonic settling after the transition's dominant emphasis.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, i, m. 13: dominant resolves to tonic with new accompaniment; basic idea begins slightly later
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:35, i, m. 36: accompaniment pattern established before basic idea
- Mozart, Clarinet Quintet in A, K. 581, i, m. 42: accompaniment begins before clarinet's basic idea

# Common Errors
- Treating the introduction as part of the transition rather than the subordinate theme
- Counting the introduction's downbeat as the theme's first real measure
- Confusing a thematic introduction (resolves to tonic) with a standing on the dominant (prolongs dominant)

# Common Confusions
- **Introduction vs. transition**: The introduction belongs to the subordinate theme, not the transition
- **"Real" first measure**: The theme's first measure begins with the basic idea, not the accompaniment onset
- **Introduction vs. standing on dominant**: An introduction resolves to tonic; a standing on dominant prolongs dominant harmony
