# Quick Definition
A brief passage preceding the subordinate theme proper, typically consisting of tonic harmony and new accompanimental patterns that begin on the downbeat following the transition, with the basic idea entering later in the measure.

# Formal Definition
The beginning of a subordinate theme can be preceded by a short introduction, analogous to the introduction that sometimes precedes a main theme. The underlying harmony is typically dominant, prolonged from the end of the transition, which resolves to tonic at the beginning of the subordinate theme. One type of introduction typical of subordinate themes is created when tonic harmony and new accompanimental patterns start on the downbeat of the measure following the end of the transition, while the basic idea begins later in that measure. The downbeat of the NEXT measure must then be seen as the actual "first" measure of the theme. This type of thematic introduction is rarely encountered with main themes (a notable exception being Mozart's Symphony No. 40, K. 550).

# Construction/Recognition
1. Brief passage preceding the subordinate theme's basic idea
2. Typically features dominant harmony (prolonged from transition) resolving to tonic
3. New accompanimental patterns begin on the downbeat; basic idea enters later
4. The "real" first measure of the theme is the NEXT downbeat after the introduction
5. The composer separates the transition end from the introduction through rests or dynamic/textural changes
6. More common with subordinate themes than with main themes
7. Usually very short (one measure or less)

# Musical Context
The introduction to a subordinate theme serves to establish the new key's tonic and set up the accompanimental texture before the thematic material proper begins. It creates a "buffer zone" between the transition and the theme, providing the listener with a moment of harmonic settling. The technique is especially useful when the transition ends with dominant emphasis and the subordinate theme needs a clear tonic establishment before the basic idea enters.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, i, m. 13 (dominant prolongation resolves to tonic with new accompanimental figuration; basic idea begins slightly later)
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:35, i, m. 36 (accompaniment pattern established before the basic idea proper)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i, m. 58 (new accompanimental figuration begins on the downbeat; basic idea enters on the upbeat)
- Mozart, Clarinet Quintet in A, K. 581, i, m. 42 (accompaniment begins before the clarinet's basic idea)

# Common Confusions
- The introduction to a subordinate theme is not part of the transition -- it belongs to the subordinate theme even though it precedes the basic idea
- The "real" first measure of the theme begins where the basic idea starts, not where the accompanimental introduction begins
- This type of introduction is specifically characteristic of subordinate themes and is rarely found with main themes
- Do not confuse with a standing on the dominant, which prolongs dominant harmony; the introduction typically resolves to tonic
