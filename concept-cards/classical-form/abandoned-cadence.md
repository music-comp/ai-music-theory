# Quick Definition
A cadential failure in which the cadential progression itself is disrupted by altering or eliminating the cadential dominant, causing the progression to lose its sense of direction before a new cadential attempt begins.

# Formal Definition
An abandoned cadence occurs when the composer initiates what seems to be a cadential progression but then "abandons" the progression by either inverting the cadential dominant or allowing the progression to bypass that dominant entirely. Unlike the deceptive and evaded cadences (where the cadential dominant remains intact), the abandoned cadence alters or eliminates the dominant itself. Cadential function can be abandoned in several ways: (1) the dominant may be placed in root position but then become inverted (usually to 6/4 position) before resolving to I; (2) the dominant may appear first in inversion; or (3) dominant harmony may be omitted altogether. Following an abandoned cadence, the music typically expresses continuation function before a new cadential progression eventually leads to a PAC.

# Construction/Recognition
1. A cadential progression is initiated (initial tonic, pre-dominant present)
2. The cadential dominant is compromised: inverted, converted from root position to inversion, or omitted
3. The dominant in inversion cannot function as a cadential harmony
4. Following the abandoned cadence, continuation function typically resumes
5. Eventually a new cadential progression appears leading to PAC
6. The cadential progression "loses its sense of direction" and the music "wanders off"
7. Less dramatic than evaded cadence but promotes significant formal loosening

# Musical Context
The abandoned cadence creates a distinctive aesthetic effect: unlike the deceptive cadence (where a partial goal is achieved) or the evaded cadence (where closure is dramatically thwarted at the last moment), the abandoned cadence involves the cadential progression simply losing its way. This is less dramatic but promotes significant formal loosening as the music tends to wander before returning to cadential activity. Cadential abandonment is particularly appropriate to the formal conventions of subordinate themes.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in B Minor, Op. 64/2, i, mm. 26-34 (after evaded cadence at m. 26, expanded cadential progression begins; cadential function is abandoned when progression breaks away at m. 28; pre-dominant VII^7/V leads nowhere cadential; cadence restored at m. 32 with II6, achieving PAC at m. 34)
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62, mm. 92-102 (expanded Neapolitan sixth implies cadential progression at m. 92; cadence abandoned at m. 96 when pre-dominant moves to diminished seventh in inversion; continuation resumes with sequence; cadential function restored at m. 99 with IV6; PAC at m. 102)

# Common Confusions
- In abandoned cadences, the cadential DOMINANT is compromised (inverted or omitted); in deceptive and evaded cadences, the dominant remains intact but the TONIC resolution fails
- The abandoned cadence is LESS dramatic than the evaded cadence but creates MORE formal loosening because the music loses its cadential direction entirely
- Do not confuse with a passage that simply lacks a cadential progression -- an abandoned cadence specifically INITIATES a progression and then disrupts it
- Following an abandoned cadence, the music enters continuation function, NOT cadential function (the cadence must be restarted from scratch)
