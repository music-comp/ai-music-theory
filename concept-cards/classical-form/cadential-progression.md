# Quick Definition
A harmonic progression that confirms a tonal center, consisting of the fundamental functions in temporal sequence -- initial tonic, pre-dominant, dominant, final tonic (authentic) or ending on dominant (half) -- with specific root-position requirements for cadential strength.

# Core Definition
"A cadential progression confirms that an implied tonality is indeed the actual tonality of the musical passage in question" (Ch. 2, p. 26). The strongest confirmation is an authentic cadential progression; a weaker one, a half-cadential progression. "A complete cadential progression is made up of the fundamental harmonic functions in the following temporal sequence -- tonic, pre-dominant, dominant, and tonic" (Ch. 2, p. 26). "For the authentic cadential progression to possess sufficient harmonic strength to confirm a tonality, both the dominant and the final tonic must be in root position" (Ch. 2, p. 27), exposing "the fundamental-bass motion of a descending fifth" in the bass. Three types: authentic, half-cadential, and deceptive.

- **Harmonic functions overview** and individual function cards

# Key Properties
1. Confirms tonality (vs. prolongational which sustains, sequential which destabilizes)
2. Complete authentic: initial tonic - PD - V - I (initial and final tonics distinguished)
3. Incomplete: omits initial tonic, pre-dominant, or both
4. Both V and final I must be root-position for authentic cadential force
5. Initial tonic usually in first inversion (I6); occasionally root position
6. II6 is more characteristic than IV as the classical pre-dominant
7. Three types: authentic (V-I), half (ending on V), deceptive (V-VI or other substitute)

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the harmonic functions in temporal sequence
- Verify root-position V and root-position final I for authentic cadence
- Check for completeness (all four functions present?)
- Determine type: authentic, half, or deceptive

# Context & Application
Cadential progressions are the harmonic foundation of cadential function in all theme types. They appear compressed (two measures) in tight-knit themes and expanded (four or more measures) in subordinate themes. The choice and elaboration of the pre-dominant element offers the greatest variety within the formula.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 2, ex. 2.5a): Basic authentic: I-II6-V-I; the II6 version is "more typical of the classical style" than I-IV-V-I.
**Example 2** (Ch. 2, ex. 2.6): With cadential six-four: I-II6-I6/4-V-I.
**Example 3** (Ch. 2, ex. 2.7c-h): With chromatic pre-dominant: I-VII7/V-V-I or augmented sixth-V-I.
**Example 4** (Ch. 2, ex. 2.8b): With initial tonic embellishment: I-V7(neighbor)-I-PD-V-I.

## Contrasts With
- Prolongational progression: sustains one harmony vs. confirms tonality
- Sequential progression: operates outside functional syntax

# Common Errors
- Accepting an inverted V or inverted final I as having genuine cadential force

# Common Confusions
- II6 is more characteristic than IV as the pre-dominant in classical cadential progressions
- The cadential six-four is an embellishment of dominant function, not a tonic chord in second inversion
- If the final tonic is inverted, the progression becomes deceptive or loses its cadential force
