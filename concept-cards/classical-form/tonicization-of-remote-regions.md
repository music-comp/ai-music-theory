# Quick Definition
A loosening technique in which a modal shift to minor within a subordinate theme brings into play tonal regions (bIII, bVI, bVII, bII) that would otherwise be considered remote in a strictly major-mode context.

# Formal Definition
A shift to minor mode in the subordinate theme brings into play tonal regions such as bIII, bVI, bVII, and bII that would otherwise be considered remote in a strictly major-mode context. The prominent tonicization of these regions is another loosening device typical of subordinate themes but not of main themes. The tonicization creates harmonic complexity and tonal instability that significantly loosens the formal organization, particularly when combined with model-sequence technique and expanded cadential progressions. These remote regions often function within the broader harmonic framework of a cadential progression, where they may substitute for more conventional harmonies.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Modal shift to minor enables access to remote tonal regions
2. Common remote regions: bIII, bVI, bVII, bII (all related to the minor mode)
3. Tonicization involves establishing these regions briefly through their own dominant
4. Often combined with model-sequence technique (descending fifth progressions through remote regions)
5. Remote regions may function within expanded cadential progressions
6. The substitution creates harmonic richness while maintaining underlying cadential logic
7. Generally absent from tight-knit main themes

# Musical Context
Tonicization of remote regions extends the harmonic palette of the subordinate theme far beyond what is available in a major-mode context. The technique is especially prominent in works that feature modal shifts within the subordinate theme. The remote regions create moments of harmonic surprise and tonal instability that contribute to the subordinate theme's characteristically loose organization. This technique is particularly common in Beethoven's subordinate themes.

# Examples
- Mozart, Clarinet Quintet in A, K. 581, i, mm. 54-60 (second subordinate theme: modal shift to minor leads to tonicization of bVI; descending fifth sequential progression through remote regions; bVI sustained as a prominent tonal region before cadential 6/4 restores the subordinate key)
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62, mm. 60-70 (subordinate theme passes through remote tonicizations via model-sequence)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i, mm. 60-70 (chromatic sequences through remote regions following modal shift)

# Common Confusions
- Tonicization is NOT modulation: the remote region is briefly emphasized but not confirmed by a PAC
- The remote regions available through modal shift (bIII, bVI, bVII, bII) are "remote" relative to the MAJOR mode -- in a minor-mode context, some of these are diatonic
- The remote tonicizations serve the broader cadential or sequential logic of the passage; they are not random harmonic excursions
- This technique is specifically a subordinate-theme loosening device, generally inappropriate for tight-knit main themes
