# Quick Definition
Although the subordinate-key ritornello occupies the position of a closing section in a sonata exposition, it is not organized as codettas, does not create a recessive dynamic, and sustains rather than dissipates the energy accumulated in reaching the cadential goal.

# Core Definition
In a sonata-form exposition, the final PAC is followed by a closing section, in which a recessive dynamic frequently helps dissipate the energy accumulated in reaching the cadential goal. At the corresponding place in concerto form, the final cadence is invariably followed by a section for orchestra alone, with the solo remaining silent until the beginning of the development. This orchestral passage is clearly the formal analogue of a closing section, but in the majority of cases it is not organized as a series of codettas. Moreover, the dynamic intensity reached by the solo's climax is seldom, if ever, lessened during this section. So even though this passage is located where a closing section might be expected, it displays few characteristics of that formal function (Caplin, p. 698).

# Key Properties
1. Occupies the same position as a closing section in sonata form
2. Not organized as a series of codettas
3. Does not create a recessive dynamic
4. Sustains or intensifies the dynamic climax
5. Features forte material with powerful rhythmic vitality
6. Displays few characteristics of a genuine closing section

# Construction / Recognition
1. Locate the orchestral section following the solo exposition's final PAC
2. Check whether it reduces energy (closing section) or sustains/intensifies it (ritornello)
3. Note whether the organization involves codettas or independent thematic material
4. If it sustains energy with ritornello-like material, it is a subordinate-key ritornello

# Context & Application
This distinction is important for accurate formal labeling. Using "closing section" for the subordinate-key ritornello misrepresents its character and function, which is to assert orchestral independence and sustain the dramatic momentum.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 699): Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i, mm. 196-206 -- subordinate-key ritornello begins at m. 199 following the cadential trill, with vigorous orchestral material sustaining the dynamic level.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Labeling the subordinate-key ritornello as a "closing section." **Correction**: It occupies the same position but functions differently.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Why the passage doesn't calm down after the solo's climax. **Clarification**: The ritornello's function is to sustain or exceed the solo's energy, not to dissipate it.
