# Quick Definition
A complete thematic unit within the coda, usually organized loosely like a subordinate theme with an expanded cadential area, ending with a PAC in the home key.

# Core Definition
The constituent coda themes are usually built in ways that resemble the loose organization of a subordinate theme, especially because of a highly expanded cadential area. Any of the loosening devices discussed for subordinate themes can appear in coda themes. The cadential function is especially prone to loosening through extension and expansion, according considerable emphasis to the movement's final cadence. Occasionally, a coda theme is structured as a simple tight-knit type (sentence, period, or hybrid), particularly in sonata-rondo form. Most coda themes close with a PAC in the home key; their formal organization thus resembles either a main theme (if tight-knit) or a subordinate theme (if loose). Some units conclude with a HC or dominant arrival, resembling transitions or developmental passages. Material may be drawn from anywhere in the movement.

# Prerequisites
- **Coda**: The section containing the coda theme
- **Subordinate theme**: The organizational model for most coda themes

# Key Properties
1. Usually loosely organized like a subordinate theme
2. Highly expanded cadential area is characteristic
3. Any loosening device from subordinate themes can appear
4. Occasionally tight-knit (sentence, period, hybrid), especially in sonata-rondo
5. Most close with PAC in the home key
6. Some close with HC, resembling transition or development
7. A coda may contain multiple coda themes
8. Material drawn from anywhere in the movement
9. The cadential function receives particular emphasis (final cadence of the movement)

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the thematic units within the coda
- Note whether each closes with PAC (theme-like) or HC (transition-like)
- Check for loosening devices: expanded cadential progressions, deceptive cadences, evaded cadences, modal shifts, internal dominant arrivals
- Determine the source of the material (main theme, subordinate theme, development, closing section)
- Note whether the organization is tight-knit or loose

# Context & Application
Coda themes provide the thematic substance of the coda. A typical coda contains one to three coda themes followed by a final closing section. They allow the composer to treat material in ways not possible during the recapitulation, especially through looser organization and expanded cadential emphasis.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62 (ex. 12.6): first coda theme (m. 244) with compound basic idea from subordinate theme, modal-shift presentation, extended continuation with ascending sequence, abandoned cadence; second coda theme (m. 276) with main-theme material, eventual PAC at m. 296
- Haydn, Symphony No. 104 ("London"), iv (ex. 12.7): first coda theme (m. 276) with weak tonic prolongation, expanded cadential progression, PAC at m. 287; second coda theme (m. 287) with modal shift to minor, internal dominant arrival, standing on dominant, expanded cadential progression, powerful PAC at m. 309

# Common Errors
- Treating coda themes as simply "extra themes" rather than functional units
- Assuming all coda themes must be loose (tight-knit types appear in sonata-rondo)

# Common Confusions
- **Theme vs. transition-like unit**: Coda themes ending with HC function like transitions, not themes
- **Tight-knit vs. loose**: The organization depends on the formal context; sonata-rondo codas often have tight-knit main-theme restatements
- **Material source**: Coda themes may use material from anywhere in the movement, not just the main theme
