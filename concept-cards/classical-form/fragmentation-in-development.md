# Quick Definition
The process of progressively breaking down the model's grouping structure into smaller units within the development core, driving the music toward cadential closure.

# Core Definition
Most cores produce an extensive process of fragmentation, which breaks down the grouping structure defined by the model (and its sequences) and eventually motivates a formal close to the core. The passage of fragmentation may continue to employ ideas from the model or may bring about a change in musical content. The fragmentation itself may establish a new model for sequential repetition at a smaller scale. Now and then, however, the sequential process becomes exhausted at the onset of fragmentation (or sometime after), and the rest of the core is supported instead by prolongational progressions of the kind found in a regular continuation phrase.

# Prerequisites
- **Core technique**: The organizational method
- **Model**: The unit being fragmented
- **Sequence**: The sequential repetitions that precede fragmentation

# Key Properties
1. Reduces the model's multi-measure grouping to progressively smaller segments (e.g., 4-bar to 2-bar to 1-bar to half-bar)
2. Often establishes new, smaller models for sequential repetition
3. May introduce new motivic content or continue with model ideas
4. Typically accelerates harmonic rhythm
5. Leads to the concluding function (HC, dominant arrival, or authentic cadence)
6. When sequential activity exhausts itself, prolongational progressions may support the rest
7. Operates at a larger scale than sentence fragmentation

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the progressive reduction in unit size from the model's dimensions
- Note whether new smaller-scale models emerge within the fragmentation
- Observe harmonic rhythm acceleration
- Locate the concluding dominant (HC, dominant arrival, or authentic cadence)
- Distinguish from sentence continuation fragmentation (core fragmentation is larger-scale)

# Context & Application
Fragmentation is the third stage of core technique (model -> sequence -> fragmentation) and creates the intensification driving toward the cadential goal. It is the stage that most directly motivates the concluding function. The process may establish nested levels of model-sequence-fragmentation at progressively smaller scales.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in A Minor, K. 310, i (ex. 10.3): four-measure model fragments to one-measure ideas (m. 70), then to one-beat units (m. 73), leading to HC at m. 74
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Hob. XVI:49, i (ex. 10.4): four-measure model reduces to two-measure units (m. 97), then one-measure segments (m. 101), leading to HC at m. 103
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C Minor, Op. 10/1, i (ex. 10.8): fragmentation establishes a new two-measure model at m. 68, sequenced down a step at m. 70

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Core technique (fragmentation is the third component)
- Model and sequence (the structures being fragmented)

## Enables
- Concluding function (HC, dominant arrival -- fragmentation drives toward it)
- Standing on the dominant (follows the concluding function)

## Related
- Continuation function (fragmentation serves a similar role at a smaller scale in sentences)
- Prolongational progressions (may replace sequential activity when it exhausts itself)

## Contrasts With
- No direct contrast; fragmentation is a component within core technique

# Common Errors
- Confusing core fragmentation with sentence continuation fragmentation (different scale)
- Assuming fragmentation begins exactly when sequential activity ends (may overlap)
- Failing to notice nested model-sequence-fragmentation patterns at smaller scales

# Common Confusions
- **Core vs. sentence fragmentation**: Core fragmentation operates at a larger scale than sentence continuation fragmentation
- **Onset timing**: The onset of fragmentation does not necessarily coincide with the end of sequential activity -- they may overlap
- **Nested patterns**: Fragmentation may establish new, smaller models that are themselves sequenced and fragmented
