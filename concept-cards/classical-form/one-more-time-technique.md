# Quick Definition
A specific type of cadential evasion (coined by Janet Schmalfeldt) in which the composer "backs the music up" after an evaded cadence to restate the previous cadential material, allowing the listener to hear the impending cadential arrival "one more time."

# Formal Definition
The "one more time" technique, as colloquially termed by Janet Schmalfeldt, is a particular type of cadential evasion in which the material following the evaded cadence obviously represents a return to previously heard cadential ideas, which are then run through again to reattempt cadential closure. The aesthetic effect depends on hearing that a structural goal is approached but NOT achieved, after which the attempt toward the goal is made once again using the same (or very similar) musical material. The technique must involve cadential evasion specifically -- repetition of material following an IAC or DC does NOT qualify, since those cadences achieve partial goals. A true PAC may or may not result on the subsequent attempt; the technique can chain multiple evasions before the final cadence arrives.

# Construction/Recognition
1. A cadential progression approaches its goal (PAC)
2. The cadence is EVADED (not deceptive, not IAC) -- no structural goal is reached
3. The music immediately restates the previous cadential material ("one more time")
4. The listener recognizes the return to earlier material and anticipates another cadential attempt
5. The final attempt may achieve PAC, or another evasion may occur
6. Must involve cadential evasion -- repetition after IAC or DC is a different phenomenon
7. Distinguished from repeated HC ideas at the end of a transition (where the dominant harmony marks cadential arrival on first hearing)

# Musical Context
The "one more time" technique is a signature concept of Caplin's theory and one of the most dramatically effective devices in subordinate-theme construction. The technique creates a powerful sense of formal drama: the listener approaches the cadential goal, is denied, and then is taken back to attempt the approach again. This process can be repeated multiple times, creating enormous tension before the final cadential arrival. The technique is especially effective in concerto and operatic genres but appears throughout the classical repertory.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:35, i, mm. 58-62 (cadential idea beginning m. 58 evaded at m. 60; same material restated; PAC at m. 62)
- Mozart, Violin Concerto in A, K. 219, i, mm. 98-112 (cadential idea evaded at m. 104; material restated "one more time"; another evasion at m. 108; expanded dominant finally brings PAC at m. 112)
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:35, i, mm. 49-62 (second subordinate theme: first statement of cadential idea at m. 49 brings quick PAC; theme repeated more loosely with cadential evasions and "one more time")

# Common Confusions
- The "one more time" technique requires cadential EVASION specifically -- repeating material after an IAC or DC is NOT this technique, because those cadences achieve a partial structural goal
- The aesthetic depends on the listener hearing that a goal was APPROACHED but NOT ACHIEVED -- this is fundamentally different from hearing a goal achieved (as with IAC or DC) and then repeated
- Do not confuse with repeated half-cadence ideas at the end of a transition -- in those cases, the dominant harmony arrives on the FIRST statement (not evaded), and subsequent repetitions simply prolong it
- The technique is named descriptively: the listener is asked to hear the cadential approach "one more time"
