# Quick Definition
A specific type of cadential evasion (coined by Janet Schmalfeldt) in which the composer "backs the music up" after an evaded cadence to restate the previous cadential material, allowing the listener to hear the impending cadential arrival "one more time."

# Core Definition
The "one more time" technique, as colloquially termed by Janet Schmalfeldt, is a particular type of cadential evasion in which the material following the evaded cadence obviously represents a return to previously heard cadential ideas, which are then run through again to reattempt cadential closure. The aesthetic effect depends on hearing that a structural goal is approached but NOT achieved, after which the attempt toward the goal is made once again using the same (or very similar) musical material. The technique must involve cadential evasion specifically -- repetition of material following an IAC or DC does NOT qualify, since those cadences achieve partial goals.

# Prerequisites
- **Evaded cadence**: The specific type of cadential failure required
- **Cadential extension**: The broader technique of delaying closure
- **Subordinate theme**: The primary formal context

# Key Properties
1. A cadential progression approaches PAC but is evaded (no closure achieved)
2. The music immediately restates previous cadential material
3. The listener recognizes the return and anticipates another cadential attempt
4. The final attempt may achieve PAC, or another evasion may occur
5. Must involve cadential EVASION -- not DC or IAC (which achieve partial closure)
6. Can chain multiple evasions before the final cadence arrives
7. Creates powerful formal drama through repeated approach-and-denial

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify an evaded cadence (post-dominant event groups forward as new beginning)
- The restated material must be recognizably from the previous cadential passage
- The restatement is not a new beginning with different material -- it is a return to try again
- Distinguish from repeated HC ideas at end of transition (where dominant arrives on first hearing)
- Multiple OMT iterations possible, building increasing cadential pressure

# Context & Application
The "one more time" technique is a signature concept of Caplin's theory and one of the most dramatically effective devices in subordinate-theme construction. The listener approaches the cadential goal, is denied, and is taken back to attempt the approach again. This creates enormous tension before the final cadential arrival. The technique is especially effective in concerto and operatic genres.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:35, i, mm. 58-62: cadential idea beginning m. 58 evaded at m. 60; same material restated; PAC at m. 62
- Mozart, Violin Concerto in A, K. 219, i, mm. 98-112: cadential idea evaded at m. 104; material restated "one more time"; another evasion at m. 108; expanded dominant finally brings PAC at m. 112

# Common Errors
- Calling any repetition after a failed cadence "one more time" technique
- Conflating OMT with repetition after deceptive cadence
- Failing to verify that the cadence was genuinely evaded (not deceptive or imperfect)

# Common Confusions
- **OMT requires cadential EVASION**: Repeating material after IAC or DC is NOT this technique, because those cadences achieve partial goals -- the aesthetic is fundamentally different
- **OMT vs. repeated HC ideas**: At a transition's end, the dominant arrives on the FIRST statement (not evaded), and subsequent repetitions prolong it -- this is different from OMT
- **The name is descriptive**: The listener is asked to hear the cadential approach "one more time"
