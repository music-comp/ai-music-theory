# Quick Definition
In rondo forms, dramatic intensification is associated primarily with the return of the refrain rather than with the contrasting couplets, making the retransition the most dramatically significant passage within the form.

# Core Definition
Unlike sonata movements, rondos are normally not as dramatic in emotional expression; they tend to be lighter and more relaxed in character. Moreover, tonal conflict early in the form may be minimized within a subordinate-theme complex or largely absent when the first couplet is an interior theme. To the extent that drama arises in a rondo form, that aesthetic sense tends to be associated with the return of the refrain. How the return is set up in the course of a retransition provides the composer with the best opportunity for imparting a dramatic effect. The retransition leading back to refrain 2 is usually longer and more elaborate than in a sonata exposition, and motives anticipating the refrain's basic idea are often included to heighten expectations for its return (Caplin, p. 675).

# Prerequisites
- **Five-part rondo** -- One context for rondo drama
- **Sonata-rondo** -- The more complex rondo context
- **Retransition (rondo)** -- The primary vehicle for dramatic effect

# Key Properties
1. Rondos are typically lighter and more relaxed than sonatas
2. Tonal conflict may be minimized or absent in early couplets
3. Drama is associated with the return of the refrain, not with couplet material
4. The retransition is the most dramatically significant passage
5. Retransitions are longer and more elaborate than in sonata form
6. Motives anticipating the refrain's basic idea heighten expectations

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the retransition leading to each refrain return
2. Assess its length and elaboration compared to the couplet material
3. Look for motivic anticipation of the refrain's basic idea
4. Note the standing on the dominant that typically precedes the refrain return

# Context & Application
This principle is fundamental for understanding the aesthetic logic of rondo forms. While sonata form dramatizes the tonal conflict between home and subordinate keys, rondo form dramatizes the return of familiar material. This makes the retransition, not the subordinate theme, the emotional climax of each rondo section.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 669): Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C minor ("Pathetique"), Op. 13, iii, mm. 79-122 -- standing on the dominant within the interior theme becomes the most dramatic event, with systematic acceleration of rhythmic values culminating in a dynamic climax before the refrain return.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Looking for sonata-like drama in the couplets. **Correction**: In rondos, dramatic emphasis falls on the refrain returns, not the contrasting material.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Whether rondos lack drama altogether. **Clarification**: Rondos do have dramatic elements, but these are associated with refrain returns rather than tonal conflict.
