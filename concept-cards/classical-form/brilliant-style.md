# Quick Definition
A texture featuring passage-work (arpeggiations, scale patterns) in a "brilliant" idiom, often the first place in a movement where continuous rhythmic accompaniment (such as Alberti bass) is employed, characteristic of transitions.

# Formal Definition
Transitions in the classical literature frequently feature melodic material characterized as "passage-work" -- arpeggiations and scale patterns projecting a "brilliant style." This style contrasts with the more tuneful melodic material typically found in main themes and subordinate themes. Transitions are often the first place in the movement where continuous rhythmic accompaniment (such as an Alberti bass) is employed. The brilliant style is also associated with concerto idioms, where virtuosic passage-work appears at climactic moments (especially the cadential trill). When this concerto-like passage-work appears in chamber or sonata genres, it signals an adoption of the concerto style.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Passage-work textures: arpeggiations, scale patterns, figuration
2. Less "tuneful" than main-theme or subordinate-theme melodies
3. Often the first use of continuous rhythmic accompaniment (e.g., Alberti bass) in the movement
4. Associated with increased dynamic intensity and forward drive
5. Prominent in transitions but also at cadential climaxes in subordinate themes
6. In concerto genres, features virtuosic solo writing with trill before cadential arrival
7. In chamber/sonata genres, adoption of concerto-like passage-work signals "brilliant" or "concerto" style

# Musical Context
The brilliant style is a characteristic textural marker of transition function. Its appearance helps the listener identify the transition, even before the harmonic goal (modulation or HC) is reached. The contrast between the main theme's tuneful melodies and the transition's passage-work contributes to the sense of formal loosening. The brilliant style also appears at cadential climaxes in subordinate themes, where it builds maximum intensity before the cadential arrival.

# Examples
- Haydn, Symphony No. 90, iv, mm. 16-28 (brilliant passage-work prolonging tonic; three presentation phrases project lengthy prolongation)
- Mozart, Clarinet Quintet in A, K. 581, i, mm. 61-64 (expanded dominant with virtuosic passage-work and trill in the clarinet -- concerto style adopted for chamber genre)
- Mozart, Violin Concerto in A, K. 219, i, mm. 108-112 (cadential trill before PAC)

# Common Confusions
- Brilliant style does NOT mean "better" or more important music -- it is a specific textural type appropriate to transitional and cadential contexts
- Not all transitions feature brilliant style -- some are more lyrical, especially in slow movements
- The brilliant style in a subordinate theme signals concerto influence, not transition function
- Passage-work should be distinguished from genuinely thematic material -- the former is conventionalized figuration, the latter has specific motivic identity
