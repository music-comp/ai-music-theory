# Quick Definition
Beethoven's practice of using development keys borrowed from the parallel minor, expanding the tonal spectrum beyond the standard minor-mode regions (VI, III, II) employed by Haydn and Mozart.

# Core Definition
Standard development keys in a major-mode home key are the minor-mode regions: the submediant (VI), the mediant (III), and the supertonic (II). Beethoven, who more than Haydn and Mozart expands the tonal spectrum, often employs development keys borrowed from the parallel minor of the home key, such as bVI, bIII, and bII. Although these are "major-mode regions," their close relationship to the parallel minor creates sufficient contrast with the keys of the exposition. A second exception to the minor-mode norm is the major-mode subdominant (IV), which sometimes appears early in the development and may be confirmed as a development key, but another minor-mode key always follows to provide modal contrast.

# Key Properties
1. Standard development keys (major mode): VI, III, II (all minor-mode regions)
2. Standard development keys (minor mode): IV and minor V
3. Beethoven adds keys borrowed from the parallel minor: bVI, bIII, bII
4. bVI and bIII are major triads but relate to the parallel minor
5. IV may appear early in the development but is always followed by a minor-mode key
6. These borrowed keys provide sufficient modal contrast with the exposition's major-mode subordinate key
7. Represents a stylistic evolution, not a departure from underlying principles
8. A development key must be confirmed by cadential function (HC, AC, or cadential deviation)

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify development keys and check whether they belong to the standard set (VI, III, II)
- If major-mode keys appear (bVI, bIII), check their relationship to the parallel minor
- Note the subdominant (IV) if it appears early -- expect a minor-mode key later
- Confirm that each key receives cadential articulation (HC or authentic cadence)

# Context & Application
This expansion represents a stylistic evolution from the Haydn-Mozart practice, augmenting but not replacing the standard keys. Understanding this palette helps explain why certain Beethoven developments explore more remote-sounding regions while maintaining the principle of modal contrast with the exposition.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in G, Op. 14/2, i (exs. 13.7, 13.8, 13.13): B-flat major (bIII) as development key, reached through modal borrowing from G minor
- Mozart, String Quintet in E-flat, K. 614, i (ex. 13.5): A-flat major (IV) appears early as a potentially confirmed development key, followed by minor-mode C minor

# Common Errors
- Treating bVI and bIII as random key choices rather than systematic borrowings from the parallel minor
- Failing to recognize that IV as a development key always requires a later minor-mode key for contrast
- Assuming all major-mode development keys indicate Beethoven (some Mozart works use IV)

# Common Confusions
- **Major-mode triads as minor-mode regions**: bVI and bIII are major triads but function as "minor-mode regions" because they are borrowed from the parallel minor
- **IV vs. bVI**: The subdominant (IV) is diatonic in the major home key; bVI, bIII, and bII are chromatic borrowings
- **Stylistic evolution**: This is an expansion, not a revolution -- the underlying principle of modal contrast remains
