# Quick Definition
The characteristic tonal procedure of the recapitulation's transition: shifting toward the flat-side subdominant region so that the return to the home-key dominant represents a sharp-side shift analogous to the exposition's modulation.

# Core Definition
Unlike the exposition, which features dramatic opposition between home and subordinate keys, the recapitulation normally remains throughout in the home key. To avoid monotony, the recapitulation often explores flat-side tonal regions -- those with more flats than the home key, such as the subdominant (IV), the supertonic (II), and regions derived from modal mixture (bVI, bIII, bII). These tonicizations arise especially in the main theme and transition. The most typical adjustment of a modulating transition sees the music shift toward the subdominant region so that the tonicized IV harmony takes on the role of pre-dominant, leading to the home-key dominant. The flat-side shift to IV is balanced by a sharp-side shift back to the home key, analogous to the sharp-side shift from home to subordinate key in the exposition (see Figure 14.1).

# Key Properties
1. Recapitulation avoids other keys receiving cadential confirmation
2. Flat-side regions (IV, II, bVI, bIII, bII) provide tonal variety within the home key
3. Tonicizations of flat-side regions occur mainly in main theme and transition
4. Subdominant (IV) functions as pre-dominant, leading to the goal dominant
5. The flat-side shift is balanced by a sharp-side shift back to tonic (parallel to the exposition)
6. The subordinate theme remains entirely in the home key
7. Tovey called inserted passages in flat-side regions "purple patches"
8. Flat-side emphasis is especially important when the main theme remains unaltered

# Construction / Recognition
- In the recapitulation's transition, look for a shift toward the subdominant region
- Note any tonicizations of IV, II, bVI, bIII, or bII
- Observe how the flat-side region becomes pre-dominant leading to the goal V
- Compare the tonal trajectory with the exposition: both achieve the goal dominant through a sharp-side shift

# Context & Application
The flat-side emphasis serves both tonal variety (preventing monotony) and structural logic (the subdominant functions as pre-dominant). If the main theme of the recapitulation already introduces flat-side tonicizations, the transition may not need them as extensively (and vice versa). The subordinate theme is the least likely section to explore flat-side regions, as it emphasizes the home-key tonic.

# Examples
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in C, K. 403, i, mm. 39-46 (ex. 14.7): home-key parallel minor at start of transition, then tonicizes bVI (A-flat major), followed by new model-sequence passage
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i, mm. 244-48 (ex. 14.4): secondary development sequences presentation into D major ("flat" region), then back to home key
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C minor, K. 457, i, mm. 118-26 (ex. 14.8): bII (Neapolitan) tonicized as expanded pre-dominant leading to HC

# Common Errors
- Expecting the recapitulation's transition to modulate like the exposition (it stays in the home key)
- Missing the structural logic: flat-side shift creates a sharp-side "return" analogous to the exposition
- Treating flat-side tonicizations as modulations rather than passing events

# Common Confusions
- **Tonicization vs. modulation**: Flat-side regions are briefly tonicized but not confirmed as new keys
- **Figure 14.1 analogy**: In the exposition, the transition shifts one step sharp-side (I to V); in the recapitulation, it first shifts one step flat-side (I to IV) then back sharp-side (IV to I)
- **"Purple patches"**: Tovey's term for flat-side insertions, used informally
