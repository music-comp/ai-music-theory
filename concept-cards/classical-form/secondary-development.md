# Quick Definition
Newly composed passages using model-sequence technique in the recapitulation (particularly in the main theme or transition), treating ideas not prominently featured in the development section proper.

# Formal Definition
In the recapitulation, the composer may illuminate ideas not treated in the development by means of newly composed passages using model-sequence technique. Charles Rosen coined the term "secondary development" for this practice. Secondary development most commonly appears in the main theme or transition of the recapitulation. It frequently treats motives not prominently featured in the development section proper, thus providing a sense of compensatory treatment.

# Construction/Recognition
- Newly composed sequential passages not found in the exposition
- Typically extends an existing continuation or creates a new one
- Employs model-sequence technique on a smaller scale than the development core
- Often treats motives underrepresented in the development
- Can appear in the main theme, transition, or (occasionally) subordinate theme area
- Compensates for limited development of certain ideas

# Musical Context
Secondary development is particularly appropriate when certain motives were not extensively treated in the development section. It also compensates when the development itself is brief or lacks a genuine core.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i (ex. 11.1): final phrase sequenced down a step into D major, then up a fifth, adding a brief secondary development to the main theme
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in G, K. 283, i (ex. 11.2): sequential passage in the main theme compensates for the lack of a core in the preceding development
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in C, K. 403 (ex. 11.5): model-sequence passage in the transition (mm. 42-43) compensates for the absence of sequential organization in the development

# Common Confusions
- "Secondary development" is not a second development section — it is localized sequential treatment within the recapitulation
- It differs from the development core in scale (smaller models, fewer sequences) and tonal context (remains within or near the home key)
- The term comes from Charles Rosen, not Caplin, but Caplin adopts and refines it
