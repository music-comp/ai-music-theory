# Quick Definition
Newly composed passages using model-sequence technique in the recapitulation (particularly in the main theme or transition), treating ideas not prominently featured in the development section proper.

# Core Definition
In the recapitulation, the composer may throw new light on old ideas by means of newly composed passages using model-sequence technique. Charles Rosen coined the term "secondary development" for this practice. Secondary development most commonly appears in the main theme or transition of the recapitulation. It frequently treats motives not prominently featured in the development section proper, thus providing a sense of compensatory treatment. It typically extends an existing continuation or creates a new one.

# Prerequisites
- **Recapitulation**: The formal context for secondary development
- **Model-sequence technique**: The compositional procedure employed

# Key Properties
1. Newly composed sequential passages not found in the exposition
2. Term coined by Charles Rosen, adopted by Caplin
3. Typically extends an existing continuation or creates a new one
4. Employs model-sequence technique on a smaller scale than the development core
5. Often treats motives underrepresented in the development
6. Can appear in the main theme, transition, or (occasionally) subordinate theme area
7. Compensates for limited development of certain ideas
8. Particularly appropriate when the development lacks a genuine core
9. Remains within or near the home key (unlike the development core's modulations)

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify sequential passages in the recapitulation that do not correspond to the exposition
- Look for model-sequence organization extending or replacing a continuation
- Note which motives are treated and whether they were prominent in the development
- These passages are typically shorter and less modulatory than development cores

# Context & Application
Secondary development is particularly appropriate when certain motives were not extensively treated in the development section. It also compensates when the development itself is brief or lacks a genuine core. The appearance of sequential passages in the recapitulation compensates for the absence of sequential organization elsewhere in the movement.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i (ex. 11.1): final phrase of main theme sequenced down a step into D major, then up a fifth; brief secondary development treating ideas not used in the development
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in G, K. 283, i (ex. 11.2): presentation sequenced up a step in the main theme; compensates for the lack of a core in the preceding development
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in C, K. 403, i (ex. 11.5): model-sequence passage in the transition (mm. 42-43) compensates for the absence of sequential organization in the development
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 7, Op. 92, i (ex. 11.4): new extended continuation in the transition featuring motivic play and ascending-stepwise sequence

# Common Errors
- Treating secondary development as a second development section
- Failing to note its compensatory relationship to the development's content

# Common Confusions
- **Not a second development**: Secondary development is localized sequential treatment within the recapitulation, not a repeat of the development section
- **Scale difference**: Differs from the development core in scale (smaller models, fewer sequences) and tonal context (remains within or near the home key)
- **Attribution**: The term comes from Charles Rosen, not Caplin, but Caplin adopts and refines the concept
