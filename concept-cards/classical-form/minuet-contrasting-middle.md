# Quick Definition
The B section of minuet form, sharing characteristics with a small ternary's contrasting middle but typically more complex and longer, whose organization depends critically on whether the preceding A section modulates.

# Core Definition
The B section of minuet form shares many characteristics with the contrasting middle of a small ternary: loose nonconventional organization, emphasis on the home-key dominant, sequential harmonies, and closure with a half cadence or dominant arrival. However, the minuet's B section is usually more complex and often considerably longer than the preceding A section. Certain techniques appear that rarely find a place in the modest scope of the small ternary, including interpolated episodes of new material in remote tonal regions, false recapitulations, and model-sequence technique reminiscent of a developmental core (Caplin, pp. 643-646).

# Key Properties
1. Usually more complex and longer than in a small ternary
2. Organization depends on whether the A section modulates
3. If A section modulates: B section may explore other tonal regions or return to the home key
4. If A section is nonmodulating: B section typically provides the fundamental tonal contrast
5. May contain an interpolated episode of new material in a remote key
6. False recapitulations may appear near the end
7. Model-sequence technique may resemble a developmental core

# Construction / Recognition
1. Determine the tonal plan of the preceding A section
2. If A modulates: look for development-like techniques, brief tonicizations, standing on the dominant
3. If A is nonmodulating: look for transition and subordinate-theme functions expressed in the B section
4. Identify any interpolated episodes with new material
5. Check for false recapitulations
6. The section typically ends with an HC or dominant arrival in the home key

# Context & Application
The critical analytical question is whether the A section modulates. After a modulating A section, the B section is free to explore additional tonal regions or simply return to the home key, concluding with dominant harmony. After a nonmodulating A section, the B section most often moves to the subordinate key using transition and subordinate-theme functions, providing the principal tonal contrast of the minuet.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 644): Haydn, Symphony No. 98 in B-flat, iii, mm. 21-42 -- B section after a modulating A section, with interpolated episode in A-flat major and model-sequence return to home key.
**Example 2** (p. 645): Mozart, Piano Sonata in A, K. 331, ii, mm. 19-32 -- B section with model-sequence technique (4-m. model followed by descending sequential repetition) resembling a developmental core.
**Example 3** (p. 646): Haydn, Symphony No. 87 in A, iii, mm. 9-26 -- B section following a nonmodulating A section, providing transition and subordinate-theme functions.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Expecting the B section to always provide tonal contrast. **Correction**: After a modulating A section, the B section may simply return to the home key without establishing a new key.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Whether the B section can bring subordinate-theme function. **Clarification**: Yes, especially after a nonmodulating A section, the B section may express transition and subordinate-theme functions.
