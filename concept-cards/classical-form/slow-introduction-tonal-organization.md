# Quick Definition
Slow introductions typically begin on home-key tonic and end on dominant (via HC or dominant arrival), though some end with an authentic cadence that elides with the exposition; their internal organization is usually loose, bipartite, or tripartite, only rarely projecting conventional theme types.

# Core Definition
Slow introductions typically begin with tonic harmony of the home key and, in short introductions, remain in that key throughout. In multipart introductions, a brief modulation typically occurs. Prominent tonicizations and emphasis on minor modality (in movements otherwise in major) are common. In the majority of cases, the harmonic goal is dominant of the home key, articulated by an HC (or dominant arrival) and followed by a standing on the dominant. This harmonic emphasis is appropriate for building up a powerful expectation for home-key tonic at the start of the exposition. Often enough, however, introductions close with an authentic cadence that elides with the beginning of the exposition. In exceptional cases, the structural end of the introduction occurs somewhat after the exposition has begun. Slow introductions are usually loose in formal organization, though tight-knit types appear occasionally. Many are bipartite or tripartite, and some larger ones contain four or more parts.

# Prerequisites
- **Slow introduction**: The formal unit being described
- **Half cadence**: The most common concluding cadence

# Key Properties
1. Begins with tonic harmony of the home key
2. Short introductions may remain in the home key throughout
3. Multipart introductions typically include a brief modulation
4. Minor modality and chromaticism are emphasized
5. Harmonic goal: usually dominant (HC or dominant arrival + standing on dominant)
6. Some introductions end with PAC eliding with the exposition
7. Exceptional: structural end of introduction after exposition has begun
8. Usually loose in formal organization
9. May be bipartite, tripartite, or contain four or more parts
10. Melodic material may or may not relate to the rest of the movement
11. Haydn/Mozart: often unrelated material; Beethoven: often anticipatory

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the slow tempo marking and the character of the material
- Determine the harmonic goal (dominant or authentic cadence)
- Note the formal organization (bipartite, tripartite, etc.)
- Check for modulations and tonicizations
- Observe the connection to the exposition (elision, dominant resolution, or continuation)

# Context & Application
Slow introductions occur most often in symphonies and overtures, occasionally in chamber music, and rarely in solo sonatas. Some are very short; others (Beethoven's Second and Seventh Symphonies) are so extensive as to occupy a significant proportion of the movement. A slow introduction can itself open with a thematic introduction. Beethoven sometimes opens finales with slow introductions (First Symphony).

# Examples
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in B-flat, K. 454, i, mm. 1-15 (ex. 16.1): bipartite; first part ends with PAC; second part is standing on dominant; resolution to tonic initiates exposition (truncated small ternary)
- Haydn, Symphony No. 104 ("London"), i, mm. 1-21 (ex. 16.3): tripartite; deviated small binary + closing section; final cadence elides with exposition
- Beethoven, Violin Sonata in A minor ("Kreutzer"), Op. 47, i, mm. 1-20 (ex. 16.4): compound sentence; ends on D minor (IV), which anticipates the main theme's pre-dominant opening

# Common Errors
- Expecting conventional theme types in slow introductions (they are usually loose)
- Assuming the slow introduction must end on dominant (some end with PAC)
- Analyzing the standing on the dominant as a separate section (it follows the HC)

# Common Confusions
- **Bipartite vs. tripartite**: Articulated by cadences; may resemble small binary or small ternary but with significant irregularities
- **Elided cadence**: When the PAC at the end of the introduction elides with the exposition's first bar
- **Motivic anticipation**: Beethoven often hints at ideas fully realized later; Haydn/Mozart often do not
