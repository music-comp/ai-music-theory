# Quick Definition
Slow movements typically employ inherently simpler formal types (large ternary, theme and variations, sonata without development, five-part rondo) because the slower pacing of events would make complex forms excessively long.

# Core Definition
If all movements of an instrumental cycle were constructed with the same degree of formal complexity, the slow movement would last considerably longer than the other movements, because of the slower pacing of its events. To maintain a relatively consistent length among the movements, composers often select an inherently simpler formal type for the slow movement. Compared with the elaborate schemes of the sonata, sonata-rondo, and concerto (common fast-movement forms), slow-movement forms -- large ternary, theme and variations, sonata without development, and five-part rondo -- are less complex in their formal organization. If composers choose regular sonata form for a slow movement, they normally do not exploit all its resources: expositions have a single subordinate theme (often tight-knit), transitions may be fused with subordinate themes or eliminated entirely, and developments are reduced in scope.

# Prerequisites
- **Sonata form**: The complex form sometimes adapted for slow movements
- **Large ternary form**: A primary slow-movement form
- **Theme and variations**: A primary slow-movement form
- **Sonata without development**: A primary slow-movement form

# Key Properties
1. Slower pacing requires simpler forms to maintain appropriate movement length
2. Four typical slow-movement forms: large ternary, theme and variations, sonata without development, five-part rondo
3. Slow movements normally occupy an interior position in the cycle
4. Usually set in a contrasting tonality (typically subdominant) or contrasting modality
5. When regular sonata form is used, it is compressed: single subordinate theme, reduced development, fusion of transition/subordinate theme
6. Theme and variations also appears frequently in finales (not exclusively a slow-movement form)
7. Sonata without development also appears in fast overtures
8. Large ternary form is used almost exclusively in slow movements

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the movement's tempo marking (Adagio, Largo, Andante)
- Determine which formal type is in use: large ternary (ABA' with modal contrast), theme and variations (repeated structure with embellishment), sonata without development (exposition-retransition-recapitulation), or regular sonata (with compressed features)
- Note techniques of formal compression: fusion, elimination, reduction

# Context & Application
The choice of form is a practical decision driven by performance timing. Haydn tends to prefer the large ternary; Mozart tends toward sonata without development. All three composers write slow movements in regular sonata form, but with characteristic compressions. The five-part rondo (discussed in Ch. 19) is also common.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 7, ii: large ternary form with interior theme resembling a development core
- Haydn, Piano Trio in A, H. 9, i, mm. 26-36 (ex. 17.14): reduced development (less than one-third the length of the exposition)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in D, Op. 10/3, ii (ex. 17.12): transition/subordinate-theme fusion in slow-movement sonata

# Common Errors
- Expecting a slow-movement sonata to have the same scope as a fast-movement sonata
- Misidentifying a large ternary as a sonata form (they are distinct despite superficial similarities)
- Assuming theme and variations is exclusively a slow-movement form (it also appears in finales)

# Common Confusions
- **Large ternary vs. sonata without development**: When a sonata without development has a truncated recapitulation, the resulting form resembles a large ternary -- but the listening experience differs
- **Real vs. notated measures**: In slow movements, each notated measure may contain more than one "real" measure of music (R = 1/2N formula)
