# Quick Definition
The second major section of concerto form, initiated by the solo entrance, functioning like a sonata exposition that modulates from the home key to the subordinate key.

# Core Definition
The solo exposition functions like a sonata exposition, containing main theme, transition, and subordinate-theme group. Unlike a sonata exposition, it does not conclude with a closing section but ends with the final PAC of the subordinate-theme group, after which the subordinate-key ritornello takes over as the formal analogue of a closing section. The solo exposition rarely represents a repetition of the opening ritornello; it almost always contains new material while bringing back a considerable amount of music from the ritornello, often varying the ideas or placing them in new formal contexts. The solo exposition can already represent a "development" of earlier material, often containing greater sequential activity than a regular sonata exposition.

# Construction / Recognition
- Begins with the entrance of the soloist (a dramatic event)
- Contains main theme, transition, and subordinate-theme group (usually multiple themes)
- Modulates from I to V (or III in minor)
- Ends with the final PAC of the subordinate-theme group (no closing section)
- Almost always introduces new material alongside ritornello material
- Greater sequential activity than in a regular sonata exposition
- The solo's entrance is often set off by a moment of silence after the ritornello
- Sometimes the solo enters before the orchestra finishes closing the ritornello

# Context & Application
After the relatively long buildup of the opening ritornello, the entrance of the soloist is a dramatic event. The solo exposition gives the soloist the opportunity to produce the major tonal event of the work (establishing the subordinate key) and to display virtuosic abilities through extended sequences and cadential expansions, especially in the subordinate-theme group.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Concerto in D Minor, K. 466, i, m. 77: Solo exposition begins with an alternative main theme, considerably more pianistic and lyrical than the orchestral opening
- Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i: Solo exposition introduces new subordinate themes while incorporating ritornello material

# Common Confusions
- The solo exposition is not a repetition of the opening ritornello (despite the "double exposition" model). It rearranges, varies, and supplements the ritornello's material.
- The solo exposition does not have a closing section; the subordinate-key ritornello fulfills that function. Students should not expect postcadential closing material from the soloist.
