# Quick Definition
A recapitulation in which the opening of the main theme is deleted and its material appears later, after the subordinate theme -- often described as a reversal of the exposition's ordering, though Caplin argues this description is misleading.

# Core Definition
Some recapitulations delete the opening material of the main theme, sometimes along with the transition, beginning directly with the subordinate-theme area. When main-theme ideas return later in the movement, the result is often called a "reversed" or "inverted" recapitulation. Caplin exercises caution about this description, since it suggests the composer can simply shift syntactical units mechanically. A careful examination reveals that either main-theme ideas are incorporated into the subordinate-theme area of the recapitulation (refashioned to function as a subordinate theme with loosening devices) or they occur as part of a subsequent coda and thus do not belong to the recapitulation proper. The musical content may be reordered, but the formal function of that content is different.

# Key Properties
1. Opening material of the main theme is deleted from the recapitulation
2. Main-theme ideas reappear later in the movement (in the subordinate-theme area or coda)
3. The procedure is normative in midcentury works and has roots in Baroque binary forms
4. Though deviant in the high-classical style, it appears in all three classical composers
5. When main-theme material returns, it is refashioned with loosening devices (subordinate-theme function)
6. The label "reversed recapitulation" is useful descriptively but theoretically misleading
7. Content (motivic material) must be distinguished from function (formal role)
8. The question of whether "recapitulation" function exists without the main theme's basic idea is raised

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for a recapitulation that begins directly with the subordinate theme or a cadential phrase
- Identify where main-theme material reappears later (in the subordinate-theme area or coda)
- Note loosening devices in the later main-theme material (it now functions as a subordinate theme)
- Observe whether the deletion of main-theme material relates to its extensive use in the development

# Context & Application
The reversed recapitulation raises fundamental questions about the nature of recapitulation function. If the main theme's basic idea is the defining signal of recapitulation (as it is for the small ternary), then a recapitulation without it is theoretically problematic. Caplin maintains the label "recapitulation" by convention, noting that the sonata principle (restoring subordinate-key material to the home key) is still fulfilled. The form resembles a small binary rather than a small ternary at the large-scale level.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in E-flat, Op. 50/3, i, mm. 86-130 (ex. 14.15): recapitulation begins with cadential idea from main theme; main-theme presentation returns later, refashioned as a "second subordinate theme" with loosening devices
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, i: well-known example of recapitulation beginning in subdominant (related but distinct procedure)

# Common Errors
- Assuming the composer simply "switches" the order of main and subordinate themes mechanically
- Failing to recognize that reordered material takes on new formal functions
- Confusing content (motivic material) with function (formal role)

# Common Confusions
- **Content vs. function**: The melodic-motivic material may be reordered, but the passage containing main-theme material does not function as a main theme -- it is refashioned as a subordinate theme
- **"Reversed" period analogy**: The same theoretical deficiency arises as with the concept of a "reversed" period -- syntactical units cannot simply be shifted around
- **Coda vs. recapitulation**: Main-theme material appearing in the coda does not belong to the recapitulation proper
