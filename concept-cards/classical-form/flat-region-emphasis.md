# Quick Definition
The tendency in recapitulations to tonicize regions on the "flat" side of the tonal spectrum -- subdominant, bVI, bIII, and the Neapolitan -- creating harmonic contrast within the home key.

# Core Definition
The harmonic plan of the main theme and especially the transition is sometimes altered in the recapitulation to emphasize the "flat" side of the tonal spectrum -- regions that introduce chromatically lowered scale-degrees, such as the subdominant (IV), the lowered mediant (bIII), the lowered submediant (bVI), and the Neapolitan (bII). This shift in the flat direction is particularly appropriate in the transition because the subsequent return to diatonic progressions of the home key at the beginning of the subordinate theme shifts the music back to the "sharp" side. This tonal change corresponds to the move from the home key to the subordinate key in the exposition, helping make the subordinate theme sound "fresh and revitalized."

# Prerequisites
- **Recapitulation**: The formal context
- **Tonal adjustment**: The fundamental procedure that motivates flat emphasis

# Key Properties
1. Most commonly appears in the transition of the recapitulation
2. Also found in the main theme (especially if the transition is left relatively unaltered)
3. Flat-side regions include: IV, bVI, bIII, bII (Neapolitan)
4. The return to diatonic home-key progressions at the subordinate theme creates a "sharp-side" shift
5. This shift corresponds to the exposition's move from home key to subordinate key
6. Makes the subordinate theme sound "fresh and revitalized"
7. Particularly important when both main and subordinate themes emphasize diatonic home-key harmonies
8. The subdominant is especially appropriate because it promotes (as pre-dominant) a logical succession to the dominant

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify tonicizations of IV, bVI, bIII, or bII in the recapitulation's transition or main theme
- Compare with the exposition to confirm these are new harmonic features
- Note the return to diatonic progressions at the subordinate theme
- The flat emphasis often coincides with mode changes (major to minor)

# Context & Application
Since the recapitulation presents the subordinate theme in the home key (rather than the subordinate key), the transition needs new means of creating harmonic contrast. Flat-region emphasis provides this contrast, functioning analogously to the exposition's modulation. The transition offers the best opportunity because both main and subordinate themes generally stress diatonic harmonies in the home key.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 7, Op. 92, i (ex. 11.3): main theme's A' section transposed to subdominant; mode change to minor shifts further flat-ward
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in C, K. 403, i (ex. 11.5): transition opens with home-key parallel minor, then moves to Ab major (bVI); tonal and modal balance restored at subordinate theme
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C Minor, K. 457, i (ex. 11.6): transition moves quickly to bIII region (Neapolitan), used as expanded pre-dominant
- Haydn, Piano Trio in E-flat, Hob. XV:30, i (ex. 11.7): mode change at m. 158 initiates flat-side passage

# Common Errors
- Treating flat-region emphasis as random chromaticism rather than a strategic compositional choice
- Failing to connect flat emphasis to the functional need for contrast in the recapitulation

# Common Confusions
- **Not random chromaticism**: Flat-region emphasis serves a specific compensatory function, replacing the exposition's sharp-side modulation
- **Sharp-side return**: The return from flat regions to home-key diatonicism mirrors the exposition's move from home key to subordinate key
- **Differs from development**: Flat-region emphasis in the recapitulation occurs within a fundamentally stable tonal context, unlike the development's tonal instability
