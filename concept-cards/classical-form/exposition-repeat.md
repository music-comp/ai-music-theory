# Quick Definition
The standard classical practice of repeating the exposition, creating a link between the exposition's closing and the main theme's return that is later varied by the development's opening.

# Formal Definition
The sonata exposition is normally repeated, as indicated by double-bar lines. The development and recapitulation are sometimes repeated together, almost always in works written before 1780. The exposition repeat creates a binary aspect to the overall form (analogous to the rounded binary version of small ternary). The repeat also establishes a musical connection between the end of the exposition (closing section/retransition) and the beginning of the main theme — a connection the pre-core of the development later varies.

# Construction/Recognition
- Indicated by repeat signs (double-bar lines) at the end of the exposition
- Creates a link: closing section → main theme that is later varied by closing section → pre-core
- Development/recapitulation repeat (less common) indicated by a second set of repeat signs
- The repeat is normative in the classical period; its omission is the exception
- The repeat reinforces the listener's grasp of the exposition's material

# Musical Context
The exposition repeat is a formal convention with important structural consequences. It ensures the listener has absorbed the thematic material before the development transforms it. The link it creates between the exposition's end and beginning is compositionally exploited when the pre-core begins with main-theme material or closing-section material.

# Examples
- Standard practice in nearly all classical sonata movements
- Development/recapitulation repeat is common in Haydn's earlier works

# Common Confusions
- The exposition repeat is not optional in the classical period — it is part of the form's design
- The development/recapitulation repeat is a separate convention, mostly pre-1780
- The repeat creates binary characteristics (two large sections), even within the ternary (three-function) design
