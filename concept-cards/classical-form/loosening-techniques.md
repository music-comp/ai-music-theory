# Quick Definition
A range of compositional devices used in subordinate themes (and other loose formal regions) to make the form less predictable, less regular, and less symmetrical compared to tight-knit main themes.

# Formal Definition
Loosening techniques are compositional procedures that create a looser formal organization compared to tight-knit themes. They operate across all three sentential functions (presentation, continuation, cadential) and include: additional repetition of the basic idea, repetition of the presentation phrase, weakening of the tonic prolongation, delayed fragmentation, extended model-sequence technique, cadential extension through failed cadences (IAC, DC, evaded, abandoned), cadential expansion through expanded cadential progressions, omission of initiating function, modal shift, tonicization of remote regions, and beginning with a standing on the dominant. The notion of formal "loosening" must not be equated with formal "weakening" -- extensions and expansions of cadential function do not make it less effective; the moment of cadential arrival is often intensified by being withheld.

# Construction/Recognition
**Presentation loosening**:
1. Additional repetition of basic idea (three statements instead of two)
2. Repetition of entire presentation phrase
3. Weakening of tonic prolongation (inversion, dominant pedal, accented subordinate harmonies)

**Continuation loosening**:
4. Extended model-sequence technique (more repetitions than in tight-knit contexts)
5. Delayed fragmentation (initial continuation units same size as presentation)

**Cadential loosening**:
6. Cadential extension through IAC, DC, evaded cadence, abandoned cadence
7. Cadential expansion through expanded cadential progressions
8. "One more time" technique

**Structural loosening**:
9. Omission of initiating function (beginning with continuation or cadential)
10. Beginning with standing on the dominant
11. Modal shift (major to minor or vice versa)
12. Tonicization of remote regions

# Musical Context
Loosening techniques are essential to the classical aesthetic. The subordinate theme's looser organization dramatizes the process of confirming the subordinate key -- the cadential goal is made more effective by being harder to achieve. Many loosening techniques also appear in transitions (extended continuation, omission of initiating function, modal shift) but some are specifically subordinate-theme techniques (chains of evaded cadences, "one more time" technique, expanded cadential progressions).

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 576, i, mm. 28-33 (additional repetition of basic idea)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i, mm. 92-98 (repetition of presentation phrase)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, i, mm. 13-16 (weakened tonic prolongation: I6 with V^4/3 embellishment)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i, mm. 58-74 (beginning with continuation: model-sequence replaces initiating function)

# Common Confusions
- "Loosening" is NOT "weakening" -- a loosened cadential function can be MORE dramatic and effective than a tight-knit one
- Not all loosening techniques are appropriate for all formal regions -- some (like chains of evaded cadences) are specific to subordinate themes; others (like extended continuation) also apply to transitions
- A string of evaded cadences rarely appears in transitions because this technique is connected with an impending authentic cadence, not the half cadence that ends a transition
- Loosening techniques appropriate to subordinate themes are generally inappropriate for tight-knit main themes
