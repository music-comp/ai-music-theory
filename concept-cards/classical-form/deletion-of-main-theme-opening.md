# Quick Definition
A deviation from standard practice where the recapitulation omits the opening material of the main theme (or the entire main theme), sometimes beginning directly with the subordinate-theme area.

# Formal Definition
Some recapitulations delete the opening material of the main theme or even the entire theme. At times the transition may be eliminated as well, and the recapitulation begins directly with the subordinate-theme area. Though deviant in the high classical style, this procedure is normative in midcentury works and has roots in baroque binary dance forms. It is especially associated with the sonata-rondo, since the theme has already had several earlier statements and will eventually return in the coda.

# Construction/Recognition
- Main theme's basic idea absent from the start of the recapitulation
- May omit just the opening or the entire main theme
- The transition may also be eliminated
- Recapitulation begins with subordinate-theme area in the home key
- If the main-theme opening is deleted, those ideas usually return later (in the coda or interpolated into the subordinate theme)
- Sometimes called a "reversed" recapitulation, though Caplin cautions against this term

# Musical Context
This deletion raises the question of whether a true "recapitulation function" is at hand, since the return of the basic idea is what normally distinguishes a recapitulation. However, the fundamental function of restoring subordinate-key material to the home key is still fulfilled.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62 (ex. 11.17): main theme returns in the subdominant at m. 152 during the development; main-theme material eventually returns in the home key in the coda (ex. 12.6, m. 276)

# Common Confusions
- A "reversed" recapitulation is not a simple rearrangement — main-theme ideas may be incorporated into the subordinate-theme area, not simply moved to the end
- The deletion does not necessarily mean the ideas are lost — they typically return later in the movement
- This procedure must be distinguished from cases where the main theme simply begins in the subdominant
