# Quick Definition
The closely related key to which the exposition may modulate or the contrasting middle may move, creating tonal contrast that must be resolved by the recapitulation's return to the home key.

# Core Definition
In a small ternary, a subordinate key may appear in two contexts. First, the A section may modulate to a subordinate key, closing with a PAC in that key. The subordinate key depends on the modality of the home key: in major, the dominant region; in minor, the mediant (relative major) or sometimes the minor dominant. Second, the B section may modulate to the dominant region and confirm it as a subordinate key through authentic cadential closure, after which a retransition leads back to the home key.

# Prerequisites
- **Contrasting middle**: The section that may confirm a subordinate key
- **Small ternary**: The overarching form

# Key Properties
1. In major: subordinate key is usually V (dominant region)
2. In minor: subordinate key is usually III (mediant/relative major); sometimes minor V
3. Exposition may end with PAC in subordinate key
4. B section may confirm dominant as key through authentic cadence
5. After cadential confirmation in B, a retransition leads back to home-key dominant
6. Recapitulation must adjust to remain in home key
7. A modulating A section followed by a B section that also modulates to V would be unusual

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify whether the exposition modulates (PAC in a key other than the home key)
2. Check the modality to predict the likely subordinate key (V for major, III for minor)
3. In the B section, check whether dominant harmony is merely emphasized (prolonged) or genuinely confirmed as a key (through cadential closure)
4. Look for retransition function after cadential confirmation of a subordinate key

# Context & Application
The subordinate key in the small ternary mirrors the larger tonal drama of sonata form at a thematic level. The choice between emphasizing the dominant (prolongation) and confirming it (modulation with cadence) has significant formal implications: the latter requires a retransition to return to the home key, while the former simply resolves to tonic at the start of A'.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in D, Hob. XVI:37, iii, mm. 1-8: exposition modulates to V (A major)
- Haydn, Symphony No. 101 ("The Clock"), iv, mm. 9-16: B section confirms dominant with PAC; retransition in mm. 17-20
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in D, Op. 28, ii, mm. 1-8: exposition modulates to minor V (A minor)

# Common Errors
- Confusing dominant emphasis in B with genuine modulation to the dominant key -- true modulation requires cadential confirmation
- Assuming a nonmodulating A requires a modulating B (it makes modulation more likely but is not required)
- Labeling dominant prolongation in the B section as a "key of V"

# Common Confusions
- Emphasizing dominant in B is NOT the same as modulating to the dominant key -- true modulation requires cadential confirmation
- A nonmodulating A does not require a modulating B, but makes it more likely
- When A modulates to V and B also begins in V, B usually moves on to other regions rather than staying in V
