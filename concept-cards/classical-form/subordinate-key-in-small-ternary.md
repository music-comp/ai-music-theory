# Quick Definition
The closely related key to which the exposition may modulate or the contrasting middle may move, creating tonal contrast that must be resolved by the recapitulation's return to the home key.

# Core Definition
In a small ternary, a subordinate key may appear in two contexts. First, the A section may modulate to a subordinate key, closing with a PAC in that key. This sets up expectations for a contrasting middle. Second, the B section may confirm the dominant as a genuine subordinate key through authentic cadential closure. A modulating B section usually follows a nonmodulatory A section. When the A section has modulated to V, the B section usually moves to other regions.

# Construction / Recognition
1. Exposition may end with PAC in subordinate key (usually V in major, III in minor)
2. B section may confirm dominant as key through authentic cadence
3. After cadential confirmation, a retransition leads back to home-key dominant
4. Recapitulation must adjust to remain in home key

# Context & Application
The subordinate key in the small ternary mirrors the larger tonal drama of sonata form at a thematic level.

# Examples
- Mozart, String Quartet in D Minor, K. 421/417b, ii, mm. 1-8 (exposition modulates to V)
- Haydn, Symphony No. 101 ("The Clock"), iv, mm. 9-16 (B section confirms dominant with PAC)

# Common Confusions
- Emphasizing dominant in B is NOT the same as modulating to the dominant key -- true modulation requires cadential confirmation
- A nonmodulating A does not require a modulating B, but makes it more likely
