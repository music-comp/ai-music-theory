# Quick Definition
A recapitulation in a small ternary that is longer than the exposition, typically through new continuations, expanded cadential progressions, evaded cadences, deceptive cadences, or added closing sections.

# Core Definition
An expanded recapitulation occurs when the A' section significantly develops earlier ideas, generating expansion relative to the exposition. Development typically takes place in a new continuation that may feature different motives from the basic idea than those developed in the original continuation. The section can also be lengthened by interpolations, expanded cadential progressions, deceptive cadences, and evaded cadences. This demonstrates that motivic development is not confined to the B section. The expansion contributes to a looser organization relative to the exposition.

# Prerequisites
- **Recapitulation in small ternary**: The section being expanded

# Key Properties
1. A' longer than A
2. May add new developmental continuation using different motives from the basic idea
3. May include expanded cadential progressions
4. May feature deceptive or evaded cadences requiring additional cadential attempts
5. May add closing section not present after A
6. Demonstrates that motivic development is not confined to the B section
7. Contributes to looser organization relative to exposition
8. The new developmental content may respond to material "left undeveloped" in the exposition

# Construction / Recognition
1. Compare A and A' measure by measure
2. Identify new material or extensions not present in the exposition
3. Note which motives from the basic idea are being developed (may differ from the exposition's continuation)
4. Check for cadential deviations (deceptive, evaded) that motivate repetition
5. Look for added closing sections

# Context & Application
Expanded recapitulations are especially prominent in Beethoven, who uses A' for dramatic new developments. The technique demonstrates that the small ternary's three sections offer different opportunities for motivic development: A establishes, B contrasts, A' develops. The expansion may compensate for material eliminated during compression of other aspects, or may develop motives that the original continuation left unexplored.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 7, ii, mm. 15-24: new continuation develops leaping dotted-rhythm motive (exposition developed stepwise motive); deceptive cadence at m. 19 leads to fortissimo outburst; second continuation develops stepwise motive; PAC brings closure
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in D, Op. 28, ii, mm. 17-22: basic idea sequenced down a step to bring a whiff of major mode; three reasons for extension discussed by Caplin

# Common Errors
- Assuming motivic development belongs only in the contrasting middle -- the recapitulation can be a major site of development
- Treating the expanded recapitulation as formally irregular rather than compositionally purposeful
- Failing to identify which specific motives from the basic idea are being developed differently from the exposition

# Common Confusions
- An expanded recapitulation is not formally deficient -- it creates a different but valid formal expression
- Closing sections added in A' may "recapitulate" ideas abandoned during restructuring of other parts
- The expansion often addresses specific compositional concerns (compensating for omitted material, developing unused motives, responding to the B section)
