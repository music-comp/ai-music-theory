# Quick Definition
The interthematic function that confirms the subordinate key through PAC, featuring distinctly looser organization than the main theme through extensions, expansions, separate continuation and cadential phrases, and an expanded cadential progression.

# Core Definition
The subordinate theme confirms the subordinate key through cadential closure (PAC). It is "distinctly looser" (Ch. 1, p. 17) than the main theme. Its looseness derives from phrase-structural means: continuation and cadential functions occupy "their own distinct phrases that possess a different melodic-motivic content and harmonic progression" (Ch. 1, p. 20), rather than fusing into a single phrase as in the tight-knit sentence. Both functions are "extended and expanded to create an asymmetrical 4 + 8 + 4 + 5 grouping structure" (Ch. 1, p. 20), and the presentation is loosened by its underlying dominant pedal.

- **Interthematic functions** -- the framework
- **Transition** -- the preceding function

# Key Properties
1. Confirms the subordinate key with PAC (essential expositional closure)
2. Distinctly looser than the main theme
3. Continuation and cadential may occupy separate phrases (not fused)
4. Continuation extended by extra fragmented units beyond the minimum
5. Cadential function supported by an expanded cadential progression (E.C.P.)
6. Cadential evasion can motivate repetition of the cadential phrase (cadential extension)
7. Asymmetrical phrase groupings
8. Presentation may be loosened by harmonic ambiguity (e.g., dominant pedal undermining tonic)
9. Followed by a closing section (codettas)

# Construction / Recognition
- Begins in the subordinate key (often after standing on the dominant)
- Look for separate continuation and cadential phrases with different material
- Identify the expanded cadential progression
- Locate the PAC that achieves essential expositional closure
- Note the closing section following the PAC

# Context & Application
The subordinate theme is the primary cadential goal of the exposition, confirming the subordinate key. Its loosening techniques create the "most loosely organized part" of the exposition (with the development being even looser). Detailed treatment in Chapter 8.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 1, pp. 19-21, ex. 1.8): Beethoven, Op. 2/1, i, mm. 21-48: new basic idea (inverted variant of main theme), presentation over dominant pedal (mm. 21-24), extended continuation (mm. 25-32, five extra measures of fragmentation), expanded cadential progression (mm. 33-41) with cadential evasion at m. 37 motivating repetition, PAC at m. 41, closing section (mm. 42-48).

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Interthematic functions, extension, expansion
## Enables
- Essential expositional closure (the PAC confirming the subordinate key)
- Closing section (follows the subordinate theme's PAC)
## Related
- Expanded cadential progression (characteristic harmonic structure)
## Contrasts With
- Main theme: tight-knit vs. loose
- Transition: both loose but subordinate theme uses phrase-structural loosening while transition uses harmonic-tonal loosening

# Common Errors
- Confusing the closing section with a "closing theme" or "cadence theme" (it is postcadential, not thematic)

# Common Confusions
- The subordinate theme's looseness is deliberate and appropriate, not a deficiency
- "Although it may be difficult to judge whether a transition or subordinate theme is more loose, the development section is almost always distinctly looser than any of the interthematic functions in the exposition" (Ch. 1, p. 21)
- Detailed treatment in Chapter 8
