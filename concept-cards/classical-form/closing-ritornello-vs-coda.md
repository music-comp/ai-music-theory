# Quick Definition
The closing ritornello should not be confused with a coda: it brings back material in its original form (unlike a coda's fresh context), lacks loosening devices, creates a formal frame through matching closing sections (unlike a coda's main-theme circularity), and is confirmed as distinct by the genuine coda in Beethoven's "Emperor" Concerto.

# Core Definition
Inasmuch as the closing ritornello follows the end of the recapitulation, one might think it functions as the coda of the movement. For several reasons, however, this ritornello should not be confused with a genuine coda. First, the ritornello generally brings back material in much the same way as it appeared earlier, whereas a coda almost always sets earlier ideas in a fresh formal context. Second, a closing ritornello does not normally feature loosening devices typical of a coda, such as sequential extensions and cadential expansions. Third, most codas refer to main-theme ideas for formal circularity, while a closing ritornello rarely contains main-theme material, preferring instead to create a formal frame through matching closing sections (a kind of "rhyme"). The distinction is confirmed by the genuine coda at the end of Beethoven's Emperor Concerto, which demonstrates how unlike a regular closing ritornello a true coda is. Concerto form does not contain a section that functions like a sonata-form coda (Caplin, pp. 717-718).

# Key Properties
1. Closing ritornello brings back material in its original form
2. Coda sets earlier ideas in a fresh formal context
3. Closing ritornello lacks sequential extensions and cadential expansions
4. Coda features loosening devices
5. Closing ritornello creates a "rhyme" through matching closing sections
6. Coda creates circularity through main-theme references
7. Concerto form normally lacks a genuine coda

# Construction / Recognition
1. After the recapitulation's cadential trill, identify the orchestral section
2. Check whether it brings back earlier material in essentially the same way
3. If so, it is a closing ritornello, not a coda
4. A genuine coda would set ideas in a new formal context with loosening devices

# Context & Application
This distinction is essential for correct formal analysis. Labeling the closing ritornello as a "coda" misrepresents its function, which is to frame the movement and give the orchestra a final statement, not to provide the developmental and reflective character typical of a coda.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 700): Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i, mm. 357-67 -- closing ritornello drawn from the second subordinate theme of the opening ritornello, creating a formal frame.
**Example 2**: Beethoven, Piano Concerto No. 5 ("Emperor"), Op. 73, i -- contains a genuine coda following the closing ritornello, demonstrating the difference.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Labeling the closing ritornello as the movement's coda. **Correction**: The closing ritornello is not a coda; it frames the movement through material recall, not through developmental loosening.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Whether concerto form has a coda. **Clarification**: Concerto form normally does not include a coda; the closing ritornello serves a different function. The Emperor Concerto is an exceptional case.
