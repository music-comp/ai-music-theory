# Quick Definition
Harmonies whose primary role is to progress to the tonic, all of which contain the leading-tone; represented primarily by V, V7, and leading-tone chords (VII6, VII7 and inversions).

# Formal Definition
Dominant function includes harmonies whose primary role is to progress to the tonic. All dominant-functioning harmonies contain the leading-tone of the key. The most common representative is the major triad or major-minor seventh chord built on the fifth scale-degree (V, V7). The leading-tone diminished triad in first inversion (VII6) and the leading-tone seventh chord (VII7 and its inversions) also have dominant function when they resolve to a tonic harmony. These leading-tone chords are not considered dominants when they progress to non-tonic harmonies in certain sequential situations.

# Construction/Recognition
- Primary chords: V (major triad on ^5), V7 (major-minor seventh on ^5)
- Additional chords: VII6 (leading-tone triad, first inversion), VII7 and inversions (leading-tone seventh chord)
- All dominant-function chords contain the leading-tone
- Must resolve to tonic to fulfill dominant function
- Root-position V is required for cadential strength (in cadential progressions)
- V7 adds instability appropriate for penultimate position in authentic cadences
- In half-cadential progressions, the dominant itself becomes the goal harmony and must be a consonant triad (no seventh)

# Musical Context
Dominant function is the second of the three fundamental harmonic functions. It plays a crucial role in cadential progressions (as the penultimate harmony before the final tonic), in prolongational progressions (as neighboring or passing chords to tonic), and as the sustained harmony in standings on the dominant. The dominant's function changes depending on context: penultimate in authentic cadences, ultimate (goal) in half cadences.

# Examples
- Authentic cadential progression: ...V(7)-I, where V resolves to final tonic
- Half-cadential progression: ...V, where the dominant is the goal harmony (consonant triad, no seventh)
- Standing on the dominant: prolonged V after a half cadence

# Common Confusions
- In a half cadence, V is the goal (ultimate) harmony, not the penultimate one. It must therefore be a consonant triad without a seventh.
- Leading-tone chords (VII6, VII7) have dominant function only when resolving to tonic. In sequential contexts, they may not function as dominants.
- The cadential six-four (I6/4 over ^5) is an embellishment of the dominant, not a tonic chord in inversion.
