# Quick Definition
Harmonies whose primary role is to progress to the tonic, all containing the leading-tone; represented by V, V7, and leading-tone chords (VII6, VII7), with different roles as penultimate (in authentic cadences) or ultimate (in half cadences) harmony.

# Core Definition
Dominant function "includes harmonies whose primary role is to progress to the tonic. These are dominant-functioning harmonies, all of which contain the leading-tone" (Ch. 2, p. 23). The most common representative is V or V7. "The leading-tone diminished triad in first inversion (VII6) and the leading-tone seventh chord (VII7 and its three inversions) also have a dominant function when they resolve to a tonic harmony" (Ch. 2, p. 23). "These leading-tone chords are not considered dominants when, in some sequential situations, they progress to non-tonic harmonies" (Ch. 2, p. 23).

# Key Properties
1. Primary chords: V, V7 (on scale-degree 5)
2. Additional chords: VII6, VII7 and inversions (when resolving to tonic)
3. All dominant-function chords contain the leading-tone
4. Must resolve to tonic to fulfill dominant function (exception: sequential contexts)
5. In authentic cadences: V is penultimate, must be root-position for cadential strength
6. In half cadences: V is the goal (ultimate) harmony, must be consonant triad (no seventh)
7. V7 adds instability appropriate for penultimate position; inappropriate as a goal harmony

# Construction / Recognition
- V or V7 resolving to I = dominant function in authentic cadence
- V as goal harmony without seventh = half cadence
- VII6 or VII7 resolving to I = dominant function
- Leading-tone chords in sequences progressing to non-tonic = NOT dominant function

# Context & Application
Dominant function is crucial in cadential progressions (penultimate before final tonic), in prolongational progressions (as neighboring or passing chords to tonic), and as the sustained harmony in standings on the dominant. Its role changes by context: penultimate in authentic cadences, ultimate in half cadences.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 2): Authentic cadential progression: ...V(7)-I, where V resolves to final tonic.
**Example 2** (Ch. 2): Half-cadential progression: ...V, where the dominant is the goal harmony (consonant triad, no seventh).
**Example 3** (Ch. 2): Standing on the dominant: prolonged V after a half cadence.

# Common Errors
- Adding a seventh to the dominant in a half cadence
- Treating leading-tone chords as dominants in sequential contexts

# Common Confusions
- In a half cadence, V is the GOAL harmony, not the penultimate one; it must be a consonant triad without seventh
- The cadential six-four (I6/4 over scale-degree 5) is an embellishment of the dominant, not a tonic chord
