# Quick Definition
A Baroque-derived practice, particularly common in Haydn, where a slow movement ends on dominant harmony rather than tonic, with the resolution occurring only at the beginning of the following movement.

# Core Definition
Most slow movements end conventionally with a final home-key PAC followed by a closing section. But as a remnant of standard high-Baroque practice, a number of slow movements, particularly by Haydn, end with dominant harmony. The resolution to tonic occurs only at the beginning of the following movement. Such weaker closure forges a stronger bond between the movements, yet it may undermine the slow movement's independence. In extreme cases, the slow movement can seem like an introduction to the subsequent movement. A variety of formal contexts and procedures can produce an ending on the dominant: the movement may close regularly with a PAC, after which an appended passage leads to a final dominant; the music may find itself "stuck" unexpectedly on the dominant; or an expected closing authentic cadence fails to materialize, yielding an HC or dominant arrival instead (usually after a deceptive cadence).

# Prerequisites
- **Slow-movement sonata form**: One context where dominant endings occur
- **Large ternary form**: Another context
- **Half cadence**: The most common form of the dominant ending

# Key Properties
1. Remnant of high-Baroque practice
2. Particularly common in Haydn
3. Resolution to tonic occurs at the start of the following movement
4. Creates stronger inter-movement bond but may undermine independence
5. Three procedures: (a) PAC + appended passage to dominant; (b) music gets "stuck" on dominant; (c) expected PAC fails (HC or DA after deceptive cadence)
6. In extreme cases, the slow movement resembles an introduction to the next movement
7. Recall similar techniques at the end of interior themes in large ternary

# Construction / Recognition
- Check the final harmony of the slow movement
- If dominant: determine which procedure produced it (appended passage, getting stuck, failed cadence)
- Note whether the following movement begins with tonic resolution
- Assess whether the dominant ending undermines the movement's independence

# Context & Application
The practice links to Baroque conventions where movements were less independent. Beethoven's "Waldstein" Sonata is an extreme case: the "Introduzione" to the finale is often considered the work's "slow movement," illustrating how a dominant-ending slow movement can blur the boundary between movements.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, H. 21, ii, mm. 40-50 (ex. 17.13b): truncated recapitulation results in the movement ending on the home-key dominant; Presto finale follows immediately
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C ("Waldstein"), Op. 53: "Introduzione" to the finale is often considered the slow movement

# Common Errors
- Assuming every slow movement must end with a PAC
- Confusing a dominant ending with an incomplete analysis (the dominant IS the intended final harmony)
- Overlooking the Baroque precedent for this practice

# Common Confusions
- **Dominant ending vs. incomplete movement**: The dominant ending is a deliberate compositional choice, not an analytical problem
- **Slow movement vs. introduction**: When the dominant ending is extreme, the slow movement may function more as an introduction to the following movement
