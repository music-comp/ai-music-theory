# Quick Definition
A compositional procedure in which a melodic-harmonic idea (the model) is repeated at a different scale degree (the sequence), creating harmonic instability characteristic of continuation function and loosely organized formal regions.

# Core Definition
Model-sequence technique involves the statement of a musical idea (the model) followed by one or more repetitions on different scale degrees (the sequences). The sequential harmonic progression expresses harmonic instability, since sequential progressions have no definite ending point. This makes model-sequence technique especially appropriate for continuation phrases, contrasting middles, and loosely organized formal regions. The fragments produced by fragmentation are frequently sequenced using this technique. If a short sequential passage is followed by a half cadence, the entire B section of a small ternary may resemble a continuation phrase.

# Prerequisites
- Continuation Function (model-sequence technique is one of its devices)

# Key Properties
- Model: the initial idea, typically one to four measures
- Sequence: the transposed repetition on a different scale degree
- Creates harmonic instability through sequential progression (descending fifths, ascending steps, descending thirds, etc.)
- The sequence may be exact, modified, or compressed
- Often combined with fragmentation (fragments become the model)
- Especially characteristic of continuation phrases and contrasting middles
- The model may not align with barlines

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify a short idea (the model), typically one or two measures
2. Find its repetition at a different pitch level (the sequence)
3. The harmonic pattern should be transposed along with the melody
4. The sequence may be exact or modified (especially at the end to fit a cadential goal)
5. Model-sequence passages are often followed by a cadential idea
6. In B sections, the model typically does not begin on tonic harmony, so it should not be considered a "basic idea"

# Context & Application
Model-sequence technique appears in continuation phrases of sentences, in contrasting middles of small ternaries, in development sections, and in transitions. In the contrasting middle, the instability of sequential progression supports the loose organization of the B section. In compound sentences, model-sequence technique often governs the fragmented continuation. In subordinate themes, sequences can be extended for many more repetitions than in tight-knit contexts.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1, i, mm. 5-7: fragmented basic-idea motives sequenced within the continuation
- Mozart, K. 457, i, mm. 5-7: model-sequence driving the continuation toward cadence
- Haydn, Symphony No. 100, iii, mm. 11-14: B section with model sequenced in descending-third pattern
- Mozart, String Quartet in E-flat, K. 428/421b, iv, mm. 17-19: B section with imitative model-sequence
- Beethoven, Piano Concerto No. 3, Op. 37, i, mm. 9-14: descending-third sequential progression in compound sentence continuation

## Contrasts With
- Tonic prolongation (sequential progression creates instability, unlike tonic prolongation's stability)

# Common Errors
- Confusing sequential repetition in a presentation with model-sequence technique in a continuation; the former transposes the entire basic idea and maintains tonic prolongation, while the latter sequences shorter fragments within an unstable harmonic context
- Treating simple motivic repetition as model-sequence technique; transposition with sequential harmonic support is required

# Common Confusions
- **Sequential repetition (pres.) vs. model-sequence (cont.)**: sequential repetition in a presentation transposes the basic idea as a whole and remains within tonic prolongation; model-sequence in a continuation sequences fragmented units within an unstable harmonic context
- **Model alignment**: the model may not start on the barline, making the organization of sequential repetition "not straightforward" (as Caplin notes for Haydn, Symphony No. 100)
