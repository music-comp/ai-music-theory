# Quick Definition
A compositional procedure in which a melodic-harmonic idea (the model) is repeated on different scale degrees (as a sequence), creating harmonic instability appropriate for loosely organized formal sections.

# Formal Definition
Model-sequence technique involves the statement of a musical idea (the model) followed by one or more repetitions at different pitch levels (the sequences). The sequential harmonic progression expresses harmonic instability, since sequential progressions have no definite ending point. This makes model-sequence technique especially appropriate for contrasting middles, continuations, and subordinate themes. If a short sequential passage is followed by a HC, the entire B section may resemble a continuation phrase.

# Construction/Recognition
1. **Model**: An idea of definite length (typically 1-4 measures) stated first
2. **Sequence**: The model repeated at a different pitch level
3. Supporting harmonies follow a sequential progression (descending fifths, ascending steps, etc.)
4. The sequence may be exact, modified, or compressed
5. Often signals continuation function when appearing at the start of a phrase
6. The model may not align with barlines

# Musical Context
Model-sequence technique appears prominently in contrasting middle sections, continuation phrases, subordinate themes, and transitions. In subordinate themes, sequences can be extended for many more repetitions than in tight-knit contexts.

# Examples
- Haydn, Symphony No. 100, iii, mm. 11-14 (B section: model sequenced with descending-third pattern)
- Mozart, String Quartet in E-flat, K. 428/421b, iv, mm. 17-19 (B section with imitations)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i, mm. 58-74 (subordinate theme: extended sequences)

# Common Confusions
- Not the same as simple motivic repetition -- requires transposition with sequential harmonic support
- In the B section, the model typically does not begin on tonic harmony, so it should not be considered a "basic idea"
