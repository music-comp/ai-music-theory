# Quick Definition
A formal compression in the recapitulation where the main theme and transition merge into a single thematic structure, typically by eliminating the end of one and the beginning of the other.

# Core Definition
The main theme and transition usually remain discrete units in the recapitulation, but occasionally they fuse into a single thematic structure. This formal compression is usually brought about by eliminating the end of the main theme and the beginning of the transition and by attaching the close of the latter to what remains of the former. In some cases, the entire transition from the exposition is eliminated, and the main theme, which closed with an authentic cadence in the exposition, ends instead with a half cadence. This fusion is often accompanied by the same alteration techniques used for both sections: deleting unnecessary repetitions, adding new model-sequence technique, and emphasizing flat tonal regions.

# Prerequisites
- **Recapitulation**: The formal context for this compression
- **Main theme**: Provides the opening of the fused unit
- **Transition**: Provides the closing (dominant-oriented) portion

# Key Properties
1. Eliminates the end of the main theme and the beginning of the transition
2. Creates a single thematic structure from two originally discrete units
3. The fused unit typically ends with a half cadence or standing on the dominant
4. May result in the main theme closing with HC instead of PAC
5. In extreme cases, the entire transition may be eliminated
6. Often accompanied by standard alteration techniques (deletions, secondary development, flat emphasis)
7. Reflects the changed functions of both sections in the recapitulation

# Construction / Recognition
- The main theme does not reach its original cadential closure
- No clear boundary between main theme and transition function
- The fused unit leads to a half cadence or dominant arrival preparing the subordinate theme
- Compare with exposition to identify what material has been eliminated from each section

# Context & Application
Fusion reflects the changed functions of both sections: the main theme no longer needs to fully establish the home key, and the transition no longer needs to modulate. The eliminated material is often redundant in the recapitulation context. This procedure only occurs in the recapitulation; the sections remain discrete in the exposition.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Concerto No. 3, Op. 37, i (ex. 11.8): same eight-measure presentation as exposition; entirely new continuation using model-sequence technique (sequential development of final motive); ends with HC instead of PAC, followed by standing on the dominant from the exposition's transition
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:21, ii (ex. 11.9): main theme brings deceptive cadence instead of PAC; subsequent cadential phrase leads to HC; entire transition from exposition deleted; final cadential phrase takes over transition function

# Common Errors
- Confusing fusion with simple deletion of the transition
- Assuming fusion in the recapitulation implies fusion existed in the exposition

# Common Confusions
- **Fusion vs. deletion**: Fusion is a genuine merging of formal functions, not merely the omission of one section
- **Resulting unit**: The fused unit is neither purely a main theme nor purely a transition but a hybrid serving aspects of both
- **Exposition independence**: The sections remain discrete in the exposition; fusion is unique to the recapitulation

# Verification Notes
Caplin describes two mechanisms: eliminating the end of one and beginning of the other, or eliminating the entire transition so the main theme ends with HC. Both examples clearly illustrate these distinct mechanisms.
