# Quick Definition
The PAC in the subordinate key that marks the structural close of the subordinate theme and fulfills the exposition's primary cadential goal of confirming the subordinate key.

# Core Definition
The essential expositional closure (EEC) is the PAC in the subordinate key that concludes the subordinate theme (or the last theme of a subordinate-theme group). This cadence is the primary formal goal of the entire exposition, as it fulfills one of the theme's principal functions: confirming the subordinate key as a competing tonality to the home key. The subordinate key must be fully confirmed by a PAC for it to acquire sufficient weight to vie for prominence with the home key. All the formal processes of the exposition -- the main theme's establishment of the home key, the transition's destabilization, and the subordinate theme's loosening techniques -- converge on this cadential moment.

# Prerequisites
- **Subordinate theme**: The formal unit whose PAC constitutes the EEC
- **Perfect authentic cadence**: The specific cadence type required (IAC does not suffice)
- **Subordinate key**: The tonal region confirmed by the EEC
- **Cadential function**: The formal function that achieves the EEC

# Key Properties
1. Must be a PERFECT authentic cadence (IAC does not suffice for structural closure)
2. Occurs in the subordinate key, not the home key
3. Ends the subordinate theme or the last theme of a subordinate-theme group
4. Represents the structural goal of the entire exposition
5. Followed by a closing section (codettas) in virtually all expositions
6. Typically delayed and dramatized through cadential extensions and expansions
7. The various techniques of cadential extension (IAC, DC, evaded cadence, abandoned cadence) all serve to delay this moment
8. Has a counterpart in the recapitulation: the essential structural closure (ESC), which confirms the home key
9. Exceptions to the PAC requirement are extremely rare in the classical repertory

# Construction / Recognition
- Locate the final PAC in the subordinate key within the exposition
- Verify it is a PERFECT authentic cadence (root-position V to root-position I, with tonic scale degree in the soprano)
- Confirm it ends the subordinate theme proper (not the closing section)
- Note the closing section (codettas) that follows postcadentially
- Distinguish from earlier PACs in a subordinate-theme group, which close individual themes but are not the EEC unless they close the final theme
- In cases of cadential evasion, track which cadential attempt finally succeeds

# Context & Application
The EEC is arguably the most important single moment in the exposition. A typical aesthetic goal of the subordinate theme is to delay and dramatize the arrival of the anticipated PAC so that its eventual appearance provides a powerful sense of confirming the subordinate key and thus allowing, at least temporarily, that key to gain a sense of structural superiority over the home key. The closing section that follows provides postcadential emphasis or dissipation of the energy built up in achieving this goal. The built-up energy needed to achieve the cadence is then released through the closing section, whose slower rhythmic values and disjunct textures help dissipate the accumulated tension.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F minor, Op. 2/1, i, m. 41: PAC in A-flat major after cadential evasion at m. 37 and repetition of the cadential phrase (Ch. 9, Ex. 9.3)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, i, m. 28: PAC in G major confirms the subordinate key
- Every properly constructed subordinate theme ends with an EEC; it is a defining requirement

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Subordinate theme (the formal unit that achieves the EEC)
- Cadential function (the formal function producing the cadence)
- Subordinate key (the tonal region confirmed)

## Enables
- Closing section (follows the EEC postcadentially)
- Exposition's structural completion (the EEC marks the exposition's cadential goal)
- Recapitulation's parallel goal: the ESC (essential structural closure)

## Related
- One-more-time technique (delays the EEC for dramatic effect)
- Evaded cadence (common technique for postponing the EEC)
- Expanded cadential progression (harmonic means of dramatizing the EEC)
- Deceptive cadence in subordinate theme (another delay technique)

## Contrasts With
- Half cadence (insufficient for structural closure of subordinate theme)
- Internal half cadence (divides the subordinate theme but does not close it)

# Common Errors
- Accepting an IAC as the EEC (only a PAC fulfills the structural requirement)
- Confusing the EEC with a PAC in the closing section (the closing section is postcadential)
- Identifying an earlier PAC in a subordinate-theme group as the EEC when later themes follow
- Mistaking a cadential evasion for a genuine cadence

# Common Confusions
- **EEC vs. IAC**: An IAC signals that the theme is NOT yet finished; only a PAC constitutes the EEC
- **EEC vs. closing-section cadence**: The EEC is the cadence of the subordinate theme proper; the closing section follows postcadentially
- **EEC in theme groups**: In a subordinate-theme group, the EEC is the final PAC of the last theme in the group
- **EEC vs. ESC**: The EEC confirms the subordinate key in the exposition; the ESC confirms the home key in the recapitulation

# Verification Notes
The concept is directly derived from Caplin's consistent requirement that subordinate themes must end with PAC in the subordinate key. The term EEC is used extensively in the Hepokoski/Darcy tradition; Caplin describes the same functional requirement without always using this exact label. The distinction between EEC and ESC is standard in form-functional analysis.
