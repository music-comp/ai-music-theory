# Quick Definition
The structural end of a transition occurs at the moment of half cadence or dominant arrival; the literal end occurs just before the entrance of the subordinate theme, often marked by a medial caesura -- these two moments frequently arise at different points in time.

# Core Definition
For most transitions, it is important to distinguish between their structural end and their literal end. The structural end arises with the moment of half cadence or dominant arrival -- the point at which the transition achieves its harmonic goal. The literal end occurs just before the entrance of the subordinate theme and is often associated with a medial caesura. Sometimes these two moments occur simultaneously (when the very last event is an HC). But they often arise at different points in time, separated by a standing on the dominant. The initial tonic of the subordinate theme signals the BEGINNING of that unit, not the END of the transition.

# Prerequisites
- **Transition**: The formal unit in question
- **Half cadence**: The most common structural ending
- **Dominant arrival**: An alternative structural ending
- **Standing on the dominant**: The passage that often separates structural and literal ends

# Key Properties
1. Structural end: moment of HC or dominant arrival (harmonic goal achieved)
2. Literal end: just before the subordinate theme begins (temporal boundary)
3. These two moments may or may not coincide
4. When they differ, a standing on the dominant fills the intervening space
5. The resolution of dominant to tonic at the subordinate theme's start is NOT the transition's end
6. A fermata on the final sonority may mark the literal end (and medial caesura)
7. In premature dominant arrivals, the structural end precedes the melodic goal

# Construction / Recognition
- Locate the HC or dominant arrival (structural end)
- Locate the beginning of the subordinate theme (the point just before this is the literal end)
- If the two moments coincide, the transition ends cleanly at the HC
- If they differ, identify the standing on the dominant between them
- Do not confuse the tonic resolution at the subordinate theme's start with a cadential ending

# Context & Application
The distinction is critical for accurate analysis. Students often confuse the moment when dominant resolves to tonic (at the subordinate theme's start) with the transition's ending. But the resolution represents a BEGINNING (of the subordinate theme), not an ending (of the transition). The standing on the dominant that frequently intervenes between structural and literal ends can itself create confusion: it prolongs the transition's harmonic goal but introduces new melodic material. In the case of premature dominant arrival, the structural end is especially early, creating analytical difficulty.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F minor, Op. 2/1, i, mm. 15-20: HC at m. 16 (structural end); standing on dominant mm. 17-20; subordinate theme begins m. 21 (literal end at m. 20)
- Haydn, String Quartet in B-flat, Op. 50/1, iv, mm. 24-34: HC at m. 24 (structural end); new material in standing on dominant; subordinate theme at m. 34 (literal end)
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 4, Op. 60, i: premature dominant arrival at m. 92 (structural end); melodic activity continues to m. 95; new standing on dominant material from m. 95

# Common Errors
- Identifying the tonic resolution at the subordinate theme's start as the transition's end
- Treating the standing on the dominant as belonging to the subordinate theme
- Confusing the resolution of dominant to tonic with a cadential arrival

# Common Confusions
- **Resolution vs. ending**: The drive to resolve dominant to tonic at the subordinate theme's beginning can mislead students into identifying a "point of arrival" as the transition's end
- **Standing on the dominant ownership**: The standing on the dominant belongs to the transition (postcadential), not to the subordinate theme
- **Premature dominant arrival**: When the harmonic goal arrives before the melodic-motivic process concludes, the structural end is especially early
