# Quick Definition
A loosening technique in which cadential function is extended because a promised perfect authentic cadence fails to materialize, motivating additional cadential units to achieve the requisite closure.

# Formal Definition
Cadential extension occurs when the expected PAC of a subordinate theme is not realized, prompting one or more additional cadential attempts. The PAC can fail through five mechanisms: (1) an imperfect authentic cadence (IAC) appears instead, (2) a deceptive cadence (DC) replaces the expected tonic, (3) an evaded cadence thwarts the resolution, (4) an abandoned cadence disrupts the cadential progression itself, or (5) rarely, a genuine PAC appears but the phrase is then repeated to bring the same cadence again. Following the failed cadence, the subsequent music may restate the prior continuation or cadential material, or introduce a new continuation, before attempting cadential closure again. The notion of formal "loosening" through cadential extension must not be equated with formal "weakening" -- the withheld cadence often intensifies the eventual arrival.

# Construction/Recognition
1. An implied PAC fails through one of five mechanisms (IAC, DC, evaded, abandoned, or repeated PAC)
2. One or more additional cadential phrases follow the failure
3. Previous material (continuation or cadential) may be restated, sometimes with adjustments
4. New continuation material may appear before the next cadential attempt
5. Multiple failures can chain together, creating an extended cadential area
6. The eventual PAC is typically more emphatic than the initially expected one
7. The technique is particularly suited to subordinate themes, less common in main themes

# Musical Context
Cadential extension is one of the most important loosening techniques for subordinate themes because it directly dramatizes the process of confirming the subordinate key. The struggle to achieve cadential closure creates formal tension that is resolved only with the final PAC. The evaded cadence is the most common and most dramatic technique; the imperfect authentic cadence and deceptive cadence provide partial closure; the abandoned cadence disrupts the progression entirely; and the repeated PAC is the least common and least effective. This hierarchy of techniques creates a spectrum of cadential drama.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62, mm. 102-118 (IAC at m. 110 extended by continuation repetition leading to PAC at m. 114)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A-flat, Op. 26, iv, mm. 42-48 (DC at mm. 44 and 46, then PAC at m. 48)
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:35, i, mm. 58-62 (evaded cadence at m. 60, "one more time" to PAC at m. 62)
- Haydn, String Quartet in B Minor, Op. 64/2, i, mm. 26-34 (abandoned cadence at m. 28, new cadential progression achieves PAC at m. 34)

# Common Confusions
- Cadential extension (prolonging cadential function through failed cadences) is distinct from cadential expansion (enlarging cadential function through expanded cadential progressions)
- An IAC followed by a PAC creates an antecedent-consequent expression, but the result should NOT normally be considered a period (lacking other periodic characteristics)
- The evaded cadence is fundamentally different from the deceptive cadence: in DC, a partial goal IS reached (the substitute harmony groups backward); in an evaded cadence, NO goal is reached (the event groups forward as a new beginning)
- "Loosening" through cadential extension does not weaken the cadence -- it intensifies the eventual cadential arrival
