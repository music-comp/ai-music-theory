# Quick Definition
The structural changes made to the main theme in the recapitulation, including deletion of restatements, addition of model-sequence technique (secondary development), emphasis on flat regions, and deletion of the home-key cadence.

# Core Definition
In many recapitulations the main theme is organized just as in the exposition, with only ornamental changes. Frequently, however, four types of structural changes occur because the main theme's functions differ in the recapitulation: (1) deletion of thematic restatements, since the melodic-motivic material is already familiar; (2) additional model-sequence technique (secondary development), throwing new light on old ideas; (3) emphasis on "flat" tonal regions (subdominant, lowered mediant, lowered submediant, Neapolitan); and (4) deletion of the home-key cadence, since confirmation can occur later in the subordinate theme. The main theme primarily signals "return" and resolves the development's final dominant at a local level.

# Prerequisites
- **Recapitulation**: The formal context for these changes
- **Main theme**: The section being altered

# Key Properties
1. The main theme's initiating and key-confirming functions are no longer required
2. Melodic-motivic material is already familiar, making restatements dispensable
3. If the entire main theme was repeated in the exposition, it is usually stated just once
4. If the main theme was a small ternary, only the A (or A') section may be used
5. Secondary development treats ideas not prominently featured in the development
6. Flat-region emphasis shifts the harmonic plan toward chromatically lowered scale degrees
7. Cadential closure is dispensable because the home key will be confirmed by the subordinate theme
8. A closing section may also be omitted
9. The home-key tonic at the start harmonically resolves the dominant from the end of the development

# Construction / Recognition
- Compare the main theme's structure in exposition and recapitulation
- Look for deleted repetitions or sections (especially second halves of repeated themes, or B/A' sections of small ternary themes)
- Identify newly composed sequential passages (secondary development)
- Note any tonicizations of IV, bIII, bVI, or bII
- Check whether the main theme's cadence has been eliminated or altered

# Context & Application
At the beginning of the recapitulation, the main theme primarily functions to signal "return." The home key was reestablished at the end of the development; a defining tight-knit organization need not be expressed again. The listener already knows the material well. These altered functional demands motivate the four types of structural changes.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i (ex. 11.1): final phrase sequenced into D major (flat region) then up a fifth; restatement of basic idea and final cadence eliminated; secondary development added
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in G, K. 283, i (ex. 11.2): presentation sequenced up a step; new continuation ends on I6 without cadential closure; compensates for lack of core in development
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 7, Op. 92, i (ex. 11.3): A' section of small ternary transposed into subdominant; mode change to minor shifts further to flat side; cadence in subdominant minor, not home key

# Common Errors
- Assuming the main theme must be identical to the exposition version
- Treating secondary development as a second development section rather than localized sequential treatment

# Common Confusions
- **Secondary development**: Not a second development section but localized model-sequence passages within the recapitulation (term from Charles Rosen, adopted by Caplin)
- **Flat-region emphasis**: The move toward flat regions is aesthetically motivated, not structurally required; it provides harmonic contrast
- **Functional motivation**: Alterations are not arbitrary choices but reflect the main theme's changed formal role

# Verification Notes
Four specific alteration types are enumerated: deletion of restatements, model-sequence addition, flat emphasis, and cadence deletion. The functional explanation (main theme's functions no longer required) is Caplin's own rationale.
