# Quick Definition
Structural changes to the main theme in the recapitulation: deletion of restatements, addition of model-sequence technique (secondary development), emphasis on flat regions, and deletion of the home-key cadence.

# Formal Definition
The main theme's function changes in the recapitulation: its material is already familiar, the home key has been reestablished, and a defining tight-knit organization need not be expressed again. It primarily signals "return" and resolves the development's final dominant. Four structural alterations regularly occur: (1) deletion of thematic restatements, (2) additional model-sequence technique (secondary development), (3) emphasis on flat regions (IV, bVI, bIII, Neapolitan), and (4) deletion of the home-key cadence.

# Construction/Recognition
1. **Deletion of restatements**: repetitions appropriate in the exposition are eliminated
2. **Secondary development**: new sequential passages illuminate ideas not treated in the development
3. **Flat-region emphasis**: harmonic plan altered toward subdominant, bIII, bVI, Neapolitan
4. **Cadence deletion**: home-key cadence dispensable since the subordinate theme will confirm it

# Musical Context
These alterations reflect the main theme's changed role -- the listener knows the material, the home key is established, and redundancy should be minimized.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i (ex. 11.1): secondary development, eliminates restatement and cadence
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in G, K. 283, i (ex. 11.2): presentation sequenced up a step, no cadential closure
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 7, Op. 92, i (ex. 11.3): main theme in subdominant, then minor

# Common Confusions
- Alterations are motivated by changed formal functions, not arbitrary choices
- "Secondary development" is localized sequential treatment, not a second development section
