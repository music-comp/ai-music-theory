# Quick Definition
A dominant arrival in which the harmonic goal (dominant) appears before the end of the prevailing melodic, rhythmic, and phrase-structural processes, creating noncongruence between harmonic and melodic closure.

# Formal Definition
A premature dominant arrival occurs when the final dominant arrives at a point that does not coincide with the phrase-structural or melodic ending. The harmonic goal precedes the melodic goal, and further activity (fragmentation, liquidation) continues over the already-arrived dominant before the structural close. Because the dominant is not synchronized with the phrase-structural end, it cannot function as a genuine HC.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Dominant arrives while melodic-motivic processes are still ongoing
2. Noncongruence between harmonic arrival and phrase-structural end
3. Further fragmentation or liquidation continues over the dominant
4. No genuine cadential progression marks the phrase-structural end
5. Distinguished from simple dominant arrivals (where chord quality is the issue)

# Musical Context
Premature dominant arrivals commonly appear in contrasting middles and transitions. The noncongruence between harmonic and structural processes is itself a loosening trait.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 7, Op. 92, i, m. 81 (dominant arrives at onset of fragmentation; section continues to fermata at m. 88)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 31/3, ii, m. 16 (dominant arrives but dotted-rhythm figure continues)
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 4, Op. 60, i, m. 92 (transition: harmonic goal precedes melodic goal at m. 95)

# Common Confusions
- Not all dominant arrivals are premature -- some are noncadential due to chord quality (inversion, seventh)
- The continuation over the dominant does not mean the section lacks a harmonic goal
- Do not confuse with a postcadential standing on the dominant, which follows a genuine HC
