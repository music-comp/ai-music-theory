# Quick Definition
Subordinate themes employ harmonic-tonal loosening through modal shift (major to minor or vice versa), chromaticism, tonicization of remote regions (bIII, bVI, bVII, bII), and in extreme cases modulation from one key to another within the theme itself.

# Core Definition
The use of modal shift (moving from major to minor or vice versa), chromaticism, tonicizations of more remote regions, and modulation from one key to the next helps to promote formal loosening. These techniques are typically associated with subordinate themes but generally absent from tight-knit main themes. Almost all subordinate themes reside in the major mode, but a change from major to minor is frequently encountered somewhere within a subordinate theme (or theme group). A modal shift introduces a broader spectrum of pitches and pitch relationships. Except at the final cadence of the theme (or theme group), a modal shift can take place anywhere, but tends to occur with continuation function. A shift to minor brings into play tonal regions (bIII, bVI, bVII, bII) that would otherwise be considered remote. The most radical loosening occurs when the theme modulates from one key to another (modulating subordinate theme).

# Prerequisites
- **Subordinate theme**: The formal context
- **Modal shift in subordinate theme**: The most common technique

# Key Properties
1. Modal shift: major to minor or vice versa, typically with continuation function
2. The major modality must be restored by the final cadence
3. Chromaticism broadens the pitch repertoire
4. Modal shift makes remote regions accessible: bIII, bVI, bVII, bII
5. Tonicization of remote regions: prominent emphasis (not just passing) on these distant tonal areas
6. The most extreme case: modulating subordinate theme (begins in one key, ends in another)
7. In modulating subordinate themes, the goal key is always V of the home key
8. These techniques are generally ABSENT from tight-knit main themes (their presence confirms subordinate-theme function)

# Construction / Recognition
- Note any shift from major to minor (or vice versa) within the subordinate theme
- Track the tonal regions tonicized -- are they remote relative to the subordinate key?
- Check whether the major modality is restored by the final cadence
- If the theme begins in a key different from where it ends, it is a modulating subordinate theme
- The absence of these techniques in the main theme confirms the contrast

# Context & Application
Harmonic-tonal loosening is one of the most powerful means of creating contrast between the subordinate theme and the main theme. The modal shift creates expressive variety and introduces harmonic color not available in a purely major-mode context. The tonicization of remote regions adds tonal adventure and instability. Modulating subordinate themes are seen most often in Beethoven and reflect his greater use of far-reaching tonal relationships.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i (Ch. 12, Ex. 12.8): modal shift and ascending-third sequential progression at the beginning; tonicization of bIII and even a tritone-distant region (Bb major = bIII/bIII)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in A, K. 331, first variation theme (Ch. 12, Ex. 12.11): modal shift at the beginning of expanded repetition; descending-fifth progression leads into bVI region; ECP with chromatic variant
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 31/3, ii, mm. 33-63 (Ch. 12, Ex. 12.14): modulating subordinate theme -- begins in F major (vi of home key), modulates to Eb (V of home key Ab)

# Common Errors
- Treating modal shift as a genuine modulation rather than a temporary coloring
- Failing to note the restoration of major mode at the final cadence
- Assuming all subordinate themes begin in the major mode (most do, but some begin with modal shift)

# Common Confusions
- **Modal shift vs. modulation**: A modal shift is temporary (major mode restored by the cadence); a modulating subordinate theme genuinely changes key
- **Remote regions**: bIII, bVI, bVII, bII are remote in a purely major context but become accessible through modal shift
- **Beethoven's boldness**: Modulating subordinate themes are primarily a Beethoven phenomenon, less common in Haydn and Mozart
