# Quick Definition
A compression technique in slow-movement sonata form where the main theme's final cadence is immediately followed by the subordinate theme, bypassing any semblance of transition function.

# Core Definition
In some slow-movement sonata expositions, the form becomes compressed when the final cadence of the main theme is immediately followed by the subordinate theme with no intervening transition. The lack of transition often results in the emphasis on dominant of the new key becoming shifted into the subordinate theme proper, usually through an internal half cadence and standing on the dominant within the subordinate theme itself. Although most common in slow movements, this technique may also appear in fast-movement rondo forms.

# Prerequisites
- **Slow-movement sonata form**: The formal context favoring this compression
- **Transition**: Understanding what is being eliminated
- **Subordinate theme**: The function that absorbs some transitional responsibilities

# Key Properties
1. Main theme's final cadence is immediately followed by subordinate theme
2. No modulation occurs between main theme and subordinate theme
3. Dominant emphasis is shifted into the subordinate theme (internal HC, standing on dominant)
4. Subordinate theme may begin directly in the subordinate key
5. Often associated with Haydn's monothematic approach
6. Recapitulation may require significant rewriting to avoid redundancy

# Construction / Recognition
- Locate the main theme's closing cadence (often a half cadence)
- Check whether the next unit begins directly in the subordinate key without any transitional material
- Look for dominant emphasis within the subordinate theme itself
- Note if the subordinate theme uses main-theme material (monothematic design)
- Anticipate significant recapitulatory consequences

# Context & Application
This technique is associated particularly with Haydn, whose monothematic approach sometimes eliminates the transition. The close proximity of main and subordinate themes in the exposition typically forces significant rewriting in the recapitulation to avoid redundant twofold succession of main-theme ideas in the home key. In extreme cases (Haydn, Hob. XVI:31, ii), the entire subordinate theme may be eliminated from the recapitulation.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E, Hob. XVI:31, ii, mm. 1-8, 33-49: Main theme closes with HC at m. 6; subordinate theme begins directly in G major with main-theme material; recapitulation (m. 33) eliminates the entire subordinate theme, ending with a standing on the dominant

# Common Errors
- Failing to recognize the absence of transition and misidentifying the subordinate theme's opening as transitional
- Not recognizing that the dominant emphasis has been displaced into the subordinate theme
- Expecting the recapitulation to follow the exposition's plan when monothematic design forces rewriting

# Common Confusions
- **Elimination vs. fusion**: Elimination removes the transition entirely; fusion combines both functions into one unit
- **Recapitulatory consequences**: The proximity of themes in the exposition often predicts significant recapitulatory changes, sometimes as radical as eliminating the entire subordinate theme
