# Quick Definition
The complete omission of transition function in a slow-movement sonata exposition, where the main theme's final cadence is immediately followed by the subordinate theme in the new key.

# Formal Definition
In some slow-movement sonata expositions, the form is compressed by eliminating the transition entirely. The final cadence of the main theme is followed directly by the subordinate theme, which begins immediately in the subordinate key. The lack of transition often results in the emphasis on the dominant of the new key becoming shifted into the subordinate theme proper, typically through an internal HC and standing on the dominant. This technique may also appear in fast-movement rondo forms.

# Construction/Recognition
- The main theme closes with a cadence (often a HC)
- The next thematic unit begins directly in the subordinate key with no intervening transitional material
- No modulation process is audible between themes
- The subordinate theme may compensate by including an internal HC and standing on the dominant
- Often associated with Haydn's "monothematicism," where the subordinate theme uses main-theme material

# Musical Context
This procedure is most common in slow movements, where it serves the goal of formal compression. Haydn favors this technique. When the subordinate theme is based on main-theme material (monothematicism), the recapitulation is typically significantly rewritten to avoid redundancy.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E, Hob. XVI:31, ii: The main theme closes with a HC at m. 6. The next unit begins directly in G major with main-theme material. The recapitulation (m. 33) eliminates the entire subordinate theme, ending instead with a standing on the dominant.

# Common Confusions
- The absence of a transition should not be confused with transition/subordinate-theme fusion: in elimination, there is no trace of transition function at all; in fusion, transition function is present but merged with subordinate-theme function.
