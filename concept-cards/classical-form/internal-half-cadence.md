# Quick Definition
A half cadence (or dominant arrival) within a subordinate theme that does not end the theme but rather divides it internally, with further material following to achieve the required PAC.

# Formal Definition
Sometimes a subordinate theme seems to end with a HC or dominant arrival, often followed by a postcadential standing on the dominant. Although a certain sense of ending is perceived, the listener knows that a more conclusive authentic cadence must eventually follow, since subordinate themes require a PAC. Because more material invariably follows, this is termed an internal half cadence (or internal dominant arrival). The appearance of an internal HC is most often motivated by how the transition ends: when the transition does not modulate and closes on the dominant of the home key, the absence of an emphasized subordinate-key dominant at the end of the transition is often rectified by an internal HC within the subordinate theme.

# Construction/Recognition
1. HC or dominant arrival within the subordinate theme (not at its end)
2. May be followed by a postcadential standing on the dominant
3. Two main strategies for what follows:
   - Resumption of continuation or cadential function leading to PAC
   - New basic idea in an initiating function (presentation), beginning a second part
4. When continuation resumes, there is no sense of a new theme beginning
5. When a new initiating function appears, a two-part subordinate theme results
6. Motivated especially by nonmodulating transitions (which lack subordinate-key dominant emphasis)

# Musical Context
The internal HC provides the subordinate-key dominant emphasis that was absent from the end of the transition. Because a nonmodulating transition closes on the home-key dominant rather than the subordinate-key dominant, the subordinate theme itself must provide this dominant articulation. The internal HC and its attendant standing on the dominant thus restore the expected dominant emphasis within the theme proper, before the music proceeds to the required PAC closure.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 576, iii, mm. 22-50 (nonmodulating transition; subordinate theme begins with main-theme material; internal HC at m. 34 followed by 10-measure standing on dominant; continuation resumes at m. 44)
- Mozart, Violin Concerto in A, K. 219, i, mm. 74-98 (internal HC at m. 80; new basic idea at m. 81 begins second part of subordinate theme)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C Minor, K. 457, i, mm. 23-37 (transition lacks functional end; subordinate theme leads to internal HC at m. 30 followed by standing on dominant)

# Common Confusions
- An internal HC is NOT the end of the subordinate theme -- a PAC must follow for the theme to be complete
- Not every dominant expansion within a subordinate theme is an internal HC -- the dominant of an expanded cadential progression is a penultimate harmony, not an ending harmony
- When a new basic idea follows the internal HC, the question arises whether this is a new subordinate theme or the second part of a single theme -- Caplin prefers the two-part interpretation to maintain the requirement that subordinate themes end with PAC
- The internal HC is distinct from the HC ending a transition; they serve different formal functions
