# Quick Definition
A half cadence (or dominant arrival) within a subordinate theme that does not end the theme but rather divides it internally, with further material following to achieve the required PAC.

# Core Definition
Sometimes a subordinate theme seems to end with a HC or dominant arrival, often followed by a postcadential standing on the dominant. Although a certain sense of ending is perceived, the listener knows that a more conclusive authentic cadence must eventually follow, since subordinate themes require a PAC. Because more material invariably follows, this is termed an internal half cadence (or internal dominant arrival). The appearance of an internal HC is most often motivated by how the transition ends: when the transition does not modulate and closes on the home-key dominant, the absence of an emphasized subordinate-key dominant is often rectified by an internal HC within the subordinate theme.

# Prerequisites
- **Subordinate theme**: The formal context (must end with PAC, not HC)
- **Half cadence**: The cadential type appearing internally
- **Standing on the dominant**: Often follows the internal HC

# Key Properties
1. HC or dominant arrival within the subordinate theme (not at its end)
2. May be followed by a postcadential standing on the dominant
3. Two main strategies for what follows:
   - Resumption of continuation or cadential function leading to PAC
   - New basic idea in an initiating function, beginning a two-part subordinate theme
4. Motivated especially by nonmodulating transitions (which lack subordinate-key dominant emphasis)
5. Provides the subordinate-key dominant emphasis absent from the transition
6. Not every dominant expansion within a ST is an internal HC

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify a HC or dominant arrival that occurs within (not at the end of) the subordinate theme
- Check whether a nonmodulating transition preceded the theme (common motivator)
- Determine what follows: continuation/cadential resumption or new initiating function
- If new initiating function appears, consider a two-part subordinate theme interpretation
- Distinguish from dominant of an expanded cadential progression (penultimate, not ultimate harmony)
- Note that the dominant of a passing-chord pattern (e.g., VII diminished seventh) may not constitute a true internal HC

# Context & Application
The internal HC restores the subordinate-key dominant emphasis that was absent from the transition. In nonmodulating transitions, the final dominant is in the home key, not the subordinate key. The subordinate theme thus takes on part of the transition's role by providing its own dominant articulation before proceeding to PAC closure.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 576, iii, mm. 22-50: nonmodulating transition; subordinate theme begins with main-theme material; internal HC at m. 34 followed by 10-measure standing on dominant; continuation resumes at m. 44
- Mozart, Violin Concerto in A, K. 219, i, mm. 74-98: internal HC at m. 80; new basic idea at m. 81 begins second part of subordinate theme (two-part ST)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C Minor, K. 457, i, mm. 23-37: transition lacks functional end; subordinate theme leads to internal HC at m. 30 followed by standing on dominant

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Half cadence (the cadential type appearing internally)
- Subordinate theme organization (the formal context)

## Enables
- Two-part subordinate theme (when new initiating function follows)
- Restored dominant emphasis (compensates for transition's missing subordinate-key dominant)

## Related
- Nonmodulating transition (the most common motivating context)
- Standing on the dominant (often follows the internal HC)
- Dominant arrival (alternative when cadential articulation is unclear)

## Contrasts With
- Half cadence ending a transition (different formal function)
- Dominant of expanded cadential progression (penultimate, not ultimate harmony)

# Common Errors
- Treating every dominant expansion in a subordinate theme as an internal HC
- Confusing the dominant of an ECP (penultimate harmony) with an internal HC (ending harmony)
- Analyzing the passage after an internal HC as a new subordinate theme rather than a second part

# Common Confusions
- **Internal HC vs. end of theme**: An internal HC is NOT the end of the subordinate theme -- a PAC must follow
- **Internal HC vs. ECP dominant**: The dominant of an expanded cadential progression is penultimate (within the progression), not an ending harmony
- **Two themes vs. two-part theme**: When new material follows an internal HC, Caplin prefers interpreting it as a two-part subordinate theme (maintaining the PAC requirement) rather than two separate themes where the first ends with HC
