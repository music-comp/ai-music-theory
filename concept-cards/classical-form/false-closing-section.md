# Quick Definition
Material following a PAC that initially appears to be a postcadential closing section (codettas) but is retrospectively understood as the beginning of a new subordinate theme, transition, or coda theme.

# Formal Definition
Within a subordinate-theme group, a true closing section appears only after the final theme. The PACs of any prior themes may be followed by material with prominent codetta characteristics (tonic pedal, prolongation of ^1 in the soprano, recessive dynamic). The subsequent development of this material, however, is revealed retrospectively to function as the beginning of a new subordinate theme. In most cases, the codetta-like idea can be reinterpreted as a basic idea whose repetition creates a presentation phrase. The false closing section also appears in transitions (where codettas following the main theme's PAC merge into transition material) and at the junction of recapitulation and coda (where closing-section material becomes the start of a coda theme).

# Construction/Recognition
1. Follows a PAC (of a subordinate theme, main theme, or recapitulation)
2. Material has prominent codetta characteristics: tonic pedal, ^1 prolongation, recessive dynamic
3. Subsequent development reveals the passage as the BEGINNING of a new theme or transition
4. Codetta-like idea reinterpreted as basic idea; repetition creates a presentation
5. **Grouping-structure test**: If codettas are followed by a LARGER unit, they likely form a genuine closing section; if followed by a SMALLER unit (fragmentation), they likely begin a transition or new theme
6. Recessive dynamic gives way to progressive dynamic as the new theme develops

# Musical Context
False closing sections are an important formal ambiguity in classical music. They exploit the listener's expectation that a PAC will be followed by a postcadential closing section, only to redirect this expectation when the material develops into something more substantial. The technique appears in three main contexts: (1) between subordinate themes in a group, (2) between the main theme and a transition, and (3) at the junction of recapitulation and coda. The same material can function as a false closing section in one context and a genuine closing section in another.

# Examples
- Mozart, Symphony No. 39, K. 543, ii, mm. 54-59 (PAC of subordinate theme elides with codetta-like material; 8-measure sentence emerges as second subordinate theme; when theme repeats at m. 60, the SAME ideas function as genuine codettas)
- Haydn, String Quartet in B Minor, Op. 64/2, i, mm. 20-23 (first subordinate theme ends PAC at m. 20; codetta-like idea over tonic pedal proves false when evaded cadence at m. 26 destroys closing-section impression)
- Mozart, Piano Trio in D Minor, K. 442, iii, mm. 16-26 (false closing section to main theme: codettas merge into transition)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 31/3, i, mm. 8-18 (false closing section after main theme leading into transition)

# Common Confusions
- A false closing section is only recognized RETROSPECTIVELY -- at the moment of hearing, it sounds like a genuine closing section
- The grouping-structure test (larger vs. smaller subsequent units) is a useful heuristic but not infallible
- The same musical material can function as a false closing section in one context and a genuine closing section in another
- A false closing section at the start of a transition differs from one between subordinate themes, though the mechanism is the same
