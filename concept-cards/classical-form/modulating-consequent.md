# Quick Definition
A modulating consequent closes with a PAC in a subordinate key (usually the dominant) rather than the home key. Despite moving to a new key, the cadence is still considered "stronger" than the antecedent's weak cadence because cadential strength is measured by harmonic-melodic closure *within* a key, not by tonal proximity to the home key.

# Core Definition
Most periods conclude in the key in which they begin. Often, however, the consequent phrase modulates, and the theme closes with a PAC in a related tonality. Cadential strength must be understood by distinguishing two types of pitch closure: (1) the degree of harmonic-melodic closure within a key (or keys), and (2) the degree of tonal closure exhibited by a succession of keys in relation to the home key. Relative strength of cadences is established on the first basis. When the antecedent ends with a weak cadence, a harmonic or melodic process remains open. When the consequent achieves a PAC in a new key, full harmonic-melodic closure in that key has been achieved. The consequent's cadence is therefore stronger than the antecedent's despite the tonal openness.

# Construction / Recognition
1. Consequent phrase modulates to a related key (most commonly the dominant)
2. Closes with a PAC in the new key
3. The antecedent always closes in the home key (one exception: reinterpreted HC)
4. Cadential strength is judged by harmonic-melodic closure, not tonal proximity
5. A modulating period rarely stands alone — it usually forms part of a larger thematic unit (small ternary, small binary)

# Context & Application
Modulating periods are common as the first part of small ternary or small binary forms. The modulation to a subordinate key creates tonal tension that must eventually be resolved by a return to the home key. The antecedent must first confirm the home key with a cadence (albeit weak) before the consequent modulates — otherwise, the home key would lack sufficient tonal presence.

# Examples
- Modulating consequents typically close with PAC in the dominant region, establishing the subordinate key for the subsequent formal section

# Common Confusions
- A PAC in a subordinate key is still a "stronger" cadence than an HC in the home key — cadential strength is about harmonic-melodic completeness within a key, not about tonal distance from the home key
- A modulating period creates tonal openness (requiring eventual return to the home key) but cadential closedness (the new key is fully confirmed)
- The antecedent must remain in the home key — if it modulated and the consequent stayed in the subordinate key, the home key would be insufficiently established
