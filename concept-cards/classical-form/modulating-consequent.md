# Quick Definition
A modulating consequent closes with a PAC in a subordinate key (usually the dominant) rather than the home key. Despite moving to a new key, the cadence is still considered "stronger" than the antecedent's weak cadence because cadential strength is measured by harmonic-melodic closure within a key, not by tonal proximity to the home key.

# Core Definition
"Most periods conclude in the key in which they begin. Now and then, the consequent phrase modulates, and the theme closes with a PAC in a related tonality" (Ch. 3, p. 90). Caplin distinguishes two types of pitch closure: harmonic-melodic closure within a key, and tonal closure in relation to the home key. "The syntactical strength of a cadence is determined on the basis of its type, not on its tonal context" (Ch. 3, p. 90). Thus the HC of the opening key remains weaker than the PAC of the related key. "Unlike the consequent, which is free to modulate, an antecedent phrase closes in the same key in which it begins" (Ch. 3, p. 90), with one major exception: the reinterpreted half cadence.

# Prerequisites
- **Consequent** -- the concluding phrase of the period
- **Period** -- the theme type built on cadential differentiation
- **Cadential strength** -- the hierarchy HC < IAC < PAC

# Key Properties
1. Consequent modulates to a related key (most commonly the dominant)
2. Closes with a PAC in the new key
3. The antecedent always closes in the home key (one exception: reinterpreted HC)
4. Cadential strength is judged by harmonic-melodic closure, not tonal proximity
5. A modulating period rarely stands alone -- it usually forms part of a larger thematic unit
6. Syntactical cadential strength is independent of tonal context

# Construction / Recognition
1. Antecedent closes with a weak cadence (HC or IAC) in the home key
2. Consequent begins with a return of the basic idea (possibly reharmonized)
3. A modulation occurs during the consequent
4. Consequent closes with a PAC in the new key
5. The PAC in the subordinate key is still syntactically "stronger" than the antecedent's HC

# Context & Application
Modulating periods are common as the first part of small ternary or small binary forms. The modulation to a subordinate key creates tonal tension that must eventually be resolved by a return to the home key. The antecedent must first confirm the home key with a cadence (albeit weak) before the consequent modulates.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 3, p. 90, ex. 3.18): Haydn, Symphony No. 99 in E-flat, iv, mm. 1-8: antecedent ends with HC in E-flat; consequent modulates and closes with PAC in B-flat (dominant).

# Common Errors
- Treating a subordinate-key PAC as "weaker" because it is not in the home key
- Confusing cadential strength (within a key) with tonal closure (relation to home key)
- Assuming the antecedent may also modulate (it may not, except via reinterpreted HC)

# Common Confusions
- A PAC in a subordinate key is still a "stronger" cadence than an HC in the home key -- cadential strength is about harmonic-melodic completeness within a key, not about tonal distance from the home key
- A modulating period creates tonal openness (requiring eventual return to the home key) but cadential closedness (the new key is fully confirmed)
- The antecedent must remain in the home key -- if it modulated and the consequent stayed in the subordinate key, the home key would be insufficiently established
