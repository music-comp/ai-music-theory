# Quick Definition
The transition serves three primary functions: (1) to destabilize the home key so the subordinate key can emerge as a competitor, (2) to loosen the formal organization as a form of phrase-structural destabilization, and (3) to liquidate characteristic motivic material to "clear the stage" for the subordinate theme's entrance.

# Core Definition
The transition of a sonata exposition serves three primary functions: (1) To destabilize the home key, so that the subordinate key can emerge as a competing tonality within the exposition. (2) To loosen the formal organization, a form of phrase-structural destabilization. (3) To liquidate characteristic motivic material (especially toward the transition's end), in order to "clear the stage" for the entrance of the subordinate theme. The harmonic goal of a transition is dominant harmony, most often of the subordinate key but occasionally of the home key. This dominant is usually articulated by means of a half cadence, though a noncadential dominant arrival may be used for an even looser formal effect. Transitions can be classified into three basic categories depending on their tonal organization: modulating, nonmodulating, and two-part.

# Prerequisites
- **Transition**: The formal unit in question
- **Main-theme function in sonata**: The preceding tight-knit unit
- **Subordinate theme**: The following unit prepared by the transition

# Key Properties
1. Function 1 (destabilize): abandon the home key through modulation or weaken it through dominant emphasis
2. Function 2 (loosen): phrase-structural instability through asymmetry, extensions, nonconventional form
3. Function 3 (liquidate): reduce motivic material toward the transition's end; "clear the stage"
4. Harmonic goal: dominant harmony (subordinate key or home key)
5. Typically articulated by HC, but also by dominant arrival or no ending at all
6. Three tonal categories: modulating, nonmodulating, two-part
7. The term "bridge" is sometimes used as an alternative label
8. The transition is a MEDIAL thematic function within the exposition

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the passage between the main theme's cadence and the subordinate theme's beginning
- Check for tonal destabilization (modulation, chromatic alteration, dominant emphasis)
- Note formal loosening (asymmetrical groupings, extensions, sequential passages)
- Look for liquidation of motivic material toward the end
- Locate the harmonic goal (HC, dominant arrival, or omission of ending function)
- Classify as modulating, nonmodulating, or two-part

# Context & Application
The three primary functions work together to create the transition's characteristic formal trajectory. Destabilization undermines the home key's authority. Loosening creates the sense of being "between" two stable points. Liquidation prepares a clean entrance for the subordinate theme's new material. Understanding these three functions helps distinguish the transition from other formal units and explains why transitions are organized the way they are.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F minor, Op. 2/1, i, mm. 9-20 (Ch. 9, Ex. 9.2): destabilization through C minor harmony disrupting F minor; loosening through asymmetrical grouping (2+6+4) and extended continuation; liquidation within standing on dominant; HC at m. 16 in A-flat major (modulating transition)
- The three categories (modulating, nonmodulating, two-part) are systematically presented in Ch. 11

# Common Errors
- Treating the transition as merely "the passage that modulates" (it has three distinct functions)
- Ignoring the liquidation function (essential for understanding the transition-ST boundary)
- Confusing formal loosening in the transition with disorganization

# Common Confusions
- **Not just modulation**: The transition destabilizes, loosens, AND liquidates
- **Bridge vs. transition**: "Bridge" is an alternative label but "transition" better captures the formal function
- **Medial function**: The transition is the MEDIAL thematic function of the exposition, standing between MT and ST
