# Quick Definition
A short unit (rarely more than two measures, sometimes just one or two chords) that precedes the beginning of a theme, consisting of minimal motivic content -- often purely accompanimental figurations -- that prolongs tonic harmony in root position and typically exhibits a progressive dynamic.

# Core Definition
A thematic introduction is a short unit that precedes the theme's beginning. Its motivic content is minimal so as not to project any sense of being a basic idea. It often consists exclusively of accompanimental figurations that continue after the beginning of the theme. Most thematic introductions prolong tonic harmony in root position. The introduction typically features a progressive dynamic (increasing buildup of energy and anticipation through changes in texture, rhythmic activity, timbre, and intensity). The thematic introduction is a "before-the-beginning" function -- it belongs to the theme it frames but does not fall within the theme's literal formal boundaries.

# Prerequisites
- **Formal function**: The framework for understanding the introduction's role
- **Basic idea**: The thematic element that the introduction precedes and must not be confused with

# Key Properties
1. Short: rarely more than two measures; sometimes just one or two chords
2. Minimal motivic content -- must NOT project a sense of basic idea
3. Often consists of purely accompanimental figurations
4. Figurations typically continue into the theme itself
5. Prolongs tonic harmony in root position
6. Progressive dynamic: increasing energy, crescendo, accumulating texture
7. Creates anticipation for the appearance of a distinctive melody
8. Not to be confused with a slow introduction (which is a much larger formal unit)

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify material that precedes the first basic idea of the theme
2. Confirm that the material has minimal melodic-motivic content
3. Check for accompanimental figurations that continue into the theme
4. Verify tonic prolongation (usually root position)
5. Note progressive dynamic (if present)
6. Count measures from the basic idea's entrance (the introduction is "measure 0")

# Context & Application
Thematic introductions are relatively common in the classical repertory. They serve to establish the tonal and textural context before the theme proper begins. The progressive dynamic creates anticipation that is resolved by the entrance of the melodic material. The lack of melodic content is crucial: if the introduction contained a genuine basic idea, it would become part of the theme's formal structure rather than a framing function.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in B-flat, Op. 22, ii, m. 1: left-hand "vamp" figure constitutes a thematic introduction; theme begins at upbeat to m. 2; m. 2 is counted as the "first" bar of the theme
- Beethoven, String Quartet in F, Op. 135, iii, mm. 1-2: progressive dynamic manifested by crescendo and accumulating texture; lack of melodic material creates anticipation
- Beethoven, Violin Concerto in D, Op. 61, i, m. 1: solo timpani's four quarter notes serve as thematic introduction to the compound sentence

# Common Errors
- Confusing a thematic introduction with a slow introduction -- they are entirely different in scale and function
- Including the introduction in the measure count of the theme -- the theme begins with the basic idea
- Mistaking a genuine basic idea for an introduction (or vice versa)

# Common Confusions
- A thematic introduction is NOT a slow introduction -- the slow introduction precedes an entire exposition section, not a single theme
- The theme begins with the basic idea, NOT with the introduction -- count measures accordingly
- If the material has genuine motivic content projecting a basic idea, it is NOT an introduction but part of the theme
