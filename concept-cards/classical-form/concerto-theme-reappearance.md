# Quick Definition
The principle that when an alternative main theme is used in the solo exposition, the ritornello main theme should promptly reappear, usually at the beginning of the solo transition.

# Core Definition
Principle 2 of concerto-form composition states that the use of an alternative main theme prompts the immediate reappearance of material from the ritornello main theme. Since the ritornello main theme is the movement's opening and most significant idea, it should not be abandoned simply because the solo has introduced its own theme. The most common scenario has the ritornello main theme beginning the solo transition. Alternatively, the ritornello main theme may appear as a "second" main theme forming part of a main-theme group (after which the transition will use new material, per Principle 1). Sometimes the ritornello transition is used for the solo transition since the succession is already different due to the alternative main theme.

# Construction / Recognition
- Triggered by the use of an alternative main theme in the solo exposition
- The ritornello main theme typically reappears at one of these locations:
  - Beginning of the solo transition (most common)
  - As a "second" main theme in a main-theme group
  - In the solo transition when the ritornello transition is also used
- Ensures that the ritornello's most important material is not lost
- Works in conjunction with Principle 1 (succession avoidance)

# Context & Application
This principle ensures that the ritornello's opening material -- the movement's most prominent idea -- maintains its presence throughout the form, even when the solo introduces its own main theme. It reflects the concerto's need to balance the identities of orchestra and soloist.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Concerto in D Minor, K. 466, i: The orchestral opening (mm. 1-4) is highly orchestral and never imitated by the piano. The alternative main theme (m. 77) is followed by the reappearance of ritornello material in the transition.

# Common Confusions
- Principle 2 does not require the ritornello main theme to return in exactly its original form. It may be varied, truncated, or placed in a new formal context (e.g., functioning as a transition rather than a main theme).
- Both principles (1 and 2) work together to govern the distribution of material in the solo exposition. They are complementary, not contradictory.
