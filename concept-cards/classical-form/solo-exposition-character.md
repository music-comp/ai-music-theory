# Quick Definition
The solo exposition functions like a sonata exposition but almost always contains new material for the soloist, does not conclude with a closing section (the subordinate-key ritornello takes that role), and can already represent a "development" of ritornello material through reordering, variation, and greater sequential activity.

# Core Definition
The entrance of the solo initiates the solo exposition, which functions essentially like a sonata exposition, containing main theme, transition, and subordinate-theme group and effecting a modulation to the subordinate key. Unlike a sonata exposition, the solo exposition does not conclude with a closing section but ends with the final PAC of the subordinate-theme group; the subordinate-key ritornello then serves as the closing-section analogue. The solo exposition rarely represents a mere repetition of the opening ritornello. Rather, it almost always contains new material for the solo part to present on its own, while also bringing back a considerable amount of earlier music in varied form or new formal contexts. Consequently, the solo exposition can already represent a "development" of previously heard material and often contains greater sequential activity than a regular sonata exposition. After the long buildup of the ritornello, the solo entrance is a dramatic event, most often preceded by a complete stop at the end of the opening ritornello (though sometimes the solo enters while the orchestra is still closing) (Caplin, pp. 706-707).

# Key Properties
1. Functions like a sonata exposition with main theme, transition, subordinate-theme group
2. Does not end with a closing section (subordinate-key ritornello serves that role)
3. Almost always introduces new material for the soloist
4. Brings back ritornello material in varied or new formal contexts
5. Can already constitute a "development" of ritornello ideas
6. Greater sequential activity than a typical sonata exposition
7. Solo entrance is a dramatic event, usually preceded by a complete stop

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the solo entrance (dramatic event after the ritornello)
2. Determine which material is new and which derives from the ritornello
3. Note formal recontextualizations of ritornello ideas
4. Observe the greater sequential activity compared to a symphonic exposition
5. The section ends with the final PAC (cadential trill), not a closing section

# Context & Application
The solo exposition is where the soloist first asserts individuality through new material and claims the principal modulatory action of the movement. Understanding it as both an exposition and a partial development of ritornello material is key to grasping concerto form's complexity.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 707): Mozart, Piano Concerto in D minor, K. 466, i, mm. 77-88 -- solo entrance with alternative main theme, dramatically different in character from the orchestral opening.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Expecting the solo exposition to copy the opening ritornello. **Correction**: The solo exposition almost always introduces new material and reorders ritornello ideas.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Why the solo exposition has greater sequential activity than a sonata exposition. **Clarification**: The reappearance of ritornello material in new contexts creates opportunities for developmental treatment that would not arise in a single-exposition sonata.
