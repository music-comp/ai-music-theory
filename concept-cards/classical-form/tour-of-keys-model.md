# Quick Definition
A complementary view of sonata form's tonal plan, derived from baroque practice, that sees the subordinate key as the first of various keys systematically explored throughout the movement.

# Formal Definition
The tour-of-keys model is derived from earlier baroque practice, in which compositions present one fundamental melodic idea in the home key and then systematically explore its possibilities by setting it in a variety of related keys. In the classical period, the tonal-polarity model gained dominance, but the tour-of-keys model continued to have an effect. Its influence is most evident when the development confirms one or more keys by authentic cadence, when extensive exposition material reappears transposed in the development (especially in Haydn), and when the recapitulation emphasizes the subdominant.

# Construction/Recognition
- Subordinate key in exposition = first key explored beyond the home key
- Development keys = further exploration of the tonal spectrum
- Authentic cadences in the development confirm new keys (as in baroque practice)
- Extensive restatement of exposition material in new keys (characteristic of Haydn)
- Subdominant emphasis in the recapitulation = a vestige of the model
- Both tour-of-keys and tonal-polarity models are needed for a complete picture

# Musical Context
Taken together with the tonal-polarity model, the tour-of-keys model provides a fairly complete picture of the classical sonata's large-scale tonal design. The polarity model emphasizes the oppositional nature of two primary keys; the tour-of-keys model emphasizes tonal diversity throughout the movement.

# Examples
- Haydn frequently restates exposition material transposed into development keys, reflecting the tour-of-keys model
- Subdominant emphasis in the recapitulation (e.g., Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62) is a vestige of this model

# Common Confusions
- The tour-of-keys model is not an alternative to the tonal-polarity model — they are complementary
- In the classical period, the tonal-polarity model is primary; the tour-of-keys model operates as a secondary influence
- Haydn's practice is closer to the tour-of-keys model than Mozart's or Beethoven's
