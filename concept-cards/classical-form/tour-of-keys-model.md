# Quick Definition
A complementary view of sonata form's tonal plan, derived from baroque practice, that sees the subordinate key as the first of various keys systematically explored throughout the movement, complementing the tonal-polarity model's emphasis on opposition between two primary keys.

# Core Definition
The tour-of-keys model is derived from earlier baroque practice, in which compositions present one fundamental melodic idea in the home key and then systematically explore its possibilities by setting it in a variety of related keys. In the classical period, the tonal-polarity model gained dominance, but the tour-of-keys model continued to exert influence. Its effect is most evident when the development confirms one or more keys by authentic cadence, when extensive exposition material reappears transposed in the development (especially characteristic of Haydn), and when the recapitulation emphasizes the subdominant. The tour-of-keys model is particularly relevant to development sections, where the systematic exploration of minor-mode regions (VI, III, II in major; IV, V in minor) reflects the baroque impulse to traverse the tonal landscape.

# Prerequisites
- **Sonata form**: The formal framework within which the model operates
- **Development key**: Keys confirmed in the development section
- **Tonal organization of development**: The broader tonal plan the model helps explain

# Key Properties
1. Derived from baroque practice of exploring a melodic idea in multiple keys
2. Subordinate key = first key explored beyond the home key
3. Development keys = further exploration of the tonal spectrum
4. Authentic cadences in the development confirm new keys (as in baroque practice)
5. Extensive restatement of exposition material in new keys (characteristic of Haydn)
6. Subdominant emphasis in the recapitulation = a vestige of this model
7. Both tour-of-keys and tonal-polarity models are needed for a complete picture
8. Haydn's practice is closest to the baroque tour-of-keys model

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for extensive restatement of exposition material transposed into development keys
- Identify authentic cadences confirming development keys (not just tonicizations)
- Note subdominant emphasis in the recapitulation
- Observe whether the development seems to "visit" a series of keys systematically
- Compare with the tonal-polarity model: if key contrast seems more about diversity than opposition, the tour-of-keys model is at work

# Context & Application
Taken together with the tonal-polarity model, the tour-of-keys model provides a fairly complete picture of the classical sonata's large-scale tonal design. The polarity model emphasizes the oppositional nature of two primary keys; the tour-of-keys model emphasizes tonal diversity throughout the movement. The development section is the primary locus of tour-of-keys influence, as it is there that new keys are explored and cadentially confirmed. Haydn's development sections, which frequently restate exposition material in transposed forms, are the clearest classical expression of this baroque heritage.

# Examples
- Haydn frequently restates exposition material transposed into development keys, directly reflecting the tour-of-keys model
- Subdominant emphasis in the recapitulation (e.g., Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62) is a vestige of the model
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in B-flat, K. 333, i: development confirms Bb through systematic tonal exploration before returning to home key

# Common Errors
- Treating the tour-of-keys model as an alternative to the tonal-polarity model (they are complementary)
- Applying the tour-of-keys model equally to all classical composers (Haydn reflects it most)
- Ignoring the baroque origins of the model

# Common Confusions
- **Not an alternative**: The tour-of-keys model is not opposed to the tonal-polarity model; they are complementary
- **Period priority**: In the classical period, the tonal-polarity model is primary; the tour-of-keys model operates as a secondary influence
- **Composer differences**: Haydn's practice is closer to the tour-of-keys model than Mozart's or Beethoven's
