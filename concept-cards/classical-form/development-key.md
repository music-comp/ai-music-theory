# Quick Definition
A key of lower structural rank than the home key or subordinate key, typically confirmed by a half cadence or dominant arrival in the development section, and usually drawn from minor-mode regions.

# Formal Definition
Development keys occupy a middle level in the tonal hierarchy, below the home key and subordinate key but above tonicized regions. Although they may at times be confirmed by an authentic cadence, more often a half cadence or dominant arrival provides their minimal confirmation. If multiple development keys appear, one is designated primary (strongest cadential articulation, usually the final key) and others secondary (weaker articulation, appearing earlier).

# Construction/Recognition
- Confirmed by at least a half cadence or dominant arrival
- The primary development key receives the most durational, dynamic, and textural emphasis
- In major: typically VI, III, or II (minor-mode regions)
- In minor: typically IV or V (minor)
- Beethoven expands the palette with borrowed keys: bVI, bIII, minor V

# Musical Context
Development keys provide the essential tonal contrast in the development section. The primary development key usually coincides with the core of the development.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Hob. XVI:49, i: C minor (HK: VI) as primary, F minor (HK: II) as secondary
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in G, Op. 14/2, i: Bb major (HK: bIII) via modal borrowing

# Common Confusions
- Development keys differ from the subordinate key in that they do not require authentic cadential confirmation
- A development key is not simply any key touched in the development -- it must receive cadential articulation
- The subdominant rarely serves as a primary development key in major
