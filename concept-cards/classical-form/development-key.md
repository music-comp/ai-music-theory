# Quick Definition
A key of lower structural rank than the home key or subordinate key, confirmed by at least a half cadence or dominant arrival in the development section, usually drawn from minor-mode regions.

# Core Definition
Development keys occupy a middle level in the tonal hierarchy, below the home key and subordinate key but above tonicized regions. Although they may at times be confirmed by an authentic cadence, more often a half cadence or dominant arrival provides their minimal confirmation. If multiple development keys appear, one is designated primary (strongest cadential articulation, usually the final key before the retransition) and others secondary (weaker articulation, appearing earlier). In major-mode movements, VI, III, and II are the most common development keys. In minor-mode movements, IV and V (minor) are used with approximately equal frequency. Beethoven expands the palette with borrowed keys: bVI, bIII, minor V.

# Key Properties
1. Confirmed by at least HC or dominant arrival (distinguishes from tonicized regions)
2. Structural rank below home key and subordinate key
3. Primary development key: strongest cadential articulation, usually final key before retransition
4. Secondary development key: weaker articulation, appears earlier
5. In major: typically VI, III, or II (minor-mode regions)
6. In minor: typically IV or V (minor)
7. Beethoven's expanded palette: bVI, bIII, minor V
8. The primary development key usually coincides with the core

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify keys that receive cadential confirmation (HC, dominant arrival, or PAC) in the development
- Distinguish from tonicized regions (which lack cadential confirmation)
- Determine primary vs. secondary based on strength of articulation and position
- Note whether the key provides modal contrast with the exposition

# Context & Application
Development keys provide the essential tonal contrast in the development section. The primary development key usually coincides with the core. The emphasis on minor-mode regions creates contrast with the major-mode exposition.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Hob. XVI:49, i: C minor (HK: VI) as primary, F minor (HK: II) as secondary
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in G, Op. 14/2, i: Bb major (HK: bIII) via modal borrowing
- Mozart, Symphony No. 36 ("Linz"), K. 425, i: A minor (HK: VI) as primary

# Common Errors
- Treating any key touched in the development as a development key (cadential confirmation required)
- Confusing tonicized regions with development keys
- Assuming the subdominant is a common development key in major (it is not)

# Common Confusions
- **Development key vs. tonicized region**: Development keys receive cadential confirmation; tonicized regions do not
- **Development key vs. subordinate key**: Subordinate keys require PAC; development keys need only HC or dominant arrival
- **Primary vs. secondary**: Primary receives strongest articulation and is usually the final key; secondary appears earlier with weaker articulation
