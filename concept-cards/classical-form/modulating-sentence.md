# Quick Definition
A modulating sentence closes with a cadence in a new, closely related key (most often the dominant or relative major). The modulation occurs within the continuation phrase, while the presentation remains firmly in the home key.

# Core Definition
"All of the themes presented thus far close in the key in which they begin. Occasionally, though, the continuation phrase modulates to a new, closely related key. Most often the new key is the dominant region of the opening key (for example C major modulating to G major), though the mediant ('relative major') is frequently used if the opening key is minor (C minor to E-flat major)" (Ch. 2, p. 66). The presentation phrase remains firmly in the home key through its tonic prolongation. The modulation takes place during the continuation, typically confirmed by a PAC in the new key.

# Prerequisites
- **Sentence** -- the fundamental theme type being modified
- **Cadential progression** -- the progression that confirms the new key

# Key Properties
1. Presentation phrase remains in the home key (tonic prolongation)
2. Modulation occurs within the continuation phrase
3. Most common modulation: to the dominant (e.g., C major to G major)
4. In minor keys: often to the mediant/relative major (e.g., C minor to E-flat major)
5. Confirmed by a PAC in the new key
6. A modulating sentence rarely stands alone -- it usually forms part of a larger formal unit

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the presentation phrase: basic idea + repetition, tonic prolongational
2. Identify the continuation phrase: fragmentation, harmonic acceleration
3. Look for a modulation during the continuation
4. Confirm: does the cadence occur in a new key?
5. If yes: modulating sentence
6. Check that the V-I motion at the end of the presentation is NOT a cadence (it is part of the tonic prolongation)

# Context & Application
Modulating sentences commonly serve as main themes in sonata expositions, minuets, and other formal contexts where the opening theme needs to establish forward harmonic motion. They also appear as components of larger forms (small ternary, small binary). Like modulating periods, modulating sentences create tonal tension requiring eventual resolution through a return to the home key.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 2, p. 66, ex. 2.28): Haydn, Piano Trio in E, Hob. XV:12, iii, mm. 1-8: "following the fragmentation in mm. 5-6, the cadential idea quickly brings a modulation to the dominant region, as confirmed by a PAC." Note that V7-I progressions at mm. 4 and 6 are NOT cadences because they support repetitions (presentation and fragmentation respectively).

# Common Errors
- Hearing a cadence at the end of the presentation when the V-I motion is prolongational
- Assuming the modulation occurs in the presentation (it occurs in the continuation)
- Confusing a PAC in a modulating sentence with a plagal cadence

# Common Confusions
- A V7-I progression at the boundary of the presentation does not create a cadence -- it supports a repetition of the basic idea, which is an initiating, not closing, function
- Modulating sentences are not "irregular" -- they are a standard variation of the sentence form
- The new key is typically closely related (dominant or relative major), not remote
