# Quick Definition
A highly complex recapitulation whose structure corresponds only minimally to the exposition, with ideas shifted, reorganized, and new material introduced, yet typically retaining the overall cadential pattern.

# Core Definition
In some highly complex cases, the recapitulation may be altered to the extent that its structure corresponds only minimally to the exposition. Ideas from the earlier section may be incorporated into the later one, but they are shifted about to produce quite a different formal plan. Moreover, new material may appear that is not even derived from the exposition. Despite these radical surface differences, the overall shape of a standard recapitulation -- including the traditional cadential articulations (PAC for main theme, dominant arrival for transition, PAC for subordinate themes) -- may be retained. This calls into question the very idea of adopting the formal labels of the exposition for use in the recapitulation.

# Prerequisites
- **Recapitulation**: The formal context

# Key Properties
1. Motivic material from the exposition is rearranged into a different formal plan
2. New, non-derived material may appear
3. The order of ideas differs significantly from the exposition
4. Despite surface differences, the underlying cadential pattern may correspond
5. Formal labels (main theme, transition, subordinate theme) become difficult to assign based on motivic content
6. Cadential articulation provides the most reliable guide to formal function
7. Especially characteristic of Haydn
8. Calls into question the applicability of exposition labels to the recapitulation

# Construction / Recognition
- Compare motivic content of exposition and recapitulation -- note radical rearrangements
- Map cadential articulations in both sections
- Despite different motivic ordering, look for preserved cadential pattern
- Use cadential function rather than motivic identity to assign formal labels

# Context & Application
Minimal correspondence recapitulations are especially characteristic of Haydn, who delights in formal surprise. They challenge the analyst to look beyond motivic identity to cadential and functional criteria. From the point of view of motivic ordering, the recapitulation has little relation to the exposition; from the point of view of cadential articulation, the connection is clearer.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in B Minor, Op. 64/2, i (ex. 11.18): recapitulation redistributes exposition material entirely; main theme followed by transition material functioning as subordinate theme; original subordinate theme material serves transition function; new material appears in mm. 80-83 and mm. 95-97; yet the cadential pattern is preserved (m. 77 PAC closing main theme; m. 80 premature dominant arrival closing transition; m. 92 PAC closing first subordinate theme; m. 102 PAC closing second subordinate theme)

# Common Errors
- Assigning formal labels based on motivic content rather than cadential function
- Assuming the recapitulation must mirror the exposition's motivic ordering

# Common Confusions
- **Motivic vs. cadential identity**: Even the most radically altered recapitulation typically preserves the fundamental cadential pattern
- **Haydn's specialty**: This degree of alteration is especially characteristic of Haydn; Mozart and Beethoven rarely go this far
- **Still a recapitulation**: Despite the radical rearrangement, recapitulation function is still fulfilled through cadential articulation and tonal resolution
