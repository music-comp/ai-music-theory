# Quick Definition
Melodic ambiguity arises when an ornamented melody makes it difficult to determine whether the final note of a cadence is on ^1 (PAC) or ^3 (IAC). The broader formal context — particularly whether the phrase functions as an antecedent — helps resolve the ambiguity.

# Core Definition
An irregularity in cadential closure of an antecedent phrase arises in connection with the definition of an IAC. As a general rule, an IAC occurs when the final note in the melody remains open on ^3. In cases of an ornamented melody, however, it can sometimes be difficult to say exactly which note is the "final note" for purposes of this definition. On occasion, the last note of the melody appears to ornament the more fundamental melodic line, whose final note remains open on ^3. Since an opening four-measure phrase of a theme does not normally end with full harmonic and melodic closure, the formal context inclines us toward hearing the phrase as an antecedent ending with an IAC.

# Construction / Recognition
1. Examine the melody at the cadential arrival
2. If the melody ends on ^1, it could be a PAC; if on ^3, an IAC
3. In ornamented passages, determine which note is structurally primary
4. A final leap down from ^3 to ^1 may represent an embellishment ("chordal skip") of the more fundamental ^3
5. Consider the formal context: an opening four-measure phrase is unlikely to end with full closure (PAC)
6. If a subsequent phrase functions as a genuine consequent ending with an unambiguous PAC, the period interpretation is confirmed

# Context & Application
This ambiguity commonly occurs at the end of antecedent phrases where the melody seems to touch ^1 but the formal context suggests that the phrase should not achieve full closure. The analyst must weigh melodic surface detail against broader formal function to arrive at the correct interpretation.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Bagatelle in D, Op. 33/6, mm. 1-8: the antecedent's final melody has both D (^1) and F-sharp (^3) at the cadence; the final D is understood as an embellishment (chordal skip) of the more fundamental F-sharp; the subsequent consequent closes with an unambiguous PAC, confirming the period design

# Prerequisites
- **Antecedent** -- the phrase context in which cadential ambiguity most commonly arises
- **Weak cadential closure** -- the antecedent requires a weak cadence, making the PAC vs. IAC distinction critical

# Key Properties
1. Melodic ornamentation can obscure whether the final note is on ^1 (PAC) or ^3 (IAC)
2. A final leap from ^3 to ^1 may be an embellishment ("chordal skip") of the more fundamental ^3
3. Formal context inclines the listener toward the weaker interpretation (IAC) for antecedents
4. An opening four-measure phrase does not normally end with full harmonic and melodic closure
5. The presence of a genuine consequent ending with unambiguous PAC confirms the period design

# Common Errors
- Mechanically determining cadence type from the literal last note without considering melodic context
- Ignoring the formal function of the phrase when determining cadence type
- Treating all cadential ambiguity as analytical failure rather than compositional subtlety

# Common Confusions
- The determination of PAC vs. IAC is not always a matter of which note is literally last — the structural weight and formal context of the melodic line must be considered
- A note that appears to be ^1 may function as an ornamental tone (passing, neighbor, chordal skip) of the more structurally significant ^3
- When in doubt, let the formal context guide interpretation — if the phrase clearly functions as an antecedent, it almost certainly ends with a weak cadence
