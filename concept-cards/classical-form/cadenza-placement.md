# Quick Definition
In most Mozart and Beethoven concertos, the cadenza is placed within the closing ritornello (dividing it into two parts), introduced by a prominent cadential six-four chord; Haydn follows the mid-18th-century convention of placing the cadenza at the end of the recapitulation.

# Core Definition
In most of Mozart's concertos and all of Beethoven's, the cadenza is placed within the closing ritornello, dividing it into a first part before the cadenza and a second part after. The second part always ends with a closing section, usually the same one that ends the opening ritornello. Haydn follows the mid-18th-century convention of having the cadenza mark the end of the recapitulation, after which comes the closing ritornello. The cadenza is conventionally introduced by the appearance of a prominent cadential six-four, and the cadenza itself can often be seen as broadly prolonging that harmony, finally resolving to a dominant seventh to create a PAC that closes the first part of the ritornello. In the score, the composer notates the cadential six-four and the penultimate dominant seventh, both under fermata signs, indicating that an improvised cadenza is to be added (Caplin, pp. 700, 717).

# Key Properties
1. Mozart/Beethoven: cadenza within the closing ritornello, dividing it into two parts
2. Haydn: cadenza at the end of the recapitulation, before the closing ritornello
3. Introduced by a prominent cadential six-four chord
4. Broadly prolongs the cadential six-four harmony
5. Resolves to dominant seventh creating a PAC
6. Cadential six-four and dominant seventh notated with fermata signs
7. Second part of the closing ritornello ends with the opening ritornello's closing section

# Construction / Recognition
1. Locate the cadential six-four with fermata in the closing ritornello
2. The dominant seventh with fermata signals the cadenza's end
3. Identify the two parts of the closing ritornello flanking the cadenza
4. In Haydn, the cadenza precedes rather than interrupts the closing ritornello

# Context & Application
The cadenza placement reflects the concerto's aesthetic goal of highlighting the soloist. By interpolating the cadenza into the closing ritornello, Mozart and Beethoven create a dramatic interruption of the orchestra's final frame, giving the soloist a last solo statement before the orchestral conclusion.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 700): Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i, mm. 357-67 -- closing ritornello with cadential six-four leading to cadenza, after which the rest of the ritornello's subordinate theme returns.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming the cadenza always falls at the same point in all concertos. **Correction**: Mozart/Beethoven place it within the closing ritornello; Haydn places it at the end of the recapitulation.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Whether the cadenza is part of the recapitulation or the closing ritornello. **Clarification**: In Mozart/Beethoven, it is part of the closing ritornello. In Haydn, it is part of the recapitulation.
