# Quick Definition
The identifiable cadences and structural markers that articulate the boundaries of sonata-form sections and thematic units -- cadences defining the END of thematic units are more reliable landmarks than salient melodic material marking beginnings.

# Core Definition
The major landmarks of sonata form include both large-scale section boundaries and internal thematic boundaries. Large-scale landmarks are normally easy to identify: the exposition's beginning (obvious), its end (defined by the repetition structure), the development's beginning (after the exposition's close), the recapitulation's beginning (return of main-theme material in home key), and the coda's beginning (where the recapitulation no longer corresponds with the exposition). Within the exposition, landmarks include: a home-key cadence (usually PAC) ending the main theme; a subordinate-key HC and standing on the dominant ending a modulating transition; an SK: PAC ending a subordinate theme; any additional SK: PACs ending additional subordinate themes; a closing section of codettas; and a retransition leading back to the home key for the repeat.

# Prerequisites
- **Sonata form overview**: The basic three-section structure

# Key Properties
1. Most landmarks are cadences defining the END of thematic units
2. Beginnings may be marked by salient melodic material, but this is less reliable
3. Exposition landmarks: HK cadence (MT end), SK: HC (TR end), SK: PAC (ST end)
4. Development landmarks: model identification (core beginning), HC or dominant arrival (core end), standing on the dominant
5. Recapitulation landmark: return of main-theme basic idea in home key
6. Coda landmark: point where recapitulation no longer corresponds with exposition
7. Exposition boundaries are clearly defined by repeat structure

# Construction / Recognition
- When listening for the first time, focus on CADENCES, not new melodies
- Identify the home-key cadence ending the main theme
- Locate the subordinate-key HC (and standing on dominant) ending the transition
- Find the SK: PAC(s) ending subordinate theme(s)
- Note the closing section and any retransition
- In the development, identify the model, sequence, fragmentation, HC, and standing on dominant

# Context & Application
This landmark identification system is presented as a practical analytical tool for first encounters with sonata-form movements. The emphasis on cadences as endpoints rather than melodies as starting points reflects the form-functional approach: formal functions are defined by their harmonic goals (cadences) more reliably than by their melodic content. This principle applies throughout sonata-form analysis and is especially important when dealing with monothematic expositions or obscured boundaries.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F minor, Op. 2/1, i: HK: HC (m. 8, MT end), SK: HC (m. 16, TR end), standing on dominant (mm. 17-20), SK: PAC (m. 41, ST end), closing section (mm. 42-48)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in G, K. 283, ii: practice example for identifying all exposition landmarks in a slow-movement sonata form

# Common Errors
- Relying on new melodies to mark section beginnings rather than cadences to mark section endings
- Confusing the dominant resolution at the subordinate theme's start with a cadential landmark
- Missing the recapitulation's start because the return of main-theme material is obscured

# Common Confusions
- **Endings are more reliable than beginnings**: Cadences marking the END of thematic units are more reliable sources of formal articulation than salient melodic material marking beginnings
- **The model = core beginning**: In the development, identifying the model-sequence pattern is the key landmark for the core's beginning
- **Coda boundary**: The recapitulation ends when it no longer corresponds with the exposition -- this can be the most difficult landmark to identify
