# Quick Definition
The way the development section's content and organization influence changes in the recapitulation -- not causally, but as a matter of "appropriateness of occurrence," through compensatory additions and motivated deletions.

# Core Definition
The content and organization of the development section seem at times to influence changes made in the recapitulation. This is not a causal connection -- "what happens in the former does not necessarily determine what will happen in the latter; the literature abounds in counterexamples to disprove such a claim." Rather, it is a question of the "appropriateness of occurrence." When an idea is highly exploited in the development, it is appropriate (though not required) that it be deleted in the recapitulation. When the development lacks sequential organization, it is appropriate that secondary development appears in the recapitulation. The influence works in both directions: additions compensating for gaps, and deletions avoiding redundancy.

# Prerequisites
- **Recapitulation**: The section being influenced
- **Development section**: The section whose content motivates changes

# Key Properties
1. The relationship is one of appropriateness, not causal determination
2. Ideas extensively used in the development may be appropriately deleted from the recapitulation
3. Ideas underrepresented in the development may receive secondary development
4. A development without a core may motivate model-sequence technique in the recapitulation
5. Material from the development's pre-core may justify deleting that material from the recapitulation
6. Counterexamples abound -- this is a tendency, not a rule

# Construction / Recognition
- Note which ideas are extensively treated in the development
- Check whether those ideas are deleted or de-emphasized in the recapitulation
- Note what the development lacks (e.g., sequential organization, treatment of certain motives)
- Check whether the recapitulation compensates for those gaps
- Remember: the relationship is suggestive, not deterministic

# Context & Application
This principle helps explain many seemingly arbitrary changes in recapitulations. The movement functions as an organic structure where the treatment of material in one section affects the appropriate treatment in others. Caplin emphasizes "when we attend carefully to certain events in the development, we may come to believe that it is particularly fitting that some other event does, or does not, occur in the recapitulation."

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C Minor, K. 457, i (ex. 11.6): first part of subordinate theme deleted because it appeared in the development's pre-core (at the subdominant)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in G, K. 283, i (ex. 11.2): added sequence in main theme compensates for lack of core in development
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in C, K. 403, i (ex. 11.5): model-sequence technique added to transition because the development lacked sequential organization

# Common Errors
- Treating the development-recapitulation relationship as causal or deterministic
- Assuming every development feature must be "compensated" in the recapitulation

# Common Confusions
- **Appropriateness, not causation**: The development does not cause recapitulation changes; "the literature abounds in counterexamples"
- **Bidirectional influence**: Works both as additions (compensating for gaps) and deletions (avoiding redundancy)
- **Not a rule**: Many movements do not follow this principle; it is an analytical observation about tendencies
