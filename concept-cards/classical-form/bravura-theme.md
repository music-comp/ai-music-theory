# Quick Definition
The final subordinate theme of a concerto solo exposition, written in a virtuosic style with continuous sixteenth notes, typically presented in compressed form and then repeated with significant expansion.

# Formal Definition
The final subordinate theme of the solo exposition is often written in a "bravura" style featuring continuous sixteenth notes. This bravura theme is typically first presented in a relatively compressed form, creating the effect of cutting short the theme's hustle and bustle and raising expectations that more will follow. The theme is then repeated and significantly expanded in accord with the nature of its material, through model-sequence technique and cadential progression. The expanded cadential dominant often stretches to great length, culminating in the "cadential trill," the conventional sign for the close of the solo exposition. The bravura theme provides the structural space for the soloist to display virtuosic abilities, exploring extreme ranges of the instrument and showcasing technique.

# Construction/Recognition
- Final subordinate theme of the solo exposition
- Written in continuous sixteenth notes (or similar rapid figurations)
- First presented in compressed form (often a simple sentence)
- Then repeated and significantly expanded
- Expansion through model-sequence technique and cadential progression
- Culminates in an extended cadential dominant and cadential trill
- Represents the rhythmic and dynamic climax of the exposition
- Explores extreme ranges of the solo instrument

# Musical Context
The bravura theme is a hallmark of the classical concerto, combining formal function (the final subordinate theme) with the concerto's aesthetic requirement for virtuosic display. The enormous expanded cadential progressions associated with the bravura theme influenced other genres of the period.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i, mm. 171-85: Second subordinate theme in sixteenth-note arpeggiations; first statement is a simple eight-measure sentence; repeated at m. 178 and expanded through model-sequence technique and cadential progression; dominant stretched to ten measures

# Common Confusions
- The bravura theme is not merely passage-work or display; it is a genuine subordinate theme with formal structure (presentation, continuation, cadential functions), distinguished by its virtuosic style.
- The initial compressed statement is not an incomplete theme; it is a deliberate technique that creates expectations for the expanded repetition that follows.
