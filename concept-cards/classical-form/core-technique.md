# Quick Definition
The characteristic phrase-structural technique of the development section, involving a model that is sequentially repeated, followed by fragmentation leading to a half cadence or dominant arrival and a standing on the dominant.

# Core Definition
The core of the development (Ratz's "Kern der Durchfuhrung") is the unit defined by the process of establishing a relatively large model (normally 4-8 measures), repeating it sequentially one or more times, fragmenting the material into progressively smaller units, and arriving at a half cadence or dominant arrival of either the home key or a development key, typically followed by a standing on the dominant. The core projects emotional instability, restlessness, and dramatic conflict (often Sturm und Drang), with forte dynamics and continuous rhythmic activity.

# Prerequisites
- **Development section**: The formal context for core technique
- **Model-sequence technique**: The repetition pattern organizing the core
- **Fragmentation**: The process of breaking down the model's grouping structure
- **Standing on the dominant**: The postcadential prolongation concluding the core

# Key Properties
1. **Model**: A relatively long unit (4-8 measures), large enough to project a structural beginning and permit extensive fragmentation
2. **Sequence**: The model is sequenced one or more times; patterns may change during the core
3. **Fragmentation**: Breaks down the grouping structure into progressively smaller units
4. **Concluding function**: Half cadence or dominant arrival (of home key or development key)
5. **Standing on the dominant**: Postcadential prolongation, often with anticipatory motives from main theme
6. The model may have sentential organization (presentation + continuation)
7. The sequence may alter the model, especially eliminating later phrases
8. Fragmentation may establish a new model for sequential repetition
9. Some cores end with authentic cadential progression in a development key (evaded, abandoned, or achieved)
10. Two-core developments: first core confirms a development key, second leads to home-key dominant

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify a model of 4-8 measures that is sequentially repeated
- Note the sequential pattern (descending fifth, ascending step, descending third, etc.)
- Track the fragmentation process (model size reduced progressively)
- Locate the concluding dominant (HC, dominant arrival, or authentic cadence in development key)
- Find the standing on the dominant (often with anticipatory main-theme motives)
- Assess whether the model is large enough (4+ measures) to qualify as a genuine core

# Context & Application
Pre-core/core technique is pervasive in Mozart and Beethoven but appears much less often in Haydn. Lengthy developments may contain two cores: the first confirming a development key, the second leading to the home-key dominant. When a core ends with an authentic cadence in a development key, the passage resembles a subordinate theme (but in a minor-mode development key rather than the subordinate key).

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in A Minor, K. 310, i (ex. 10.3): four-measure model in E minor, sequenced twice down a fifth, fragmented to one-measure and one-beat units, leading to HC
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Hob. XVI:49, i (ex. 10.4): four-measure model sequenced by descending thirds
- Mozart, String Quintet in E-flat, K. 614, i (ex. 10.6): ten-measure sentential model sequenced down a step
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C Minor, Op. 10/1, i (ex. 10.8): eight-measure model with nested model-sequence-fragmentation processes
- Mozart, Piano Concerto in C Minor, K. 491, i (ex. 10.10): first core approaches AC in development key but cadence evaded; second core begins

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Development section (the formal context)
- Model-sequence technique (the organizational method)
- Fragmentation (the process of reducing unit size)

## Enables
- Standing on the dominant (the postcadential prolongation)
- Retransition (follows the core)
- Recapitulation preparation (anticipatory motives)

## Related
- Pre-core (the passage preceding the core)
- Pseudo-core (resembles core in character but lacks model-sequence technique)
- Core substitute (transition-like or subordinate-theme-like units replacing the core)
- Two-core development (two successive cores)

## Contrasts With
- Pre-core (relaxed, quiet, anticipatory vs. dramatic, loud, restless)
- Pseudo-core (similar character but lacks genuine model-sequence technique)

# Common Errors
- Assuming all developments contain a core (Haydn frequently omits it)
- Treating the core as the entire development (it is preceded by pre-core, may be followed by retransition)
- Failing to recognize that the model must be large enough (4+ measures) to constitute a genuine core
- Confusing smaller-scale sequences within a continuation with a true core

# Common Confusions
- **Core vs. development**: The core is not the entire development -- it is one component
- **Core vs. pseudo-core**: The core requires genuine model-sequence technique; a pseudo-core has core-like character without it
- **Model size**: The model must be 4-8 measures to qualify; smaller models suggest continuation function, not core technique
- **Composer preferences**: Mozart and Beethoven favor core technique; Haydn often omits it

# Verification Notes
The term "Kern der Durchfuhrung" is attributed to Ratz. The four-component structure (model, sequence, fragmentation, concluding function) is explicitly described. The 4-8 measure model size is stated as normative.
