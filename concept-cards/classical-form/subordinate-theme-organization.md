# Quick Definition
The detailed formal organization of subordinate themes, which are constructed primarily from loosened sentential functions (presentation, continuation, cadential) and must always close with a PAC in the subordinate key.

# Formal Definition
Subordinate themes are constructed out of the intrathematic functions associated with tight-knit main themes, albeit in a significantly looser manner. The constituent phrases frequently express an initiating function (antecedent, presentation, or compound basic idea), a medial function (continuation), and a concluding function (cadential or, more rarely, consequent). Introduction, closing section, codetta, and standing on the dominant are frequently associated with the theme as well. Unlike main themes, subordinate themes almost never use the ternary functions of exposition, contrasting middle, and recapitulation. Following Schoenberg and Ratz, the subordinate theme is viewed as loosely organized relative to a tight-knit main theme, with loosening achieved through specific compositional techniques across all three sentential functions.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Built primarily from sentential functions: presentation, continuation, cadential
2. Each function may be loosened independently through specific techniques
3. Continuation and cadential functions are more often separated into distinct phrases (unlike tight-knit themes where they fuse)
4. PAC in the subordinate key is required at the end
5. Periodic functions (antecedent, consequent) appear less frequently
6. May begin with an initiating function, continuation, cadential, or standing on the dominant
7. In a group of multiple subordinate themes, the overall group (not each individual theme) should be compared for looseness

# Musical Context
The subordinate theme's organization reflects the classical aesthetic of dramatizing the subordinate key. The looser organization makes the eventual PAC more dramatically effective -- the cadential goal is intensified by being withheld through various extensions and complications. This chapter is the longest in the book because the subordinate theme introduces the most diverse array of loosening techniques in classical form.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, i, mm. 13-28 (presentation with weakened I6 tonic prolongation, 4-measure continuation with model-sequence, 4-measure cadential phrase)
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:35, i, mm. 36-67 (first theme: expanded cadential progression throughout; second theme: begins with continuation, cadential evasion, "one more time" technique)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 576, i, mm. 28-58 (two subordinate themes: first with additional basic idea repetition; second with expanded periodic design)

# Common Confusions
- "Loosely organized" does not mean disorganized -- subordinate themes use specific, identifiable techniques to achieve their looser character
- The contrast between main and subordinate themes is principally in formal organization, not in melodic character (reject the 19th-century "masculine/feminine" dualism)
- Each subordinate theme in a group must end with PAC in the subordinate key
- When comparing main and subordinate themes, consider the ENTIRE subordinate-theme group, not just one individual theme
