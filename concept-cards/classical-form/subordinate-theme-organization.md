# Quick Definition
The characteristic organizational pattern of subordinate themes, featuring systematic loosening of all intrathematic functions compared to tight-knit main themes.

# Core Definition
Subordinate themes are constructed out of the intrathematic functions associated with tight-knit main themes, albeit in a significantly looser manner. The constituent phrases frequently express an initiating function (antecedent, presentation, or compound basic idea), a medial function (continuation), and a concluding function (cadential or, more rarely, consequent). Introduction, closing section, codetta, and standing on the dominant are frequently associated with the theme as well. Unlike main themes, subordinate themes almost never use the ternary functions of exposition, contrasting middle, and recapitulation. Following Schoenberg and Ratz, the subordinate theme is viewed as loosely organized relative to a tight-knit main theme, with loosening achieved through specific compositional techniques across all three sentential functions.

- **Subordinate theme**: The formal unit being organized

# Key Properties
1. All three intrathematic functions can be loosened independently
2. Asymmetrical phrase groupings replace the regular proportions of tight-knit themes
3. Sentential organization is more common than periodic (consequent function is rare)
4. Ternary functions (exposition, contrasting middle, recapitulation) are not used
5. Continuation and cadential functions are more often separated into distinct phrases
6. PAC in the subordinate key is required at the end
7. When comparing looseness, consider the entire subordinate-theme group, not just one theme

# Construction / Recognition
- **Presentation loosening**: Additional repetition (3 statements), repeated presentation phrase, weakened tonic prolongation (I6, dominant pedal, accented subordinate harmonies)
- **Continuation loosening**: Extended model-sequence technique, delayed fragmentation (initial continuation units same size as presentation), continuation = cadential fusion
- **Cadential loosening**: Expanded cadential progressions, cadential extension through IAC/DC/evaded/abandoned cadences, "one more time" technique
- **Structural loosening**: Omission of initiating function, beginning with continuation or cadential, standing on the dominant, modal shift, tonicization of remote regions

# Context & Application
Chapter 8 is the longest in the book because the subordinate theme introduces the most diverse array of loosening techniques in classical form. The looser organization serves the aesthetic goal of dramatizing cadential closure -- the cadential goal is intensified by being withheld through various extensions and complications. The contrast between main and subordinate themes is principally in formal organization, not in melodic character.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, i, mm. 13-28: presentation with weakened I6 tonic prolongation, 4-measure continuation with model-sequence, 4-measure cadential phrase
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:35, i, mm. 36-67: first theme uses expanded cadential progression throughout; second theme begins with continuation, cadential evasion, "one more time" technique
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 576, i, mm. 28-58: two subordinate themes -- first with additional basic idea repetition; second with expanded periodic design

# Common Errors
- Analyzing subordinate themes with the same expectations as main themes
- Confusing looseness with lack of structure
- Expecting periodic organization where sentential organization predominates

# Common Confusions
- **"Loosely organized" vs. disorganized**: Subordinate themes use specific, identifiable techniques to achieve their looser character
- **Melodic dualism vs. formal contrast**: The contrast between main and subordinate themes is principally in formal organization (reject the 19th-century "masculine/feminine" dualism)
- **Individual theme vs. group**: When comparing main and subordinate themes for looseness, consider the ENTIRE subordinate-theme group
