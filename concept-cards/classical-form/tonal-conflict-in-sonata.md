# Quick Definition
Sonata form creates, intensifies, and resolves a dramatic conflict between the home key and subordinate key: the exposition establishes the conflict (emphasizing major modality), the development intensifies it through minor-mode regions, and the recapitulation resolves it by restating everything in the home key.

# Core Definition
Sonata form is often described as creating, and then resolving, a dramatic conflict of tonalities, namely between the home key and a closely related subordinate key. The exposition begins in the home key (HK), confirmed by a cadence (usually PAC). It then modulates to the subordinate key, confirmed by a PAC. As a general rule, the exposition emphasizes major modality, since even in minor-mode movements most of the exposition sounds in the major-mode subordinate key. The development often explores one or more additional tonal regions (development keys), normally confirmed by HC. In major-mode movements, the development keys are the minor-mode regions of vi, iii, and ii. In minor-mode movements, the development keys are the minor-mode regions of iv and v. In contrast to the exposition, the development tends to emphasize minor modalities. The recapitulation begins in the home key and remains there throughout, eventually reconfirming it with a PAC.

# Key Properties
1. Exposition: creates the conflict (HK vs. SK)
2. Development: intensifies the conflict (explores DK regions, emphasizes minor)
3. Recapitulation: resolves the conflict (restates everything in HK)
4. Major-mode movements: SK = V; DK = vi, iii, ii (minor regions)
5. Minor-mode movements: SK = III (or v); DK = iv, v (minor regions)
6. Exposition emphasizes major modality; development emphasizes minor modality
7. Recapitulation typically includes subdominant emphasis early but returns to HK
8. The subordinate key needs PAC confirmation to become a genuine competitor

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the home key (first cadence of the movement)
- Identify the subordinate key (PAC closing the subordinate theme)
- Note the modal contrast: exposition major, development minor
- Track key areas in the development
- Verify that the recapitulation resolves everything to the home key

# Context & Application
The tonal-conflict model is one of two complementary models for understanding sonata-form tonality (the other being the tour-of-keys model). The conflict creates the fundamental dramatic tension of the movement. The exposition's emphasis on major modality and the development's emphasis on minor modality create a large-scale modal contrast that adds another dimension to the tonal conflict. The recapitulation's resolution is made more emphatic when the subordinate theme, now in the home key, achieves its final PAC (the essential structural closure).

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F minor, Op. 2/1, i: HK = F minor, SK = A-flat major (III), DK regions tonicize B-flat minor (iv) and C minor (v); recapitulation restates subordinate theme in F minor (Ch. 9, Exs. 9.1-9.11)

# Common Errors
- Treating the tonal conflict as merely "modulation and return" without recognizing the dramatic narrative
- Ignoring the modal contrast (major exposition, minor development)
- Assuming the subordinate key is always V (in minor, it is usually III)

# Common Confusions
- **Major emphasis in exposition**: Even in minor-mode movements, the exposition sounds mostly in major because the subordinate key (III) is major
- **Minor emphasis in development**: The development explores minor-mode regions, creating contrast with the exposition's major emphasis
- **Subdominant in recapitulation**: The typical subdominant emphasis early in the recapitulation is not a genuine key confirmation -- it occurs within the home-key context
