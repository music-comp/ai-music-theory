# Quick Definition
A harmonic progression that sustains a single harmony through time by means of subordinate chords, using pedal point, neighboring chords, passing chords, or substitute chords, creating two levels of harmonic activity.

# Core Definition
"A harmonic prolongation is created when a single harmonic entity is perceived in the listener's imagination to be sustained through time, despite the presence of an intervening chord (or chords) of different harmonic meaning" (Ch. 2, p. 24). The prolonged harmony "'remains in effect without being literally represented at every moment'" (Ch. 2, p. 24). "Prolongation thus entails two levels of harmonic activity: a local level that contains the succession of prolonged and subordinate harmonies and a deeper level that contains the prolonged harmony alone" (Ch. 2, p. 24). The four main types: (1) pedal point, (2) neighboring chords, (3) passing chords, (4) substitute chords.

# Prerequisites
- **Harmonic functions overview** -- understanding the functions being prolonged

# Key Properties
1. Sustains a single harmony (usually tonic) through time
2. Subordinate harmonies intervene but remain under the control of the prolonged harmony
3. Two levels: local (successive harmonies) and deeper (prolonged harmony alone)
4. Four techniques: pedal point, neighboring chords, passing chords, substitute chords
5. Subordinate harmonies placed in parentheses in analysis
6. A prolongational progression is independent of specific formal function until a key is assigned
7. Most progressions prolong tonic, but any harmony can be prolonged

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the prolonged harmony (usually appearing at beginning and end)
- Determine the prolongational technique (pedal, neighbor, passing, substitute)
- Place subordinate harmonies in parentheses in analysis

# Context & Application
Prolongational progressions are the harmonic foundation of presentation function (tonic prolongation), postcadential function (tonic or dominant prolongation), and standing on the dominant (dominant prolongation).

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 2, ex. 2.1): Pedal point: tonic pedal with subordinate chords above; tonicized IV over tonic pedal frequently used in codettas (ex. 2.1b); dominant pedal typical of standing on the dominant (ex. 2.1c).
**Example 2** (Ch. 2, ex. 2.2): Neighboring: I-V-I where V functions as a neighbor (same position at beginning and end).
**Example 3** (Ch. 2, ex. 2.3): Passing: I to I6 with passing chord connecting root position and first inversion via stepwise bass.
**Example 4** (Ch. 2, ex. 2.4): Substitute: I-VI sharing two common tones; VI expresses the same tonic function.

# Common Errors
- Confusing prolongational I-V-I with cadential I-V-I (context determines which)

# Common Confusions
- A simple I-V-I may look cadential but is often prolongational in actual compositional practice
- Some passing chords arise entirely from counterpoint and should not be given Roman numeral analysis (use "p" label and parentheses)
