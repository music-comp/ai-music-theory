# Quick Definition
A harmonic progression that sustains a single harmony through time by means of subordinate chords, using techniques such as pedal point, neighboring chords, passing chords, or substitute chords.

# Formal Definition
A harmonic prolongation is created when a single harmonic entity is perceived to be sustained through time, despite the presence of intervening chords of different harmonic meaning. The prolonged harmony "remains in effect without being literally represented at every moment." The intervening chord is a subordinate harmony under the influence and control of the prolonged harmony. Prolongation entails two levels of harmonic activity: a local level containing the succession of prolonged and subordinate harmonies, and a deeper level containing the prolonged harmony alone. The four main types are pedal point, neighboring chords, passing chords, and substitute chords.

# Construction/Recognition
- Identify the prolonged harmony (usually tonic, but can be any function)
- Look for one of four prolongational techniques:
  1. Pedal point: bass note sustained throughout
  2. Neighboring chords: prolonged harmony in same position at beginning and end, with neighbor-tone motion
  3. Passing chords: prolonged harmony changes position (root position to first inversion or vice versa), with passing bass
  4. Substitute chords: chord sharing two common tones substitutes for the prolonged harmony (usually a third below)
- Subordinate harmonies in parentheses in analytical notation

# Musical Context
Prolongational progressions are the harmonic foundation of presentation function (tonic prolongation), postcadential function (tonic or dominant prolongation), and standing on the dominant (dominant prolongation). A prolongational progression sustains a harmony irrespective of the formal function it may ultimately serve--only when a specific function is assigned does a tonal center become implied.

# Examples
- Pedal point: tonic pedal with subordinate chords above (ex. 2.1); used in codettas and standings on the dominant
- Neighboring: I-V-I where V functions as a neighbor to I (ex. 2.2a)
- Passing: I-passing chord-I6, with stepwise bass motion (ex. 2.3a-h)
- Substitute: I-VI (two common tones), creating functional similarity (ex. 2.4a-c)

# Common Confusions
- A simple I-V-I progression may look cadential but is often better understood as prolongational in actual compositional practice. Context determines whether V is a neighboring chord (prolongational) or a penultimate dominant (cadential).
- Some passing chords arise entirely from counterpoint and should not be given Roman numeral analysis (e.g., a passing m7 chord that is not a genuine II7).
