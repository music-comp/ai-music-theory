# Quick Definition
The large ternary is NOT a bigger version of the small ternary -- they differ in three fundamental ways: the first part is itself a small ternary, the second part is an interior theme (not a contrasting middle), and the first part always begins and ends in the home key.

# Core Definition
Although both forms consist of three parts, the large ternary differs from the small ternary in three critical ways. First, whereas the A section of a small ternary is a simple theme, the first part of a large ternary is usually constructed as a small ternary (or small binary). Second, the second part of a large ternary rarely resembles a contrasting middle: the prominent dominant emphasis typical of a contrasting middle is rarely found; the interior theme may end with tonic harmony (though not of the home key). Third, the first part of a large ternary always begins and ends in the home key; it never modulates and closes in a subordinate key as the A section of a small ternary may do. Consequently, the third part does not require tonal adjustment. For these reasons, the large ternary is not a "bigger" small ternary. The component parts of the small ternary are more truly akin to the three sections of sonata form. Different labels are used: main theme, interior theme, and (return of) main theme -- NOT A-B-A'.

# Prerequisites
- **Large ternary form**: One of the two forms being compared
- **Contrasting middle**: The small-ternary unit that differs from the interior theme
- **Small ternary overview**: The other form being compared

# Key Properties
1. Small ternary A section = simple theme; large ternary first part = small ternary/binary
2. Small ternary B = contrasting middle (dominant emphasis); large ternary second part = interior theme (modal contrast)
3. Small ternary A may modulate; large ternary first part always begins and ends in home key
4. Large ternary third part needs no tonal adjustment
5. Small ternary components are akin to sonata form sections
6. Large ternary labels: main theme, interior theme, main theme -- NOT A-B-A'
7. Interior theme never uses the standard subordinate key (V in major, III/V in minor)
8. Interior theme usually contrasts by modal shift (minore/maggiore)

# Construction / Recognition
- If the first part is itself a multi-sectional theme (small ternary or binary): large ternary
- If the second part features modal shift rather than dominant emphasis: large ternary
- If the first part always closes with a PAC in the home key: large ternary
- Use labels "main theme" and "interior theme" (not A-B-A')

# Context & Application
The large ternary is used almost exclusively in slow movements. Haydn especially favors it. The distinction matters because the analytical frameworks for small and large ternary are fundamentally different. Using A-B-A' labels for both would obscure their different formal functions and organizational principles.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 7, ii: large ternary; main theme is a small ternary; interior theme begins in minore
- Haydn, Piano Trio in A-flat, H. 14, ii: large ternary; main theme is a small binary (ex. 8.1); interior theme also a small binary

# Common Errors
- Treating the large ternary as a scaled-up small ternary
- Using A-B-A' labels for the large ternary (reserved for the small ternary)
- Expecting dominant emphasis in the interior theme (this is a contrasting-middle trait)

# Common Confusions
- **Interior theme vs. contrasting middle**: The interior theme provides modal contrast (usually minore); the contrasting middle provides dominant emphasis
- **Sonata without development vs. large ternary**: A truncated recapitulation in a sonata without development creates a form resembling a large ternary, but the listener experiences it as a sonata until very late
- **Labels**: Main theme / interior theme / main theme -- NOT exposition / contrasting middle / recapitulation
