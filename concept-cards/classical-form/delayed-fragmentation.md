# Quick Definition
A loosening technique in which continuation function is extended by initially maintaining the same unit size as the presentation rather than immediately fragmenting, thereby delaying the characteristic reduction in unit size.

# Formal Definition
In tight-knit sentences, fragmentation typically appears in measures 5-6 (the beginning of the continuation), as a single repetition of the fragment is sufficient to project continuation function. In subordinate themes, the composer can extend continuation function by creating units of repetition initially the same size as those in the presentation (e.g., two-measure units continuing into the continuation). Without fragmentation, the sense of continuation must be expressed by other characteristics such as harmonic acceleration, quicker surface rhythms, or harmonic sequence. Fragmentation then appears somewhat later in the theme, sometimes in a separate distinct phrase, extending the continuation function across multiple phrases.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Continuation begins with units the same size as presentation units (e.g., 2-measure units)
2. Fragmentation is absent at the start of the continuation
3. Other continuation characteristics compensate: harmonic acceleration, surface-rhythm acceleration, sequential harmony
4. Fragmentation eventually appears in a later phrase or later in the same phrase
5. The continuation function extends across two or more distinct phrases
6. The final phrase combining fragmentation often serves as continuation=>cadential

# Musical Context
Delayed fragmentation is a hallmark of subordinate-theme construction. By withholding the characteristic reduction in unit size, the composer extends the theme's middle portion and delays the cadential goal. When combined with other loosening techniques (model-sequence, cadential evasion), delayed fragmentation contributes to the expansive quality typical of subordinate themes. The technique is particularly effective because fragmentation is one of the strongest signals of continuation function, and delaying it prolongs the listener's sense of being "in the middle" of the theme.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 576, i, mm. 34-41 (continuation begins at m. 34 with 2-measure units -- same size as presentation; fragmentation delayed until m. 38, where change of material and 1-measure units signal continuation=>cadential)

# Common Confusions
- Delayed fragmentation does not mean the absence of continuation function -- other characteristics (harmonic acceleration, surface rhythm) still signal continuation
- The continuation function can extend across multiple phrases when fragmentation is delayed, unlike tight-knit themes where continuation and cadential functions fuse in a single phrase
- Do not assume fragmentation must begin at the start of continuation -- in subordinate themes, delay is normative, not exceptional
