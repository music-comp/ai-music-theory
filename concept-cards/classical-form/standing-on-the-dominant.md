# Quick Definition
A postcadential prolongation of the dominant harmony, typically appearing at the end of a development core or transition, that builds expectation for the resolution to tonic.

# Formal Definition
The half cadence (or dominant arrival) closing a core is usually followed by a standing on the dominant. This postcadential unit is often highly extended by means of several distinct sections, each with its own melodic-motivic content. When occurring at the end of the development, anticipatory motives derived from the basic idea of the exposition's main theme often appear to prepare for the recapitulation.

# Construction/Recognition
- Prolongation of dominant harmony following a half cadence or dominant arrival
- May consist of multiple sections with distinct motivic content
- Often features reduced dynamics and texture approaching the recapitulation
- May include anticipatory references to the main theme's basic idea
- Can be quite extensive
- The dominant may be articulated as a pedal point, repeated bass notes, or sustained harmony

# Musical Context
The standing on the dominant at the end of the development is one of the most dramatically charged moments in sonata form. It creates maximum expectation for the recapitulation's "double return."

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in A Minor, K. 310, i (ex. 10.3): standing on the dominant from m. 74 to m. 80
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F Minor, Op. 2/1, i (ex. 10.8): two-part standing on the dominant (mm. 82-94 and 95-100)

# Common Confusions
- The standing on the dominant is postcadential -- it comes after the cadence, not before
- By the time it begins, the home key has already been achieved via the half cadence
