# Quick Definition
A couplet organized as a unified complex of transition, subordinate theme(s), closing section, and retransition, used in all sonata-rondo first couplets and many five-part rondo first couplets, but typically less emphatic in establishing the subordinate key than in a sonata exposition.

# Core Definition
The subordinate-theme complex groups transition, subordinate theme, closing section, and retransition as a single large-scale unit making up a rondo couplet. This concept comes into its own in rondo forms because main-theme function is so strongly associated with the refrain that all contrasting material constitutes a couplet. The establishment and confirmation of the subordinate key in a rondo is often less emphatic than in a sonata: a distinct transition may be entirely omitted; the subordinate theme may lack cadential closure (unlike a sonata, where PAC closure is required); the subordinate theme can be relatively compressed and simple; and transition and subordinate-theme functions may be fused. The tonal conflict of home and subordinate keys -- so often dramatized in sonata form -- tends to be tempered in rondo forms (Caplin, pp. 673-675).

# Key Properties
1. Groups transition, subordinate theme, closing section, and retransition as a single unit
2. Less emphatic subordinate-key establishment than in sonata form
3. Transition may be omitted entirely (subordinate theme begins directly in new key)
4. Subordinate theme may lack PAC closure in the subordinate key
5. Subordinate theme may be compressed and simple relative to sonata norms
6. Transition/subordinate-theme fusion is common, especially in slow movements
7. Retransition is more prominent than in sonata form

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the couplet's constituent functions: transition, subordinate theme, closing section, retransition
2. Assess the degree of subordinate-key emphasis
3. Check whether transition is present or omitted
4. Determine whether the subordinate theme achieves PAC closure
5. Note the relative weight of the retransition compared to a sonata exposition

# Context & Application
The dramatic emphasis in a rondo is associated more with the returns of the refrain than with the contrasting couplets. The retransition leading back to refrain 2 is usually longer and more elaborate than in a sonata exposition, and motives anticipating the refrain's basic idea are often included to heighten expectations for its return.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 667): Mozart, Piano Sonata in C, K. 545, iii, mm. 9-22 -- highly compressed subordinate-theme complex with short transition, brief subordinate theme, and retransition making up a full third of the complex.
**Example 2** (p. 674): Beethoven, Piano Sonata in D, Op. 10, No. 3, iv, mm. 15-26 -- extremely incomplete subordinate theme consisting only of a weak initiating function and brief retransition, with continuation and cadential functions eliminated.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Expecting the rondo subordinate-theme complex to match sonata-form standards. **Correction**: Rondo subordinate-theme complexes are typically more compressed with less emphatic key confirmation.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Whether absent PAC closure means the subordinate theme is deficient. **Clarification**: In rondo forms, the requirement for PAC closure in the subordinate key may be waived, unlike in sonata form.
