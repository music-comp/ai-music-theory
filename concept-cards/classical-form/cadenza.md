# Quick Definition
An unaccompanied solo passage interpolated into the closing ritornello, prolonging the cadential dominant (6/4) and completing the cadential progression, representing the ultimate display of the soloist's virtuosity.

# Formal Definition
In most of Mozart's concertos and all of Beethoven's, the closing ritornello includes a cadenza for the solo instrument. The closing ritornello divides into two parts around the cadenza: the first part leads to a cadential 6/4 (an embellishment of the dominant), and the cadenza provides a large-scale prolongation of this dominant, eventually completing the cadential progression. The second part of the closing ritornello follows the cadenza and brings the closing section. In Haydn's practice, the cadenza marks the end of the recapitulation (before the closing ritornello) rather than being placed within the ritornello itself. The cadenza may be improvised or written out by the composer.

# Construction/Recognition
- Unaccompanied solo passage (orchestra silent)
- Located within the closing ritornello (Mozart, Beethoven) or at the end of the recapitulation (Haydn)
- Begins after the cadential 6/4 chord
- Prolongs the dominant harmony
- Completes the cadential progression at its conclusion
- May be improvised or composed (written out)
- Represents the climactic display of virtuosity

# Musical Context
The cadenza is one of the defining features of the classical concerto, providing the soloist with an opportunity for the ultimate display of virtuosity and musicianship. Its placement within the form (whether within the closing ritornello or at the end of the recapitulation) varies between composers. In the nine-part sonata-rondo form, Mozart occasionally interpolates an elaborate, written-out cadenza before the close of the final couplet.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i: Cadenza within the closing ritornello, between the cadential 6/4 and the returning closing section
- Mozart, Rondo for Piano in F, K. 494: Elaborate written-out cadenza interpolated before the close of the final couplet in a nine-part sonata-rondo

# Common Confusions
- The cadenza is not an independent section of the form; it is an interpolation within the closing ritornello (or at the end of the recapitulation in Haydn). It prolongs a harmony (the dominant) that is already part of the ongoing cadential progression.
- Not all cadenzas are improvised; from Beethoven onward, composers increasingly wrote out the cadenza. Even in Mozart's time, the composer sometimes provided written cadenzas.
