# Quick Definition
A boundary process in which the moment of a theme's cadential arrival introduces new accompanimental figuration belonging to the next thematic unit, while the structural beginning of that next unit occurs in the following measure.

# Core Definition
An accompanimental overlap occurs when the moment of a theme's cadential arrival brings with it the new accompanimental figuration of the next thematic unit, whose first true downbeat occurs in the following measure. Only the accompaniment overlaps with the end of the preceding theme; the structural beginning of the next unit is in the next measure. This differs fundamentally from elision, where the cadential arrival of one theme corresponds with the moment when the next theme structurally begins -- a single complete measure functioning simultaneously as both an ending and a beginning.

# Prerequisites
- **Main-theme function in sonata**: The preceding theme that ends with a cadence
- **Transition**: The subsequent thematic unit whose accompaniment overlaps
- **Cadential function**: The formal function producing the cadential arrival

# Key Properties
1. New accompanimental figuration appears at the moment of cadential arrival
2. The structural beginning of the next thematic unit is in the FOLLOWING measure
3. Only the accompaniment overlaps, not the structural beginning
4. Generates greater rhythmic continuity than no overlap at all
5. Less powerful effect than structural elision
6. Frequently associated with the main-theme/transition boundary
7. The new accompaniment pattern (e.g., Alberti bass variant) signals the transition's character

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the cadential arrival of the preceding theme
- Note whether new accompanimental figuration begins at that exact moment
- Check whether the melodic/structural beginning of the next unit is at the cadence or in the following measure
- If the structural beginning is in the next measure: accompanimental overlap
- If the structural beginning coincides with the cadence: structural elision

# Context & Application
Accompanimental overlap is one of two boundary processes frequently associated with the main-theme/transition boundary. It creates rhythmic continuity while maintaining clear structural articulation. The new accompaniment pattern (often an Alberti bass or similar conventional pattern) signals the shift from the main theme's character to the transition's forward drive. This technique helps the movement seem to "get under way" at the transition.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Trio in E-flat, H. 30, i, m. 16: main theme ends on the downbeat; new Alberti-bass variant accompaniment appears; the transition's first genuine downbeat is m. 17 (Ch. 11, Ex. 11.2)
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in B-flat, K. 454, ii, m. 21: PAC of main theme coincides with new flowing accompaniment pattern; transition begins structurally at the upbeat to m. 22 (Ch. 11, Ex. 11.10)

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Cadential function (provides the moment of overlap)
- Transition (the unit whose accompaniment overlaps)

## Enables
- Greater rhythmic continuity at formal boundaries
- Smooth transition from main theme to transition character

## Related
- Transition (the most common context)
- Energy gain (the accompanimental overlap contributes to the transition's energy)
- Brilliant style (the new accompaniment often has this character)

## Contrasts With
- Structural elision (both ending and beginning coincide at a single measure; more powerful effect)

# Common Errors
- Confusing accompanimental overlap with structural elision (the key question: where does the next unit STRUCTURALLY begin?)
- Placing the transition's beginning at the moment of accompanimental overlap rather than at the following measure
- Treating the new accompaniment as belonging to the closing section of the main theme

# Common Confusions
- **Overlap vs. elision**: In accompanimental overlap, the theme begins in the bar following the cadence; in elision, the new theme begins at the very moment of cadential arrival
- **Overlap vs. closing section**: The new accompaniment belongs to the transition, not to a postcadential closing section of the main theme
- **Both create continuity**: Both processes generate greater rhythmic continuity, but elision is more powerful since the overlap involves structure, not just texture
