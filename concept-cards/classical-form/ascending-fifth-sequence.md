# Quick Definition
A sequential progression with ascending-fifth root motion that entirely lacks the dominant-to-tonic functional drive of the descending-fifth sequence, typically moving from I to VI before breaking the pattern.

# Formal Definition
The ascending-fifth sequential progression features root motion by ascending fifths (or descending fourths). The strong functional drive of the descending-fifth pattern is entirely absent in ascending-fifth progressions. Most such sequences begin with tonic harmony and progress to the submediant, at which point the sequential chain is broken and the music proceeds to pre-dominant harmony (usually IV). This category expresses little harmonic functionality, since the ascending-fifth motion reverses the dominant-to-tonic relationship.

# Construction/Recognition
- Root motion: consistently ascending fifths (or descending fourths)
- Typical pattern: I -> V -> II -> VI, then breaks to IV
- Lacks the dominant-to-tonic functional implication of the descending fifth
- The sequential chain typically breaks at VI and proceeds to IV
- Less common than descending-fifth sequences

# Musical Context
Ascending-fifth sequences appear in continuation passages and development sections but are less common than descending-fifth sequences. Their lack of functional drive makes them useful for creating harmonic motion without strong tonal implications.

# Examples
- Basic pattern: I-V-II-VI, breaking to IV (ex. 2.12)

# Common Confusions
- The ascending-fifth sequence is functionally the "reverse" of the descending fifth, but this does not make it a retrograde or inversion of the same process. It has a fundamentally different character--lacking functional drive rather than reversing it.
