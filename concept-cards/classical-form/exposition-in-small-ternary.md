# Quick Definition
The A section of a small ternary, built as a relatively tight-knit, self-contained theme that ends with a PAC in the home key or a subordinate key.

# Formal Definition
The exposition section of a small ternary (rounded binary) is constructed as a tight-knit theme whose internal harmonic and melodic processes are brought to a close by a PAC. All conventional theme-types (sentence, period, hybrid) can be used. The period and periodic hybrids are most common. When nonmodulatory, it could initially be heard as a standalone main theme; only when B and A' follow does the listener retrospectively understand it as the exposition of a ternary form. When modulatory, the change of key immediately signals that more sections must follow.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Most often an 8-measure period or periodic hybrid
2. Sentences and sentential hybrids less frequent; compound themes even rarer
3. Always ends with a PAC (in home or subordinate key)
4. May include a brief postcadential closing section of codettas
5. Emphasizes tonic harmony (begins and ends with tonic function)
6. Nonconventional forms possible but tight-knit character usually maintained

# Musical Context
The exposition establishes the fundamental melodic-motivic material and tonal center of the small ternary. A modulatory exposition sets up strong expectations for a contrasting middle, since a modulating theme cannot function alone as a main theme.

# Examples
- Mozart, Eine kleine Nachtmusik, K. 525, ii, mm. 1-8 (nonmodulating period)
- Haydn, Symphony No. 100 ("Military"), iii, mm. 1-10 (modulating period with closing section)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 7, ii, mm. 1-8 (sentential hybrid)

# Common Confusions
- A nonmodulating period as exposition cannot be distinguished from a standalone main theme until the contrasting middle appears
- Do not confuse with the exposition of a sonata form, which contains multiple thematic functions
