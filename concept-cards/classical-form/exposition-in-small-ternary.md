# Quick Definition
The A section of a small ternary, built as a relatively tight-knit, self-contained theme that ends with a PAC in the home key or a subordinate key.

# Core Definition
The exposition section of a small ternary is constructed as a tight-knit theme. All conventional theme types (sentence, period, hybrid) can be used; the period and periodic hybrids are most common. It usually occupies eight measures, though phrase and cadential deviations may alter the length. The A section always ends with a PAC -- either in the home key or, after a modulation, in a subordinate key (usually V in major, III in minor). Its tight-knit organization derives from emphasis on tonic harmony, closing with a PAC, symmetrical grouping structure, and conventional thematic design. The A section may include a postcadential closing section of codettas.

# Prerequisites
- **Small ternary**: The overarching form
- **Period**: The most common theme type for the A section
- **Sentence**: An alternative theme type for the A section

# Key Properties
1. Most often an 8-measure period or periodic hybrid
2. Sentences and sentential hybrids less frequent; compound themes even rarer
3. Always ends with a PAC (in home or subordinate key)
4. May include a brief postcadential closing section of codettas
5. Emphasizes tonic harmony (begins and ends with tonic function)
6. Nonconventional forms possible but tight-knit character usually maintained
7. Most tight-knit section of the entire form
8. When modulating, the subordinate key is V (major) or III/minor V (minor)

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the theme type (period, sentence, hybrid, compound, nonconventional)
2. Confirm PAC at the end (home key or subordinate key)
3. Assess tight-knit organization (tonic emphasis, symmetry, conventional design)
4. Note whether the section modulates (this affects the recapitulation's tonal adjustment)
5. Look for a possible postcadential closing section

# Context & Application
The exposition establishes the fundamental melodic-motivic material and tonal center. A nonmodulating A could initially be heard as a standalone main theme; only when B and A' follow does the listener retrospectively understand it as the A section of a ternary form. A modulatory A immediately signals that more sections must follow. On occasion, the A section takes a nonconventional form, though even then it may bring functional elements of conventional theme types.

# Examples
- Mozart, Eine kleine Nachtmusik, K. 525, ii, mm. 1-8: nonmodulating period
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in D, Hob. XVI:37, iii, mm. 1-8: modulating hybrid (c.b.i. + continuation => cadential)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 7, ii, mm. 1-8: nonmodulating sentence
- Haydn, Capriccio in G, Hob. XVII:1, mm. 1-5: nonconventional form (consequent phrase only)
- Mozart, String Quartet in E-flat, K. 428, iv, mm. 1-16: nonconventional, two complete themes (hybrid + modulating period)

# Common Errors
- Assuming the A section must remain in the home key -- many A sections modulate to a subordinate key
- Calling the final cadence of a modulating A section a "reinterpreted HC" -- it is a PAC in the subordinate key
- Confusing with the exposition of sonata form, which contains multiple thematic functions

# Common Confusions
- A nonmodulating period as exposition cannot be distinguished from a standalone main theme until the contrasting middle appears
- When the A section modulates and is then repeated (rounded binary), the PAC in the subordinate key should NOT be called a reinterpreted HC -- it is a genuine PAC
- Do not confuse with the exposition of a sonata form, which contains multiple thematic functions
