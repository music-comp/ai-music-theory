# Quick Definition
The observation that all material appearing in the subordinate key in the exposition should be transposed back into the home key in the recapitulation, thereby resolving the tonal conflict.

# Core Definition
The sonata principle, as articulated by Edward T. Cone and Charles Rosen, holds that all material presented in the subordinate key in the exposition should be transposed back into the home key in the recapitulation. This principle explains the regular practice of Mozart and Beethoven to maintain the same structure of the subordinate theme in the recapitulation. Hepokoski and Darcy have recently challenged the validity and significance of the sonata principle, noting the major alterations Haydn regularly gives the recapitulation's subordinate theme. Even if viewed more as a "tendency" than a "rule," the principle highlights a persistent question: why do later classical composers (Mozart and Beethoven) seem satisfied to retain the subordinate theme's structure most of the time?

# Prerequisites
- **Sonata form**: The formal context in which the principle operates
- **Recapitulation**: The section where tonal resolution occurs
- **Subordinate theme**: The material originally in the subordinate key
- **Subordinate key**: The key that must be resolved into the home key

# Key Properties
1. Material in the subordinate key in the exposition should return in the home key in the recapitulation
2. Mozart and Beethoven regularly follow this practice
3. Haydn frequently departs from it, especially with monothematic expositions
4. The principle is better viewed as a tendency than a strict rule
5. Challenged by Hepokoski and Darcy's analysis of Haydn's practice
6. A simple answer to why the principle holds remains elusive
7. When material is deleted from the recapitulation, it sometimes reappears in the coda

# Construction / Recognition
- Compare the subordinate-theme area of the exposition with that of the recapitulation
- Note whether all subordinate-key material returns transposed into the home key
- Identify any deletions, additions, or structural changes
- In Haydn, expect significant departures from the principle
- Check whether deleted material reappears in the coda (compensatory function)

# Context & Application
The sonata principle is one explanation for why the subordinate theme often undergoes fewer structural changes than the main theme or transition in the recapitulation. It also explains why deleted subordinate-theme material may be "restored" in the coda. However, the principle should not be applied mechanically: the recapitulation is not a simple transposition but involves fundamentally different formal functions.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 576, i (ex. 14.10): subordinate theme significantly restructured to avoid redundant home-key statement of monothematic basic idea -- partial departure from the principle
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C minor, K. 457, i (ex. 14.8): first part of two-part subordinate theme deleted in recapitulation; its material appeared in the development instead

# Common Errors
- Treating the sonata principle as an inviolable rule (it is a tendency)
- Assuming Haydn follows it as consistently as Mozart and Beethoven
- Expecting a mechanical transposition rather than a functional recomposition

# Common Confusions
- **Rule vs. tendency**: The sonata principle describes a general practice, not a structural requirement
- **Transposition vs. recapitulation**: The recapitulation does not simply transpose the exposition; it serves fundamentally different formal functions
- **Haydn vs. Mozart/Beethoven**: Haydn's practice frequently departs from the principle, particularly with monothematic expositions
