# Quick Definition
A transition that destabilizes the home key without abandoning it, ending on the home-key dominant rather than modulating to the subordinate key, with the subordinate theme then beginning directly in the new key.

# Core Definition
The primary function of a transition -- to destabilize the home key -- can be achieved without modulation. If the main theme ends with tonic harmony (authentic cadence), the home key can be significantly weakened by allowing the transition to conclude on dominant harmony. The subordinate theme then begins directly in the subordinate key. Nonmodulating transitions are found most often in major-mode movements, since the final dominant can simply be sustained as the tonic of the new key. If the main theme already closes with a HC, a nonmodulating transition is not normally used, since a second ending on the home-key dominant would not further destabilize.

# Prerequisites
- **Transition**: The broader formal function
- **Main theme**: Must end with PAC for nonmodulating transition to be appropriate
- **Subordinate key**: The new key entered without modulation

# Key Properties
1. Remains in the home key throughout
2. Ends on the dominant of the home key (which = tonic of subordinate key in major mode)
3. Subordinate theme begins directly in the subordinate key
4. Most common after main theme ending with PAC (not HC)
5. Most often found in major-mode movements
6. The dominant ending is typically not greatly prolonged (no extended standing on dominant)
7. Consequence: subordinate theme often features an internal HC to provide missing dominant emphasis
8. May create a "reversed period" impression when combined with PAC-ending main theme

# Construction / Recognition
- Verify the transition remains in the home key (no modulation)
- Check that it ends on the home-key dominant
- Confirm the subordinate theme begins in the subordinate key without transitional modulation
- Note the main theme's cadence type (should be PAC, not HC)
- Look for compensatory internal HC in the subordinate theme

# Context & Application
Nonmodulating transitions provide a more subtle form of tonal destabilization. The reinterpretation of home-key dominant as new tonic is a moment of harmonic "sleight of hand." Because no subordinate-key dominant emphasis occurs in the transition, the subordinate theme often compensates with an internal HC and standing on the dominant.

# Examples
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in C, K. 403/385c, i, mm. 5-8: main theme (sentence) repeated with HC ending instead of PAC; G-major harmony sustained as tonic of subordinate key
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D, K. 576, iii, mm. 22-26: nonmodulating transition; subordinate theme compensates with internal HC at m. 34

# Common Errors
- Using nonmodulating transition after a HC-ending main theme (would not further destabilize)
- Failing to note the consequences for the subordinate theme (missing dominant emphasis)
- Confusing the "reversed period" impression with an actual period

# Common Confusions
- **Nonmodulating does not mean non-functional**: Destabilization is achieved by ending on dominant
- **HC after HC**: If the main theme already ends with HC, a second HC (nonmodulating transition) would not represent further destabilization
- **Consequences for ST**: Because the transition provides no subordinate-key dominant emphasis, an internal HC often appears within the subordinate theme
- **"Reversed period"**: When a PAC-ending main theme is followed by an HC-ending transition using the same material, the impression of a "reversed period" may arise, but the thematic functions (main theme + transition) provide a better explanation
