# Quick Definition
A transition that destabilizes the home key without abandoning it, ending on the home-key dominant rather than modulating to the subordinate key, with the subordinate theme then beginning directly in the new key.

# Formal Definition
The primary function of a transition -- to destabilize the home key -- can be achieved without modulation. If the main theme ends with tonic harmony (authentic cadence), the home key can be significantly weakened by allowing the transition to conclude on dominant harmony. The subordinate theme then begins directly in the subordinate key. Nonmodulating transitions are found most often in major-mode movements, since the final dominant can simply be sustained as the tonic of the new key (V of home key = I of subordinate key). If the main theme already closes with a HC, a nonmodulating transition is not normally used, since a second ending on the home-key dominant would not represent further destabilization.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Remains in the home key throughout
2. Ends on the dominant of the home key (which = tonic of subordinate key in major mode)
3. The subordinate theme begins directly in the subordinate key
4. Most common after a main theme ending with PAC (not HC)
5. Most often found in major-mode movements
6. The dominant at the end is typically not greatly prolonged (no standing on dominant)
7. Because no subordinate-key dominant emphasis occurs, the subordinate theme often features an internal HC and standing on the dominant

# Musical Context
Nonmodulating transitions provide a more subtle form of tonal destabilization than modulating ones. The reinterpretation of the home-key dominant as the new tonic is a moment of harmonic "sleight of hand" that creates the transition between keys without an overt modulatory process. Because the nonmodulating transition provides no subordinate-key dominant emphasis, the subordinate theme itself often compensates with an internal HC and standing on the dominant. This is an important structural consequence that affects the organization of the subordinate theme.

# Examples
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in C, K. 403/385c, i, mm. 5-8 (main theme repeated with HC ending instead of PAC; G-major harmony at m. 8 sustained as tonic of subordinate key; very short, subtle transition)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C, Op. 2/3, iv, mm. 19-20 (recapitulation of main-theme material steers toward subordinate key; transition closes with HC in home key)

# Common Confusions
- A nonmodulating transition does NOT fail to fulfill its function -- destabilization of the home key can be achieved by ending on the dominant, even without modulation
- If the main theme already ends with HC, a nonmodulating transition is inappropriate (a second HC would not destabilize further)
- The consequences for the subordinate theme are important: because the transition provides no subordinate-key dominant emphasis, an internal HC often appears within the subordinate theme
- In a nonmodulating transition, the "reversed period" impression (consequent followed by antecedent) can arise when a PAC-ending main theme is followed by an HC-ending transition using the same material
