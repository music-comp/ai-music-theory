# Quick Definition
A chord that prolongs a given harmony by expressing the same fundamental function, sharing two common tones with the original chord, typically lying a third below or above it.

# Formal Definition
Some chords can participate in prolonging a given harmony because they express the same fundamental function as the prolonged harmony. In such cases, the original and substitute harmonies have two chord-tones in common, which largely accounts for their functional similarity. The most common substitute lies a third below the original harmony (e.g., VI substituting for I, sharing two common tones). Passing chords can be introduced between the original and substitute harmonies to form more complex prolongations. In some situations, a chord lying a third above participates in the prolongation, understood to arise from passing motion in the soprano with simultaneous elimination of the root.

# Construction/Recognition
- The substitute chord shares two common tones with the prolonged harmony
- Most common: substitute a third below (I -> VI, sharing two tones)
- Less common: substitute a third above (I -> III, arising from soprano passing motion)
- Passing chords may intervene between original and substitute to create complex prolongations
- The substitute chord expresses the same fundamental harmonic function as the original

# Musical Context
Substitute chords are an important tool for extending prolongational passages while maintaining harmonic stability. The most prominent example is VI substituting for I (tonic function) in deceptive cadences, but the technique is broader and applies to any functional harmony.

# Examples
- I-VI: VI substitutes for I, sharing two common tones (ex. 2.4a-c)
- I-passing chord-VI: passing chord connects the original and substitute (ex. 2.4d-e)
- I-III: substitute a third above, arising from soprano passing motion with root elimination (ex. 2.4f-h)

# Common Confusions
- Substitute chords prolong the original harmony; they do not introduce a new harmonic function. The shared common tones create functional equivalence.
- The substitute a third above (e.g., III for I) is less intuitive but arises from voice-leading processes (soprano passing motion with root elimination).
