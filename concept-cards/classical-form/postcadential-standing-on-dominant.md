# Quick Definition
A standing on the dominant that directly follows a half cadence at the end of the contrasting middle, prolonging the cadential dominant and building expectations for tonic resolution at the beginning of the recapitulation.

# Core Definition
A postcadential standing on the dominant appears toward the end of a contrasting middle, directly following the HC that marks the structural close of that section. This prolongation builds powerful expectations for harmonic resolution. Two devices are typically found: (1) systematic reduction of texture, ending with just a single voice, to "clear the stage" for the return of the basic idea; (2) motives derived from and anticipating the upcoming basic idea. The passage may follow directly after the HC or elide with the cadential arrival.

# Prerequisites
- **Standing on the dominant**: The formal function being applied postcadentially
- **Contrasting middle**: The section whose HC is being extended

# Key Properties
1. Follows directly after a genuine HC in the B section
2. Prolongs the dominant established by the HC
3. Systematic reduction of texture to clear the stage for A'
4. May introduce motives anticipating the basic idea
5. May follow the HC or elide with it
6. Creates the most expectation-laden moment before the recapitulation
7. Distinguished from a standing on the dominant that opens or constitutes the entire B section

# Construction / Recognition
1. Locate the structural HC that ends the B section
2. Identify dominant prolongation following the cadence
3. Look for textural dissolution and motivic anticipation
4. Check whether the passage follows or elides with the HC
5. Distinguish from a B section that IS a standing on the dominant (that is initiating, not postcadential)

# Context & Application
In small ternaries, prepares the return of the basic idea in the recapitulation. In transitions (discussed in later chapters), prepares the entrance of the subordinate theme. Represents the most expectation-laden moment before a major formal boundary. The dominant prolongation vs. standing on the dominant distinction is important: the first is a harmonic progression, the second is a formal function.

# Examples
- Mozart, String Quartet in E-flat, K. 428, iv, mm. 24-26: B section closes with HC at m. 24, which elides with standing on dominant; reduction to single voice; motives anticipate contrasting idea
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in G, Hob. XVI:40, ii, mm. 11-14: postcadential standing on dominant with textural dissolution

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Standing on the dominant (the formal function)
- Half cadence (the cadence being extended)

## Enables
- Recapitulation preparation (builds expectations for basic idea return)

## Related
- Contrasting middle (the section it extends)
- Closing section (the analogous postcadential function after a PAC)

## Contrasts With
- Standing on the dominant as entire B section: the entire-B-section version is an initiating function, not postcadential; it follows a PAC (not an HC) and could never elide with that cadence

# Common Errors
- Placing a postcadential standing on the dominant after a dominant arrival (no cadence) -- it requires a preceding HC with a cadential progression
- Confusing with a standing on the dominant forming the entire B section (that is an initiating function, not postcadential)
- Failing to distinguish dominant prolongation (harmonic progression) from standing on the dominant (formal function)

# Common Confusions
- Requires a preceding HC -- if the section ends with a dominant arrival, the subsequent prolongation is not truly postcadential
- Do not confuse with a standing on the dominant forming the entire B section (that is an initiating function)
- Dominant prolongation is a harmonic progression; standing on the dominant is a formal function whose harmonic support is always a dominant prolongation
