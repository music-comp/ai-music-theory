# Quick Definition
A standing on the dominant that directly follows a half cadence, prolonging the cadential dominant and building expectations for tonic resolution at the beginning of the next section.

# Core Definition
A postcadential standing on the dominant appears toward the end of a contrasting middle or transition, directly following the HC that marks the structural close of that section. This prolongation builds powerful expectations for harmonic resolution. The passage typically features dissolution of texture, reduction to a single voice, and gradual reintroduction of motives from the basic idea.

# Construction / Recognition
1. Follows directly after a genuine HC (not a dominant arrival)
2. Prolongs the dominant established by the HC
3. New melodic-motivic material or anticipation of basic idea motive
4. Systematic reduction of texture common
5. May become highly extended (especially in transitions)
6. Distinguished from a standing on the dominant that opens the B section (which expresses initiation, not postcadential function)

# Context & Application
In small ternaries, prepares the return of the basic idea in the recapitulation. In transitions, prepares the entrance of the subordinate theme. Represents the most expectation-laden moment before a major formal boundary.

# Examples
- Mozart, String Quartet in E-flat, K. 428/421b, iv, mm. 24-26 (B section: dissolution of texture, anticipation of contrasting idea motive)
- Mozart, Piano Trio in B-flat, K. 502, iii, mm. 22-32 (transition: extended with accented neighboring chords)

# Common Confusions
- Requires a preceding HC -- if the section ends with a dominant arrival, the subsequent prolongation is not truly postcadential
- Do not confuse with a standing on the dominant forming the entire B section (that is an initiating function)
