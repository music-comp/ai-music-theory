# Quick Definition
A change from major to minor mode (or vice versa) within a subordinate theme that introduces a broader spectrum of pitches and creates a looser formal expression, generally absent from tight-knit main themes.

# Formal Definition
A change from major to minor is frequently encountered within a subordinate theme or theme group. This modal shift introduces a broader spectrum of pitches and pitch relationships, similar to the use of chromaticism, and creates a sense of loosening. The technique can be considered a loosening device, confirmed by its general absence in tight-knit main themes. The modal shift can occur anywhere in the subordinate theme: at the beginning, in connection with continuation function, or at the initial tonic of an expanded cadential progression. It is particularly dramatic when placed at the very beginning of the theme. No matter where the shift takes place, the major modality is ultimately restored by the final cadence.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Change from major to minor mode (or, less commonly, minor to major)
2. Can occur at any point in the subordinate theme
3. Major modality must be restored by the final PAC
4. Brings into play remote tonal regions (bIII, bVI, bVII, bII)
5. Generally absent from tight-knit main themes (confirms loosening function)
6. Often combined with tonicization of remote regions
7. Particularly dramatic at the beginning of the subordinate theme

# Musical Context
Modal shift is one of the loosening techniques that differentiates subordinate themes from main themes. In major-mode movements, shifting to minor in the subordinate theme introduces chromaticism and tonal regions that would otherwise be remote, creating harmonic instability and complexity appropriate to the subordinate theme's looser organization. The technique is particularly prominent in Beethoven's works.

# Examples
- Mozart, Clarinet Quintet in A, K. 581, i, m. 50 (second subordinate theme begins with shift to minor mode; leads to tonicization of bVI and significant cadential expansion)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i, m. 58 (first subordinate theme begins with shift to minor; model-sequence technique in minor mode)
- Haydn, String Quartet in B Minor, Op. 64/2, i, m. 26 (modal shift at point of evaded cadence)

# Common Confusions
- A modal shift is a LOOSENING device -- its absence in main themes confirms this status
- The major mode must be restored by the final PAC, no matter where the shift occurs within the theme
- Modal shift is distinct from modulation: a modal shift changes the mode within the same key region, while modulation moves to a different key
- The remote regions made available by modal shift (bIII, bVI, bVII) would be considered "remote" in a strictly major-mode context
