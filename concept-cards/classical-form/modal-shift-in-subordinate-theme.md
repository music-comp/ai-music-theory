# Quick Definition
A change from major to minor mode (or vice versa) within a subordinate theme that introduces a broader spectrum of pitches and creates a looser formal expression, generally absent from tight-knit main themes.

# Core Definition
A change from major to minor is frequently encountered within a subordinate theme or theme group. This modal shift introduces a broader spectrum of pitches and pitch relationships, similar to the use of chromaticism, and creates a sense of loosening. That this can be considered a loosening device is confirmed by its general absence in tight-knit main themes. The modal shift can occur anywhere in the subordinate theme: at the beginning, in connection with continuation function, or at the initial tonic of an expanded cadential progression. It is particularly dramatic when placed at the very beginning of the theme. No matter where the shift takes place, the major modality is ultimately restored by the final cadence.

# Prerequisites
- **Subordinate theme**: The formal context for modal shift
- **Loosening techniques**: The broader category this belongs to

# Key Properties
1. Change from major to minor mode (or, less commonly, minor to major)
2. Can occur at any point in the subordinate theme
3. Major modality must be restored by the final PAC
4. Brings into play remote tonal regions (bIII, bVI, bVII, bII)
5. Generally absent from tight-knit main themes (confirms loosening function)
6. Particularly dramatic at the beginning of the subordinate theme
7. Often combined with tonicization of remote regions

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify a change of mode within the subordinate theme or theme group
- Check that major modality is restored by the final PAC
- Note the position within the theme (beginning, continuation, cadential)
- Observe whether the shift enables tonicization of remote regions
- Compare with main theme to confirm absence of modal shift there

# Context & Application
Modal shift is one of the loosening techniques that differentiates subordinate themes from main themes. In major-mode movements, shifting to minor introduces chromaticism and remote tonal regions, creating harmonic instability appropriate to the subordinate theme's looser organization. Almost all subordinate themes reside in the major mode; even in minor-mode movements, the subordinate theme usually lies in the relative major. The modal shift thus typically represents a departure from major toward minor.

# Examples
- Mozart, Clarinet Quintet in A, K. 581, i, m. 50: second subordinate theme begins with shift to minor mode; leads to tonicization of bVI and significant cadential expansion
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i, m. 58: first subordinate theme begins with shift to minor; model-sequence technique in minor mode
- Haydn, String Quartet in B Minor, Op. 64/2, i, m. 26: modal shift at point of evaded cadence

# Common Errors
- Confusing modal shift with modulation (modal shift changes mode within the same key, modulation changes key)
- Failing to note that major modality must be restored by the final PAC
- Treating modal shift as a random expressive gesture rather than a specific loosening technique

# Common Confusions
- **Modal shift vs. modulation**: A modal shift changes the mode within the same key region; modulation moves to a different key
- **Restoration requirement**: The major mode must be restored by the final PAC, no matter where the shift occurs
- **Remote regions**: The regions made available by modal shift (bIII, bVI, bVII) are "remote" relative to the MAJOR mode -- in a minor-mode context, some are diatonic
