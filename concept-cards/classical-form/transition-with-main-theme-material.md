# Quick Definition
A transition that opens with a restatement of the main theme's basic idea, initially suggesting a repetition of the main theme but retrospectively understood as the transition's beginning when the music modulates or diverges from the original theme.

# Formal Definition
This opening is typically employed when the main theme ends with a HC. The return of the opening basic idea initially creates the impression that the main theme is not yet over and that a consequent is beginning. When the music no longer corresponds to the antecedent and instead modulates to the new key, the listener understands retrospectively that the return of the basic idea marked the beginning of the transition and that the HC truly closed the main theme. The strategy can also be used when the main theme ends with a PAC, frequently with the beginning of the transition eliding with the end of the main theme. In such cases, the combined main theme and transition can give the impression of a "reversed period" (consequent followed by antecedent).

# Construction/Recognition
1. Opens with the main theme's basic idea (or close variant)
2. Most common after a main theme ending with HC
3. Initially suggests the beginning of a consequent phrase
4. The moment of modulation or divergence marks where main-theme material ends and transition material begins
5. Retrospective reinterpretation: what seemed like a consequent was actually the transition
6. After PAC-ending main themes, structural elision is frequent (last note of PAC = first note of transition)
7. May create a "reversed period" impression (PAC-ending main theme + HC-ending transition)
8. The formal beginning of the transition is at the return of the basic idea, not at the point of modulation

# Musical Context
Beginning with main-theme material creates a powerful sense of formal continuity while also generating the ambiguity characteristic of transitions. The retrospective reinterpretation of function -- what seemed like a consequent becoming a transition -- is a sophisticated formal strategy that rewards attentive listening. The technique also exemplifies the principle that the beginning of a formal unit may not be immediately recognizable as such; its function becomes clear only in retrospect.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in B-flat, Op. 50/1, iv, mm. 13-24 (main theme [sentence] ends with HC at m. 8; sentence repeated beginning m. 9; modulation at m. 12 and HC at m. 15 confirm transition function; formal beginning is at m. 9, not m. 12)
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in C, K. 403/385c, i, mm. 5-8 (sentence repeated but ends with HC instead of PAC; "reversed period" impression)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C, Op. 2/3, iv, mm. 19-20 (return of basic idea after PAC; elision; modulation follows)

# Common Confusions
- The formal beginning of the transition is at the return of the basic idea, NOT at the point where the music diverges from the original theme -- measure 12 is in the MIDDLE of the transition, not its beginning
- A "reversed period" impression does NOT mean the form is actually a period -- the thematic functions (main theme + transition) explain the syntax better than reversed phrase functions
- Structural elision at the start of the transition does not prevent the transition from being a separate formal unit
- Analysts should not locate the transition's start at the moment of modulation; the return of the basic idea is the true formal beginning
