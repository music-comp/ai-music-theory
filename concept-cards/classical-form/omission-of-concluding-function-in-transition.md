# Quick Definition
A transition ending in which the final dominant gives no sense of being an ending harmony, resolving directly to tonic at the beginning of the subordinate theme with little or no rhythmic break, making the boundary between transition and subordinate theme difficult to determine.

# Formal Definition
Now and then, the final dominant of a transition gives no sense of being an ending harmony whatsoever. The dominant does not appear to be the goal of the progression; it receives no emphasis; and it resolves directly to tonic at the beginning of the subordinate theme with little or no rhythmic break. With a minimal sense of functional end for the transition, it can be difficult to determine where the subordinate theme begins. Usually, however, the composer provides some means of expressing a beginning, such as the appearance of a new basic idea in the context of a presentation phrase. Because the transition lacks a concluding function, an internal HC and standing on the dominant often appear within the subordinate theme proper to restore the missing dominant emphasis.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Final dominant receives no special emphasis or elongation
2. No standing on the dominant follows
3. Dominant resolves directly to tonic with little or no rhythmic break
4. No sense of an "ending" at the transition's close
5. Difficult to determine where the subordinate theme begins
6. New basic idea or presentation phrase usually signals the subordinate theme's start
7. The subordinate theme often compensates with an internal HC and standing on the dominant
8. The missing dominant emphasis is "regained" within the subordinate theme

# Musical Context
The omission of concluding function represents the extreme end of a continuum from clear half-cadential articulation to complete absence of concluding function. This situation creates the most fluid transition between formal sections, with no gap or pause separating the transition from the subordinate theme. The structural consequence is significant: the subordinate theme must compensate for the missing dominant emphasis by providing its own internal HC and standing on the dominant. This creates a cause-and-effect relationship between transition ending and subordinate-theme structure.

# Examples
- Haydn, Symphony No. 90, iv, mm. 28-35 (transition consists largely of brilliant passage-work prolonging tonic; dominant at m. 29 resolves directly to tonic at m. 30; no sense of "ending"; basic idea from main theme returns at m. 30 to begin subordinate theme)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C Minor, K. 457, i, mm. 19-37 (transition ends at mm. 22-23 with dominant of subordinate key; no cadential articulation, no standing on dominant; subordinate theme begins at m. 23 with presentation supported by root-position tonic; internal HC at m. 30 compensates for missing dominant emphasis)

# Common Confusions
- The omission of concluding function is not a compositional deficiency -- it is a deliberate formal choice with structural consequences
- Even without a concluding function, the subordinate theme's beginning can usually be identified through new melodic material and tonic support
- The missing dominant emphasis at the end of the transition is compensated within the subordinate theme -- this compensation is analytically predictable
- The absence of a medial caesura at the transition's end does not mean the transition and subordinate theme are the same formal unit
