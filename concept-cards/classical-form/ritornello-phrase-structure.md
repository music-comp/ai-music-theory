# Quick Definition
The opening ritornello's subordinate themes are kept relatively tight-knit, minimizing formal loosening (sequences, cadential expansions) because (1) the home key needs no special emphasis, (2) sequential development might draw attention from the solo, and (3) virtuosic cadential expansions are reserved for the soloist.

# Core Definition
The opening ritornello differs from a regular sonata exposition not only in tonality but also in phrase structure. Specifically, the "subordinate theme" (or themes) is generally more tight-knit than would ordinarily be expected in a sonata exposition of a comparable orchestral movement. Formal loosening is kept to a minimum for three reasons. First, the home key, in which the subordinate theme resides, does not require any particular emphasis of the cadential kind typically used to consolidate a subordinate key. Second, long extensions of continuation function usually call for sequential treatment, whose developmental potential might undermine the solo part as the bearer of prominent harmonic activity. Third, major cadential expansions normally bring in material of a virtuosic character best reserved for the solo part (Caplin, p. 703).

# Key Properties
1. Subordinate themes are more tight-knit than in a sonata exposition
2. Formal loosening minimized for three specific reasons
3. Home key needs no cadential emphasis
4. Sequential treatment reserved for solo (would undermine solo prominence)
5. Cadential expansions reserved for virtuosic solo display
6. Compound sentence favored for the main theme

# Construction / Recognition
1. Compare the ritornello subordinate themes to those in a comparable symphonic exposition
2. Note the relative brevity and tighter organization
3. Look for absence of extensive sequential extensions or cadential expansions
4. The main theme typically closes with a PAC (never an HC, unlike sonata)

# Context & Application
The tight-knit quality of the ritornello explains why the solo exposition can introduce so much new material and formal expansion. The ritornello sets up expectations that the solo will fulfill through greater virtuosic elaboration.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 696): Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i, mm. 50-76 -- two subordinate themes in the ritornello, the second of which is more extensive due to evaded cadences but still would be insufficient for a symphonic sonata exposition.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Expecting the ritornello subordinate theme to match the elaboration of a symphonic sonata exposition. **Correction**: The ritornello intentionally minimizes loosening, reserving it for the solo.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Whether the tight-knit ritornello is formally deficient. **Clarification**: The tight-knit quality is a deliberate structural feature, not a deficiency.
