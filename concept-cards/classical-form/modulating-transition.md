# Quick Definition
A transition that destabilizes the home key by abandoning it entirely through modulation to the subordinate key, ending with a half cadence or dominant arrival in the new key.

# Core Definition
The most thorough way of destabilizing the home key is to abandon it entirely by modulating to the subordinate key. The majority of transitions are modulating. In major-mode movements (subordinate key usually V), modulation is typically achieved through pivot-chord modulation: the home-key tonic becomes the subdominant of the subordinate key, or a prominent move to VI pivots to become II in the new key. Sequential progressions also create smooth modulation. In minor-mode movements (subordinate key usually III), modulation frequently involves reinterpreting VI as IV in the new key, or IV as II in the new key. The modulating transition ends with dominant harmony of the subordinate key.

# Prerequisites
- **Transition**: The broader formal function
- **Subordinate key**: The tonal goal of the modulation

# Key Properties
1. Modulates from the home key to the subordinate key
2. Ends with dominant of the subordinate key
3. Major-mode modulation: I becomes IV of new key; or VI pivots to II of new key; sequential progressions
4. Minor-mode modulation: VI reinterpreted as IV; IV becomes II of new key
5. The dominant ending may be genuine HC, dominant arrival, or noncadential
6. Usually followed by standing on the dominant prolonging the goal harmony
7. The subordinate theme then begins in the new key with tonic harmony
8. The most common type of transition

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the point of modulation (pivot chord, sequential progression)
- Verify that the transition ends with dominant of the subordinate key
- Note the specific modulatory procedure (pivot chord type, sequence type)
- Observe whether the dominant ending is a genuine HC or a less cadential arrival
- Check for standing on the dominant following the harmonic goal

# Context & Application
Modulating transitions are the most common type and provide the strongest destabilization of the home key. The specific modulatory path varies with each movement, though certain procedures are regularly encountered. The modulation process often intensifies the energy and forward drive characteristic of transitions.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:21, ii, mm. 7-12: new material; modulation through model-sequence; pre-dominant II6 leads to cadential 6/4 and HC in subordinate key
- Mozart, Piano Trio in B-flat, K. 502, iii, mm. 18-32: modulation to F major at the very start; HC in new key at m. 22 followed by extended standing on dominant
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 4, Op. 60, i, mm. 81-108: modulation through descending fifth sequential progression; dominant arrival at m. 92 noncadentially

# Common Errors
- Assuming the modulation point is the transition's beginning (it is usually in the middle)
- Confusing the HC at the transition's end (in subordinate key) with a home-key HC
- Treating the standing on the dominant as part of the subordinate theme rather than the transition

# Common Confusions
- **HC location**: The HC at the end of a modulating transition is in the SUBORDINATE KEY, not the home key
- **Modulation point vs. transition start**: The transition begins before the modulation occurs; the modulation is a process within the transition
- **Standing on the dominant ownership**: The standing on the dominant that follows the HC usually belongs to the transition, not to the subordinate theme
