# Quick Definition
A transition that destabilizes the home key by abandoning it entirely through modulation to the subordinate key, ending with a half cadence or dominant arrival in the new key.

# Formal Definition
The most thorough way of destabilizing the home key is to abandon it entirely by modulating to the subordinate key. The majority of transitions are modulating. In major-mode movements (subordinate key usually V), modulation is typically achieved through a pivot-chord modulation: the home-key tonic becomes the subdominant of the subordinate key, or a prominent move to VI pivots to become II in the new key. Sequential progressions also effectively create smooth modulation. In minor-mode movements (subordinate key usually III), modulation frequently involves reinterpreting VI as IV in the new key, or IV in the home key as II in the new key. The modulating transition ends with dominant harmony of the subordinate key, achieved through HC, dominant arrival, or noncadential means.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Modulates from the home key to the subordinate key
2. Ends with dominant of the subordinate key
3. **Major-mode modulation**: I becomes IV of new key; or VI pivots to II of new key; sequential progressions
4. **Minor-mode modulation**: VI reinterpreted as IV; IV becomes II of new key
5. The dominant ending may be a genuine HC, a dominant arrival, or noncadential
6. Usually followed by a standing on the dominant prolonging the goal harmony
7. The subordinate theme then begins in the new key with tonic harmony

# Musical Context
Modulating transitions are the most common type and provide the strongest destabilization of the home key. The modulation itself is a dramatic process, as the listener hears the home key gradually undermined and replaced. The specific modulatory path (through which pivot chord and by what voice-leading) varies with each movement, though certain procedures are regularly encountered. The modulation process often intensifies the energy and forward drive characteristic of transitions.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C, Hob. XVI:21, ii, mm. 7-12 (new material; modulation through model-sequence; pre-dominant II6 leads to cadential 6/4 and HC in subordinate key)
- Mozart, Piano Trio in B-flat, K. 502, iii, mm. 18-32 (modulation to F major occurs at the very start; HC in new key at m. 22 followed by extended standing on dominant)
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 4, Op. 60, i, mm. 81-108 (modulation through descending fifth sequential progression; dominant arrival at m. 92 noncadentially)

# Common Confusions
- A modulating transition does not simply "arrive" in the new key -- it navigates a specific modulatory path through pivot chords or sequential progressions
- The HC at the end of a modulating transition is in the SUBORDINATE KEY, not the home key
- Not all modulating transitions are long -- some accomplish modulation very efficiently in just a few measures
- The standing on the dominant that often follows the transition's HC belongs to the transition, not to the subordinate theme (unless the subordinate theme begins with a standing on the dominant)
