# Quick Definition
A subordinate chord that prolongs a harmony when the prolonged chord changes position (e.g., root position to first inversion), typically featuring a passing tone in the bass voice between the two positions.

# Formal Definition
A given harmony is prolonged by one or more passing chords when the prolonged harmony changes position from the beginning to the end of the progression. Such prolongations usually see a passing tone in the bass voice lying between the root-position and first-inversion forms of the prolonged harmony. A variety of chords can be built over this passing tone. Some passing chords are not independent harmonies due to their unstable six-four position or weak functional relation to the prolonged harmony; such chords are placed in parentheses and given a label "p" (passing) to show they arise primarily from contrapuntal processes.

# Construction/Recognition
- The prolonged harmony changes position (e.g., I to I6 or I6 to I)
- A passing tone in the bass connects the two positions of the prolonged harmony
- Various chords can be built over the passing bass note (ex. 2.3a-f)
- Some passing chords arise entirely from counterpoint and should not receive Roman numeral analysis (use "m7" or similar label instead)
- A passing chord may also arise without literal passing motion in any voice (ex. 2.3h)
- Ascending passing motion in soprano (^3-^4-^5) with contrary bass leaps is another common pattern (ex. 2.3g)

# Musical Context
Passing-chord prolongations are among the most common prolongational techniques in classical music. They appear in presentation phrases, in accompaniment patterns, and in transitions between structural harmonies. The distinction between genuine harmonic passing chords and purely contrapuntal ones is analytically important.

# Examples
- I-V4/3-I6: passing V4/3 connects root-position and first-inversion tonic (ex. 2.3a)
- I-(p)-I6: a purely contrapuntal passing chord with no independent harmonic function, labeled with "p" and parentheses (ex. 2.3c)
- I-I6 with ascending soprano ^3-^4-^5 against contrary bass motion (ex. 2.3g)

# Common Confusions
- Not all chords between I and I6 are genuine harmonies. A chord arising entirely from counterpoint (e.g., doubled seventh, incorrect resolution) should be labeled as a contrapuntal passing chord (p), not with a Roman numeral.
- The progression I-"II7"-I is nonfunctional; such a chord is better labeled as m7 (minor seventh chord) to show its contrapuntal rather than harmonic origin.
