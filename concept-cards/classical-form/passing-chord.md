# Quick Definition
A subordinate chord that prolongs a harmony when the prolonged chord changes position (e.g., I to I6), typically featuring a passing tone in the bass connecting the two positions.

# Core Definition
"A given harmony is prolonged by one or more passing chords when the prolonged harmony changes position from the beginning to the end of the progression" (Ch. 2, p. 25). "Such prolongations usually see a passing tone in the bass voice lying between the root-position and first-inversion forms of the prolonged harmony" (Ch. 2, p. 25). Some passing chords are "not an independent harmony because of its unstable six-four position or its weak functional relation to the prolonged harmony" and are "placed in parentheses in the analysis at all times and are given an added label, p (passing), to show that they arise primarily from contrapuntal processes" (Ch. 2, p. 25). For example, a passing chord that looks like "II7" but has a doubled seventh and incorrect resolution should be labeled "m7 (minor seventh chord)" rather than with a Roman numeral.

# Prerequisites
- **Prolongational progression** -- the category to which passing chords belong

# Key Properties
1. Prolonged harmony changes position (e.g., I to I6 or I6 to I)
2. Passing tone in bass connects the two positions
3. Various chords can be built over the passing bass note
4. Purely contrapuntal passing chords get "p" label and parentheses (not Roman numerals)
5. Passing chords can arise without literal passing motion in any voice
6. Ascending soprano passing motion (scale-degrees 3-4-5) against contrary bass is common

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for the prolonged harmony in different positions at beginning and end
- Identify the passing tone(s) in the bass
- Determine whether the passing chord has genuine harmonic function or is purely contrapuntal
- Use "p" label for purely contrapuntal chords

# Context & Application
Passing-chord prolongations are among the most common prolongational techniques. The distinction between genuine harmonic passing chords and purely contrapuntal ones is analytically important.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 2, ex. 2.3a): I-V4/3-I6: passing V4/3 connects root-position and first-inversion tonic.
**Example 2** (Ch. 2, ex. 2.3c): I-(p)-I6: purely contrapuntal passing chord with doubled "seventh" and incorrect resolution; labeled "m7" not "II7."
**Example 3** (Ch. 2, ex. 2.3g): I-I6 with ascending soprano (3-4-5) against contrary bass.

# Common Errors
- Assigning Roman numerals to purely contrapuntal passing chords

# Common Confusions
- Not all chords between I and I6 are genuine harmonies. The progression I-"II7"-I is nonfunctional; use "m7" label
- A passing chord may arise without literal passing motion in any voice
