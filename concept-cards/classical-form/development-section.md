# Quick Definition
The central section of sonata form that generates the greatest degree of tonal and phrase-structural instability, standing between the exposition and recapitulation.

# Core Definition
As a formal unit, the development stands between an exposition and a recapitulation. As a formal function, a development generates the greatest degree of tonal and phrase-structural instability in the movement and thus motivates a restoration of stability (to be accomplished by the recapitulation). It is a higher-level analogue to the contrasting middle of the small ternary form, featuring looser organization, emphasis on sequential progressions, avoidance of authentic cadential closure in the home key, and an ending on dominant harmony. "Development" can refer to both a particular section and a distinct formal function.

# Prerequisites
- **Sonata form**: The formal context containing the development
- **Sonata exposition**: The preceding section whose material is developed
- **Contrasting middle**: The smaller-scale analogue in the small ternary
- **Model-sequence technique**: The primary organizational technique of the core

# Key Properties
1. Generates the greatest degree of tonal and phrase-structural instability
2. Motivates the recapitulation's restoration of stability
3. Typically organized as pre-core followed by core (model-sequence-fragmentation)
4. Avoids authentic cadential closure in the home key
5. Usually ends with standing on the dominant of the home key
6. Material derives from exposition, though new ideas may appear
7. Explores minor-mode regions (VI, III, II in major; IV, V in minor)
8. Dynamic level typically forte during core sections
9. Not all developments contain a genuine core (especially Haydn)
10. "Development" refers both to a section and a function

# Construction / Recognition
- Locate the section between exposition and recapitulation
- Identify the pre-core (opening passage) and core (model-sequence-fragmentation)
- Note tonal exploration through development keys and tonicized regions
- Observe avoidance of home-key authentic cadence
- Find the standing on the dominant that typically concludes the section
- In developments without a core, look for transition-like units, pseudo-cores, or subordinate-theme-like units

# Context & Application
The development section appears in sonata form, sonata-rondo, and concerto movements. Mozart and Beethoven favor pre-core/core technique; Haydn often constructs developments without a genuine core. The development explores minor-mode regions of the home key and uses sequential progressions to traverse the tonal landscape. Caplin focuses on tonal, phrase-structural, and form-functional contexts rather than motivic transformation.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in A Minor, K. 310, i (ex. 10.3): clear core with model, sequence, fragmentation, and standing on dominant
- Haydn, Symphony No. 102 in B-flat, ii (ex. 10.17): development without a core, using transition-like units
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Hob. XVI:49, i (ex. 10.4): two-core development
- Mozart, Piano Concerto in C Minor, K. 491, i (ex. 10.10): evaded cadence leads to second core

## Contrasts With
- Exposition (tonal stability vs. instability)
- Recapitulation (restoration of stability vs. generation of instability)

# Common Errors
- Treating the development as a "free" section with haphazard modulations (it follows a clear tonal plan)
- Confusing development as a section with development as a function
- Assuming all developments must contain a core (Haydn frequently omits it)
- Analyzing motivic transformation without attention to tonal and phrase-structural context

# Common Confusions
- **Section vs. function**: "Development" refers both to a formal unit (section) and a formal function (generating instability)
- **Development vs. contrasting middle**: The development differs primarily in length, complexity, and number of thematic units
- **Core requirement**: Not all developments contain a core -- Haydn frequently omits it, using transition-like or pseudo-core units instead
- **Tonal plan**: The development follows a clear tonal hierarchy, not random modulation
