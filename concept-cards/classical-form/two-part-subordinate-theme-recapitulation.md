# Quick Definition
When a two-part subordinate theme from the exposition is recapitulated, one part may be deleted to avoid overemphasizing the home-key dominant, since the internal HC and the transition's ending would both articulate V in the home key.

# Core Definition
The use of a two-part subordinate theme in the exposition can lead to major structural changes in the recapitulation. In the exposition, an internal half cadence and standing on the dominant emphasize dominant harmony of the subordinate key, especially if the previous transition is nonmodulating. If the two-part subordinate theme is left intact in the recapitulation, two cadential articulations of home-key dominant will result: the cadence of the transition and the internal half cadence of the two-part subordinate theme. To avoid overemphasizing the home-key dominant, the composer sometimes retains only one of these cadences, usually the internal half cadence. Therefore, either the first part of the subordinate theme is deleted, or portions of it are assimilated into the preceding transition. The second part then follows as the actual subordinate theme in the recapitulation.

# Key Properties
1. Two-part subordinate theme has an internal HC and standing on the dominant
2. In the recapitulation, both the transition ending and the internal HC would articulate V of the home key
3. This double dominant emphasis is structurally redundant
4. One solution: delete the first part, retain only the internal HC
5. Another solution: assimilate the first part into the transition
6. The second part functions as the recapitulation's subordinate theme
7. Not all two-part subordinate themes are altered; only those where redundancy is problematic

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify a two-part subordinate theme in the exposition (with internal HC and standing on the dominant)
- In the recapitulation, check whether both parts are retained
- If not, identify whether part 1 was deleted or assimilated into the transition
- Note which dominant articulation was retained (transition ending or internal HC)

# Context & Application
This adjustment avoids the structural redundancy of two dominant emphases in close succession. It is one of the more significant structural changes that can affect the subordinate theme in the recapitulation, alongside monothematic and modulating situations.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in C Minor, K. 457, i (ex. 11.6): transition of recapitulation concludes at mm. 126-30 with material that earlier functioned as the internal HC and standing on the dominant in the subordinate theme; first part (exposition mm. 23-29) deleted; second part functions as the subordinate theme of the recapitulation

# Common Errors
- Failing to recognize that the internal HC's function changes when transposed to the home key
- Assuming all two-part subordinate themes must be altered

# Common Confusions
- **Tonal motivation**: The deletion is motivated by tonal adjustment needs (avoiding double dominant emphasis), not simply a desire for brevity
- **Selective retention**: The composer usually retains the internal HC rather than the transition's ending
- **Assimilation vs. deletion**: The first part may not simply be deleted but rather absorbed into the transition's concluding function
