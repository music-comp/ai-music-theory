# Quick Definition
The widespread merging of transition and subordinate-theme functions into a single group within the minuet A section, resulting from the relatively small scale of the minuet exposition compared to sonata form.

# Core Definition
Given the relatively small scale of the minuet's exposition compared to that of sonata form, the use of form-functional fusion is widespread, especially that of transition and subordinate-theme functions. The modulation and cadential articulation in the subordinate key frequently occur in the same phrase, creating a fused group rather than distinct thematic units. Subordinate-theme function is expressed at minimum by cadential confirmation of the subordinate key; transition function emerges when the modulation occurs through a harmonic pivot within a phrase. At times, however, these functions may occupy their own distinct groups, especially in more complex expositions (Caplin, pp. 637-638).

# Prerequisites
- **Minuet form** -- The formal context requiring compression
- **Form-functional fusion** -- The general technique
- **Transition** -- One of the fused functions
- **Subordinate theme** -- The other fused function

# Key Properties
1. Transition and subordinate-theme functions share a single phrase or group
2. Modulation and cadential confirmation occur within the same phrase
3. More common in minuets than in sonata form due to smaller scale
4. Loosening devices typical of subordinate-theme function accompany the modulation
5. Direct modulation (new phrase beginning in the subordinate key) eliminates transition function

# Construction / Recognition
1. Look for a phrase that both modulates and confirms the subordinate key
2. Identify loosening devices that mark subordinate-theme function
3. Check whether the modulation uses a harmonic pivot (indicating transition function)
4. If a new phrase begins directly in the subordinate key, there is no transition function

# Context & Application
The prevalence of this fusion in minuets is a natural consequence of the form's compact scale. In sonata form, the larger dimensions allow transition and subordinate-theme functions to be fully differentiated. In the minuet, compression makes fusion the norm rather than the exception.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 632): Haydn, String Quartet in G, Op. 54, No. 1, iii, B section -- transition and subordinate-theme functions fused through model-sequence pattern and expanded cadential progression.
**Example 2** (p. 638): Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 7, iii, mm. 9-21 -- consequent phrase fuses transition and subordinate-theme functions with deceptive resolution triggering the modulation.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Expecting always to find distinct transition and subordinate-theme sections in a minuet. **Correction**: Fusion is the norm in minuet form due to the compressed scale.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Whether the fused passage is a transition or a subordinate theme. **Clarification**: It is both simultaneously; the fusion means neither function is independently expressed.
