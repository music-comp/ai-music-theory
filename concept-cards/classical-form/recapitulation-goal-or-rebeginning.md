# Quick Definition
The dual character of the recapitulation's beginning: it functions as "ending" of the form (especially toward its conclusion) yet normally gives the impression of "rebeginning" -- a distinction that is especially important in Beethoven, whose forceful openings may misleadingly suggest a structural goal.

# Core Definition
From the perspective of the overall form, the recapitulation functions as the "end" of the sonata form. But this quality of ending emerges with full force only toward the conclusion of the section, especially in connection with the final PAC of the subordinate-theme area. At its very beginning, the recapitulation normally gives the impression of "rebeginning" the form. Haydn and Mozart almost always open the recapitulation just as the exposition opened, reinforcing the character of "starting over again." With Beethoven, however, the start of the recapitulation is often given an entirely different dynamic, usually louder, more forceful, and more dramatically charged. In those cases, the effect of apotheosis may give the impression that the recapitulation is the structural goal of the form from its very start, which may further reinforce a misguided interpretation that the recapitulation begins with an authentic cadence.

# Prerequisites
- **Recapitulation**: The section under discussion
- **Double return**: The simultaneous return of theme and key
- **Development section**: The section whose dominant resolves at the recapitulation's start

# Key Properties
1. The recapitulation's ending quality emerges only toward the section's conclusion
2. At its beginning, the recapitulation normally sounds like "starting over"
3. Haydn and Mozart reinforce the rebeginning character by opening as in the exposition
4. Beethoven often gives the recapitulation a louder, more forceful opening
5. Beethoven's apotheosis effect may misleadingly suggest a structural goal from the start
6. The V-I at the start of the recapitulation is NOT an authentic cadence
7. The dominant at the end of the development is an "ultimate" dominant
8. The tonic at the start of the recapitulation initiates rather than concludes

# Construction / Recognition
- Note whether the recapitulation opens as in the exposition (rebeginning) or with heightened dynamics (goal)
- Regardless of dynamic character, do NOT interpret V-I as an authentic cadence
- Observe how the ending quality gradually emerges as the subordinate theme approaches its final PAC

# Context & Application
This distinction has practical analytical consequences. Interpreting the opening of the recapitulation as a cadential goal would distort the formal analysis. The V-I resolution is initiating, not concluding. The sense of structural fulfillment comes only with the final PAC of the subordinate-theme area, which resolves the tonal conflict of the entire form.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i (ex. 14.1): recapitulation opens with the same material as the exposition (rebeginning character)
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 3, i: famous fortissimo recapitulation opening creates apotheosis effect (goal character)

# Common Errors
- Interpreting V-I at the recapitulation's start as an authentic cadence
- Confusing dynamic force with cadential function
- Treating the recapitulation as purely an "ending" section from its very start

# Common Confusions
- **Apotheosis vs. cadence**: Beethoven's forceful openings create an effect of climactic arrival, but this is not cadential function
- **Ultimate dominant**: The dominant at the end of the development resolves to initiate (not conclude), even when the tonic arrives fortissimo
- **End vs. rebeginning**: Both interpretations are valid at different structural levels; the rebeginning is the more aurally immediate
