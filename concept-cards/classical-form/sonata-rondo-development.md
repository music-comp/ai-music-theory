# Quick Definition
The second couplet of sonata-rondo form when organized as a genuine development section with transition-like pre-core and core(s), typically beginning either after a complete refrain 2 or emerging from an incomplete refrain 2 whose consequent is rewritten as a pre-core.

# Core Definition
The second couplet of sonata-rondo form is often built as a development section in the manner of a regular sonata, containing a transition-like pre-core followed by one or more cores (or core substitutes). If refrain 2 is complete, the development begins with a pre-core that opens in the home key and modulates to a new region. Two common strategies: the original transition (from couplet 1) reappears and moves to a new key, or the refrain starts over but leads into new material. A shift to minor typically signals that the exposition will not be repeated. If refrain 2 is incomplete, the development begins when the refrain's consequent departs from its expected course to become a pre-core, or when the refrain merges with transitional material. False recapitulations, where refrain material appears in the wrong key, are more common in rondos than in sonatas (Caplin, pp. 678-682).

# Prerequisites
- **Sonata-rondo** -- The formal context
- **Development section** -- The sonata-form analogue
- **Pre-core** -- The initiating unit of the development
- **Core technique** -- The primary developmental technique

# Key Properties
1. Contains transition-like pre-core followed by core(s) or core substitutes
2. After a complete refrain 2, pre-core opens in home key and modulates
3. A shift to minor signals the exposition will not be repeated
4. After an incomplete refrain 2, the consequent becomes a pre-core
5. Pre-core may reuse the original transition material
6. False recapitulations are more common in rondos than in sonatas
7. Beethoven particularly favors false recapitulations in rondo developments

# Construction / Recognition
1. Determine whether refrain 2 is complete or incomplete
2. If complete: identify the pre-core beginning after the refrain's PAC
3. If incomplete: identify where the refrain departs from its expected course
4. Look for a shift to minor as a signal of development onset
5. Identify core(s) with model-sequence technique
6. Check for false recapitulations near the end

# Context & Application
The development section is a "sonata" aspect of the sonata-rondo form. When couplet 2 is organized as an interior theme rather than a development, the overall form is considerably more like a rondo than a sonata.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 679): Beethoven, Violin Sonata in D, Op. 12, No. 1, iii, mm. 58-71 -- complete refrain 2 followed by its beginning repeated with shift to minor, revealing the movement as a sonata-rondo rather than a repeated exposition.
**Example 2** (p. 681): Mozart, Piano Trio in B-flat, K. 502, iii, mm. 86-103 -- incomplete refrain 2 with antecedent closing at m. 87; consequent departs at m. 93 to become a transition-like pre-core.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Hearing the start of refrain 2 as a repeat of the exposition. **Correction**: A shift to minor or departure from expected material signals the onset of a development rather than an exposition repeat.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Whether couplet 2 is a development or an interior theme. **Clarification**: If it contains pre-core/core technique with sequential activity, it is a development; if it is a self-contained theme in a contrasting key, it is an interior theme.
