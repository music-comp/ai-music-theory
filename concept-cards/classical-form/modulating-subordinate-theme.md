# Quick Definition
A subordinate theme that begins in a key other than the one in which it closes, creating the most radical loosening through harmonic-tonal means as the theme modulates from an initial key region to the true subordinate key.

# Formal Definition
The most radical loosening of a subordinate theme by harmonic-tonal means occurs when the theme begins in a key other than that in which it ends. In all cases, the goal subordinate key (in which the theme closes with a PAC) is the dominant region of the home key. The key in which the theme begins can vary, but the submediant region in major-mode movements and the mediant (relative major) in minor-mode ones are usually favored. The initial key is already established by its dominant at the end of the transition. Some writers have adopted the expression "three-key exposition" for such procedures, but this is somewhat of a misnomer: the initial key rarely functions as a fully confirmed key (lacking cadential closure) and instead serves as an emphasized tonal region in the broader modulatory process from home key to true subordinate key.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Theme begins in a key different from the subordinate key
2. Theme modulates during its course to the true subordinate key
3. Closes with PAC in the subordinate key (always the dominant region)
4. Initial key established by dominant at the end of the transition
5. Common initial keys: VI in major, III in minor
6. The initial key is rarely confirmed cadentially -- it functions as a tonal region, not a true key
7. Model-sequence technique often facilitates the modulation
8. The modulation may pass through multiple tonal regions

# Musical Context
Modulating subordinate themes are most common in Beethoven and represent the most extreme tonal loosening available within the subordinate theme. The technique creates a subordinate theme that also possesses a degree of transition function, since it effects a modulation. The initial key's dominant emphasis (from the transition) gives the theme a sense of beginning in "the wrong key," heightening the drama of eventually arriving at the true subordinate key. The closing section often compensates for the theme's harmonic instability by emphasizing root-position tonic.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 31/3, ii, mm. 31-50 (begins in F major [VI of Ab]; 4-measure model sequenced down a fifth to Bb [IV of VI]; fragmentation leads to expanded cadential I6; PAC in Eb [V of home key]; long closing section emphasizes root-position tonic)
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62, mm. 52-102 (begins in Eb [III]; presentation repeated with sequence up a step; arrives on G minor [mediant of Eb] with internal HC; modulates to true subordinate key of G minor for PAC)

# Common Confusions
- The initial key of a modulating subordinate theme is NOT a fully confirmed key -- it lacks cadential closure and functions as an emphasized tonal region
- "Three-key exposition" is somewhat of a misnomer because the "second key" is rarely established as a genuine key
- A modulating subordinate theme is NOT the same as a transition -- it maintains subordinate-theme character (cadential function, loosening techniques) despite its modulatory nature
- The closing section after a modulating subordinate theme often emphasizes root-position tonic to compensate for its absence within the theme proper
