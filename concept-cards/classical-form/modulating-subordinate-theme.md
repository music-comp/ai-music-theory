# Quick Definition
A subordinate theme that begins in a key other than the one in which it closes, creating the most radical loosening through harmonic-tonal means as the theme modulates from an initial key region to the true subordinate key.

# Core Definition
The most radical loosening of a subordinate theme by harmonic-tonal means occurs when the theme begins in a key other than that in which it ends. In all cases, the goal subordinate key (in which the theme closes with PAC) is the dominant region of the home key. The key in which the theme begins can vary, but the submediant region in major-mode movements and the mediant (relative major) in minor-mode ones are usually favored. The initial key is already established by its dominant at the end of the transition. This initial key is rarely confirmed as such by cadential closure; rather, it functions more as an emphasized tonal region in the broader modulatory process from home key to true subordinate key. A modulating subordinate theme also possesses a degree of transition function.

# Prerequisites
- **Subordinate theme**: The formal unit being loosened
- **Loosening techniques**: The broader category
- **Subordinate key**: The tonal goal (always dominant region)

# Key Properties
1. Theme begins in a key different from the subordinate key
2. Modulates during its course to the true subordinate key
3. Closes with PAC in the subordinate key (always the dominant region)
4. Initial key established by dominant at end of transition
5. Common initial keys: VI in major, III in minor
6. Initial key is rarely cadentially confirmed -- functions as tonal region, not true key
7. Model-sequence technique often facilitates the modulation
8. The theme possesses a degree of transition function
9. Most common in Beethoven; also in Haydn and Mozart

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify a subordinate theme whose opening key differs from its closing key
- Verify that PAC occurs in the true subordinate key (dominant region)
- Note the initial key and how it was established by the transition
- Observe the modulatory path (often through model-sequence technique)
- Check whether the closing section compensates for missing root-position tonic

# Context & Application
Modulating subordinate themes represent the most extreme tonal loosening available within the subordinate theme. The technique creates a theme that also possesses a degree of transition function. The closing section often compensates for the theme's harmonic instability by emphasizing root-position tonic, which may have been entirely absent during the modulating portion.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 31/3, ii, mm. 31-50: begins in F major (VI of Ab); 4-measure model sequenced down a fifth to Bb; fragmentation leads to ECP; PAC in Eb (V of home key); long closing section emphasizes root-position tonic
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62, mm. 52-102: begins in Eb (III); presentation repeated with sequence; arrives on G minor with internal HC; modulates to true subordinate key

# Common Errors
- Treating the initial key as a fully confirmed key (it lacks cadential closure)
- Failing to recognize that the theme possesses transition function alongside ST function
- Missing the compensatory function of the closing section

# Common Confusions
- **Initial key vs. true key**: The initial key is NOT fully confirmed; it functions as an emphasized tonal region
- **"Three-key exposition" as misnomer**: Only two keys are truly established (home key and subordinate key)
- **Modulating ST vs. transition**: A modulating ST maintains subordinate-theme character (cadential function, loosening techniques) despite its modulatory nature
