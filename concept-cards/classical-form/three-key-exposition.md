# Quick Definition
An exposition in which the subordinate theme begins in a key different from the true subordinate key, creating the impression of three tonal areas, though the initial "second key" functions more as an emphasized tonal region than a fully confirmed key.

# Formal Definition
Some writers have adopted the expression "three-key exposition" to characterize situations in which a modulating subordinate theme begins in one key and ends in another. This term is somewhat of a misnomer: in the classical style, the initial "second" key is rarely confirmed by cadential closure and thus functions more as an emphasized tonal region in the broader modulatory process from the home key to the true subordinate key (dominant). The initial key is established by its dominant at the end of the transition. Common initial keys include the submediant in major-mode movements and the mediant (relative major) in minor-mode movements. The true subordinate key, confirmed by PAC, is always the dominant region of the home key.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Transition ends with the dominant of an intermediate key (not the true subordinate key)
2. Subordinate theme begins in this intermediate key
3. Theme modulates during its course to the true subordinate key (V of home key)
4. PAC confirms only the true subordinate key
5. The intermediate key is NOT cadentially confirmed -- it functions as a tonal region
6. Common intermediate keys: VI in major, III in minor
7. Most often found in Beethoven; also in Mozart and Haydn

# Musical Context
The three-key exposition represents the most radical form of tonal loosening in the exposition. The modulatory path -- home key to intermediate key to true subordinate key -- creates a more complex and dramatic tonal journey than the standard two-key exposition. However, the term "three-key" overstates the case: only the home key and the subordinate key are truly established as keys through cadential confirmation. The intermediate key is better understood as a waypoint in the modulatory process.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 31/3, ii (home key Ab; transition leads to F major [VI]; subordinate theme begins in F major, modulates to Eb [V]; PAC in Eb)
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62 (home key C minor; transition leads to Eb [III]; subordinate theme begins in Eb, modulates through G minor region; PAC in G minor [v])

# Common Confusions
- "Three-key exposition" is a MISNOMER -- the middle key is rarely a fully confirmed key; it is an emphasized tonal region
- Only TWO keys are truly established: the home key (by the main theme) and the subordinate key (by the subordinate theme's PAC)
- The intermediate key's dominant (established by the transition) does not confirm that key -- cadential closure would be required
- The concept is most associated with Beethoven's more adventurous tonal practices
