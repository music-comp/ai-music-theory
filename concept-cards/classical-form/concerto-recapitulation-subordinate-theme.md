# Quick Definition
The subordinate-theme group of the solo recapitulation, which frequently incorporates material from the opening ritornello not used in the solo exposition, particularly the ritornello's first subordinate theme.

# Core Definition
The subordinate-theme group of the solo recapitulation offers several opportunities for altering the structure relative to the earlier expositions. Since the solo exposition typically introduces one or two new subordinate themes while eliminating one or more from the opening ritornello, the recapitulation frequently incorporates ritornello material not found in the solo exposition. The ritornello's first subordinate theme is especially likely to return in the recapitulation because: (1) it was often eliminated from the solo exposition to give the solo its own theme; (2) its soft, lyrical character makes it unsuitable for the subordinate-key ritornello; (3) it "cries out" to be performed by the solo at some point. It may return at the beginning of the subordinate-theme group, after the start of the first solo subordinate theme, or at the start of a second subordinate theme. It does not normally return later, since a lyrical theme would not help build the climax needed for the exposition's end.

# Construction / Recognition
- Incorporates material from both the ritornello and the solo exposition
- The ritornello's first subordinate theme commonly reappears here
- Three possible positions for the returning ritornello theme:
  1. Beginning of the subordinate-theme group (in its original position)
  2. After the start of the first solo subordinate theme
  3. At the start of a second subordinate theme
- The ritornello theme is typically lyrical and quiet, so it appears early in the group
- Later themes build the climax needed for the cadential trill

# Context & Application
The recapitulation's subordinate-theme group is where the concerto's "double exposition" structure is most fully synthesized. Material from both the ritornello and the solo exposition is woven together to create a group that is genuinely new in its arrangement, even though its individual components have been heard before.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i: Ritornello's first subordinate theme (m. 51, quiet and peaceful) returns at the start of the recapitulation's subordinate group (m. 312), played by solo piano; the solo exposition's lyrical theme (m. 153) then returns at m. 328 as a second subordinate theme

# Common Confusions
- The recapitulation's subordinate-theme group is not simply a transposition of the solo exposition's group. Students should expect a new arrangement that draws on both earlier expositions.
- The ritornello's first subordinate theme returns in the recapitulation, not in the subordinate-key ritornello (which uses different, more forceful material).
