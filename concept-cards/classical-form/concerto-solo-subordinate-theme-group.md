# Quick Definition
The solo subordinate-theme group typically contains at least two themes (often three), mixing material from the opening ritornello with new material for the soloist, and is substantially loosened compared to the ritornello's tight-knit subordinate themes.

# Core Definition
The solo subordinate-theme group is responsible for expressing and confirming the subordinate key. It contains at least two themes (each ending with a PAC), though three is extremely common. One or more themes is based on ideas from the opening ritornello, but new material is almost always added. The entire group can sometimes be unrelated to the ritornello. When drawing on ritornello material, the group typically uses the ritornello's subordinate-theme content, but may also derive from the main theme or especially the transition (which was often replaced by a new solo transition). In the majority of concertos, the first subordinate theme is entirely new, allowing the solo to project its own melodic-motivic profile. The ritornello's first subordinate theme usually reappears as either the second subordinate theme or the second part of a two-part subordinate theme. The group is substantially loosened: one theme prominently extends continuation function through harmonic sequence, and cadential areas are greatly expanded (Caplin, pp. 709-711).

# Key Properties
1. At least two subordinate themes (three is common)
2. Mixes ritornello material with new material
3. First subordinate theme is usually entirely new
4. Ritornello's first subordinate theme typically reappears as the second theme
5. Substantially loosened compared to the ritornello subordinate themes
6. Extended continuation function through harmonic sequence
7. Greatly expanded cadential areas culminating in the cadential trill
8. Final subordinate theme often written in bravura style

# Construction / Recognition
1. Count the subordinate themes within the solo exposition
2. Identify which themes are new and which derive from the ritornello
3. Note the substantial formal loosening (sequences, cadential expansions)
4. Look for the bravura theme with continuous sixteenth-note figuration
5. Locate the cadential trill marking the end of the group

# Context & Application
The solo subordinate-theme group is where the concerto's aesthetic goals converge most fully: new material gives the soloist individuality, formal loosening creates space for virtuosic display, and the subordinate key is dramatically confirmed.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 709): Mozart, Violin Concerto in A, K. 219, i, mm. 73-84 -- Part 1 of first subordinate theme consists of new ideas; Part 2 brings back material from the ritornello subordinate theme.
**Example 2** (p. 711): Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i, mm. 170-83 -- bravura second subordinate theme, initially an 8-m. sentence, repeated and expanded with model-sequence technique and 10-m. dominant expansion.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Expecting the solo subordinate-theme group to match the ritornello's. **Correction**: The solo group is typically much more elaborate and loosened.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Why the first subordinate theme is often entirely new. **Clarification**: A new theme allows the soloist to project a distinctive melodic-motivic profile.
