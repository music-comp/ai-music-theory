# Quick Definition
Sonata form consists of three required sections -- exposition, development, and recapitulation -- analogous to the rounded binary version of small ternary, with the exposition establishing a tonal conflict between home and subordinate keys that is intensified in the development and resolved in the recapitulation.

# Core Definition
Sonata form consists of three required sections with unique section functions: exposition, development, and recapitulation. The structure ||: Exposition :|| ||: Development--Recapitulation :|| resembles the rounded binary version of small ternary, with the three sections corresponding to A, B, and A' sections. Two optional functions may frame the form: a slow introduction before the exposition and a coda after the recapitulation. The exposition creates a dramatic conflict of tonalities between the home key and subordinate key. This conflict is intensified in the development through exploration of additional (usually minor-mode) tonal areas and resolved in the recapitulation, which restates the exposition's material firmly in the home key. The exposition emphasizes major modality; the development tends to emphasize minor modalities.

# Key Properties
1. Three required sections: exposition, development, recapitulation
2. Two optional framing functions: slow introduction, coda
3. Exposition normally repeated; development-recapitulation sometimes repeated
4. Large-scale tonal structure: HK (exposition) -> DK regions (development) -> HK (recapitulation)
5. Exposition emphasizes major modality; development emphasizes minor modalities
6. Major-mode subordinate key: V; minor-mode subordinate key: III (or v)
7. Development keys (major): vi, iii, ii; development keys (minor): iv, v
8. Recapitulation remains in home key throughout; subdominant emphasis typically occurs early
9. Most instrumental cycles contain at least one sonata-form movement
10. The period's most highly developed and complex compositional design

# Construction / Recognition
- **Beginning of exposition**: obvious (change to fast tempo if preceded by slow introduction)
- **End of exposition**: articulated by repetition structure (repeat signs or written-out repeat)
- **Beginning of development**: right after the close of the exposition
- **End of development / start of recapitulation**: return of main theme's basic idea in home key
- **End of recapitulation / start of coda**: when the recapitulation no longer corresponds with the exposition
- Identify landmarks by cadences defining the END of thematic units (more reliable than new melodies marking beginnings)

# Context & Application
This chapter presents sonata form through a single principal example: Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F minor, Op. 2/1, i. The approach emphasizes identifying formal functions at all levels of structure throughout the full-movement form -- what distinguishes this textbook from all prior approaches. The concept of tight-knit vs. loose organization, introduced for small ternary, now assumes major prominence. The main theme defines a standard of tight-knit organization for the entire movement, against which all other sections are compared.

# Examples

# Common Errors
- Using melodic markers (new tunes) to identify section boundaries rather than cadences
- Treating loosening techniques as compositional weakness rather than functional appropriateness
- Confusing the structure of the standing on the dominant with a sentence theme type

# Common Confusions
- **Sentential grouping vs. sentence**: Many units (core, standing on dominant) resemble sentences in grouping but lack genuine presentation function (no tonic prolongation), so they are not true sentence theme types
- **Loosening as weakness**: "Structural loosening does not reflect compositional inadequacy. Rather, deft handling of loosening devices is a sure sign of compositional skill"
- **Landmarks**: Cadences defining the END of thematic units are more reliable than salient melodic material marking beginnings
