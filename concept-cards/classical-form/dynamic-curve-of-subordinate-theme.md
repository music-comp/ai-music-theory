# Quick Definition
The characteristic pattern of dynamic fluctuation in a subordinate theme, typically beginning in relative calm, building progressively to a climax near the cadential arrival, and then dissipating or sustaining in the closing section.

# Formal Definition
A subordinate theme (or theme group) usually features a dynamic curve involving a fluctuation of progressive and recessive dynamics, with a distinct climax at some point in the theme. The theme typically begins in a state of relative calm (exceptions exist, such as Beethoven's Op. 31/3, ii, which begins fortissimo). Most themes feature a progressive dynamic leading to a high point near the end of the theme or at the moment of cadential arrival. The major climax of the subordinate-theme area is usually associated with an expanded cadential progression. In concerto movements, the climax occurs where increasing rhythmic activity reaches its highest state with the trill in the solo part just before cadential arrival (concerto style). The closing section then exhibits varied dynamic states.

# Construction/Recognition
1. **Opening**: Typically calm, piano dynamic (even if rhythmically active)
2. **Progressive phase**: Building intensity through the continuation and cadential functions
3. **Climax**: Usually near the end, associated with expanded cadential progression or cadential arrival
4. **Closing section options**:
   - Recessive dynamic (energy dissipation -- most common)
   - Sudden return to forte at very end (decisive conclusion)
   - Sustained intensity (highly charged to end of exposition)
   - Recessive then progressive (false closing section leading to new theme)
5. Concerto-style climax features virtuosic passage-work and trill before cadential arrival

# Musical Context
The dynamic curve is an important analytical tool for understanding the large-scale shape of the subordinate theme. The progressive-to-climax pattern dramatizes the achievement of cadential closure in the subordinate key, which is the theme's primary formal goal. The closing section's dynamic state reflects how the composer handles the energy built up through the cadential process. The dynamic curve also helps distinguish false closing sections (recessive then progressive) from genuine ones (consistently recessive).

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A, Op. 2/2, i, mm. 58-116 (calm opening; progressive buildup; recessive closing section dissipates energy)
- Mozart, Clarinet Quintet in A, K. 581, i, mm. 42-79 (progressive dynamic to expanded dominant with trill [concerto style] at m. 64; closing section returns to forte at mm. 78-79)
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62, mm. 102-118 (closing section sustains high intensity to end of exposition)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Op. 31/3, ii, mm. 31-63 (exceptional: begins fortissimo; progressive dynamics resume after brief calm; closing section emphasizes root-position tonic)

# Common Confusions
- A calm opening does not mean the theme lacks energy -- intensity may be expressed through rhythmic activity even at soft dynamic
- The climax is usually near the END of the theme, not the beginning (exceptions like Op. 31/3, ii are rare)
- A false closing section's dynamic pattern (recessive then progressive) differs from a genuine closing section's (consistently recessive or consistently intense)
- Concerto-style climax (with trill) can appear in chamber and solo-sonata genres as well, signaling a "brilliant" idiom
