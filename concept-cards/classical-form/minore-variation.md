# Quick Definition
A variation in the opposite modality from the theme (minor if the theme is major, or vice versa), which often requires significant changes to harmonic progressions, internal phrase functions, and even the overall form -- sometimes resembling an interior theme of a large ternary.

# Core Definition
A classical variations movement normally includes one variation written in a modality opposite from the others. This minore variation (or maggiore, in the rarer case of a minor-mode movement) often changes the original harmonic-tonal organization of the theme. Simply shifting harmonies into minor can result in nonsyntactical progressions, requiring new ones to be written. The modal shift also provides an opportunity for exploring different tonal realms. As a result, internal phrase functions are sometimes altered even while the overall form is retained. At other times, the small ternary at the basis of the theme is restructured to become a small binary for the minore, or vice versa. In more extreme cases, the minore takes on a distinctly looser organization, resembling the interior theme of a large ternary. Finally, the minore can be altered to such an extent that it gives the impression of being a variation of an entirely different theme.

# Prerequisites
- **Theme and variations**: The formal context
- **Minore/maggiore contrast**: The modal shift at the heart of the variation

# Key Properties
1. Written in the opposite modality from the theme
2. May require new harmonic progressions (minor-mode transposition may create nonsyntactical harmonies)
3. Internal phrase functions may be altered while overall form is retained
4. Small ternary may become small binary (or vice versa)
5. May take on looser organization resembling a large ternary interior theme
6. In extreme cases, sounds like a variation of a different theme
7. The minore usually occupies a penultimate or antepenultimate position in the variation set

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the variation in the opposite mode from the theme
- Compare its harmonic-tonal organization with the theme
- Note changes in phrase functions and overall form
- Assess the degree of structural alteration: from minimal to extreme
- Check whether the minore resembles an interior theme or a completely new theme

# Context & Application
The minore variation is one of the most distinctive structural events in a variations movement. Its degree of alteration varies enormously: from a simple modal shift of the theme's harmonies to a complete recomposition. The parallel with the interior theme of the large ternary is significant, as both feature modal contrast and both may distort the underlying small-ternary form.

# Examples
- Mozart, String Quartet in D minor, K. 421, iv, mm. 97-112 (ex. 17.18): maggiore variation of a minor-mode theme
- Beethoven, Violin Sonata in A, Op. 30/1, iii, mm. 97-151 (ex. 17.19): minore variation with significant structural alterations
- Haydn, theme shown in Ex. 7.21 with minore in Ex. 17.3: phrase functions and overall form differ from the theme

# Common Errors
- Expecting the minore to retain the exact formal structure of the theme
- Treating nonsyntactical harmonies in a mechanical minor-mode transposition as the composer's intention
- Missing the parallel between the minore variation and the large ternary's interior theme

# Common Confusions
- **Minore vs. maggiore**: Minore = minor variation of a major theme; maggiore = major variation of a minor theme
- **Degree of alteration**: Ranges from minimal (same form, different harmonies) to extreme (different form, different theme)
- **Interior theme parallel**: Both the minore variation and the interior theme feature modal contrast, but they belong to different formal types
