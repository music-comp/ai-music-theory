# Quick Definition
A cadential progression in which the expected root-position final tonic is replaced by a different harmony (most commonly VI), thwarting the anticipated authentic cadential closure.

# Formal Definition
The deceptive cadential progression is created when the final tonic of the authentic cadential progression is replaced by a related harmony. The most common form sees the bass ascend stepwise from ^5 to ^6, supporting a submediant (VI) substituting for the implied final tonic. This progression can be embellished by a passing secondary dominant of VI. Less frequent variants include the dominant leading to a first-inversion tonic (I6) rather than the expected root-position form, sometimes via a passing V7. A more dramatic deception converts the final tonic into a secondary dominant seventh of the subdominant (V7/IV), making the tonic too unstable to create cadential closure.

# Construction/Recognition
- Most common: I(6) - pre-dominant - V - VI (bass: ^5 ascending to ^6)
- With passing chord: V - V7/VI - VI (ex. 2.9b)
- To first-inversion tonic: V - I6 (ex. 2.9d), often via passing V7 (ex. 2.9e)
- To V7/IV: V - V7/IV (ex. 2.9f), converting tonic into a secondary dominant
- Various harmonies can be built over ^6 in the bass (ex. 2.9c)
- The six-four embellishment often precedes the root-position dominant (ex. 2.9e)

# Musical Context
Deceptive cadences are an important loosening technique. They deny expected cadential closure, requiring the cadential progression to be attempted again. In subordinate themes, deceptive cadences create cadential extensions by motivating repetition of the cadential phrase. The technique is also used dramatically in development sections and retransitions.

# Examples
- Basic: V-VI with stepwise ascending bass (ex. 2.9a)
- Beethoven, Op. 2/1, i, mm. 33-37: an evaded cadence (related concept) where the expected PAC leads instead to I6, motivating repetition of the entire cadential phrase

# Common Confusions
- A deceptive cadence is a specific type of cadential progression (where the final tonic is replaced), not merely any unexpected harmonic event.
- The deceptive resolution to VI works because VI shares two common tones with I (substitute chord relationship).
- Deceptive cadences should be distinguished from evaded cadences, where the cadential process is more fundamentally disrupted.
