# Quick Definition
The contrast created by shifting between major and minor modes of the same tonic, used prominently in interior themes of large ternary and rondo forms, and in theme-and-variations movements.

# Core Definition
Minore/maggiore contrast is a technique of modal opposition that retains the same tonal center while shifting to the opposite mode. In large ternary slow movements, the interior theme shifts immediately to the minor mode (labeled "minore" in the score). In movements whose main theme is in minor, the interior theme shifts to major ("maggiore"). This contrast also appears as a standard feature of theme-and-variations movements, where one variation is written in the opposite mode. The modal shift may necessitate new harmonizations and altered phrase functions, since simply shifting harmonies into minor can produce nonsyntactical progressions.

# Prerequisites
- **Large ternary form**: The primary context for this contrast

# Key Properties
1. The tonic pitch remains the same; only the mode changes
2. In large ternary: the interior theme begins immediately in the opposite mode
3. In theme and variations: typically one variation is designated minore or maggiore
4. The modal shift may necessitate reharmonization and new progressions
5. Minor mode typically brings more intense, Sturm und Drang affect
6. The interior theme is never set in the standard subordinate key

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for an immediate modal shift at the beginning of the interior theme or variation
- Confirm the tonic pitch is retained while the mode changes
- Note any reharmonization necessitated by the modal shift
- In variations, identify the minore variation by its altered harmonic-tonal scheme

# Context & Application
This technique is pervasive in classical slow movements and variation sets. In alternating variations (Haydn), the second theme is typically a minore. The minore variation in a theme-and-variations movement may alter the small ternary structure, sometimes becoming a small binary or even a loosely organized passage resembling an interior theme.

# Examples
- Haydn's large ternary slow movements: interior themes consistently shift to minore
- Haydn's alternating variations: the second theme is typically a minore whose opening idea may derive from the first theme

# Common Errors
- Confusing modal shift with modulation to a new key
- Expecting the minor-mode passage to function as a development

# Common Confusions
- **Mode change vs. key change**: Minore/maggiore involves changing the mode while retaining the tonic pitch; it is not a modulation to a new key
- **Minore variation vs. standard variation**: The minore variation may require entirely new harmonies and may alter the formal structure of the theme
