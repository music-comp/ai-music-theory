# Quick Definition
The coda's function of bringing back expositional material that was deleted from the recapitulation, "recapitulating" it in a new context.

# Formal Definition
To fulfill its own particular formal functions, the recapitulation frequently deletes passages from the exposition. The possibility of "recapitulating" this deleted material is offered by the coda. The restored material may appear at its original tonal level (rather than being transposed as it would have been in the recapitulation), or it may be recomposed to serve a new function within the coda's context.

# Construction/Recognition
- Identify passages deleted from the recapitulation (compared with the exposition)
- Look for those passages appearing in the coda, often at their original tonal level
- The restored material may serve a different formal function in the coda (e.g., providing a dramatic tonicization rather than a continuation)
- If the recapitulation's closing section was eliminated, the coda may restore it as its own closing section

# Musical Context
Restoration of deleted material demonstrates the organic wholeness of the movement: material that cannot be accommodated in the recapitulation finds its place in the coda.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Trio in C, Hob. XV:27, iii (ex. 12.8): a dramatic tonicization of B minor (m. 214) in the coda restores a similarly dramatic B-minor tonicization from the exposition's subordinate theme (ex. 12.8b); Haydn deleted it from the recapitulation because transposing it down a fifth would have been tonally awkward
- The same work: the closing section deleted from the recapitulation reappears as the coda's own closing section

# Common Confusions
- Restored material does not appear in the same formal context — it may serve an entirely different function
- The material may appear at its original tonal level specifically because transposition in the recapitulation would have been problematic
- Not all deleted material is restored — only material the composer deems worthy of a final hearing
