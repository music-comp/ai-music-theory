# Quick Definition
The coda's function of bringing back expositional material that was deleted from the recapitulation, "recapitulating" it in a new formal context, often at its original tonal level.

# Core Definition
To fulfill its own particular formal functions, the recapitulation frequently deletes passages from the exposition. The possibility of "recapitulating" this deleted material is offered by the coda. The restored material may appear at its original tonal level (rather than being transposed as it would have been in the recapitulation), or it may be recomposed to serve a new function within the coda's context. If the recapitulation's closing section was eliminated (because the coda starts immediately after the subordinate-theme group), the coda will likely restore that deleted material as its own closing section. When this occurs, the coda can sometimes give the impression of being interpolated in, rather than following on, the recapitulation.

# Prerequisites
- **Coda**: The section restoring deleted material
- **Compensatory functions**: The broader category
- **Subordinate-theme alterations in recapitulation**: The source of many deletions

# Key Properties
1. Recapitulation frequently deletes exposition passages for functional reasons
2. The coda offers the opportunity to "recapitulate" this deleted material
3. Material may appear at its original tonal level (not transposed)
4. Material may serve a different formal function in the coda
5. Deleted closing sections may be restored as the coda's own closing section
6. When this occurs, the coda may seem "interpolated" rather than appended
7. The restoration demonstrates the organic wholeness of the movement

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify passages deleted from the recapitulation (compared with the exposition)
- Look for those passages appearing in the coda
- Note whether the material appears at its original tonal level or transposed
- Check whether the restored material serves a different formal function
- If the recapitulation's closing section was eliminated, check whether the coda restores it

# Context & Application
Restoration demonstrates the organic wholeness of the movement: material that cannot be accommodated in the recapitulation finds its place in the coda. Material may appear at its original tonal level specifically because transposition in the recapitulation would have been problematic.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Trio in C, Hob. XV:27, iii (ex. 12.8): dramatic B-minor tonicization from the exposition's subordinate theme (ex. 12.8b) was deleted from the recapitulation (because transposing it down a fifth would have been tonally awkward); restored in the coda as an unusual chromatic pre-dominant (mm. 214-21) at its original tonal level
- Same work: closing section deleted from the recapitulation reappears as the coda's own closing section

# Common Errors
- Assuming deleted material must appear in the same formal context

# Common Confusions
- **Different function**: Restored material does not appear in the same formal context; it may serve an entirely different function
- **Original tonal level**: Material may appear at its original tonal level specifically because transposition would have been problematic
- **Selective restoration**: Not all deleted material is restored; only material the composer deems worthy of a final hearing
- **Interpolation effect**: When the coda restores the recapitulation's closing section, the coda seems inserted between the subordinate theme and the closing section rather than appended
