# Quick Definition
The systematic tonal plan underlying a development section, involving development keys and tonicized regions drawn primarily from minor-mode regions of the home key, contrasting with the predominantly major-mode exposition.

# Core Definition
The tonal organization of the development follows a clear and logical plan despite the section's characteristic instability. A hierarchy of tonal levels structures the development: the home key remains the ultimate reference; the subordinate key from the exposition may linger at the start; one or more development keys receive cadential confirmation; and various tonicized regions arise through sequential activity. The development emphasizes minor-mode regions, contrasting with the predominantly major-mode exposition. In major-mode movements, VI is the most common primary development key, followed by III and II. In minor-mode movements, IV and V (minor) are used with approximately equal frequency. The subdominant rarely serves as a primary development key in major because it does not provide modal contrast and because V/IV would poorly anticipate the home-key tonic at the recapitulation.

# Prerequisites
- **Development section**: The formal context
- **Development key**: The keys confirmed within the development
- **Tonal hierarchy**: The ranked system of tonal levels

# Key Properties
1. Clear tonal plan despite characteristic instability
2. Minor-mode emphasis contrasts with major-mode exposition
3. In major: VI most common primary development key; III and II also common
4. In minor: IV and V (minor) used with approximately equal frequency
5. IV rarely primary in major (lacks modal contrast; V/IV poorly anticipates I)
6. Primary development key receives strongest cadential articulation
7. Secondary development keys receive weaker articulation
8. Development typically ends on dominant of home key
9. Beethoven expands the tonal palette with borrowed keys (bVI, bIII, minor V)

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the keys explored in the development
- Determine which receive cadential confirmation (development keys) vs. brief tonicization
- Designate the primary development key (strongest articulation, usually final key before retransition)
- Note the emphasis on minor-mode regions
- Observe Beethoven's expanded palette when present

# Context & Application
The development is not a section of "free modulation" but follows predictable tonal patterns based on the home key's mode. The emphasis on minor modality creates maximum contrast with the exposition's major-mode stability. The subdominant's avoidance as primary development key in major is a consistent stylistic norm.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in A Minor, K. 310, i: development explores E minor (HK: V) as development key
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E-flat, Hob. XVI:49, i: development moves through C minor (HK: VI) and F minor (HK: II)
- Mozart, Symphony No. 36 ("Linz"), K. 425, i: A minor (HK: VI) as development key

# Common Errors
- Treating the development as "free modulation" without pattern
- Confusing tonicized regions with development keys (different levels of confirmation)
- Expecting IV as a primary development key in major (it is rarely used)

# Common Confusions
- **Free vs. organized**: The development follows predictable tonal patterns, not haphazard modulation
- **Tonicization vs. key**: Tonicized regions lack cadential confirmation; development keys receive at least a HC
- **IV in major**: The subdominant rarely serves as primary development key because it lacks modal contrast
