# Quick Definition
The characteristic combination of two seemingly incompatible expressive worlds in a slow introduction: the stately and solemn alongside the uncertain and anticipatory.

# Core Definition
A notable characteristic of slow introductions in the classical style is the special way in which they unite the expression of two seemingly incompatible affective worlds. The realm of the stately, heraldic, and solemn is combined with the realm of the anticipatory, uncertain, and unstable. Solemnity is expressed by slow tempo, fanfare gestures, tutti textures, dotted rhythms, and an initial forte dynamic. Anticipation is created by marked discontinuities in melody, rhythm, texture, and dynamics; harmonic instabilities; minor modality; and chromaticism (at times extreme). Some introductions play down the stately character, but they almost always contain something of the uncertain and hesitant.

# Prerequisites
- **Slow introduction**: The formal context

# Key Properties
1. Two seemingly incompatible expressive worlds coexist
2. Solemn qualities: slow tempo, fanfare gestures, tutti, dotted rhythms, forte
3. Anticipatory qualities: discontinuities, harmonic instability, minor modality, chromaticism
4. Both qualities coexist, often alternating within phrases
5. Some introductions primarily anticipatory/uncertain, playing down the stately character
6. The anticipatory quality builds expectation for the exposition's livelier character
7. Distinguishes classical introductions from the more uniformly grave baroque type

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify solemn features (tempo, texture, dynamics, rhythmic profiles)
- Identify anticipatory features (rests, fermatas, harmonic instability, chromaticism, mode shifts)
- Note the coexistence and alternation of both worlds

# Context & Application
This dual affective quality distinguishes classical slow introductions from the more uniformly grave introductions of the baroque era. The combination of gravity and instability creates a unique expressive tension that heightens the impact of the exposition's beginning.

# Examples
- Haydn, Symphony No. 104 ("London"), i (ex. 13.11): fortissimo fanfare with fermatas (solemn); missing harmonic third and dangling leading tones create uncertainty; continued fanfare rhythms maintain heraldic impression while content becomes hesitant
- Beethoven, Violin Sonata in A Minor ("Kreutzer"), Op. 47, i (ex. 13.12): stately sarabande-like opening (solemn); unaccompanied violin creates textural gap (uncertain); shift to minor and chromaticism (anticipatory)

# Common Errors
- Treating solemn and anticipatory qualities as contradictory rather than complementary

# Common Confusions
- **Not contradictory**: Solemn and anticipatory qualities work together to create the introduction's unique character
- **Not equally solemn**: Some introductions are primarily anticipatory/uncertain
- **Not just slow tempo**: The affective combination, not merely tempo, defines the introduction's character
