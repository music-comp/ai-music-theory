# Quick Definition
An imperfect authentic cadence within a subordinate theme signals that the theme has not yet reached its true end, typically motivating a repetition of the preceding phrase to achieve the required perfect authentic cadence.

# Formal Definition
Since subordinate themes almost always close with a PAC, the appearance of an IAC signals that the theme's true end has not yet been reached. Typically, the phrase ending with the IAC (be it continuation, cadential, or continuation=>cadential) is repeated, leading this time to a PAC. When a phrase ending with an IAC is followed by a repetition achieving a PAC, a sense of antecedent-consequent functionality is expressed. However, the resulting structure should not normally be considered a period, since the passage does not function as a self-contained, tight-knit theme and seldom contains other characteristics of a true antecedent or consequent phrase. Moreover, the IAC phrase sometimes elides with the beginning of the following phrase, which does not occur with genuine periods.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Authentic cadential progression resolves with ^3 (not ^1) in the soprano voice
2. The cadence provides partial closure but not the conclusive PAC required
3. The preceding phrase is typically repeated with adjustments to achieve PAC
4. The repetition may feature a stronger cadential dominant
5. An antecedent-consequent expression results, but not a true period
6. Elision between IAC phrase and its repetition is possible (unlike true periods)

# Musical Context
The IAC is the mildest form of cadential extension in subordinate themes. It provides partial closure (unlike evaded and abandoned cadences) but leaves the theme's primary cadential goal unfulfilled. The open-ended quality of the IAC effectively motivates the subsequent repetition. As a loosening technique, the IAC-to-PAC pattern is somewhat redundant but less dramatically powerful than the evaded or deceptive cadence.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Overture to Coriolanus, Op. 62, mm. 102-118 (8-measure sentence closes with IAC at m. 110; continuation repeated with stronger cadential dominant, achieving PAC at m. 114)

# Common Confusions
- An IAC followed by a PAC creates an antecedent-consequent expression but is NOT a true period -- too many other periodic characteristics are lacking
- The IAC is less dramatic than the evaded cadence because it provides PARTIAL closure, whereas the evaded cadence provides NO closure
- Elision between the IAC phrase and its repetition further confirms that this is not a true period (genuine periods do not elide)
- In main themes, an IAC closing a theme is acceptable; in subordinate themes, an IAC always demands a subsequent PAC
