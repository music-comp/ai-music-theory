# Quick Definition
A linking harmony is a shared sonority that functions as both the final harmony of one progression and the first harmony of the next, connecting two adjacent progressions at their boundary.

# Core Definition
"Typically, the individual progressions are fully distinct one from the other. Often, however, the final harmony of one progression can be seen to function as the first harmony of the next progression; this shared sonority can be termed a *linking* harmony" (Ch. 1, p. 4). A linking harmony thus serves a dual role: it provides closure for the preceding progression and initiation for the following one. This technique allows for smooth, continuous harmonic motion across progression boundaries.

# Prerequisites
- **Prolongational progression** -- one of the three progression types that may be linked
- **Cadential progression** -- another progression type that may be linked
- **Sequential progression** -- the third progression type that may be linked

# Key Properties
1. A single harmony that belongs to two adjacent progressions
2. Functions as the final chord of one progression and the initial chord of the next
3. Creates smooth connectivity between progression types
4. Common at boundaries between sequential and cadential progressions
5. The linking harmony must be functionally interpretable in both progressions
6. Its dual role is a matter of analytical interpretation, not notation

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify two adjacent progressions
- Check whether the last harmony of the first is the same as the first of the second
- If yes, this shared harmony is a linking harmony
- Note that "the overall musical context" may sometimes favor assigning the harmony to one progression rather than the other (Ch. 1, p. 7)

# Context & Application
Linking harmonies appear throughout classical music wherever progressions are joined smoothly. They are especially common at the boundary between a sequential progression and the cadential progression that follows it. Identifying linking harmonies helps clarify the boundaries of individual progressions in an analysis.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 1, p. 7, ex. 1.4): Mozart, K. 332, iii, mm. 50-57: "the IV6 at m. 56 is a *linking* harmony: it both ends the sequential progression and initiates the half-cadential one."
**Example 2** (Ch. 1, p. 7, ex. 1.5): Haydn, Symphony No. 93, i, mm. 29-36: the root-position tonic at the end of m. 34 could link the sequential and cadential progressions, though "the overall musical context (and especially the *piano* dynamic at m. 35) suggests that this harmony belongs more to the sequence than to the cadence."

# Common Errors
- Failing to recognize that a harmony can belong to two progressions at once
- Always assigning the linking harmony to one progression and ignoring its dual function
- Treating progression boundaries as always clear-cut

# Common Confusions
- A linking harmony is not a modulation -- it connects progressions within a key (or across keys) by shared sonority, not by pivot-chord function
- The decision about whether a harmony truly links or belongs primarily to one progression depends on musical context -- there is not always a single correct answer
