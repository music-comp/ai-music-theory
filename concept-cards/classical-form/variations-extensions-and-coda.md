# Quick Definition
Passages that break the regular succession of variations -- either extensions/interpolations/links between variations (typically before the finale) or a genuine coda after the last variation that breaks formal symmetry and permits structural expansion.

# Core Definition
Besides creating structural changes within a given variation, the composer can alter the general formal plan by adding passages that function as extensions, interpolations, or links from one variation to the next. Such additions typically occur just before the final variation, breaking the regular succession and drawing attention to the finale by preceding it with something entirely new. The added material is often short but can be a more lengthy, developmental passage. The final variation is often followed by a genuine coda, which serves several functions: breaking the pattern of formal symmetry created by the regular succession of equal-sized units; providing the only real opportunity for structural expansion (such as cadential expansions); and permitting the composer to create a circular effect by bringing back the theme in its original, unvaried version at the very end.

# Prerequisites
- **Theme and variations**: The formal context

# Key Properties
1. Extensions/interpolations typically occur before the final variation
2. They break the regular succession of variations, drawing attention to the finale
3. Extensions may be short or may be lengthy developmental passages
4. Extensions often feature model-sequence technique and end with a fermata
5. The coda follows the final variation
6. The coda breaks the pattern of formal symmetry (equal-sized units)
7. The coda provides the only opportunity for large-scale structural expansion
8. The coda may bring back the theme in its original (unvaried) version for circular effect

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for passages between variations that do not conform to the theme's formal structure
- Note tempo changes, fermatas, and model-sequence technique as markers
- After the final variation, identify whether a coda is present
- Check whether the coda brings back the original theme

# Context & Application
The regular succession of variations creates a distinctive formal challenge: how to provide a satisfying conclusion when each unit is essentially the same length. Extensions and codas solve this by introducing asymmetry and expansion precisely where they are most needed -- toward the end of the movement.

# Examples
- Mozart, Clarinet Quintet in A, K. 581, iv, mm. 79-86 and 99-107 (ex. 17.10): two extensions, each ending with a fermata; first precedes the Adagio variation, second precedes the Allegro finale
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in G, Op. 14/2, ii (ex. 17.11): brief coda returns to the original staccato eighth-note texture of the theme, set an octave higher

# Common Errors
- Treating an extension as another variation (it does not follow the theme's formal plan)
- Missing the coda's function of breaking formal symmetry
- Expecting the coda to follow the theme's structure

# Common Confusions
- **Extension vs. variation**: An extension does not conform to the theme's formal structure
- **Coda in variations vs. coda in sonata form**: Both serve compensatory functions, but the variations coda uniquely addresses the problem of formal symmetry
