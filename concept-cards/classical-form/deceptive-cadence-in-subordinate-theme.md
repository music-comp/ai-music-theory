# Quick Definition
A cadential progression in a subordinate theme where the final tonic is replaced by a substitute harmony (usually VI), providing partial closure and motivating further cadential attempts to achieve the required PAC.

# Core Definition
An authentic cadential progression can be changed into a deceptive cadential progression if the final tonic is replaced by a related harmony, usually built over the sixth scale-degree in the bass. If the arrival of the substitute harmony truly groups with the preceding material and represents its melodic and harmonic goal, a deceptive cadence brings partial closure to the theme. The theme then continues (usually by repeating the previous idea or phrase) to reach full closure through a PAC. The sense of partial closure is projected largely through the soprano voice's motion from the cadential dominant to the substitute harmony.

# Prerequisites
- **Cadential function**: The function involved in the deceptive resolution
- **Deceptive cadence**: The general harmonic concept (V-VI)
- **Subordinate theme**: The context where DC motivates further cadential attempts

# Key Properties
1. Final tonic replaced by substitute (usually VI)
2. The substitute harmony GROUPS WITH preceding material (backward grouping = goal)
3. Provides partial closure -- more closure than evaded cadence, less than PAC
4. The melody determines whether DC substitutes for PAC (ending on 1) or IAC (ending on 3)
5. Following the DC, previous idea or phrase is typically repeated
6. Provides greater motivation for PAC than IAC because lack of harmonic closure is more palpable

# Construction / Recognition
- Authentic cadential progression with final tonic replaced by a substitute (usually VI)
- The substitute harmony groups with preceding material as a goal (not forward as a new beginning)
- The melody determines the type of cadence being substituted (PAC or IAC)
- Following the DC, the previous cadential idea is typically repeated
- Subsequent attempts lead eventually to PAC

# Context & Application
The deceptive cadence provides greater motivation for a subsequent PAC than does the IAC, because the lack of harmonic closure is more palpable than the merely melodic incompleteness of the IAC. The DC is intermediate in drama -- more dramatic than the IAC but less dramatic than the evaded cadence. It is effective when used in series, building intensity toward the final PAC.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A-flat, Op. 26, iv, mm. 42-48: after standing on dominant, deceptive cadential progression concludes on VI at m. 44; melody ends on 1 so DC substitutes for PAC; idea repeated, another DC at m. 46; third attempt achieves PAC at m. 48

## Contrasts With
- Evaded cadence: in DC, a GOAL is reached (substitute groups backward); in evaded cadence, NO GOAL is reached (event groups forward)

# Common Errors
- Confusing deceptive cadence with evaded cadence based on harmony alone (the distinction is in grouping direction)
- Treating repetition after DC as "one more time" technique (which specifically involves cadential evasion)

# Common Confusions
- **DC vs. evaded cadence**: The fundamental distinction is grouping direction -- backward (DC, partial closure) vs. forward (evaded, no closure)
- **Repetition after DC vs. "one more time"**: "One more time" involves cadential evasion specifically; repetition after DC is motivated by partial closure, a fundamentally different aesthetic
- **Multiple DCs**: DCs in succession create effective buildup but are less common than chains of evaded cadences
