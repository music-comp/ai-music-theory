# Quick Definition
The specific point in the recapitulation where the tonal path diverges from the exposition, marking where the music is "adjusted" to remain in the home key.

# Core Definition
The tonal adjustment required to keep the subordinate theme in the home key must occur at some specific point in the recapitulation. This moment -- where the recapitulation's harmonic path begins to differ from the exposition's -- can be called the crux or point of recapitulatory adjustment. The adjustment can occur at any place in the transition (early, middle, or late) and can be accomplished by various harmonic and phrase-structural means. Frequently, the subdominant region is tonicized at this point, promoting a logical succession to the dominant. The location of the crux determines how much of the transition is recomposed.

# Prerequisites
- **Recapitulation**: The formal context
- **Tonal adjustment**: The procedure whose starting point the crux marks

# Key Properties
1. The first moment where the recapitulation's harmonic content diverges from the exposition
2. May occur early in the transition (much of the transition recomposed)
3. May occur late in the transition (only the final modulation adjusted)
4. May occur within the subordinate theme itself (if the transition is nonmodulating)
5. Often involves a shift toward the flat side (IV, bVI) before returning to the home key
6. The subdominant is frequently tonicized at this point
7. An early crux gives more freedom for secondary development and flat emphasis
8. A late crux preserves more of the exposition's structure

# Construction / Recognition
- Compare the recapitulation with the exposition measure by measure
- The crux is the first moment where harmonic content diverges
- Note whether the divergence occurs early, middle, or late in the transition
- Often coincides with a mode change or chromatic shift
- In nonmodulating transitions, the crux may be within the subordinate theme

# Context & Application
The location of the crux determines how much of the transition is recomposed. An early crux gives the composer more freedom to create new material (secondary development, flat-region emphasis); a late crux preserves more of the exposition's structure. The crux need not be a dramatic moment -- it may be a subtle harmonic change.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Symphony No. 7, Op. 92, i (ex. 11.4): early crux -- transition begins with material from the middle of the exposition's transition, deleting the opening tonic-pedal passage
- Haydn, Piano Trio in E-flat, Hob. XV:30, i (ex. 11.7): mode change at m. 158 marks the crux
- Mozart, Violin Sonata in C, K. 403, i (ex. 11.5): immediate shift to parallel minor at the very beginning of the transition marks an early crux

# Common Errors
- Assuming the crux must always be a dramatic or obvious moment
- Assuming the crux always occurs at the same formal position

# Common Confusions
- **Subtlety**: The crux is not always a dramatic moment; it may be a subtle harmonic change
- **Variable position**: The crux need not occur at the same formal position in every movement; it depends on the exposition's tonal structure
- **Nonmodulating transitions**: When the transition is nonmodulating, the crux occurs within the subordinate theme or at its transposition into the home key
- **Terminological note**: Caplin does not use the term "crux" himself, but the concept of "point of adjustment" is implicit throughout Chapter 11
