# Quick Definition
A transition in which tonal destabilization occurs in two distinct stages: a first part leading to a half cadence or dominant arrival in the home key, followed by a second part that modulates to the subordinate key.

# Formal Definition
The process of tonal destabilization sometimes takes place in two distinct stages, yielding a two-part transition. Following a main theme ending with a PAC, the first part leads to a HC (or dominant arrival) in the home key, functioning like a single nonmodulating transition. The second part then modulates to the subordinate key, functioning like a modulating transition. Frequently, the second part begins with the basic idea of the main theme supported by the home-key tonic. The resulting structure then resembles the case in which a small-ternary recapitulation is retrospectively understood as the beginning of the transition, but the fundamental difference is whether the passage leading to the home-key dominant is understood as a contrasting middle or as the first part of a transition.

# Construction/Recognition
1. Two distinct stages of tonal destabilization
2. **First part**: Leads to HC or dominant arrival in the home key (like a nonmodulating transition)
3. **Second part**: Modulates from the home key to the subordinate key (like a modulating transition)
4. First part often begins with a false closing section to the main theme
5. Second part frequently opens with the main theme's basic idea over home-key tonic
6. Standing on the dominant may follow the HC of the first part
7. A second standing on the dominant may follow the HC ending the second part
8. The principle prohibiting elision between A section and B section can help distinguish a two-part transition from an incomplete small ternary

# Musical Context
The two-part transition represents the most expansive form of transitional process. It combines both types of destabilization: first weakening the home key by moving to its dominant, then fully abandoning the home key through modulation. The two-part structure is particularly useful in large-scale movements where the distance between the main theme and subordinate theme requires a more gradual and extensive transitional process. The return of the basic idea at the start of the second part creates a moment of apparent recapitulation that is then redirected toward the subordinate key.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Trio in D Minor, K. 442, iii, mm. 16-48 (first part: false closing section leads to HC in home key at m. 26; second part: begins with basic idea at m. 32; modulates; HC in subordinate key at m. 42 followed by standing on dominant)
- Mozart, Piano Trio in D Minor, K. 442, iii, mm. 16-26 (first part); mm. 33-48 (second part with basic idea return, imitation, and modulation)

# Common Confusions
- The return of the basic idea at the start of the second part may look like a recapitulation of a small ternary, but if the passage leading to the home-key dominant cannot be a contrasting middle (e.g., because it elides with the preceding A section), the two-part transition interpretation applies
- Both parts of the two-part transition are part of a SINGLE formal function (transition), not two separate transitions
- The HC ending the first part is in the HOME key; the HC ending the second part is in the SUBORDINATE key
- Do not confuse the first part's standing on the dominant with the second part's standing on the dominant -- they serve different functions within the overall two-part structure
