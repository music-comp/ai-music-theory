# Quick Definition
The subordinate-key ritornello fulfills four functions: giving the orchestra its own music in the subordinate key, bringing back deleted ritornello material, sustaining or intensifying the solo's climax, and allowing the soloist to rest before the development.

# Core Definition
The subordinate-key ritornello fulfills a variety of functions. First, it gives the orchestra another opportunity for sounding its own music, now in the context of the subordinate key, allowing the orchestra to participate in the tonal conflict as more than mere accompaniment. Second, it frequently brings back ideas from the opening ritornello that were eliminated from the solo exposition. Third, it sustains or intensifies the dynamic level attained by the solo (a climax created by a single instrument can always be superseded by the full orchestral mass). Fourth, it offers the solo a chance to rest following the virtuosic workout of the exposition and to reappear as a fresh sonority at the beginning of the development. To fulfill its dynamic function, the ritornello usually contains forte material of powerful rhythmic vitality; soft, lyrical gestures are not found at its beginning. A favorite passage to reappear is the transition from the opening ritornello, which is often eliminated in the solo exposition (Caplin, pp. 712-713).

# Key Properties
1. Gives the orchestra independent music in the subordinate key
2. Brings back opening ritornello material deleted in the solo exposition
3. Sustains or intensifies the dynamic climax
4. Allows the soloist to rest before the development
5. Usually begins with forte material of rhythmic vitality
6. Transition material from the opening ritornello is a favorite passage to reuse
7. Usually structured as a complete thematic unit ending with a PAC in the subordinate key
8. May remain open-ended, modulating to set up the development

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the orchestral section following the solo exposition's cadential trill
2. Check whether the material comes from the opening ritornello
3. Note the dynamic level and character (typically forte and vigorous)
4. Determine whether the ritornello achieves cadential closure in the subordinate key

# Context & Application
The subordinate-key ritornello is the formal analogue of a closing section in a sonata exposition, but it displays few characteristics of a typical closing section (no codetta structure, no recessive dynamic). Its content and energy level are distinctly ritornello-like.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 699): Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K. 482, i, mm. 196-206 -- subordinate-key ritornello modeled on the second subordinate theme and closing section of the opening ritornello, transposed to the subordinate key.
**Example 2** (p. 713): Haydn, Trumpet Concerto in E-flat, H. VIIe:1, i, mm. 81-94 -- subordinate-key ritornello drawn from the opening ritornello's subordinate theme, with descending sequence that fails to cadence in B-flat, instead modulating to set up the development.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the subordinate-key ritornello as a closing section. **Correction**: Though analogous in position, it lacks the recessive dynamic and codetta structure of a closing section.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Why the ritornello begins with vigorous material rather than calming material. **Clarification**: Its function is to sustain or exceed the solo's climax, not to wind down.
