# Quick Definition
A reinterpreted half cadence occurs when an antecedent phrase appears to modulate to the dominant region and close there with a PAC, but when the home key is immediately reinstated at the beginning of the consequent, the dominant-key cadence is retrospectively heard as a HC in the home key.

# Formal Definition
One common irregular cadential formation occurs when a presumed antecedent appears to modulate to the dominant region, closing there with a PAC. Two principles of antecedent function are thus violated — that it close with a weak cadence and that it not modulate. Nonetheless, when the home key is immediately reinstituted at the beginning of the consequent phrase, the sense of modulation is instantly canceled, and we recognize that the antecedent has closed with a reinterpreted half cadence. The cadential formation is *literally* a PAC in the dominant key. Only in the broader context of antecedent-consequent functionality does it become a reinterpreted HC in the home key.

# Construction/Recognition
1. The antecedent phrase appears to modulate briefly to the dominant region (V as temporary tonic)
2. The phrase closes with what sounds like a PAC in the dominant key
3. A brief codetta may confirm the dominant-key arrival
4. The consequent phrase begins immediately in the home key
5. The return to the home key retrospectively cancels the modulation
6. The dominant harmony is reinterpreted as the final harmony of a HC in the home key

# Musical Context
The reinterpreted HC is a common cadential irregularity in classical periods. It represents the only situation in which an antecedent phrase may seem to modulate — the modulation is apparent rather than real, revealed as such by the consequent's return to the home key. This creates a subtle harmonic ambiguity that enriches the period form without undermining its fundamental structure.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Serenade for Violin, Viola, and Cello in D, Op. 8, iv, mm. 23-26: apparent PAC in dominant key at end of antecedent; home key returns at m. 27 for the consequent, retrospectively reinterpreting the cadence
- Haydn, String Quartet in G, Op. 64/4, i, mm. 1-4: the opening phrase makes a quick surface modulation to D major (dominant of G); apparent PAC in D, even with a brief codetta; the emphatic return to G major at m. 5 reveals the D-major cadence as a reinterpreted HC

# Common Confusions
- The cadence is *literally* a PAC in the dominant key — it is only reinterpreted as a HC in the broader context of the period; both readings are simultaneously valid at different structural levels
- This is not a "half cadence that sounds like a PAC" — it genuinely *is* a PAC at the surface, but functions as a HC in the home-key periodic context
- The reinterpreted HC is the major exception to the rule that antecedent phrases do not modulate
