# Quick Definition
A reinterpreted half cadence occurs when an antecedent phrase appears to modulate to the dominant region and close there with a PAC, but when the home key is immediately reinstated at the beginning of the consequent, the dominant-key cadence is retrospectively heard as a HC in the home key.

# Core Definition
"In some cases, however, a presumed antecedent appears to modulate to the dominant region, closing there with a perfect authentic cadence. Two principles of antecedent function would thus be violated: that it close with a weak cadence, and that it not modulate" (Ch. 3, p. 91). "Nonetheless, when the home key is immediately reinstituted at the beginning of the consequent phrase, the sense of modulation is instantly canceled, and we can recognize instead that the antecedent has closed with a *reinterpreted half cadence*" (Ch. 3, p. 91). "It must be emphasized that the cadential formation in mm. 3-4 ... is, on the musical surface, a literal PAC. Only in the broader context of antecedent-consequent functionality does it become a reinterpreted half cadence" (Ch. 3, p. 92). The double arrow symbol (=>) represents this retrospective reinterpretation: "a PAC 'becomes' an HC" (Ch. 3, p. 91).

# Prerequisites
- **Antecedent** -- the first phrase of the period, normally closing with a weak cadence
- **Half cadence** -- the cadential type that the PAC is reinterpreted as
- **Period** -- the formal context in which the reinterpretation occurs
- **Authentic cadence** -- the cadential type that appears on the surface

# Key Properties
1. Surface-level: a literal PAC in the dominant key
2. Deeper level: a reinterpreted HC in the home key
3. Both interpretations are valid at their respective structural levels
4. The reinterpretation is triggered by the consequent's return to the home key
5. Uses the retrospective reinterpretation symbol (=>): PAC => HC
6. The only exception to the rule that antecedent phrases do not modulate
7. Arises only rarely in simple 8-m. periods; more frequent in compound 16-m. periods

# Construction / Recognition
1. The antecedent phrase appears to modulate briefly to the dominant region
2. The phrase closes with what sounds like a PAC in the dominant key
3. A brief codetta may confirm the dominant-key arrival
4. The consequent phrase begins immediately in the home key
5. The return to the home key retrospectively cancels the modulation
6. The dominant harmony is reinterpreted as the final harmony of an HC in the home key

# Context & Application
"The reinterpreted half cadence arises only rarely in simple 8-m. periods. We revisit the topic again, in Chapter 6, in connection with the compound 16-m. period, where the reinterpreted half cadence is more frequently used" (Ch. 3, p. 92). This creates a subtle harmonic ambiguity that enriches the period form without undermining its fundamental structure.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 3, p. 91, ex. 3.19): Haydn, String Quartet in G, Op. 64/4, i, mm. 1-8: the opening 4-m. phrase makes a quick surface modulation to D major (dominant of G); apparent PAC in D. "When the music returns emphatically to G major at the beginning of the consequent, however, it is easy to hear that the D major harmony at the end of the antecedent marks a *reinterpreted* HC in the home key."

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- Half cadence and authentic cadence (the two cadence types involved)
- Retrospective reinterpretation (the general cognitive mechanism)
## Enables
- Analysis of periods where the antecedent seems to modulate
- Compound periods (where this technique is more common)
## Related
- Weak cadential closure (the antecedent requires a weak cadence)
- Modulating consequent (the consequent is free to modulate; the antecedent normally is not)
## Contrasts With
- Regular half cadence (which is unambiguously an HC from the start)

# Common Errors
- Calling the cadence "just an HC" without acknowledging its surface-level PAC identity
- Calling it "just a PAC" without recognizing the periodic context that reinterprets it
- Assuming any dominant-key arrival at the end of an antecedent is automatically a reinterpreted HC

# Common Confusions
- The cadence is *literally* a PAC in the dominant key -- it is only reinterpreted as an HC in the broader context of the period; both readings are simultaneously valid at different structural levels
- This is not a "half cadence that sounds like a PAC" -- it genuinely *is* a PAC at the surface, but functions as an HC in the home-key periodic context
- The reinterpreted HC is the major exception to the rule that antecedent phrases do not modulate
