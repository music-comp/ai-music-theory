# Quick Definition
The formal space S of a GIS must be theoretically complete (satisfying Condition B), even though practical musical contexts use only a portion of this extended space. Lewin insists we "conceive the formal space of a GIS as a space of theoretical potentialities, rather than as a compendium of musical practicalities."

# Core Definition
"This is the methodological point: We must conceive the formal space of a GIS as a space of theoretical potentialities, rather than as a compendium of musical practicalities. In a specific compositional or theoretical context, the space S of a GIS might be perfectly accessible in practice. ... On the other hand, in other compositional or theoretical contexts, the space S of a GIS might be pertinent as an entirety only to the extent it is suggested or implied by the actually stated musical material, plus the characteristic relationships actually employed" (Lewin, pp. 47-48).

# Prerequisites
- **Generalized Interval System** — The distinction arises from Condition (B) of the GIS definition
- **Musical space S** — The space that must be theoretically extended

# Key Properties
1. Condition (B) requires: for every s in S and every i in IVLS, a unique t exists with int(s, t) = i
2. S must therefore contain all theoretical targets of all intervals from all points
3. Practical usage occupies only a subset of the full theoretical space
4. Some spaces (e.g., 12 pitch classes) are both practically and theoretically complete
5. "Conceivability" is the criterion: we can conceive supersonic pitches even if we cannot hear them

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Identify the practical musical elements of interest
2. Determine the interval group IVLS
3. Extend S as needed so that every interval from every element lands in S
## To Recognize:
1. A space where practical usage covers only a region of the full theoretical extent
2. Theoretical extension required by Condition (B)

# Context & Application
This distinction is fundamental to GIS theory. Lewin draws an analogy: "In just this way a painting or statue might suggest or imply the entire extension of Euclidean two-or-three-dimensional space, or some other geometrical space. (I am thinking in particular of the parabolic space in some of Van Gogh's late work.)" The analogy clarifies that a composition occupies a region of its theoretical space, just as a painting occupies a region of geometric space.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 47): Pitch space (Examples 2.1.1, 2.1.2) -- Must include "pitches" beyond the audible range. "We shall need to conceive supersonic and subsonic 'pitches' in order to accommodate the idea of being able to go up or down one scale degree from any note."

**Example 2** (p. 47): Harmonic space (Figure 2.2) -- "Obviously no finite musical context can explore the entire extent of this map, which accommodates the idea of being able to conceive the dominant, mediant, subdominant, and submediant of any pitch class." Yet "we need only the pitch classes and the intervals we can infer from one tonic triad (!) in order to generate the entire conceptual group of intervals."

**Example 3** (p. 48): Pitch-class space (2.1.3) -- "Every one of its twelve pitch-classes is easily referenced by any pertinent music." Here practical = theoretical.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Restricting S to only audible or performable elements
  **Correction**: Condition (B) demands theoretical completeness; supersonic pitches, infinitely remote times, etc. must be included

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking theoretical extension makes the impractical elements musically important
  **Clarification**: Theoretical extension doesn't mean these elements are musically useful; they are needed for mathematical completeness. Different GIS structures may have the same practical content but different theoretical extensions.
