# Quick Definition
The senario is Zarlino's foundational set of numbers 1-6 from which all consonant intervals derive through ratios, forming the mathematical basis of his harmonic space.

# Formal Definition
The senario (from Italian/Latin "six") comprises the numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6. All ratios formed between these numbers produce the consonant intervals: octave (2:1), fifth (3:2), fourth (4:3), major third (5:4), minor third (6:5), major sixth (5:3). The minor sixth (8:5) requires extending beyond the senario, creating theoretical difficulties for Zarlino.

# Mathematical Formulation
Senario ratios and their intervals:
- 2:1 = octave
- 3:2 = perfect fifth
- 4:3 = perfect fourth
- 5:4 = major third
- 6:5 = minor third
- 5:3 = major sixth (= major third + perfect fourth)
- 3:1 = octave + fifth
- 4:1 = two octaves
- 5:1 = two octaves + major third
- 6:1 = two octaves + fifth

Problematic ratio:
- 8:5 = minor sixth (requires number 8, outside senario)

# Musical Context/Application
The senario provides a self-contained mathematical foundation for consonance theory. Intervals derivable from senario ratios are "natural" consonances; intervals requiring numbers beyond 6 (like the minor sixth with ratio 8:5) have theoretically ambiguous status. This framework influenced Renaissance and Baroque theories of consonance and dissonance.

# Examples
**Generation of consonances:**
The senario generates all consonances except the minor sixth:
- Octave: 2:1
- Fifth: 3:2 (also: "3/2 = 6/4")
- Fourth: 4:3
- Major third: 5:4
- Minor third: 6:5
- Major sixth: 5:3

**The minor sixth problem:**
"Zarlino has an embarrassing moment, confusing melodic with harmonic space, when he comes to discuss the minor sixth. He wants to analyze the major and the minor sixths as analogous structures."

"Unfortunately he does not do so [provide modal explanation]. Probably he was not as sensitive as we are to the thirds above the modal tonics C4 and D4 in the harmonic structures above; those thirds are not over the bass notes of the structures."

"He has to adjoin the number 8 to the senario in order to get the harmonic ratio 8:5 at hand for the minor sixth."

**Hindemith's parallel problem:**
"Hindemith can generate E2, Eb2, and A2 without using partials of those pitches, or of C2, that involve numbers greater than 6... But in order to find Ab2 by this method, he would have to have used an eighth-partial relationship."

This echoes Zarlino's difficulty: both theorists encounter the minor sixth as problematic for systems limited to six elements.

# Common Confusions
The senario is not arbitrary but reflects overtone relationships: the first six harmonics produce these ratios. However, the cutoff at 6 is somewhat conventional; the seventh harmonic is notably absent from Western consonance theory. Students should understand the senario as historically significant rather than acoustically necessary.
