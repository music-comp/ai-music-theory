# Quick Definition
Lewin's analysis of Chopin's Bb-minor Sonata, first movement, demonstrates how a formally valid intervallic relation (same interval class for two dyads) can become perceptually meaningful through musical development, illustrating GIS methodology in practice.

# Core Definition
The analysis examines four motives (a)-(d) from the Chopin Bb-minor Sonata, first movement (Figure 4.6, pp. 118-121). The central claim is that dyad x from motive (a) (the Grave opening) and dyad y from motive (b) (the first theme) belong to the same interval class. This relation is initially inaudible but becomes perceptually significant after the exposition repeat, when the chain (b)-(c)-(d)-(a bis) reveals (b) as a rhythmic transformation of (a).

# Prerequisites
- **GIS methodology** — The philosophical framework that justifies the analysis

# Key Properties
1. Dyads x and y share the same interval class despite sounding very different
2. The relation is initially inaudible but becomes meaningful through musical development
3. A rhythmic augmentation chain links (b) through (c) and (d) back to (a)
4. The exposition repeat is essential for the analysis to work
5. Enharmonic reinterpretation (Fb vs. E natural) mediates the perception

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Identify the four motives (a)-(d) and their intervallic content
2. Trace the rhythmic augmentation chain: (b) -> (c) -> (d) -> (a)
3. Show that the chain makes the x-y interval-class relation audible at the repeat
## To Recognize:
1. A formal intervallic relation that is not immediately perceptible
2. A musical process (development, repetition) that makes the relation perceptible over time

# Context & Application
The analysis supports the methodological claim (Section 4.3) that formal relations need not be immediately perceptible to be musically valid. It suffices that they become potentially meaningful in suitable musical contexts. The Chopin analysis demonstrates this principle: the x-y relation is "formally true but intuitively problematic" at first hearing, but becomes audible through the piece's own processes.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Figure 4.6, pp. 118-121): Rhythmic chain: (b) -> (c): augment by 2, then augment last two notes by 2; (c) -> (d): augment by 2, exchange first two note durations; (d) -> (a bis): augment by 2. The consistent doubling creates a perceptible connection.

**Example 2** (p. 120): The Db cadence at the end of the exposition helps the listener hear Fb (as third of Db major) rather than E natural. This enharmonic reinterpretation links dyad x to the Db harmony, mediating the x-y interval-class relation.

**Example 3** (p. 121): At the repeat, the listener hears the chain (a bis)-(b bis) and recognizes (b) as an "ironically scurrying transformation" of (a), making the interval-class relation between x and y audible.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Claiming the x-y relation is immediately obvious at first hearing
  **Correction**: The analysis explicitly acknowledges the relation is initially inaudible; it becomes meaningful only through musical development

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The analysis is purely formal and has no perceptual content
  **Clarification**: The entire point is that formal relations can become perceptually meaningful through appropriate musical contexts; the analysis traces this process
- **Confusion**: The enharmonic distinction (Fb vs. E) is irrelevant to interval-class theory
  **Clarification**: While interval classes are enharmonically equivalent in theory, the perceptual route to hearing the relation passes through the tonal interpretation of the pitch
