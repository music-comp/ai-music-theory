# Quick Definition
The minor sixth poses recurring difficulties for theorists attempting to derive all intervals purely from harmonic space, as the ratio 8:5 falls outside systems limited to six elements (senario) and requires special treatment in overtone-based constructions.

# Formal Definition
The minor sixth (ratio 8:5, approximately 814 cents) creates theoretical difficulties because the number 8 lies outside the senario (1-6) and requires eighth-partial relationships in overtone-based derivations. Both Zarlino and Hindemith encounter this interval as problematic, suggesting a fundamental tension between the minor sixth and purely harmonic-mathematical approaches.

# Mathematical Formulation
Minor sixth ratio: 8:5 = 1.6

Senario limitation:
- Senario contains only {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}
- 8:5 requires the number 8
- Alternative: adjoin 8 to senario, but this raises questions about why 7 is excluded

Hindemith's overtone problem:
- To derive Ab2 from C2: 5th partial of Ab2 = 8th partial of C2
- But eighth-partial relationships may be deemed too "remote"
- Seventh-partial relationships would be closer but produce non-twelve-tone pitches

# Musical Context/Application
The minor sixth problem reveals that purely harmonic (ratio-based) derivations of intervals cannot fully account for the consonant intervals of Western music without special pleading or ad hoc extensions. This suggests that either the minor sixth has a different theoretical status, or that melodic-modal considerations must supplement harmonic ones.

# Examples
**Zarlino's approach:**
"He has to adjoin the number 8 to the senario in order to get the harmonic ratio 8:5 at hand for the minor sixth, and then he has to argue that the proportion 8:6:5 is somehow analogous to the proportion 5:4:3 in his harmonic world."

"He even claims that 6 is a 'harmonic mean term' between 8 and 5; this is simply false if 'harmonic' is to mean anything at all in the context."

**What Zarlino should have said:**
Lewin suggests a modal alternative: "In a major mode such as Ionian, the fourth G3-C4 plus the modal major third C4-E4 yields the major sixth G3-E4, while in a minor mode such as Dorian, the fourth A3-D4 plus the modal minor third D4-F4 yields the minor sixth A3-F4."

But Zarlino "attempts to realize the analogy of the sixths as a feature of his harmonic space rather than his modal theory, and that leads him into confusion."

**Hindemith's parallel:**
"The one pitch with which Hindemith has trouble is Ab2, the minor sixth above the tonic C2. Curiously enough, his troubles resemble Zarlino's troubles with the minor sixth."

"Hindemith can generate E2, Eb2, and A2 without using partials of those pitches, or of C2, that involve numbers greater than 6... But in order to find Ab2 by this method, he would have to have used an eighth-partial relationship."

**Modal vs. harmonic character:**
"To the extent that we hear it [8:5] in a 'C-major' modal context as third-degree-to-octave, inverting tonic-to-third-degree = C4-E4 = 5:4, a major third, the sixth itself has a 'major' modal character about it, despite its small size."

# Common Confusions
The minor sixth problem is not merely a historical curiosity but reveals fundamental issues in music theory. The consistent difficulty suggests that pure harmonic derivation is insufficient and that melodic/modal considerations play an irreducible role in understanding consonance. Students should not dismiss these historical "failures" but learn from what they reveal.
