# Quick Definition
Stufen theory assigns structural priority to contextual scale degrees (Stufen), enabling discriminations between intervals that are harmonically identical but modally different, such as "major" versus "minor" sixths in different modal contexts.

# Formal Definition
Stufen (German: "scale degrees") theory refers to the understanding that scale degrees have structural meaning within a tonal context. The identity and function of an interval depends not just on its acoustic size but on the scale degrees involved. This enables distinctions that purely harmonic (ratio-based) theories cannot make, such as the different modal characters of the same interval size in different contexts.

# Mathematical Formulation
Stufen as contextual functions:
- In C major: C = 1, D = 2, E = 3, F = 4, G = 5, A = 6, B = 7
- In D Dorian: D = 1, E = 2, F = 3, G = 4, A = 5, B = 6, C = 7

Modal character of intervals:
- G3-E4 in C major: 5-3 = "major" modal sixth
- A3-F4 in D Dorian: 5-3 = "minor" modal sixth
- Both are "major sixths" (ratio 5:3) harmonically
- But their Stufen relations give them different modal characters

# Musical Context/Application
Stufen theory enables Schenker's sophisticated discriminations between structurally identical intervals appearing in different contexts. Without Stufen, analysts cannot distinguish the modal character of an interval from its acoustic size, limiting the analytical power of pure harmonic theory.

# Examples
**Enabling discrimination:**
"Schenker, quite willing to assign structural priority to contextual modal tonics inter alia, uses his Stufen theory to powerful effect in related connections."

**Zarlino's limitation:**
"Zarlino had no Stufen theory that could enable him to make such discriminations. And yet it is quite possible that, even if one had been available to him, he might have rejected it. He would have been uncomfortable making the meaning of his harmonic intervals so dependent on the contextual assignment of a modal tonic. For him this would have weakened the context-free universality of his harmonic theory."

**Modal sixths:**
Lewin explains the modal character of sixths through Stufen:
- "F4-D5 in D-Dorian, inverting D4-F4, has a 'minor' modal character despite its large size"
- "G3-E4 in C-Ionian has both a 'major' modal character and a large size"
- "To the extent that we hear it [E4-C5] in a 'C-major' modal context as third-degree-to-octave, inverting tonic-to-third-degree = C4-E4 = 5:4, a major third, the sixth itself has a 'major' modal character about it, despite its small size"

**What Zarlino should have done:**
"In a major mode such as Ionian, the fourth G3-C4 plus the modal major third C4-E4 yields the major sixth G3-E4, while in a minor mode such as Dorian, the fourth A3-D4 plus the modal minor third D4-F4 yields the minor sixth A3-F4."

This modal explanation uses implicit Stufen reasoning that Zarlino resisted.

# Common Confusions
Stufen are not simply scale degree numbers but carry functional meaning within a tonal context. The same pitch (e.g., E) has different Stufen identity in C major (3) versus A minor (5), affecting how intervals involving E are perceived. Students should distinguish Stufen from pitch-class integers.
