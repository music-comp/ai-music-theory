# Quick Definition
Lewin's analysis of Prokofieff's Melodies op. 35, no. 1 demonstrates how pitch-class inversion operations with progressing centers (G -> F#/G -> F#) explain cadential harmonic substitutions where traditional Klang analysis proves inadequate.

# Core Definition
This analysis (Section 10.3) applies pitch-class set theory and inversion operations to explain unexpected harmonic substitutions in a diatonic context. Where Klang analysis is hindered by parallel voice-leading, pitch-class inversions with structurally significant centers reveal deeper organization. The progression of inversion centers develops the chromatic G/F# relationship established melodically in the opening measure (Lewin, pp. 262-266).

# Prerequisites
- **Transformation network** — the analytical framework
- **Inversion operation** — the primary analytical tool (I_G, I_{F#/G}, I_{F#})
- **Isography** — the inversion-center network is isographic to the opening accompaniment

# Key Properties
1. At cadences, expected Eb major is replaced by E minor (m. 2.5) and Eb minor (m. 5.5)
2. I_G relates Eb major to E minor (G is plausible center: accented by melodic boundary functions)
3. I_{F#/G} relates Eb major to Eb minor (develops chromatic G/F# relation from m. 1)
4. I_{F#} relates Eb minor to D major AND C major to F minor simultaneously (m. 6)
5. Network of inversion centers (Figure 10.9a) is isographic to the accompaniment figure G-F#-F in m. 1
6. Both Klang and pitch-class-set approaches are employed; the latter proves more revealing

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Identify "strange" harmonic substitutions

3. Identify the inversional center and its structural significance
4. Trace the progression of centers through the passage
## To Recognize:
1. Look for triadic substitutions relatable by pitch-class inversion
2. Check if inversion centers have motivic or structural significance

# Context & Application
The analysis shows how chromatic pitch relations established in the opening melody (specifically G and F#) generate the inversional centers organizing subsequent harmonic substitutions. It demonstrates that pitch-class set operations can provide clearer insight than Klang-functional analysis when parallel voice-leading obscures functional relationships. Lewin credits Neil Minturn for bringing this passage to his attention.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Figures 10.7-10.8, pp. 262-264): Cadential substitutions. At m. 2.5, expected Eb major -> heard E minor via I_G. At m. 5.5, expected Eb major -> heard Eb minor via I_{F#/G}. At m. 6, I_{F#} relates Eb minor/D major and C major/F minor.

**Example 2** (Figure 10.8a, p. 263): Klang analysis shows SLIDE relates Eb minor to D major, paralleling Eb major to E minor. But the proportion among triads in m. 6 "seems hard to portray by Klang-relations that mesh with our aural intuitions."

**Example 3** (Figure 10.9, p. 265): The network of inversion operations I_G -> I_{F#/G} -> I_{F#} with T_{11} arrows is isographic to the G-F#-F figure in the accompaniment of m. 1.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Forcing all triadic analysis into Klang-functional terms
  **Correction**: Pitch-class set operations may provide clearer insight when voice-leading is parallel and functional relations are unclear

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking SLIDE and I_{F#} accomplish the same thing
  **Clarification**: SLIDE is a Klang transformation (inverting about the mediant); I_{F#} is a pitch-class inversion. They are defined on different families of objects and have different analytical implications
