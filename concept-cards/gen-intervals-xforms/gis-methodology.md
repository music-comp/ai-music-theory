# Quick Definition
GIS methodology addresses what we should expect from a musical theory: not that every formally true statement be perceptually obvious, but that a preponderance of true statements be potentially meaningful in sufficiently developed musical contexts.

# Formal Definition
**Section 4.3:** The methodological point: It is unfair to demand of a musical theory that it always address our sonic intuitions faithfully in all potentially musical contexts under all circumstances. It is enough to ask that the theory do so in a sufficient number of contexts and circumstances.

Perhaps, too, it is fair to ask that the theory be *potentially* able to address our intuitions in any given musical situation, provided that the situation develops in a suitable musical manner.

# Mathematical Formulation
The issue arises because formal GIS relations may hold without being perceptually obvious:

int(s1, t1) = int(s2, t2) may be mathematically true
while the intuitive "proportion" between s1 and t1 may not "sound like" that between s2 and t2.

The theory is valid if such relations *can become* audible in appropriate contexts, not if they *must always* be immediately audible.

# Musical Context/Application
This methodology defends GIS structures (especially timbral ones) against the objection that they don't always match perception. The defense has two parts:

1. Many theories (even amplitude ratios for loudness) don't always match perception
2. Formal relations may become perceptually significant as a piece develops

The methodology emphasizes *potential* rather than *immediate* perceivability.

# Examples
**Amplitude example:**
sin waves s1, t1 at low frequency with amplitude ratio i
sin waves s2, t2 at middle frequency with same ratio i
The loudness relations may SOUND different despite equal amplitude ratios.

We don't reject amplitude ratios as theoretically useless just because of this.

**Chopin Bb-minor Sonata example (Figure 4.6):**
Motive (a): opening Grave figure
Motive (b): first theme
Dyad x (from (a)) and dyad y (from (b)) belong to the same interval class.

This relation is "formally true but intuitively problematic" at first hearing.

After the repeat, hearing the chain (b)-(c)-(d)-(a bis)-(b bis), the relation becomes audible: (b) and (a) are recognized as rhythmic variants, and the interval-class relation of x and y becomes perceptually significant.

**Perceptual development:**
- First time through exposition: x-y relation inaudible
- After development of (b) through (c), (d), back to (a): x-y relation becomes meaningful
- The Db cadence at repeat helps hear Fb as third of Db, linking x and y

# Common Confusions
1. **Not "anything goes":** The methodology doesn't excuse arbitrary formal relations. It asks that relations be *potentially* meaningful, not that they must be *immediately* perceived.

2. **Context matters:** A relation may be imperceptible in one context and highly significant in another. The theory should be judged by its best applications.

3. **Perception is not fixed:** As we learn to hear a piece, relations that were initially obscure may become obvious.

4. **Parallel with pitch:** We accept that equal frequency ratios may not sound equally "in tune" across registers. This doesn't invalidate frequency ratios as theoretical tools.
