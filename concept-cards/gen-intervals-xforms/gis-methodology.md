# Quick Definition
GIS methodology holds that a musical theory need not always address sonic intuitions faithfully in all contexts; it suffices that the theory do so in a sufficient number of contexts, and that it be potentially able to address our intuitions in any situation that develops in a suitable musical manner.

# Core Definition
Lewin articulates his methodological stance in Section 4.3 (pp. 117-121): "It is unfair to demand of a musical theory that it always address our sonic intuitions faithfully in all potentially musical contexts under all circumstances. It is enough to ask that the theory do so in a sufficient number of contexts and circumstances." Furthermore, "it is fair to ask that the theory be *potentially* able to address our intuitions in any given musical situation, provided that the situation develops in a suitable musical manner."

# Key Properties
1. Formal GIS relations (e.g., int(s1, t1) = int(s2, t2)) may hold without being perceptually obvious
2. This does not invalidate the theory; even familiar theories (amplitude ratios for loudness) have similar limitations
3. The standard is *potential* perceivability, not *immediate* perceivability
4. Musical development can make initially obscure relations perceptually meaningful
5. The theory should be judged by its best applications, not its worst

# Context & Application
The methodology arises specifically in defense of timbral GIS structures (Section 4.2) where formal interval equality may not correspond to perceived similarity. Lewin argues by analogy: just as we accept amplitude ratios as theoretically useful despite their imperfect correlation with perceived loudness across frequencies, we should accept GIS structures whose formal relations may not always be immediately audible. The Chopin Bb-minor Sonata analysis (Section 4.3) provides the extended demonstration.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 117): Amplitude analogy: sine waves s1, t1 at low frequency with amplitude ratio i, and sine waves s2, t2 at middle frequency with the same ratio i. The loudness relations may sound different despite equal amplitude ratios. We do not reject amplitude ratios as theoretically useless because of this.

**Example 2** (pp. 118-121): Chopin Bb-minor Sonata: dyads x and y share the same interval class but the relation is initially inaudible. After the repeat, through the chain (b)-(c)-(d)-(a bis), the relation becomes perceptually significant. This illustrates potential perceivability through musical development.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Taking the methodology as license for arbitrary formal assertions
  **Correction**: The methodology requires that relations be *potentially* meaningful in appropriate contexts, not that any formal statement is valid

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The methodology says perception does not matter
  **Clarification**: It says perception matters, but perception develops; a relation that is initially obscure may become obvious through musical experience
- **Confusion**: The parallel with amplitude ratios undermines all formal theories
  **Clarification**: The parallel shows that formal theories with imperfect perceptual correlates are standard in music and acoustics; this is not a defect unique to GIS
