# Quick Definition
An extended analysis of Brahms's Horn Trio op. 40 (last movement) showing how the nuclear gesture (4 = 2 + 2 with pickup 5) and complementary gesture (8 = 10 + 10) govern intervallic/transpositional configurations, progressing from subordination to pitch events toward autonomy.

# Core Definition
Lewin traces the nuclear and complementary gestures through eight passages (a)-(h) in the last movement of Brahms's Horn Trio (Figure 7.7). In passages (a)-(d), the intervallic structures are "fallout from the pitch and pitch-class motives" (p. 165). But from passage (f) onward, "the intervallic/transpositional networks take on a life of their own. They become autonomous structures; no longer subordinated to concomitant local pitch or pitch-class events, they rather interact with such events or perhaps even determine them" (7.3, p. 166).

# Prerequisites
- **Nuclear gesture** — The primary intervallic pattern (4 = 2 + 2 with pickup 5)
- **Complementary gesture** — The paired pattern (8 = 10 + 10)
- **Intervals as transpositions** — The dual language used throughout

# Key Properties
1. Passages (a)-(d): gestures subordinate to pitch/pitch-class motives on Eb-F-G and G-F-Eb
2. Passage (e): G and F as degrees 6 and 5 in Bb; transposition-by-10 exfoliates from int(G, F) = 10
3. Passage (f): development section sequence governed by nuclear gesture autonomously
4. Passage (g): horn solo mm. 137-44 projects complementary gesture; mm. 145-49 project "times 2" variant
5. Passage (h): first movement already presented the "times 2" gesture in bass line

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Identify nuclear and complementary gesture patterns in each passage
2. Track the evolution from subordination to autonomy
3. Note where pitch-class intervals (not pitch intervals) are required
## To Recognize:
1. Recurring patterns of 4 = 2 + 2 and 8 = 10 + 10 across diverse passages
2. Increasing independence of intervallic structure from pitch content

# Context & Application
Lewin uses pitch-class intervals despite the piece being "highly structured registrally and diatonic," noting that "our reasons will become clear by the end of this discussion" (p. 164). The analysis demonstrates how Schenkerian approaches reveal more in early passages (a)-(d), while intervallic/transpositional approaches become more illuminating in later passages (f)-(h).

# Examples
**Example 1** (Figure 7.7f, p. 166): Extended developmental sequence (mm. 103-123) governed by nuclear gesture, with pitch-class (not pitch) listening required.

**Example 2** (Figure 7.7g-h, pp. 167-169): The "complementary gesture times 2" in the horn solo (mm. 145-49) was already presented in the first movement's bass line (passage h).

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Using pitch intervals instead of pitch-class intervals for this analysis
  **Correction**: Pitch-class intervals are necessary to reveal the nuclear/complementary gesture relationships

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking intervallic/transpositional analysis replaces Schenkerian analysis
  **Clarification**: Lewin notes that "a traditional Schenkerian approach to (a)-(b)-(c)-(d) reveals more than does our intervallic/transpositional approach" (p. 165); the two complement each other
