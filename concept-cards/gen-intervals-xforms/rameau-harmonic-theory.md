# Quick Definition
Rameau's harmonic theory (Traite de l'harmonie, 1722) asserts harmony's priority over melody but encounters logical difficulties when melodic concepts like "thirds" are implicitly invoked to justify harmonic operations without having established a melodic scale.

# Core Definition
Lewin identifies a logical difficulty in Rameau's theory: Rameau argues that the harmonic intervals 5/4 and 6/5 may be exchanged in register within the triad because both are "thirds." But at this point he has not presented any scale along which distances of "three" degrees can be measured, and he has strongly asserted melody's subordination to harmony. The appeal to "thirds" implicitly imports melodic-scalar concepts into a supposedly pure harmonic framework (Lewin, Appendix A, pp. 245-246).

# Key Properties
1. Harmony is asserted as primary over melody
2. The major/minor triad derivation invokes "thirds" — a melodic concept
3. No scale has been established to measure "three" degrees
4. An alternative purely harmonic argument (via senario adjacency) leads to unwanted conclusions
5. This is cited as an "embarrassing moment" in the history of tonal theory

# Context & Application
Rameau's difficulty illustrates a general problem: purely harmonic reasoning cannot generate all the structures needed for tonal music theory without importing melodic concepts. This makes explicit the need for integrating multiple musical spaces rather than deriving all from one.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 246): The "thirds" problem: 5/4 and 6/5 both called "thirds" without a scale to count three degrees.

**Example 2** (p. 246): Alternative harmonic argument: 5/4 and 6/5 divide 3/2 harmonically. But the same logic applied to 4/3 and 5/4 dividing 5/3 would yield conclusions Rameau would reject (violating fundamental bass principles).
