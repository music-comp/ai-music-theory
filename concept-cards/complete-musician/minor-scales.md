# Quick Definition
The minor mode uses three scale forms -- natural, harmonic, and melodic -- sharing a lowered ^3 compared to major and varying in their treatment of ^6 and ^7 to balance smooth melodic motion with goal-directed harmonic function.

# Core Definition
The **minor mode** is distinguished primarily by its **mediant** (^3), which lies one half step lower than in major. The **natural minor scale** has half steps between ^2-^3 and ^5-^6, with both ^6 and ^7 in lowered form (W-H-W-W-H-W-W). The **harmonic minor scale** raises ^7 to create a leading tone, producing a half step between ^7 and ^1 but introducing an augmented second (three half steps) between ^6 and ^7. The **melodic minor scale** raises both ^6 and ^7 ascending and lowers both descending. These three forms should be viewed as variations of a single nine-pitch-class minor collection.

# Prerequisites
- **Diatonic Scale** -- the minor scale is a diatonic scale type
- **Half Step and Whole Step** -- minor scales have different H/W patterns than major
- **Major Scale** -- minor is defined partly by contrast with major

# Key Properties
1. The lowered ^3 is the primary distinguishing feature of minor versus major
2. ^6 and ^7 are variable, depending on melodic direction
3. Raised ^7 (leading tone) gravitates toward ^1; lowered ^7 (subtonic) descends toward ^5
4. Lowered ^6 gravitates toward ^5; raised ^6 supports ascent toward ^7 and ^1
5. The augmented second between lowered ^6 and raised ^7 rarely occurs within a single melodic line
6. Key signatures for minor keys reflect the natural minor form; raised ^6 and ^7 appear as accidentals

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. **Natural minor**: W-H-W-W-H-W-W from the tonic
2. **Harmonic minor**: raise ^7 one half step from natural minor
3. **Melodic minor**: raise ^6 and ^7 ascending; lower both descending

## To Recognize:
1. Identify the lowered ^3 relative to major
2. Check the treatment of ^6 and ^7 to determine the form

# Context & Application
Composers draw freely from all three forms within a single passage based on melodic context. The minor mode is considered darker than major and more flexible due to its variable ^6 and ^7.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 14): D natural minor: D-E-F-G-A-Bb-C-D.
**Example 2** (p. 14): D harmonic minor: D-E-F-G-A-Bb-C#-D.
**Example 3** (p. 14): D melodic minor ascending: D-E-F-G-A-B-C#-D; descending: D-C-Bb-A-G-F-E-D.
**Example 4** (p. 15): Mozart, K. 284 (Example 1.17A) shows both forms of ^7 and ^6 in a chromatic descending line.
**Example 5** (p. 15): Bach, "Verleih' uns Frieden" (Example 1.17C) shows raised ^7 from ascent through raised ^6, while lowered ^6 gravitates toward ^5.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Major Scale** -- minor is defined partly by comparison with major

## Enables
- **Relative Keys** -- each major key has a relative minor sharing the same key signature
- **Triads in Minor Keys** -- variable ^6 and ^7 produce different chord qualities

## Contrasts With
- **Major Scale** -- brighter sound, fixed ^6 and ^7

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating the three minor forms as three separate scales.
  **Correction**: They are three aspects of a single flexible minor collection. Composers freely mix forms within a passage.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The augmented second in harmonic minor is a common melodic interval.
  **Clarification**: It rarely occurs within a single melodic line because ^6 and ^7 arise from different directional contexts.
