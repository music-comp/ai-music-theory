# Quick Definition
A passing tone fills the space of a third between two consonances by stepwise motion in a single direction; it may be consonant (as in 5-6 technique) or dissonant (occurring on a weak beat).

# Core Definition
**Passing tones** (PTs) fill the space within the interval of a third, creating smooth stepwise motion. The motion into and out of a passing tone must occur in a single direction (either ascending or descending). In second-species counterpoint, dissonant passing tones occur on weak beats between two strong-beat consonances a third apart. Consonant passing tones also exist, notably in the **5-6 technique**. Passing tones are the most fundamental type of nonchord tone in tonal music.

# Prerequisites
- **Second-Species Counterpoint** -- passing tones are the primary dissonance type

# Key Properties
1. Approached and left by step in the same direction
2. Dissonant PTs occur on weak beats only (in second species)
3. Consonant PTs occur when both approach and departure intervals are consonant (5-6)
4. The goal in second species is to incorporate as many passing tones as possible
5. A dissonance can never mediate poor voice leading
6. Passing tones create forward melodic motion between stable pitches

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Identify two chord tones a third apart
2. Insert the diatonic pitch between them
3. Place on a metrically weak beat

## To Recognize:
1. Find a stepwise pitch between two chord tones
2. Confirm it moves in a single direction
3. Verify it is not a member of the underlying harmony (if dissonant PT)

# Context & Application
The passing tone appears at every level of musical structure, from surface decoration to large-scale harmonic motion. It is the most common nonchord tone.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Confusing passing tones with neighbor tones.
  **Correction**: Passing tones move in one direction through a third; neighbors return to the starting pitch.
