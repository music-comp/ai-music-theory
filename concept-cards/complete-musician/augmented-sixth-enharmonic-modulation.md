# Quick Definition
Augmented sixth enharmonic modulation exploits the sonic identity between the Ger65 and V7 (or Ger7 and V42) to pivot between two keys, typically modulating to bII.

# Core Definition
The Ger65 and V7 are sonically interchangeable, differing by only one enharmonic pitch. Composers modulate by treating this sonority as a pivot: a Ger65 in the original key is respelled as V7 of a new key (most commonly bII), or a V7 is respelled as Ger65. The Ger7 and V42 are also enharmonically equivalent, providing a second pair for enharmonic pivoting. Any V7 in a key can potentially be reinterpreted as an augmented sixth, granting access to both closely and distantly related keys (Laitz, Ch. 30, pp. 692-696).

# Prerequisites
- **German Augmented Sixth** -- the chord being reinterpreted
- **Enharmonic Reinterpretation** -- the general technique

# Key Properties
1. Ger65 = V7 enharmonically (one pitch respelled)
2. Ger7 = V42 enharmonically
3. Most common destination: bII of the original key
4. Can be notated as a single pivot chord (two labels) or as successive chords
5. The reverse (V7 to Ger65) modulates to #VII (less common)
6. Works like a musical pun -- leading the listener in an unexpected direction

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 693): C minor Ger65 (Ab-C-Eb-F#) respelled as V7 of Db (Ab-C-Eb-Gb), modulating to bII.

**Example 2** (p. 694): Beethoven, "Rage Over a Lost Penny" -- Ger65 in G minor becomes V7 of Ab (bII).

**Example 3** (p. 696): Chopin, Mazurka op. 56 no. 1 -- tonic B major gains a seventh and is reinterpreted as Ger65 in the new key of D#/Eb major.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Not distinguishing between augmented sixth and V7 function
  **Clarification**: The distinction lies in resolution: augmented sixth resolves to V by contrary motion; V7 resolves to I by bass leap
