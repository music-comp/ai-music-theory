# Quick Definition
The chord built on ^3, a minor triad in major (iii) and major triad in minor (III). It provides color contrast and functions primarily in ascending-bass arpeggiations and descending-fifth progressions. In minor, III has great structural significance as the relative major.

# Core Definition
The mediant occurs less frequently than any other diatonic harmony within phrases, but its structural significance is great -- in minor-key pieces, III is second only to tonic and dominant in structural importance (as the relative major). The mediant provides the same color contrast as the submediant: minor triad in major, major triad in minor. Within phrases, the mediant appears primarily in (1) ascending-bass arpeggiations (I-iii-V with bass ^1-^3-^5) and (2) descending-fifth progressions (iii-vi-ii-V). (Laitz, Ch. 19, pp. 446-449)

# Key Properties
1. Minor triad in major (iii), major triad in minor (III)
2. Provides color contrast to prevailing key quality
3. In minor, III = relative major, with great structural importance
4. Two main phrase-level functions: ascending arpeggiations and descending-fifths
5. In ascending arpeggiation, iii replaces I6 as the middle chord between I and V

# Construction / Recognition
## Voice Leading:
1. I to iii shares one common tone (^3); keep it and move other voices by step
2. iii to V: move all voices by step (no common tones in major)
3. In iii-vi progressions, keep common tones

# Context & Application
Within phrases, iii is relatively rare. But in the large-scale tonal plans of minor-key sonatas and concertos, III (the relative major) often serves as the key of the second theme area, making it structurally crucial at the level of form.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 447): Ascending arpeggiation I-iii-V with bass ^1-^3-^5, where iii replaces I6 and provides a richer sonority.
