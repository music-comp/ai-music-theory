# Quick Definition
Applied chords in inversion use the same inversions available to V7 (V6, V65, V43, V42) applied to non-tonic harmonies, producing smoother bass lines than root-position applied chords.

# Core Definition
Just as inversions of V prolong I (e.g., V6 as a lower-neighbor chord), any major or minor harmony in a key can be prolonged by its applied dominant chord in inversion. The inversions produce smoother, more polished bass lines compared to the angular motion of root-position applied chords. No matter how extensive the expansion of a nontonic harmony through applied chord inversions, that harmony ultimately retains its function within the phrase model (Laitz, Ch. 24, p. 540).

# Prerequisites
- **Applied Chord** -- the chords being inverted
- **Chord Inversions** -- understanding of figured bass and inversion types

# Key Properties
1. All inversions available to V7 can be applied to non-tonic chords
2. Inversions create smoother bass lines than root-position applied chords
3. Extended use of applied chord inversions can prolong a non-tonic harmony for several beats
4. The prolonged harmony retains its structural function (T, PD, or D)

# Context & Application
Applied chords in inversion are essential for creating smooth, polished voice leading in chromatic progressions. They allow composers to expand non-tonic harmonies in the same ways that V and its inversions expand the tonic.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 540): The supertonic is prolonged by V7/ii and its inversions in the same way that tonic is prolonged by V7 inversions, with D# acting as the leading tone to ii (E minor) in the key of D major.

- **Error**: Using only root-position applied chords, creating angular bass motion
