# Quick Definition
The whole-tone scale divides the octave into six equal whole steps, creating a symmetrical collection lacking tonal direction. Only two distinct whole-tone tracks exist (one starting on C, one on C#).

# Core Definition
The whole-tone scale is one of the equal divisions of the octave, dividing it into six major seconds. Because every scale step is identical, goal-directed motion and tonal grounding disappear -- every step is as stable (or unstable) as every other. Only two distinct whole-tone tracks exist: one beginning on C (C-D-E-F#-G#-A#) and one on C# (C#-D#-F-G-A-B). The whole-tone scale is intimately connected to the augmented triad (which outlines alternating scale degrees) and the altered dominant seventh chord (whose intervals are all whole-tone-related: M2, M3, tritone) (Laitz, Ch. 37).

# Prerequisites
- **Equal Divisions of the Octave** -- the whole-tone scale is one such division

# Key Properties
1. Six whole steps dividing the octave equally
2. Only two distinct collections (tracks) exist
3. Every interval between adjacent tones is identical
4. Lacks tonal direction or gravitational center
5. Contains augmented triads and tritones but no perfect fifths or minor seconds
6. Connected to augmented triads and altered dominants

# Context & Application
The whole-tone scale became increasingly important in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, particularly in the music of Debussy and Scriabin. It represents one extreme of the symmetrical approach to harmony.
