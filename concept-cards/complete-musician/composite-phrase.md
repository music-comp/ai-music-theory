# Quick Definition
A phrase composed of three or more subphrases, typically eight or more measures long, giving an additive, stage-by-stage impression rather than a single sweeping motion.

# Core Definition
Composite phrases comprise three or more subphrases but contain only one structural harmonic progression and one cadence. They are usually eight or more measures and sound as if composed in an additive, stage-by-stage process. Two common types: (1) the harmonic progression unfolds gradually, with each subphrase supported by a new harmonic function; (2) the harmonic progression stays on tonic through most subphrases, changing to PD-D-T only at the very end. (Laitz, Ch. 17, pp. 420-422)

# Prerequisites
- **Subphrase** — Composite phrases are built from subphrases
- **Phrase model** — The overall T-PD-D-T governs the structure

# Key Properties
1. Three or more subphrases
2. One overall harmonic progression and one cadence
3. Usually eight or more measures
4. Additive quality distinguishes them from compact four-measure phrases
5. Two types: gradually unfolding harmony vs. late-arriving PD-D-T

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 420): Haydn, String Quartet in B major, op. 64, no. 3, Menuetto -- a 14-measure composite phrase with three subphrases and a single I-ii6-V-I progression.

**Example 2** (p. 421): Haydn, Piano Sonata no. 30 in D major -- three subphrases where the first two close on tonic and the final subphrase brings the cadence.

- **Confusion**: Composite phrases contain multiple cadences
  **Clarification**: By definition, they contain only one structural cadence; subphrase endings are not cadences
