# Quick Definition
A motivic development technique in which only a portion of the original motive is used, typically creating acceleration and momentum as the units become shorter.

# Core Definition
Fragmentation is the process of isolating and repeating a portion of a motive, typically its most characteristic element. By progressively shortening the motivic units, fragmentation creates a sense of acceleration, urgency, and climax. It is one of the most powerful development techniques and is closely related to sentence structure, where the B section often fragments material from A and A'. (Laitz, Ch. 16, pp. 375-376)

# Prerequisites
- **Motive** — The original motive must be established before fragments can be recognized

# Key Properties
1. Uses only a portion of the motive
2. Often involves progressive shortening
3. Generates momentum and intensity
4. Common in development sections and in the B portion of sentence structures

# Context & Application
Fragmentation is especially prominent in Beethoven's developmental writing and in the continuation portions of sentences. It is a primary means of generating climax and forward drive.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 375): A four-beat motive is fragmented to its first two beats, then to its first beat, creating acceleration toward a cadence.
