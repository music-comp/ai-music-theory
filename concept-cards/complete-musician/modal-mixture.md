# Quick Definition
Modal mixture is the technique of borrowing harmonies from the parallel mode, most commonly importing elements of the minor mode (b3, b6, b7) into a major-mode piece.

# Core Definition
Modal mixture (sometimes called simply "mixture") is a chromatic procedure in which pitches or harmonies from the parallel mode are incorporated into a passage. Although borrowing can occur in either direction, it is far more common for minor-mode elements to be imported into the major mode. The major mode lacks alternate forms of scale degrees 3, 6, and 7, so their introduction from the minor creates a striking effect. Mixture chords usually carry pre-dominant function, distinguishing them from applied chords, which carry dominant function. The Picardy third (major I in minor) and the fully diminished vii°7 in major are two previously encountered instances of mixture (Laitz, Ch. 27, pp. 616-618).

# Prerequisites
- **Parallel Keys** -- mixture borrows from the parallel mode
- **Pre-Dominant Function** -- mixture chords typically serve this function

# Key Properties
1. Borrows from the parallel mode (usually minor into major)
2. b6 is the most common scale degree involved in mixture
3. Alters chord quality without changing root or function
4. Mixture chords function as pre-dominants (not dominants)
5. Two types: melodic mixture (altered chord member) and harmonic mixture (altered root)
6. Became increasingly prominent in the Romantic era

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Identify a pre-dominant chord in the major mode
2. Lower the appropriate scale degrees (b3, b6, or b7) to create the mixture chord
3. Label using minor-mode roman numerals (ii°, iv, bVI, bIII)

## To Recognize:
1. Look for chromatically lowered pitches that do not function as temporary leading tones
2. Check if the altered chord serves a pre-dominant function
3. If the chromaticism points toward a non-tonic chord as dominant, it is an applied chord, not mixture

# Context & Application
Modal mixture ranges from subtle harmonic coloring to saturation of a major-mode piece with minor elements. Schubert's practice sometimes fuses major and minor into "major-minor" keys. Mixture chords create expressive contrasts, often associated with sorrow, darkness, or intensity in text-setting.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 616): Mascagni, "A casa amici" -- extensive borrowing from F minor in an F-major passage, creating a progression that briefly tonicizes bIII.

**Example 2** (p. 619): Schumann, "Ich grolle nicht" -- b6 appears as part of ii°7, underscoring the text "even if my heart breaks."

# Relationships
## Enables
- **Melodic Mixture** -- altered chord members
- **Harmonic Mixture** -- altered roots (bVI, bIII)
- **Chromatic Modulation** -- mixture provides pivot chords for chromatic modulations
- **Neapolitan Chord** -- product of mixture in major

## Contrasts With
- **Applied Chord** -- applied chords function as dominants; mixture functions as pre-dominant
- **Tonicization** -- tonicization creates temporary leading tones; mixture creates lowered scale degrees

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Confusing mixture chords with applied chords
  **Correction**: Mixture chords are independent pre-dominants; applied chords function as dominants pointing to other chords

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking modal mixture changes the key
  **Clarification**: Mixture borrows from the parallel mode but does not establish a new key; the home key remains
