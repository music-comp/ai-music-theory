# Quick Definition
An off-tonic beginning is a technique in which a piece or section begins on a harmony other than the tonic, postponing tonal stability to create ambiguity and heightened expectation.

# Core Definition
Nineteenth-century composers often postpone tonal stability by beginning a piece on a harmony other than the tonic. In the Classical period, this was approximated in slow introductions where a weakly stated initial tonic was quickly displaced. In the nineteenth century, off-tonic beginnings became more explicit, with pieces beginning on extended pre-dominants or applied chord chains that eventually resolve to the tonic. This technique became a mainstay of twentieth-century popular music as well (Laitz, Ch. 34).

# Key Properties
1. Piece begins on a harmony other than tonic
2. Tonal stability deliberately postponed
3. Creates ambiguity and expectation
4. Applied chord chains often lead to eventual tonic
5. Common in both art music and popular music

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 797): Brahms, "Parole," op. 7, no. 2 -- Begins on an extended pre-dominant (C major sounding like V7/IV), not establishing E minor until m. 9.

**Example 2** (p. 806): Pinkard, "Sweet Georgia Brown" -- G-major refrain begins with applied dominants E7-A7-D7 before reaching G at m. 33.
