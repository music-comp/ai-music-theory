# Quick Definition
An altered dominant seventh chord has its fifth raised or lowered by a half step, creating an augmented sixth interval within the chord that gives it ambiguous function as both a dominant and a pre-dominant (augmented sixth chord).

# Core Definition
The fifth of V7 (scale degree 2) can be altered in two ways: raised (creating an augmented sixth interval with the chordal seventh, scale degree 4, requiring resolution up to scale degree 3 with doubled third in tonic) or lowered (creating an augmented sixth with the leading tone). The lowered-fifth version in second inversion is identical to a French augmented sixth chord, creating a reciprocal process: the chord sounds like it participates in a half cadence (Fr-to-V) but actually functions as an authentic cadence (altered V-to-I). This chord is labeled FrV4/3, showing both its dominant function and French-sixth intervallic properties (Laitz, Ch. 35).

# Prerequisites
- **Augmented Triad** -- the raised-fifth version involves augmented-triad structure
- **Augmented Sixth Chord** -- the lowered-fifth version is identical to French sixth

# Key Properties
1. Fifth of V7 raised or lowered by half step
2. Raised fifth: augmented sixth between scale degrees 4 and #2; resolves with doubled third
3. Lowered fifth: identical to French augmented sixth in second inversion
4. Creates reciprocal process: sounds like PD-D but functions as D-T
5. Labeled FrV4/3

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 820): Brahms, Symphony no. 4, finale -- Altered V4/3 (with Fb in bass = enharmonic Fr4/3) resolves to E major tonic; sounds like a half cadence but is an authentic cadence.
