# Quick Definition
Two tonal processes account for chromaticism: tonicization (dominant function, temporary leading tones) and modal mixture (pre-dominant function, altered scale degrees retaining their function), and they must be distinguished in analysis.

# Core Definition
Tonicization is associated with dominant function, as new chromatic tones act as temporary leading tones. Modal mixture usually occurs within pre-dominant function, as new chromatic tones retain their scale degree function but in altered form. Additionally, major chords on diatonic 3 (III) and diatonic 6 (VI) are neither mixture nor applied chords but independent chromatic harmonies. For example, in E major, G# major (III with raised root) leads to IV as an independent harmony. The distinction: if a chromatic chord's altered tone ascends to resolve like a leading tone, it is an applied chord; if it descends or serves as an independent harmony, it may be mixture or a chromatic mediant/submediant (Laitz, Ch. 27, pp. 629-631).

# Prerequisites
- **Modal Mixture** -- one category of chromaticism
- **Applied Chord** -- the other category

# Key Properties
1. Applied chords: dominant function, temporary leading tones ascend
2. Mixture chords: pre-dominant function, altered degrees retain function
3. Chromatic III (raised 3): independent harmony, not mixture or applied
4. Chromatic VI (raised 6): independent harmony if it does not function as V/ii
5. Context and voice leading determine the analysis

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Labeling a chromatic III as V/vi
  **Clarification**: If the altered tone descends (rather than ascending as a leading tone), the chord is an independent harmony, not an applied chord
