# Quick Definition
Chromatic modulation moves to a key related by a chromatic third (bVI, bIII, or less commonly VI, III), typically using mixture chords as pivot chords.

# Core Definition
Chromatic modulations move to keys that result from modal mixture, most commonly bVI and bIII. Since there are often no diatonic triads common to both keys, the knowledge that a major key borrows from its parallel minor introduces triads that appear in both keys. The rule for chromatic pivot-chord modulations: the pivot chord must be a mixture chord in the original key. This rule holds so consistently that the presence of modal mixture often signals an upcoming chromatic modulation (Laitz, Ch. 28, pp. 637-639).

# Prerequisites
- **Modulation** -- the general concept
- **Modal Mixture** -- provides pivot chords for chromatic keys

# Key Properties
1. Targets keys related by chromatic third (bVI, bIII, VI, III)
2. Pivot chord must involve mixture in the original key
3. Modal shift of tonic (I to i) often signals upcoming chromatic modulation
4. The minor tonic loses its anchoring power and acts as PD for the new key
5. Chromatic modulations participate in the overall harmonic progression

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Establish the original key
2. Introduce mixture (often changing I to i)
3. Use the mixture chord as pivot (e.g., i becomes vi or iii in the new key)
4. Expand PD in the new key with inversions or brief tonicization
5. Close with PAC in the new key

## To Recognize:
1. Look for modal shift from major to minor tonic
2. Look for chromatic pivot chord (mixture in original key, diatonic in new key)
3. New key is typically bVI, bIII, VI, or III

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 639): E major to C major (bVI) -- E minor tonic functions as iii in C major, bridging the two distantly related keys.

**Example 2** (p. 640): Schubert, Waltz in F major -- major tonic converted to minor (m. 13) as pivot leading to Ab major (bIII).
