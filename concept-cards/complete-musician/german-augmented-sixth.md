# Quick Definition
The German augmented sixth chord (Ger65) substitutes b3 (a perfect fifth above the bass) for one of the doubled scale-degree 1 pitches, creating four distinct pitch classes that sound like a dominant seventh chord.

# Core Definition
The German augmented sixth chord (Ger65) differs from the Italian in substituting b3 (a perfect fifth above the bass) for one of the doubled pitches on scale-degree 1. The figured bass is 6/5. The Ger65 should move first to a cadential 6/4 to offset the parallel fifths that would occur with a direct move to V. In major keys, b3 must also be lowered; composers sometimes notate #2 instead of b3 to show the chromatic ascent to 3 visually, creating the "doubly augmented sixth" or "Swiss" augmented sixth. The Ger65 is enharmonically equivalent to a complete V7 chord (Laitz, Ch. 30, pp. 681-682).

# Prerequisites
- **Augmented Sixth Chord** -- the general concept

# Key Properties
1. Four distinct pitch classes: b6, 1, b3, #4
2. Sounds like a dominant seventh chord (enharmonic equivalence)
3. Usually resolves to cadential 6/4 (to avoid parallel fifths), then to V
4. In major: b3 must also be lowered; "Swiss" variant uses #2 notation
5. Contains a perfect fifth above the bass
6. The figured bass is 6/5

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Place b6 in the bass
2. Add scale-degree 1, b3 (perfect fifth above bass), and #4
3. In major: lower both b6 and b3

## To Recognize:
1. Four distinct pitches sounding like a V7 chord
2. Resolution through cadential 6/4 to V
3. Fuller sound than Italian

# Context & Application
The Ger65 is perhaps the most common augmented sixth chord. Its enharmonic equivalence to V7 enables enharmonic reinterpretation for modulation. When bVI needs to move directly to V, composers convert it to a Ger65 to solve the voice-leading problems.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 687): Beethoven, Piano Sonata op. 27 no. 1 -- Ger65-V motion reiterated seven times with sforzando markings.
