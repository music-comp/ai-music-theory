# Quick Definition
Sonata form is a large-scale musical process based on a three-part tonal strategy: stating material in the tonic, presenting additional material in a contrasting key, and restating all material in the tonic.

# Core Definition
Sonata form arises from the combination of balanced and rounded continuous binary forms. It consists of three main sections: the exposition (presenting material in tonic and a contrasting key), the development (transforming and exploring material in remote keys), and the recapitulation (restating all material in the tonic). The FTA is dependent on rounded-binary characteristics, returning with the original material after a digression and interruption. The STA is dependent on balanced-binary characteristics: material presented at the end of the first section returns at the end of the piece in the tonic. Sonata form is not a rigid mold but a flexible compositional process (Laitz, Ch. 33).

# Prerequisites
- **Binary Form** -- sonata form arises from binary form
- **Rounded Binary Form** -- the FTA's return mirrors rounded binary
- **Balanced Binary Form** -- the STA's tonic return mirrors balanced binary
- **Key Relationships** -- understanding tonic-dominant polarity

# Key Properties
1. Three main sections: exposition, development, recapitulation
2. Exposition divides into FTA (tonic) and STA (contrasting key, usually V or III)
3. Development transforms and fragments material from the exposition
4. Recapitulation restates all material in the tonic
5. Organic form -- each part depends on every other part
6. Not a rigid mold but a flexible process

# Construction / Recognition
## To Recognize:
1. Identify the exposition (often marked by repeat signs)
2. Locate the FTA in the tonic and STA in a contrasting key
3. Find the transition connecting FTA to STA
4. Identify the development section (fragmentation, remote keys, sequences)
5. Locate the recapitulation (return of FTA material in tonic)
6. Check for coda after the recapitulation

# Context & Application
Since the 1780s, sonata form has been featured in virtually all important genres: symphonies, concertos, operas, and instrumental sonatas. The terms "sonata-allegro form" and "first-movement form" are misnomers -- sonata form may appear in any tempo and any movement. It is an organic form where each part depends on every other part, unlike the additive, composite ternary and rondo forms.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 754): Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 19 in G minor, op. 49, no. 1 -- Clear sonata form: exposition (i to III), development (VI to V//), recapitulation (i throughout).

**Example 2** (p. 766): Haydn, Piano Sonata no. 48 in C major, Hob. XVI.35 -- Features motivic development of a descending fifth through all sections.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating sonata form as a rigid template
  **Correction**: Sonata form is a dynamic process with conventions that composers freely modify

- **Error**: Defining the STA by theme rather than key
  **Correction**: The STA is defined by its contrasting key, not by a "second theme"; monothematic sonatas use the same theme in both tonal areas

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Sonata form is just an expanded ternary (exposition = A, development = B, recap = A)
  **Clarification**: Sonata form is organic, not composite. Its parts are tonally interdependent. Ternary sections are self-contained; sonata sections are not.
