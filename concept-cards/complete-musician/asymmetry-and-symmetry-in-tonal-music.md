# Quick Definition
Tonal music is paradoxically "balanced" yet built on asymmetrical structures (major/minor triads, unequal scale steps, mixed-interval sequences); when symmetrical structures replace asymmetrical ones, tonal direction and stability dissolve.

# Core Definition
The tonal system is predicated on asymmetrical structures: major and minor triads contain unequal intervals; root motions divide the octave unequally (I-V vs. V-I); diatonic sequences contain mixed intervals including the tritone that creates goal-directed motion. These asymmetries create the gravitational field that pulls scale degrees and harmonies toward tonic. By contrast, scales composed solely of whole steps or half steps, or chords with equal intervals (diminished seventh, augmented triad), lack goal-directed motion. The rise of symmetrical harmonic structures in the nineteenth century progressively weakened tonal gravity, leading toward the dissolution of tonality (Laitz, Ch. 35).

# Prerequisites
- **Tonal Ambiguity** -- the result of increased symmetry

# Key Properties
1. Asymmetrical structures (unequal intervals) create tonality's gravitational field
2. Symmetrical structures (equal intervals) create tonal ambiguity
3. Diatonic sequences are asymmetrical; chromatic sequences are symmetrical
4. The tritone in diatonic sequences creates goal-directed motion; its absence in chromatic sequences removes direction
5. Symmetry in nineteenth-century harmony developed from chromatically altered dominants and chromatic sequences

# Context & Application
Understanding the symmetry/asymmetry dialectic is essential for grasping how nineteenth-century harmony evolved toward the dissolution of tonality. The shift from asymmetrical fifth-related tonal paths to symmetrical third-related paths represents one of the most important harmonic developments of the period.
