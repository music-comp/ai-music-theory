# Quick Definition
Parallel major and minor keys share the same tonic but have different key signatures, differing by three accidentals (e.g., D major and D minor).

# Core Definition
**Parallel** major and minor keys share the same tonic but do not share the same key signatures. The key signatures of parallel keys differ by three accidentals. For example, the parallel minor of D major is D minor, and the parallel major of B minor is B major.

# Prerequisites
- **Key** -- parallel keys are two keys sharing a tonic
- **Mode** -- parallel keys differ only in mode

# Key Properties
1. Parallel keys share the same tonic pitch but have different modes
2. Key signatures differ by exactly three accidentals
3. The distinction is primarily the position of ^3 (and ^6, ^7 in minor)
4. Modal shifts between parallel keys are a common expressive device

# Context & Application
Composers use parallel key shifts for dramatic contrast. A passage in major that shifts to parallel minor creates a darkening effect, while minor to parallel major creates brightening.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 21): Schubert's "Gute Nacht" (Example 1.10) shows D major (2#) and D minor (1b), differing by three accidentals.
**Example 2** (p. 21): Mozart, Piano Sonata in C major, K. 545 (Example 1.21) presents material in G major followed by G minor.

# Relationships
## Contrasts With
- **Relative Keys** -- share a key signature but have different tonics

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Confusing parallel and relative keys.
  **Correction**: Parallel keys share a tonic (D major/D minor). Relative keys share a key signature (D major/B minor).
