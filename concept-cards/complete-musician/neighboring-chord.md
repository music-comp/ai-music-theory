# Quick Definition
A neighboring chord harmonizes a bass neighbor tone, creating a subordinate chord that extends the surrounding structural harmony (e.g., I-V6-I, where V6 is a neighboring chord).

# Core Definition
A **neighboring chord** results from harmonizing a neighbor tone in the bass. The most common example is V6 used as a neighboring chord to extend tonic harmony: I-V6-I, where the bass moves ^1-^7-^1. Although a new chord is created, it is still ornamental and subordinate to the surrounding root-position tonic chords. An **incomplete neighboring chord** occurs when the neighbor is not approached by step (e.g., I6-V6-I, where the bass leaps from ^3 to ^7). Neighboring chords usually fall on metrically weak beats (Laitz, Ch. 10, pp. 231-232).

# Prerequisites
- **Neighbor tone** -- the neighboring chord harmonizes a bass neighbor
- **First-inversion triads** -- V6 is the most common neighboring chord

# Key Properties
1. Harmonizes a neighbor-tone motion in the bass
2. Subordinate to surrounding structural harmony
3. Usually on metrically weak beats
4. Most common: I-V6-I (bass ^1-^7-^1)
5. Often has ^5 in soprano (common tone with I)
6. Incomplete neighboring chord: approached by leap (I6-V6-I)

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 232): Beethoven's Symphony no. 7, Allegretto opens with I-V6/5-I, using V6 (with added seventh) as a neighboring chord to prolong tonic.
