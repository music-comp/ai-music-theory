# Quick Definition
A pitch's function depends entirely on its musical context -- melody alone cannot determine whether a pitch is consonant or dissonant; the underlying harmony must be considered.

# Core Definition
**Contextual analysis** is the principle that a pitch's consonant or dissonant status, and therefore its structural importance, cannot be determined from the melody alone. The underlying harmonic support, rhythm, and metric placement must all be considered. A pitch that appears to be a passing tone when examining melody alone may actually be a chord tone when the harmony is taken into account, and vice versa (Laitz, Ch. 7, pp. 166-168).

# Prerequisites
- **Consonance and dissonance** -- must understand stability vs. instability
- **Nonchord tones** -- must know types of embellishments

# Key Properties
1. A pitch's function depends on its harmonic, rhythmic, and metric context
2. Melody alone is insufficient to determine consonance or dissonance
3. The same pitch can be consonant in one harmonic context and dissonant in another
4. Harmonic changes can completely redefine the function of melodic pitches

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 166-168): In Schubert's "Der Lindenbaum," F#4 appears to be a dissonant passing tone when examining the melody alone. But with the accompaniment, the harmony changes to B major (V) in m. 11, making F# a consonant chord tone, while the previously "stable" G#4 becomes a dissonant passing tone.
