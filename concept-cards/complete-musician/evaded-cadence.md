# Quick Definition
An evaded cadence occurs when a strong cadential arrival is thwarted by adding the chordal seventh in the bass (creating V4/2), which must resolve to the weak I6 instead of root-position I.

# Core Definition
An **evaded cadence** occurs when a composer circumvents a strong cadential arrival by adding the chordal seventh in the bass of the cadential dominant (converting V to V4/2). Since the seventh must resolve down to ^3, the music arrives on the weaker I6 rather than root-position I, dissolving the cadence. This technique serves two purposes: (1) **Elision** -- hiding the seam between two phrases to create one large phrase; (2) **Cadential extension** -- intensifying a cadence by repeating PD and cad 6/4 elements before finally stating the authentic cadence. The evaded cadence often creates a "harmonic crescendo" leading to the structural cadence (Laitz, Ch. 14, pp. 336-339).

# Prerequisites
- **Cadential six-four chord** -- the cadence being evaded
- **V4/2** -- the mechanism of evasion

# Key Properties
1. V4/2 replaces expected V(5/3) at the cadence
2. Must resolve to I6 (not root-position I)
3. Creates elision (hiding phrase boundaries) or extension (repeating cadential material)
4. Intensifies listener desire for closure
5. Multiple evasions can create increasingly dramatic attempts at closure

# Examples
**Example 1** (pp. 337-338): Mozart's Symphony K. 114: cad 6/4 in m. 12 converts to V4/2, requiring resolution to I6 and thereby covering the seam between two phrases, creating a single eight-measure phrase.

**Example 2** (pp. 338-339): Mozart's Magic Flute quintet: three attempts at cadential closure, with the first two evaded by V4/2 -> I6, before the third finally achieves a PAC.
