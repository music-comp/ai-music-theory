# Quick Definition
Tonal music is hierarchical: certain pitches, intervals, and harmonies are structurally more important than others, and subordinate events embellish the more important ones.

# Core Definition
Hierarchy in tonal music means that not all pitches, intervals, or chords are created equal. The tonic triad and its members (^1, ^3, ^5) are the most structurally important pitches; the remaining scale degrees function as embellishments. Similarly, the tonic and dominant harmonies form the structural axis, while other chords elaborate them. This hierarchical principle operates at every level -- from individual pitches within a beat to large-scale harmonic progressions spanning entire movements (Laitz, Ch. 7).

# Prerequisites
- **Consonance and dissonance** -- the stability ranking that creates hierarchy
- **Triads** -- stable entities surrounded by unstable forces

# Key Properties
1. The tonic triad (^1, ^3, ^5) provides the structural foundation
2. Dissonant pitches are subordinate to and embellish consonant pitches
3. Hierarchy operates at multiple structural levels simultaneously
4. Analysis reveals how subordinate pitches embellish more important ones
5. The deeper you analyze, the fewer structural events remain

# Construction / Recognition
## To Recognize:
1. Identify the tonic and its triad members as primary structural tones
2. Classify remaining pitches as embellishments (passing, neighbor, etc.)
3. Consider which harmonies are structural (usually root-position I and V) and which are ornamental
4. Observe how the hierarchy extends across multiple levels of structure

# Context & Application
Understanding hierarchy is the gateway to meaningful analysis. It moves beyond simple labeling of surface events to reveal the deeper relationships that actually control the music's unfolding.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 163): In a descending major scale over sustained C, the stable pitches are C, E, G, and A (consonances), with C and G most stable (perfect consonances), E next (imperfect consonance), and A least stable (tendency to fall to G).

**Example 2** (p. 167): Both "Clementine" and "God Save the King" share the same underlying structure -- arpeggiation of the tonic triad followed by stepwise descent to ^1 -- despite very different surface melodies.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating all pitches and chords as equally important
  **Correction**: Use metric placement, duration, harmonic context, and contour to determine relative importance

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Hierarchy means some pitches don't matter
  **Clarification**: Subordinate pitches are essential for musical interest and expression; they embellish the structural pitches
