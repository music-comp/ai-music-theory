# Quick Definition
Plagal motion describes progressions in which harmonies other than the dominant lead directly to the tonic, including iv-I, ii-half-dim-65-I (the "Hollywood cadence"), and bVI-I.

# Core Definition
During the nineteenth century, the dominant's role at cadences gradually changed. Mixture harmonies, with their dramatic effects, became popular substitutes for the dominant. The term "plagal motion" (abbreviated PL in second-level analysis) describes progressions in which harmonies other than the dominant lead to the tonic: iv-I, ii-half-dim-65-I (the "Hollywood cadence," common in popular music of the 1920s-1950s and in film scores), and bVI-I. The Hollywood cadence is a chromatically embellished plagal cadence where a 5-6 motion and mixture transform IV into ii-half-dim-65 (Laitz, Ch. 27, pp. 627-628).

# Key Properties
1. Dominant is absent from the cadence
2. Mixture chords (iv, ii-half-dim, bVI) resolve directly to tonic
3. The "Hollywood cadence" (ii-half-dim-65-I) is a chromaticized plagal cadence
4. Labeled "PL" in second-level analysis
5. Became increasingly common in 19th-century music

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 628): Brahms, Symphony no. 3, Andante -- bVI and iv resolve to tonic in a plagal cadence without any dominant harmony.
