# Quick Definition
A period comprising an uneven number of phrases (three, five, or more), where all cadences except the final one are weaker, and phrases are distributed through repetition, new material, or a combination of both.

# Core Definition
Asymmetrical periods contain an uneven number of phrases (typically three or five). Each phrase must close with a cadence weaker than the final phrase's cadence. Composers distribute the phrases in three ways: (1) **immediate repetition** of antecedent or consequent (aab or abb); (2) **new material** for each phrase (abc); (3) **combination** of repetition and new material (aabbc). Asymmetrical periods differ from phrase groups in that the final cadence provides clear closure to the preceding phrases. (Laitz, Ch. 21, pp. 485-489)

# Prerequisites
- **Period** — Must understand standard two-phrase period

# Key Properties
1. Odd number of phrases (3, 5, or more)
2. Final cadence strongest
3. Three distribution strategies: repetition, new material, or combination
4. Distinguished from phrase groups by the strength of the final cadence

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 485): Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C minor ("Pathetique"), Rondo -- abb structure (antecedent-consequent-consequent).

**Example 2** (p. 486): Gruber, "Silent Night" -- abc structure with three distinct sections.

**Example 3** (p. 487): Haydn, String Quartet "Emperor" -- aabbc structure with five phrases.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Period** — Extends the period concept to uneven phrase counts

## Contrasts With
- **Phrase group** — A phrase group lacks the clear final-cadence closure of an asymmetrical period

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Any series of phrases with weak cadences is an asymmetrical period
  **Clarification**: The final cadence must be strong enough to close the entire passage; otherwise it is a phrase group
