# Quick Definition
A compositional technique in which two melodic voices swap registers -- what was on top moves to the bottom and vice versa -- while retaining their original contour.

# Core Definition
Invertible counterpoint (also called double counterpoint) is a procedure in which melodic material is exchanged between voices: the soprano melody becomes the bass and vice versa. The most common type is **invertible counterpoint at the octave**, where rearrangement is accomplished solely by octave leaps in one or both voices. Because intervals inverted at the octave maintain their consonant or dissonant character -- except that a fifth becomes a dissonant fourth -- composers must treat all perfect fifths as potentially dissonant (preparing, resolving, and placing them on weak beats). Inversion at the twelfth is also possible but less common. (Laitz, Ch. 15, p. 349)

# Prerequisites
- **Interval inversion** — Understanding that when intervals are inverted at the octave, consonance/dissonance is preserved except for the 5th/4th pair
- **Contrapuntal expansion** — The technique extends and elaborates underlying harmonic progressions

# Key Properties
1. Economical: yields twice as much musical value from a single idea
2. Unifying: the listener hears the same material in different registral contexts, creating variety within coherence
3. Perfect fifths become the only problematic interval (becoming dissonant fourths upon inversion)
4. Can operate on the musical surface (literal swap) or at deeper structural levels beneath a homophonic texture

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Write a two-voice contrapuntal passage with correct voice leading
2. Treat all perfect fifths as if dissonant (prepare and resolve them, place on weak beats)
3. Swap the voices -- upper becomes lower and vice versa -- by transposing at the octave
4. Verify that the inverted version maintains correct treatment of dissonance

## To Recognize:
1. Look for two sections where melodic material appears in swapped registers
2. Label the material X and Y and trace how each moves between voices
3. Check whether the underlying harmonic progression remains coherent in both versions

# Context & Application
Invertible counterpoint appears in all genres -- piano sonatas, symphonies, chamber music, fugues, and chorale preludes. Brahms, Clara Schumann, and Joseph Joachim formed a "counterpoint club" in which invertible counterpoint was central. The technique is especially important in developmental passages and in contrapuntal expansions of tonic harmony.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 349): Mozart, Piano Sonata in G major, K. 283 -- outer-voice melodies marked X and Y swap places between two sections, retaining their original contour.

**Example 2** (p. 349): Haydn, Symphony no. 91 in F major -- invertible counterpoint in the developmental section of a large symphonic movement.

**Example 3** (p. 349): Brahms, Intermezzo in A minor, op. 76, no. 7 -- invertible counterpoint extends C major harmony; recognizing the inversion is more revealing than roman numeral analysis.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Writing parallel fifths in the original, which become parallel fourths (dissonant) in inversion
  **Correction**: Treat all perfect fifths as potentially dissonant -- prepare and resolve them, place on weak beats

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Invertible counterpoint must be an obvious, literal swap on the musical surface
  **Clarification**: It can operate at deeper structural levels beneath a homophonic texture, where the structural soprano and bass exchange roles (as in Schubert, Piano Sonata, op. 42)
