# Quick Definition
Melodic fluency refers to underlying scalar patterns that support the surface variety of melodic embellishments, revealed when embellishing pitches are pruned away.

# Core Definition
**Melodic fluency** refers to the underlying scalar patterns that support the infinite variety of melodic embellishments on the music's surface. By reducing (pruning away) embellishing pitches, analysts discover structural stepwise lines that provide the skeleton of phrases. These lines are aurally "marked" by accents of various kinds -- duration, metric placement, dynamics, articulation, harmonic change, and contour. Composers from all eras share this property: melodies typically rise from a stable initiating point, ascend to a climax, and descend to a point of repose (Laitz, Ch. 7, pp. 172-176).

# Prerequisites
- **Hierarchy in tonal music** -- fluent lines emerge from hierarchical pitch analysis
- **Passing tone** and **neighbor tone** -- the embellishments that are reduced away

# Key Properties
1. Structural stepwise lines underlie embellished melodies
2. These lines are revealed through reduction (removing embellishments)
3. Accented pitches (metric, durational, dynamic) mark the structural line
4. Common contour: rise from ^1, ascend to climax, descend to repose
5. Different surface melodies can share the same underlying structural line

# Construction / Recognition
## To Recognize:
1. Identify accented pitches (metrically stressed, durationally long, repeated)
2. Connect them to reveal stepwise lines
3. Verify these lines are supported by the harmonic structure
4. Note ascending and descending patterns

# Context & Application
Melodic fluency is central to both analysis and performance. Performers who sense these underlying lines can shape phrases more convincingly. Composers use them to create unified, goal-directed melodies.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 174): In Donizetti's Lucrezia Borgia, the surface melody conceals a structural descent B-A-G#-F#-E (a falling fifth), with each pitch accented by duration, metric placement, or both.

**Example 2** (pp. 167-170): Both "Clementine" and "God Save the King" share the same underlying structure: arpeggiation of the tonic triad (F4-A4-C5) in their first phrases and descent from C5 to F4 in their second phrases.

- **Error**: Analyzing melody without considering underlying harmonic support
