# Quick Definition
An intervallic cell is a small, malleable motivic structure defined by its intervallic content and contour rather than by its specific pitches, capable of development in both melodic and harmonic domains at multiple structural levels.

# Core Definition
Intervallic cells (or cells) are short pitch collections defined by their component intervals and overall contour rather than by specific pitches or rhythms. Unlike traditional motives, cells are more abstract. They appear early in a composition and are developed through transposition, inversion (reversing contour), interpolation (adding pitches between members), rhythmic diminution, and rhythmic augmentation. Cells can influence both surface-level and deep-level melodic and harmonic events. They represent a shift from periodic, singable melodic constructions to more abstract pitch collections, bridging tonal and atonal composition. A copy need not be exact; the intervallic content (e.g., a fourth and a third containing a second) remains constant even if specific intervals or contour change (Laitz, Ch. 37).

# Prerequisites
- **Tonal Ambiguity** -- cells thrive in harmonically ambiguous contexts

# Key Properties
1. Defined by intervallic content and contour, not specific pitches
2. More abstract than traditional motives
3. Can be transposed, inverted, interpolated, augmented, or diminished
4. Operate at multiple structural levels simultaneously
5. Influence both melodic and harmonic domains
6. Bridge tonal and atonal compositional practice
7. Copies need not be exact; overall intervallic content is maintained

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 860): Brahms, Intermezzo in A minor, op. 118, no. 1 -- Three-note descending cell (C6-Bb5-A5) transposed, inverted, and expanded across multiple levels.

**Example 2** (p. 862): Chopin, Prelude in A minor, op. 28, no. 2 -- Three-note cell (fourth + third, containing a second) appears in melody, ostinato, bass, and at the deepest structural level.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Cell vs. motive
  **Clarification**: Motives are defined by specific rhythmic and pitch profiles. Cells are defined by intervallic content and can appear in varied rhythmic, registral, and contour configurations. A cell's identity is preserved when its intervals are maintained even if pitches, rhythm, or direction change.
