# Quick Definition
Equal divisions of the octave are symmetrical progressions that segment the octave into intervals of the same size (1, 2, 3, 4, or 6 half steps), creating inherently nonfunctional, ambiguous harmonic motion.

# Core Definition
Equal divisions of the octave incorporate two or more identical intervals into progressions that partition the octave symmetrically. The possible divisions are: by semitone (12 divisions, chromatic scale), by whole tone (6 divisions, whole-tone scale), by minor third (4 divisions, diminished seventh chord), by major third (3 divisions, augmented triad), and by tritone (2 divisions). These divisions operate at both the chord level (symmetrical harmonies) and the key-relationship level (sequential progressions that divide the octave into equal transpositions). Because all steps are identical, these progressions lack the asymmetry that creates tonal direction (Laitz, Ch. 37).

# Key Properties
1. Octave divided by identical intervals
2. Five possible divisions: 1, 2, 3, 4, or 6 half steps
3. Operate at chord and key-relationship levels
4. Lack tonal direction due to symmetry
5. Increasingly important from late 18th to early 20th century

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 853): All five possible equal divisions: chromatic (12x m2), whole-tone (6x M2), minor third (4x m3), major third (3x M3), tritone (2x TT).

**Example 2** (p. 855): Beethoven, "Waldstein" Sonata, development -- Key areas by major thirds (C-Ab-E).

**Example 3** (p. 855): Schubert, Wanderer Fantasy -- Key areas by minor thirds (C#-E-G-Bb).

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Calling any chromatic progression an equal division
  **Correction**: True equal divisions require consistent, identical intervals between all steps
