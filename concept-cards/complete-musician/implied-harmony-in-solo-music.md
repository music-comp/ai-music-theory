# Quick Definition
Even solo instrumental music consisting of a single line often unfolds within a strong, albeit implied, underlying harmonic context that allows analysts to distinguish consonant from dissonant tones of figuration.

# Core Definition
Solo instrumental music -- even a single melodic line -- often contains a strong **implied harmonic context**. Arpeggiations and chordal leaps outline harmonies; passing tones and neighbors create dissonances against these implied harmonies. The harmonic rhythm (rate of implied chord changes) usually coincides with metric accents, and the principle of good continuation helps predict harmonic changes. Identifying implied harmony allows analysts to apply the same consonance/dissonance distinctions used in full-textured music (Laitz, Ch. 7, pp. 165-166, 176-177).

# Prerequisites
- **Contextual analysis** -- implied harmony is determined by context
- **Nonchord tones** -- identifying NCTs requires knowing the implied harmony

# Key Properties
1. Single-line music still implies harmonic progressions
2. Arpeggiations and chordal leaps outline the implied chords
3. Harmonic rhythm usually aligns with metric accents
4. Good continuation helps predict chord changes
5. Same analytical principles apply as in full-textured music

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 165): Bach's Presto in G minor for solo violin opens with cascading broken-chord figures that clearly imply G minor for five measures, then V7 for two measures.

**Example 2** (p. 176): Bach's Sonata no. 3 for solo violin implies two harmonic changes per measure, with the two-beat harmonic rhythm established by accented bass notes.
