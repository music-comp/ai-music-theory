# Quick Definition
An applied dominant chord is a major triad or dominant seventh chord (V or V7) that functions as the dominant of a diatonic chord other than the tonic.

# Core Definition
For a chord to function as an applied dominant, it must be a major triad (V) or a dominant seventh chord (V7) and it will usually move to its tonic. The applied dominant is labeled V/X or V7/X, where X is the target diatonic chord. Some diatonic chords are already major triads and can serve as applied dominants; in such cases, adding the minor seventh clarifies the applied function (Laitz, Ch. 24, p. 539).

# Prerequisites
- **Applied Chord** -- general concept of secondary function
- **Dominant Seventh Chord** -- the chord type used for applied dominants

# Key Properties
1. Must be major triad or dominant seventh chord
2. Diatonic chords already major (e.g., I in major functioning as V/IV) typically need the added seventh to clarify applied function
3. In major: V/IV is diatonic (built on scale-degree 1); in minor: V/VI and V/III are diatonic
4. The third of the applied dominant acts as a temporary leading tone

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Determine the target chord (e.g., ii in C major = D minor)
2. Build the dominant of that chord (A major = V/ii)
3. Optionally add the seventh (A-C#-E-G = V7/ii)

## To Recognize:
1. A chromatically raised note signals a temporary leading tone (third of the applied chord)
2. A chromatically lowered note may be the seventh of an applied dominant
3. The chord resolves to a consonant diatonic triad

# Context & Application
Applied dominants are the most common form of applied chord. They can appear in root position or inversion, and inversions are frequently used to create smoother bass lines. Any inversion of V7 can prolong its target chord just as inversions of V7 prolong I.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 539): In C major, to find V/V: V is G major; the dominant of G is D major; D major is not diatonic to C, so it is labeled V/V.

**Example 2** (p. 540): Applied dominant chords in inversion create smoother bass motion, as demonstrated by comparing root-position and inverted applied chord progressions.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Writing V/vii° in major
  **Correction**: vii° is diminished and cannot be tonicized; the root of IV lies an augmented fourth from vii°

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Hearing I as V/IV without the seventh
  **Clarification**: Without the added seventh, the progression I-IV sounds like a simple tonic-to-subdominant motion, not a tonicization
