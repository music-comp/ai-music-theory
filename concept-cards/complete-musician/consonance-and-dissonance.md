# Quick Definition
Consonance and dissonance describe the relative stability of intervals; their status depends on musical context, not just the interval itself.

# Core Definition
Perfect consonances (unison, octave, P5) are the most stable. Imperfect consonances (M3, m3, M6, m6) are less stable but fluid, permitting parallel motion. Dissonant intervals (seconds, sevenths, P4, augmented and diminished intervals) create tension requiring resolution. Crucially, the consonant or dissonant status of an interval depends on its context -- the same interval (e.g., a minor sixth or perfect fourth) can be consonant or dissonant depending on the surrounding harmony and bass note (Laitz, Ch. 7).

# Key Properties
1. Perfect consonances are the most stable intervals
2. Imperfect consonances are less stable but more fluid (can move in parallel)
3. Dissonant intervals require resolution to consonant intervals
4. A pitch's consonant/dissonant status depends entirely on its harmonic context
5. The hierarchy of stability (PC > IC > D) creates goal-directed motion

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Identify the bass note and the interval formed above it
2. Classify the interval as perfect consonance, imperfect consonance, or dissonance
3. Consider the harmonic context to determine functional status

## To Recognize:
1. Listen for stability (consonance) vs. tension (dissonance)
2. Note whether the interval resolves stepwise to a more stable interval
3. Check whether the bass participates in the interval formation

# Context & Application
Consonance and dissonance form the foundation of tonal hierarchy. Dissonant pitches create tension and energy that drive music forward; consonant pitches provide stability and rest. This principle governs voice leading, counterpoint, and the behavior of nonchord tones.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 162): The minor sixth C-Ab can be consonant (when F or Ab is the bass, creating an F minor or Ab major triad) or dissonant (when C is the bass, yearning for resolution to a P5).

**Example 2** (p. 162): A perfect fourth above C is dissonant when C is the bass (F strives to resolve down by step) but consonant when A or Ab appears below (creating a first-inversion triad).

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating consonance and dissonance as fixed properties of intervals
  **Correction**: Always consider the musical context; the same interval can be consonant or dissonant depending on surrounding harmony

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: The perfect fourth is always dissonant
  **Clarification**: The P4 is dissonant only when the bass participates in forming it; when it occurs between upper voices, it is consonant
