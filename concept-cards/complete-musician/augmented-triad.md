# Quick Definition
The augmented triad consists of two major thirds spanning an augmented fifth, partitions the octave into three equal parts, and retains its intervallic structure under inversion, making it inherently ambiguous.

# Core Definition
The augmented triad is symmetrical, consisting of two major thirds spanning an augmented fifth. Like the diminished seventh chord, it partitions the octave equally (into three major thirds). Unlike every other triadic structure, inversions of the augmented triad retain major third and augmented fifth intervals (or their enharmonic equivalents), making inversions aurally indistinguishable. There are only four distinct augmented triads. Originally a byproduct of melodic passing motion (e.g., raised fifth as passing tone), the augmented triad evolved into an independent sonority in late nineteenth-century music (Laitz, Ch. 35).

5. Originally arose from melodic passing motion
6. Evolved into an independent sonority in late Romantic music

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 816): Beethoven, Bagatelle in C major, op. 119 -- Chromatic passing tone G# creates an augmented triad that also suggests an applied dominant to A minor.

**Example 2** (p. 817): Grieg, "Illusion," op. 57 -- Opening sonority E-G#-C is an augmented triad that creates tonal ambiguity before the key of A minor is established.

**Example 3** (p. 818): Liszt, "Nuages gris" -- All chords but the first are augmented triads, descending chromatically to obscure all sense of tonality.
