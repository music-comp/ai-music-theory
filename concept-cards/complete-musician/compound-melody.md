# Quick Definition
A single melodic line that implies two, three, or even four independent voices through registral leaps, creating the illusion of a multi-voice texture within a monophonic line.

# Core Definition
A compound melody occurs when a composer achieves the illusion of multiple voices within a single melodic line by means of registral leaps that split the line into voices delineated by register. Each implied voice follows standard voice-leading rules to progress to the next harmony. The technique allows composers to imply a clear harmonic structure without using fully voiced chords. (Laitz, Ch. 15, p. 356)

# Prerequisites
- **Voice leading** — Each implied voice within a compound melody must follow standard voice-leading rules
- **Triads and inversions** — Must recognize chord tones to identify the implied harmonies

# Key Properties
1. A single line splits into multiple voices delineated by register
2. Each implied voice follows standard voice-leading rules
3. The harmonic rhythm typically moves at one chord per measure or similar slow rate
4. Can arise from broken-chord patterns (Alberti bass), arpeggiations, or stepwise motion with leaps
5. Nonchord tones may disguise the compound-line structure

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Write a chord progression with good voice leading in three or four voices
2. Arpeggiate the voices temporally rather than sounding them simultaneously
3. Add tones of figuration (passing tones, neighbors) to create a flowing melodic line
4. Ensure each register-defined strand maintains voice-leading independence

## To Recognize:
1. Group notes by register to identify separate implied voice strands
2. Verticalize the implied voices to reveal the underlying chord progression
3. Verify that the voice leading between chords is correct
4. Identify any nonchord tones that embellish the compound structure

# Context & Application
Compound melody is essential in solo instrumental music, especially Bach's works for solo violin and solo cello. Knowledge of the harmonic structure implied by compound melody is essential for effective performance -- it affects dynamic shaping, color, contrast, coordination of rubato, and projection of melodic line. The Alberti bass is a common type of compound melody in Classical-period keyboard music.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 356): Mozart, Piano Sonata in D major, K. 284 -- left-hand Alberti bass unfolds a tonic harmony in m. 1, with three voices following voice-leading rules to progress through contrapuntal expansion.

**Example 2** (p. 359): Bach, Partita for Solo Violin in B minor, BWV 1002, Corrente -- solo violin plays an arpeggiated pattern with passing tones; harmonic rhythm of one chord per measure; the highest and lowest implied strands form outer voices in note-against-note counterpoint.

**Example 3** (p. 358): Handel, Suite no. 3 in D minor -- right-hand voice partitioned into two voices moving primarily in parallel sixths, providing the sensation of full three-note triads in a two-voice texture.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Analyzing each successive note as a separate chord change
  **Correction**: Group notes by register into implied voice strands; the harmonic rhythm is usually much slower than the surface rhythm

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Compound melody only occurs in arpeggiated textures
  **Clarification**: It can also occur in stepwise motion with occasional leaps (as in Bach's Corrente Double), where the contour disguises the underlying voice structure
