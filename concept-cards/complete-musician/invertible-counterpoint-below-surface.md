# Quick Definition
Invertible counterpoint that operates at a structural level beneath the musical surface, where a full homophonic texture disguises the exchange of melodic roles between bass and soprano.

# Core Definition
Occurrences of invertible counterpoint are often subtler than literal registral swaps. Through embellishing processes, composers camouflage their use of invertible counterpoint as a structural agent, in which it becomes the backbone of the harmonic progression itself. A contrapuntal reduction reveals that large-scale melodic figures (e.g., neighbor figures, passing motions) swap between outer voices across sections. (Laitz, Ch. 15, p. 352)

# Prerequisites
- **Invertible counterpoint** — Must understand the basic concept of voice-swapping
- **Two-level analysis** — Must be able to distinguish structural pitches from surface embellishment

# Key Properties
1. The exchange is visible only through contrapuntal reduction, not on the musical surface
2. Roman numeral analysis alone identifies "symptoms" rather than the underlying cause
3. Large-scale melodic figures (neighbor, passing) trade between soprano and bass
4. Voice exchanges at a large scale often accompany the technique

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Write a structural two-voice counterpoint with exchangeable figures
2. Elaborate each voice with embellishing tones and full harmonic texture
3. Swap the structural voices for the next section while maintaining the surface texture

## To Recognize:
1. Reduce the passage to its structural outer-voice counterpoint (stemmed pitches only)
2. Identify large-scale melodic figures (neighbor, passing) in each voice
3. Compare successive sections to see if these structural figures have swapped registers

# Context & Application
This technique is particularly important in analysis, where it reveals compositional logic that chord-by-chord roman numeral labeling misses. Schubert frequently uses this technique, as do Brahms and other Romantic composers.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 352): Schubert, Piano Sonata -- a full homophonic texture disguises the invertible relationship between soprano and bass. A two-voice reduction reveals a large neighbor figure (^1-^7-^1) in the soprano swapping with a passing motion (^1-^2-^3) in the bass.

- **Confusion**: If the surface does not show an obvious voice swap, invertible counterpoint cannot be present
