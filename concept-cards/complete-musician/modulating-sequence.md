# Quick Definition
A modulating sequence is a sequence that is prematurely stopped and reinterpreted, with one of its chords functioning as a pre-dominant that leads to a dominant in a new key.

# Core Definition
Sequences make excellent pivots because they touch on many chords, sometimes every chord within a key. By prematurely quitting a sequence and reinterpreting one of its chords as a pre-dominant moving to a dominant, one can effectively modulate to a new key. A single sequence can modulate to several different closely related keys depending on where it is stopped and how the final chord is reinterpreted (Laitz, Ch. 25, pp. 575-578).

# Prerequisites
- **Harmonic Sequences** -- the sequential foundation
- **Modulation** -- the process facilitated by the sequence

# Key Properties
1. The sequence is "quit" at a chosen point
2. The final chord is reinterpreted as PD in the new key
3. A single sequence can serve modulations to multiple different keys
4. D2 (-5/+4) and A2 (-3/+4) are commonly used for modulating sequences

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 575): Vivaldi, Trio Sonata in C minor -- a D2 sequence stops on VI (Ab chord), reinterpreted as IV in Eb major (III).

**Example 2** (p. 577): From a D2 sequence in G minor, modulations to III, VI, and v are all possible by stopping at different points.
