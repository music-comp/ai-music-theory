# Quick Definition
Counterpoint ("melody against melody") is the art of combining two or more independent melodic voices so that they work together harmonically while maintaining their individual melodic integrity.

# Core Definition
**Counterpoint** (or **polyphony**, meaning "multiple sounds") is the relationship between and movement of two or more voices. Contrapuntal relationships depend on two elements: (1) the behavior of consonant and dissonant intervals, and (2) the harmonies implied by the interaction of voices. The **outer voices** (soprano and bass) are aurally the most prominent and provide the structural skeleton; harmony fills the space between them.

# Prerequisites
- **Consonant and Dissonant Intervals** -- counterpoint controls the interplay of consonance and dissonance
- **Melody** -- each contrapuntal voice must be a well-crafted melody

# Key Properties
1. Each voice must be melodic, moving primarily by step or small leap
2. Voices should combine to produce mostly consonant intervals
3. Voices should maintain independence (contrary motion preferred)
4. The outer voices are the most audible and structurally important
5. Johann Fux's species counterpoint provides the pedagogical framework
6. Counterpoint underlies all tonal harmony -- chords result from the convergence of melodic voices

# Context & Application
Counterpoint has been the focus of Western music study since the tenth century. Understanding counterpoint is essential for voice leading, harmonic analysis, and composition. Fux's Gradus ad Parnassum was studied by Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, and Brahms.
