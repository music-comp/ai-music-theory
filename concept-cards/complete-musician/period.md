# Quick Definition
A formal structure of two or more phrases in which the final phrase provides cadential closure to something left harmonically or melodically open by the first phrase. Labeled by three terms: melodic relationship + harmonic type + "period."

# Core Definition
A period consists of an **antecedent** phrase ending with a weaker cadence (typically HC or IAC) followed by a **consequent** phrase ending with a stronger cadence (typically PAC). The antecedent creates expectation through incompleteness; the consequent fulfills it through closure. Periods are labeled with three descriptors: (1) **melodic relationship** -- parallel (similar openings) or contrasting (different openings); (2) **harmonic structure** -- interrupted (antecedent ends on V), continuous (antecedent ends on non-tonic), sectional (both end on tonic but second stronger), or progressive (consequent modulates); (3) the word "period." Example: PIP = parallel interrupted period. (Laitz, Ch. 20, pp. 464-475)

# Prerequisites
- **Cadence types** — Must distinguish HC, IAC, PAC
- **Phrase model** — Each phrase within a period has its own T-PD-D-T

# Key Properties
1. Minimum two phrases with cadences
2. Final cadence must be stronger than earlier cadences
3. Antecedent creates expectation; consequent provides closure
4. A single phrase with no internal cadence is not a period (even with a caesura)
5. Period labeling: melodic + harmonic + "period" (e.g., PIP, CCP, PSP, PPP)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Recognize:
1. Identify cadences: where are the phrase endings?
2. Compare cadence strengths: is the final cadence stronger?
3. Compare phrase openings: parallel or contrasting?
4. Classify the harmonic structure: interrupted, continuous, sectional, or progressive?
5. Combine labels: e.g., parallel interrupted period (PIP)

## To Construct:
1. Write an antecedent phrase ending with HC or IAC
2. Write a consequent phrase with a similar or contrasting opening ending with PAC
3. Ensure the consequent provides closure to the antecedent's open question

# Context & Application
Periods are fundamental formal structures appearing in themes, dance movements, and as building blocks of larger forms. They are essential to Classical-era composition and pervade music from folk songs to art music.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 466): Mozart, Piano Sonata in B-flat major, K. 333 -- PIP with HC in m. 4 and PAC in m. 8.

**Example 2** (p. 470): Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 11 in B-flat major, op. 22 -- CCP where antecedent ends on ii.

**Example 3** (p. 471): Mozart, Piano Sonata in B-flat major, K. 281 -- PSP where both phrases end on tonic but second is stronger.

## Contrasts With
- **Phrase group** — A series of phrases without the antecedent-consequent relationship

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Identifying any pair of phrases as a period
  **Correction**: The second cadence must be stronger than the first

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: An eight-measure phrase with a caesura is a period
  **Clarification**: A single phrase with no internal cadence is not a period, even if it has a break

- **Confusion**: Sentence and period are the same thing
  **Clarification**: A sentence (1:1:2 proportion) is a single phrase or can span a period; a period is defined by the antecedent-consequent cadential relationship
