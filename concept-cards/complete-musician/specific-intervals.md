# Quick Definition
A specific interval combines a generic size with a quality label (perfect, major, minor, augmented, or diminished) determined by whether the upper pitch belongs to the major or minor scale of the lower pitch.

# Core Definition
The **specific size** of an interval combines the generic number with a **quality**: **P** (perfect) for unisons, fourths, fifths, and octaves; **M** (major) or **m** (minor) for seconds, thirds, sixths, and sevenths. Quality is determined by scale membership: if the upper pitch lies in the major scale of the lower pitch, the interval is perfect (for P1, P4, P5, P8) or major (for M2, M3, M6, M7). If it lies in the natural minor scale (except for the second, which is major in both), it is minor.

# Prerequisites
- **Generic Intervals** -- specific intervals add quality to the generic size
- **Major Scale** -- the reference for determining quality
- **Minor Scales** -- minor intervals occur in the minor scale

# Key Properties
1. Two categories: perfect intervals (P1, P4, P5, P8) and major/minor intervals (2, 3, 6, 7)
2. Perfect intervals retain their quality in both major and minor modes
3. Major intervals occur above ^1 in the major scale; minor intervals in the natural minor scale
4. The process: (1) determine generic size, (2) check if upper pitch is in the major or minor scale of lower pitch
5. Abbreviations: P = perfect, M = major, m = minor

# Construction / Recognition
## To Determine:
1. Find the generic size (count letter names)
2. Ask: is the upper pitch in the major scale of the lower pitch? If yes, P or M
3. Is it in the natural minor scale? If yes, m (for 3, 6, 7) or P (for 4, 5)
4. If neither, the interval is augmented or diminished

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 73): D up to G: generic fourth; G is in D major, so P4.
**Example 2** (p. 73): Ab up to C: generic third; C is in Ab major, so M3.
**Example 3** (p. 73): E up to G: generic third; G is in E minor, so m3.
