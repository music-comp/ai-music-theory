# Quick Definition
The progression V-vi (or V-VI in minor), where the listener expects tonic resolution but hears the submediant instead, creating a surprise that delays closure and typically "rewinds" the phrase back to the pre-dominant.

# Core Definition
A deceptive motion occurs when V resolves to vi instead of I. The submediant substitutes for the expected tonic, thwarting cadential closure. The term "deceptive motion" is preferred to "deceptive cadence" because the motion typically does not create actual phrase closure -- it usually rewinds the phrase model back to PD (or sometimes T), followed by an authentic cadence. In the soprano, ^2-^1 or ^7-^1 enhances the deceptive effect. (Laitz, Ch. 18, pp. 428-430)

# Prerequisites
- **Submediant** — vi is the destination chord
- **Cadence types** — Understanding authentic cadence expectation

# Key Properties
1. V resolves to vi instead of I
2. Root motion by ascending second (^5-^6)
3. Does not typically create true closure -- more a "motion" than a "cadence"
4. Usually followed by a return to PD or T, then a proper PAC
5. Creates dramatic suspense and delays gratification

# Context & Application
Deceptive motions are among the most dramatic harmonic events in tonal music. They extend phrases and create heightened expectations for the eventual authentic cadence. Haydn was especially fond of dramatic deceptive motions.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 428): Haydn, String Quartet in D minor ("Quinten") -- V moves to vi with a fermata and florid ascent in first violin, dramatizing the thwarted resolution.

**Example 2** (p. 429): Mozart, Trio in E major, K. 498 -- deceptive motion in m. 12 followed by return to tonic and varied repetition leading to PAC.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Labeling V-vi at the beginning of a piece as a "deceptive cadence"
  **Correction**: A cadence requires phrase-ending context; early V-vi is a deceptive motion within a progression
