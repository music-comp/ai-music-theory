# Quick Definition
Nonchord tones are pitches that do not belong to the underlying harmony; they create tension that requires resolution to chord tones.

# Core Definition
When a melody unfolds over an underlying harmony, some pitches are members of the chord (chord tones) while others are not. Those that are not members of the underlying harmony are called **nonchord tones**. These include passing tones and neighbor tones, which connect or embellish stable pitches. Laitz notes that the preferred generic term for all melodic embellishments is **tones of figuration**, since some embellishments (like chordal leaps) are consonant members of the underlying harmony (Ch. 7, p. 163).

# Prerequisites
- **Consonance and dissonance** -- must understand what makes a pitch stable or unstable
- **Triads** -- must know chord membership to identify non-members

# Key Properties
1. Nonchord tones are pitches not belonging to the prevailing harmony
2. They create tension that resolves to chord tones
3. They typically move by step to their resolution
4. Their function depends on the underlying harmonic context

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Establish the underlying harmony
2. Insert stepwise pitches between chord tones (passing) or around a chord tone (neighbor)

## To Recognize:
1. Identify the underlying harmony for each beat or group of beats
2. Determine which pitches are chord members and which are not
3. Label non-members by their melodic behavior (passing, neighbor, etc.)

# Context & Application
Nonchord tones are fundamental to melodic construction. Nearly every melody contains them. Identifying the underlying harmony is essential before labeling any pitch as a nonchord tone, since the same pitch may be a chord tone in one harmonic context and a nonchord tone in another.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 163): In a descending C major scale over a sustained C bass, B, A, F, and D are nonchord tones (not members of the C major triad); they function as passing tones connecting the stable pitches C, E, and G.

**Example 2** (p. 165): In Vivaldi's Sonata in C minor, F4 in the oboe melody is a nonchord tone (dissonant neighbor) over C-minor harmony, but Eb5 and C5 become nonchord tones in m. 3 when the harmony changes to dominant.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Consonance and dissonance** -- nonchord tones create dissonance against the harmony

## Enables
- **Tones of figuration** -- broader category including both consonant and dissonant embellishments
- **Passing tone** -- specific type of nonchord tone
- **Neighbor tone** -- specific type of nonchord tone

## Related
- **Contextual analysis** -- determining NCT status requires harmonic context

## Contrasts With
- **Chordal leap and arpeggiation** -- these are consonant embellishments that are chord members

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Labeling pitches as nonchord tones without first identifying the underlying harmony
  **Correction**: Always determine the prevailing harmony before classifying pitches

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: All embellishing tones are nonchord tones
  **Clarification**: Chordal leaps and arpeggiations are embellishments that ARE members of the chord; use the broader term "tones of figuration" for all melodic embellishments
