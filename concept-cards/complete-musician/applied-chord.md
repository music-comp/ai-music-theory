# Quick Definition
An applied chord is a chord that temporarily functions as the dominant of a diatonic chord other than the tonic, creating a brief tonicization through chromatic alteration.

# Core Definition
An applied chord (also called a secondary dominant or applied dominant) is a major triad or dominant seventh chord that functions as V or V7 of a diatonic harmony other than I. It introduces chromatic alteration--typically a raised pitch acting as a temporary leading tone--that creates a momentary sense of a new key. Any major or minor diatonic triad can be preceded by its applied dominant. The label "V/X" indicates "the dominant of X" (Laitz, Ch. 24, p. 538).

# Prerequisites
- **Dominant Function** -- understanding how V resolves to I provides the model for all applied chord behavior
- **Triads and Seventh Chords** -- applied chords are major triads or dominant seventh chords
- **Chromatic Alteration** -- the process by which diatonic pitches are replaced by chromatic ones

# Key Properties
1. Must be a major triad (V) or dominant seventh chord (V7) to function as an applied dominant
2. Contains a temporary leading tone (chromatically raised pitch) that resolves upward to the root of the target chord
3. The seventh (if present) resolves downward
4. Remains subordinate to the diatonic framework--the tonicized chord retains its original function in the key
5. Cannot tonicize diminished triads (vii° in major, ii° in minor)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Identify the target diatonic chord
2. Build a major triad or dominant seventh whose root lies a perfect fifth above (or perfect fourth below) the target chord's root
3. Add any necessary chromatic alterations (sharps/flats)

## To Recognize:
1. Look for chromatically raised notes--these are often temporary leading tones (the third of the applied chord)
2. Look for chromatically lowered notes--these may be the seventh of the applied chord
3. The applied chord will resolve to a consonant diatonic triad

# Context & Application
Applied chords enrich and enliven harmonic progressions by intensifying motion toward structural harmonies. They are voice-leading tools that stretch out diatonic functions; for example, a tonicization of ii expands the pre-dominant area with two chords rather than one. Applied chords commonly appear on metrically weak beats, precipitating motion toward a stronger-beat goal.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 538): In C minor, V/V is a D-major chord functioning as the dominant of G major (V). The C# acts as a temporary leading tone to G.

**Example 2** (p. 540): Beethoven, String Quartet op. 59 no. 3, Menuetto--every applied chord appears on a metrically weak beat in this parallel interrupted period.

## Related
- **Applied Leading-Tone Chord** -- vii° chords functioning as dominant substitutes for non-tonic chords
- **Cross Relation** -- potential voice-leading issue when chromatic preparation occurs in different voices

## Contrasts With
- **Modal Mixture** -- mixture borrows from the parallel mode and functions as pre-dominant; applied chords function as dominants
- **Modulation** -- modulation establishes a new key with cadences; applied chords create only fleeting tonicizations

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Doubling the temporary leading tone or the chordal seventh
  **Correction**: Never double tendency tones; the temporary leading tone resolves up, the seventh resolves down
- **Error**: Creating V/vii° in major or V/ii° in minor
  **Correction**: Diminished triads cannot be tonicized because they are dissonant

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Treating an applied chord as a modulation
  **Clarification**: Applied chords create brief tonicizations within a phrase, not modulations; the home key remains primary
