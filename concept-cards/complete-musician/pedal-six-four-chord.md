# Quick Definition
A pedal six-four chord arises when two upper voices move as neighbors (or passing tones) above a sustained bass, creating an apparent second-inversion triad that merely embellishes the controlling harmony.

# Core Definition
A **pedal six-four chord** (Ped 6/4) arises when the bass holds steady while two upper voices ascend by step as neighbors (or passing tones) above it, creating an apparent second-inversion triad. This six-four chord is not a true harmonic entity but rather a byproduct of upper-voice motion. It most commonly prolongs tonic (I-IV6/4-I, where IV6/4 is the pedal six-four) or dominant (V-I6/4-V). At the first level, label as IV6/4; at the second level, label as Ped 6/4 or I 5/3-6/4-5/3 (Laitz, Ch. 14, pp. 325-327).

# Prerequisites
- **Pedal point** -- shares the concept of sustained bass
- **Neighbor tone** -- upper voices create neighbor-tone motion

# Key Properties
1. Bass sustains while upper voices create neighbor motion
2. Occurs on metrically weak beats or weak measures
3. Can prolong tonic (IV6/4 over ^1) or dominant (I6/4 over ^5)
4. Not a true harmonic chord -- byproduct of upper-voice motion
5. Upper voices may use passing motion as well as neighboring

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 325): Gruber's "Stille Nacht" opens with tonic prolonged by a pedal six-four chord: bass holds Bb while upper voices neighbor up and return.
