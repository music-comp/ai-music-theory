# Quick Definition
The reciprocal process occurs when the listener loses tonal grounding because conflicting tonal implications make a chord function simultaneously as both a tonic and a dominant of another key.

# Core Definition
The reciprocal process occurs when the listener loses tonal grounding because of conflicting tonal implications that confuse the three harmonic functions (T, PD, and D). Typically, the tonic is treated as an applied chord of iv, so that what appears to be a tonic closure actually sounds like a dominant preparation for another key. Composers such as Schumann and Brahms were fond of this technique (Laitz, Ch. 34).

# Prerequisites
- **Plagal Relation** -- the harmonic context that enables the reciprocal process
- **Tonal Ambiguity** -- the aesthetic goal

# Key Properties
1. Tonic sounds simultaneously like V of iv
2. Conflicting T and D functions
3. Listener cannot determine whether the piece has ended on tonic or dominant
4. Often involves Picardy third or major tonic functioning as V7/iv

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 791): Chopin, "Revolutionary" Etude, op. 10, no. 12 -- Final section treats the major tonic as V7/iv, making the ending ambiguous.

**Example 2** (p. 791): Schumann, "Auf einer Burg," op. 39, no. 7 -- E major looks like tonic but sounds like V of A minor (iv), linking to the next song.
