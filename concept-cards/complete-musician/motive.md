# Quick Definition
The smallest formal unit of musical organization -- a short recurring pitch and/or rhythmic pattern that provides substance, logic, coherence, and dramatic energy to a composition.

# Core Definition
A motive (from Latin *motus*, "motion") is the smallest formal unit of musical organization. It is a short pattern (one beat in slow tempo to one or more measures in fast tempo) composed of characteristic pitch contours and/or rhythmic profiles. Motives must be audible, possessing a clear identity and profile. They are typically given a prominent position at the beginning of a work. Most pieces contain more than one motive. Two types exist: **independent motives** (self-standing, generating much of the piece, often set off by rests) and **thematic motives** (components of longer themes that work together). (Laitz, Ch. 16, p. 364)

# Prerequisites
- **Pitch contour** — Motives are defined partly by their characteristic pitch shapes
- **Rhythm** — Motives carry distinctive rhythmic profiles

# Key Properties
1. Smallest formal unit of organization
2. Must be audible and possess a clear identity
3. Prominently placed, usually at or near the beginning
4. Can be pitch-based, rhythm-based, or both
5. Multiple motives in a theme must relate to one another (shared contour, length, or intervals) for coherence
6. Motives combine to form phrase members, which form phrases, which form periods and larger structures

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Create a short, distinctive pitch-and-rhythm pattern
2. Ensure it has a clear profile that can be recognized when repeated or varied
3. Place it prominently at the opening of the work or section

## To Recognize:
1. Identify the smallest recurring pitch/rhythm patterns at the opening
2. Distinguish motives (distinctive, set in relief) from figures (undifferentiated, background)
3. Track how the motive recurs -- literally or transformed -- throughout the piece

# Context & Application
Motives are building blocks of all larger musical structures. Composers must maintain the delicate balance between repetition and variation of motives on one hand, and introduction of new material on the other. Wagner used motives to represent characters and events (Leitmotiven). The hierarchy runs: motive -> phrase member -> phrase -> period -> larger forms.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 366): Beethoven, String Quartet in F major, op. 18 no. 1 -- a self-standing four-beat independent motive (turn figure followed by falling skip) set off by rests.

**Example 2** (p. 367): Beethoven, Symphony no. 6 ("Pastorale") -- three thematic motives (X, Y, Z) combine as components, each occupying one measure, sharing length, contour, and underlying third-intervals.

**Example 3** (p. 365): Bach, Prelude in C major, WTC Book I -- the recurring broken-chord pattern is a **figure**, not a motive, because it is undifferentiated background.

# Relationships
## Builds Upon
- **Pitch and rhythm** — Motives are the first level of organized pitch-rhythm patterns

## Enables
- **Motivic transformations** — Motives are the raw material for augmentation, diminution, inversion, etc.
- **Motivic parallelism** — Recurring motives at different structural levels create parallelism
- **Fragmentation** — Motives can be broken into sub-components for development

## Related
- **Linkage** — Connecting the end of one phrase to the beginning of the next via shared motivic material
- **Sequence** — Repeated transposition of a motive (melodic sequence)

## Contrasts With
- **Figure** — A figure is an undifferentiated recurring pattern (like a broken-chord accompaniment) that becomes background; a motive is a distinctive event set in relief

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Identifying every recurring pattern as a motive
  **Correction**: Only distinctive patterns set in relief against other material qualify; background figurations are figures, not motives

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: A motive must be an entire theme
  **Clarification**: Motives are the building blocks from which themes are constructed; a theme typically contains multiple motives
