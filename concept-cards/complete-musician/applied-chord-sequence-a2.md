# Quick Definition
The A2 (-3/+4) applied-chord sequence converts the ascending 5-6 sequence into a chromatic form by raising each first-inversion bass note a half step, creating a harmonized chromatic passing tone as a temporary leading tone.

# Core Definition
Of the two A2 sequences, A2 (-3/+4) is much more likely to incorporate applied chords. The ascending 5-6 sequence with first-inversion triads may be converted into an applied-chord sequence simply by raising the bass a half step. This creates a powerful harmonized chromatic passing tone that functions as the temporary leading tone, leading to the upcoming root-position triad. The chromatic passing motion may appear in the bass (with first-inversion applied chords) or in the soprano (with root-position applied chords) (Laitz, Ch. 24, p. 555).

# Prerequisites
- **Applied Chord** -- the chords added to the sequence

# Key Properties
1. Based on the ascending 5-6 sequence
2. Raising the bass a half step transforms the first-inversion chord into an applied dominant
3. The seventh can be added to the applied chords
4. Root-position variant places chromatic passing motion in the soprano

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Begin with an ascending 5-6 sequence
2. Raise each first-inversion bass note a half step to create applied chords
3. Optionally add sevenths

## To Recognize:
1. Ascending chromatic bass line alternating with diatonic root-position chords
2. Each chromatic bass note acts as a temporary leading tone

# Context & Application
This sequence appears in Leclair's Sonata in C major for Flute and Continuo (Example 24.16), where applied 6/5 chords alternate with diatonic triads in a rising pattern.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 551): Leclair, Sonata in C major for Flute and Continuo, Corrente -- applied 6/5 chords alternate with diatonic triads in an A2 (-3/+4) pattern.
