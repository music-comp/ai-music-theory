# Quick Definition
A triad is in root position when the root is in the bass (5/3); first inversion when the third is in the bass (6/3); second inversion when the fifth is in the bass (6/4).

# Core Definition
A triad is in **root position** if the root is the lowest-sounding pitch (bass). When the third appears in the bass, the chord is in **first inversion** (figured bass: 6/3, abbreviated 6). When the fifth is in the bass, the chord is in **second inversion** (figured bass: 6/4). Only the bass note determines the inversion; the arrangement of pitches above the bass does not matter.

# Prerequisites
- **Triads** -- inversion rearranges the triad members

# Key Properties
1. Root position (5/3): most stable; root in bass
2. First inversion (6/3 or just 6): less stable; third in bass; bound by sixths (imperfect consonances)
3. Second inversion (6/4): least stable; fifth in bass; P4 with bass creates dissonance
4. The root is the generating pitch; the bass is the lowest-sounding pitch -- they may differ
5. Root-position and first-inversion triads are relatively interchangeable
6. Second-inversion triads are restricted to specific contexts due to the dissonant P4
7. Diminished triads are actually more consonant in first inversion (no tritone with the bass)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Determine Inversion:
1. Identify the bass note
2. Stack remaining pitches in thirds to find the root
3. If bass = root: root position; bass = third: first inversion; bass = fifth: second inversion

# Context & Application
Understanding inversion is essential for figured bass, harmonic analysis, and voice leading. Inversions provide variety in bass lines while maintaining harmonic function.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Confusing root with bass.
  **Correction**: The root is the generating pitch; the bass is the lowest-sounding pitch. In the sonority E-G-C, E is the bass and C is the root (first inversion of C major).
