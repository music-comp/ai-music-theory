# Quick Definition
Tendency tones are scale degrees with strong melodic pulls toward an adjacent pitch (usually a half step away), most importantly the leading tone (^7), which tends to resolve upward to ^1.

# Core Definition
Some scale members have strong melodic pulls toward another member lying usually a half step away. These are called **tendency tones**. The most important tendency tone is the **leading tone** (^7), which should ascend by step to ^1, except when part of the descending line ^1-^7-^6-^5. Other tendency tones include ^4 (which tends toward ^3) and raised ^6 in minor (which tends toward ^7). In the V7 chord, all four members (^5, ^7, ^2, ^4) function as tendency tones. Tendency tones should not be doubled in voice leading because resolving both correctly would create parallel octaves.

# Prerequisites
- **Scale Degrees** -- tendency tones are specific scale degrees

# Key Properties
1. ^7 (leading tone): ascends to ^1 (must resolve upward in outer voices)
2. ^7 may fall to ^5 in an inner voice to create a complete tonic triad
3. Exception: ^7 may descend in the line ^1-^7-^6-^5
4. ^4 (chordal seventh of V7): must descend to ^3 in any voice
5. ^2 is unique: can resolve in either direction (to ^1 or ^3)
6. In minor, raised ^6 and ^7 create ascending tendency; lowered forms create descending tendency
7. Never double tendency tones
8. The augmented second between ^6 and ^7 in harmonic minor should be avoided in melody

# Context & Application
Understanding tendency tones is essential for melody writing, voice leading, and harmonic resolution. Tendency tones are the engine of tonal motion. Their resolution creates the fundamental voice-leading connections between dominant and tonic. Unresolved tendency tones create tension; resolved tendency tones provide satisfaction and closure.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 90): In Mozart's Sonata in A minor, K. 310 (Example 3.21), G# (^7) resolves to A (^1) at the cadence.
**Example 2**: In V7 in C major (G-B-D-F), B (^7) rises to C, F (^4) falls to E, D (^2) moves to C or E.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Doubling the leading tone (^7).
  **Correction**: Never double ^7; resolving both correctly produces parallel octaves.

- **Error**: Resolving the chordal seventh (^4) upward.
  **Correction**: ^4 must always descend by step to ^3.
