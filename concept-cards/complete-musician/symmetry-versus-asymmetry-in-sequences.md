# Quick Definition
Diatonic sequences are inherently asymmetrical (varying chord qualities and interval sizes) and goal-directed, while chromatic sequences are symmetrical (consistent chord quality and intervals) and tonally ambiguous.

# Core Definition
The fundamental distinction between diatonic and chromatic sequences lies in their relationship to the diatonic scale's asymmetry. Diatonic sequences conform to the scale, resulting in varying chord qualities (major, minor, diminished) and varying interval sizes between repetitions. This asymmetry creates goal-directed motion, as the tritone within the scale breaks the pattern and pulls toward resolution. Chromatic sequences maintain consistent chord quality and exact intervallic distances, dividing the octave into equal parts. This symmetry eliminates the tritone-based pull toward a tonal goal, creating ambiguity. To restore tonal focus, chromatic sequences must break off (usually at the pre-dominant) and rejoin functional harmonic syntax (Laitz, Ch. 36).

# Key Properties
1. Diatonic: varying chord qualities, varying intervals, goal-directed
2. Chromatic: consistent chord quality, exact intervals, ambiguous
3. Tritone in diatonic sequences creates directionality
4. Absence of tritone in chromatic sequences removes direction
5. Chromatic sequences must break off to rejoin tonal syntax

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 830): D3 (-4/+2) in G major: diatonic version has varying qualities (G major, E minor, C major) and reaches ii; chromatic D2 (-P4/+m3) has all major triads and ends on bIII.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Accidentals make a sequence chromatic
  **Clarification**: Applied-chord sequences contain accidentals but are diatonic because their main structural chords are diatonic. True chromatic sequences have chromatically altered main chords.
