# Quick Definition
The spectrum of ways a motive can recur, ranging from exact (literal) repetition through modified repetition to transposition (tonal or real), each maintaining recognizability while introducing variety.

# Core Definition
Motivic repetition spans a continuum from exact restatement to highly varied transformation. The principal types are: (1) **exact repetition** -- the motive returns identically; (2) **modified repetition** -- the motive returns with alterations to pitch, rhythm, or both while maintaining its essential identity; and (3) **transposition** -- the motive is restated at a different pitch level, either **tonal** (maintaining the diatonic intervals of the prevailing key, so interval quality may change) or **real** (maintaining exact interval sizes, potentially leaving the key). (Laitz, Ch. 16, p. 369)

# Prerequisites
- **Motive** — Must understand what constitutes a motive before studying its repetition types

# Key Properties
1. Exact repetition provides maximum recognizability but minimum variety
2. Modified repetition balances recognizability with variety
3. Tonal transposition stays within the key but may alter interval quality (e.g., major 2nd becomes minor 2nd)
4. Real transposition preserves exact interval sizes but may leave the key
5. All types must maintain enough of the motive's identity for the listener to recognize the connection

# Construction / Recognition
## To Recognize:
1. Identify the original motive statement
2. Compare subsequent appearances: Are pitch and rhythm identical (exact)?
3. If not identical, are the changes minor enough to preserve identity (modified)?
4. If at a different pitch level, does it follow diatonic intervals (tonal) or chromatic/exact intervals (real)?

# Context & Application
Composers use the full spectrum of repetition types throughout a work. Exact repetition is common at phrase openings; tonal transposition is typical in sequential passages; real transposition is often used for dramatic effect or to modulate. Modified repetition is the most flexible and ubiquitous type.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 369): Tonal transposition -- a motive beginning on ^1 with a major 2nd is restated beginning on ^2 with a minor 2nd, preserving diatonic scale membership.

**Example 2** (p. 369): Real transposition -- the same motive is restated preserving exact interval sizes, potentially introducing accidentals.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Confusing tonal and real transposition
  **Correction**: Tonal transposition preserves scale membership; real transposition preserves exact interval sizes

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Modified repetition means the motive is unrecognizable
  **Clarification**: Modified repetition preserves the motive's essential identity while altering secondary features
