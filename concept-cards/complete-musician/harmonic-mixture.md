# Quick Definition
Harmonic mixture occurs when the root of a chord is shifted (lowered), creating bVI or bIII -- major triads built on lowered scale degrees that require additional mixture (b3 or b7) to remain consonant.

# Core Definition
When b6 appears as the root of a chord (rather than as a third or fifth), the root has been shifted down, creating harmonic mixture. The lowered root alone would create a dissonant augmented triad, so additional mixture is applied: bVI requires both b6 and b3; bIII requires both b3 and b7. Both continue to function as their diatonic counterparts (submediant and mediant). The generic "b" symbol indicates harmonic mixture even when a natural (not flat) sign is used in notation (Laitz, Ch. 27, pp. 620-622).

- **Modal Mixture** -- the overarching concept

# Key Properties
1. Root of the chord is lowered (b6 for bVI, b3 for bIII)
2. Additional scale degrees must also be lowered for consonance
3. bVI: participates in descending arpeggiations, descending-fifth motions, deceptive motions, and as PD
4. bIII: divides the fifth I-V into two thirds, bridges T and PD, leads to V43
5. bVI lies a half step above V, making it an effective dramatic upper neighbor

# Context & Application
bVI is particularly versatile: it can participate in descending arpeggiations, descending-fifth motions, precede V as PD, and substitute for tonic in deceptive motions (V-bVI). Its half-step proximity to V makes it a powerful dramatic neighbor.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 621): bVI in C major (Ab-C-Eb) participates in descending arpeggiations and as a deceptive resolution of V.
