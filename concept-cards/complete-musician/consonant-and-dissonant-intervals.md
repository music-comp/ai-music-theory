# Quick Definition
Consonant intervals (stable) include perfect consonances (P1, P5, P8) and imperfect consonances (M3, m3, M6, m6); dissonant intervals (unstable) include seconds, sevenths, the P4 in two voices, and all augmented and diminished intervals.

# Core Definition
Stable intervals are called **consonant**; unstable intervals are called **dissonant**. Consonances are further divided: **perfect consonances** (P1, P5, P8) are the most stable; **imperfect consonances** (M3, m3, M6, m6) are moderately stable yet more fluid, central to moving music forward. **Dissonant intervals** include all seconds, sevenths, the perfect fourth (in two-voice contexts), and all augmented and diminished intervals. The P4 is usually dissonant when sounding above the bass.

# Key Properties
1. **Perfect consonances**: P1, P5, P8 -- most stable
2. **Imperfect consonances**: M3, m3, M6, m6 -- moderately stable, propulsive
3. **Dissonances**: all 2nds, all 7ths, P4 (usually), all A and d intervals
4. Consonance and dissonance exist on a continuum but are classified into two categories
5. Musical context can affect the perception of stability (Example 3.18 shows P4 in different contexts)
6. Inversionally related pairs share similar stability levels (m2 and M7 are both very dissonant)

# Construction / Recognition
## To Classify:
1. Identify the specific interval
2. Check: is it P1, P5, P8? = perfect consonance
3. Is it M3, m3, M6, m6? = imperfect consonance
4. Is it a 2nd, 4th, 7th, or any A/d interval? = dissonance

# Context & Application
Composers create motion by circulating through consonances and dissonances. The interplay of tension (dissonance) and resolution (consonance) is the fundamental engine of tonal music.
