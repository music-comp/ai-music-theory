# Quick Definition
The chord built on ^6, a minor triad in major keys (vi) and major triad in minor keys (VI), providing color contrast and functioning as a bridge between tonic and pre-dominant, as a tonic substitute, or as a pre-dominant itself.

# Core Definition
The submediant harmony (vi in major, VI in minor) provides color contrast to the prevailing key quality. It functions in four main contexts tied to the three basic root motions: (1) as a **bridge** in descending-thirds progressions (I-vi-IV); (2) as the start of a **descending-fifths** progression (vi-ii-V); (3) as a **tonic substitute** in the deceptive motion V-vi; (4) as the **pre-dominant** itself (vi-V). The submediant shares two common tones with I (^1 and ^3), making it a natural extension of tonic. At the second level, vi is usually analyzed under "T" with an arrowhead showing its bridging function. (Laitz, Ch. 18, pp. 425-432)

# Prerequisites
- **Phrase model** — Must understand T-PD-D-T to see where vi fits
- **Root motions** — vi's functions arise from descending 5ths, ascending 2nds, and descending 3rds

# Key Properties
1. Shares two common tones with I (^1 and ^3)
2. Minor triad in major, major triad in minor -- provides color contrast
3. Four functional contexts: bridge, circle-of-fifths, tonic substitute, pre-dominant
4. In V-vi, the leading tone may descend to ^6 (major) or must rise to ^1 (minor, to avoid augmented 2nd)
5. When V-vi occurs, the vi chord often has a doubled third (^1)

# Construction / Recognition
## Voice Leading for I-vi:
1. Keep both common tones (^1 and ^3)
2. The moving voice steps up

## Voice Leading for V-vi:
1. In major: soprano ^2-^1 or ^7-^1; if ^7-^1, double the third of vi
2. In minor: ^7 must ascend to ^1 (always); double the third of VI

# Context & Application
The submediant appears in rock progressions (I-vi-IV-V, the "doo-wop" progression), art music from all periods, and functions as a dramatic device in deceptive cadences.

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 427): The I-vi-IV progression (descending harmonic arpeggiation) characterizes 1950s rock and roll.

**Example 2** (p. 428): Beethoven, Violin Sonata no. 5 ("Spring") -- vi appears twice: first in an EPM (descending fifths) and then in the structural cadence.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: In minor, moving ^7 down to ^6 in V-VI (creates augmented 2nd)
  **Correction**: In minor, ^7 must always ascend to ^1
