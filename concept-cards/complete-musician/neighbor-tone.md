# Quick Definition
A neighbor tone steps away from a chord tone and returns to it, embellishing a single pitch rather than connecting two different pitches.

# Core Definition
A **neighbor tone** (also called **auxiliary tone** or simply **neighbor**) is a nonchord tone that moves by step above or below a chord tone and then returns to the original pitch. Upper neighbors (UN) lie above the chord tone; lower neighbors (LN) lie below. Because it embellishes a single pitch, the neighbor-tone figure is more static than the passing tone, which moves to a new pitch a third away (Laitz, Ch. 7, p. 163).

# Prerequisites
- **Nonchord tones** -- neighbor tones are a specific type

# Key Properties
1. Steps away from a chord tone by a second (up or down) and returns
2. Can be consonant or dissonant depending on the interval formed with the bass
3. More static than passing tones -- decorates rather than connects
4. Upper neighbors are more common than lower neighbors
5. The 5-6 technique (consonant neighbor between P5 and m6/M6) has significant compositional ramifications

# Construction / Recognition
## To Construct:
1. Start on a chord tone
2. Move up or down by step to a non-chord tone
3. Return by step to the original chord tone

## To Recognize:
1. Identify a three-note stepwise figure that returns to its starting pitch
2. The middle note is the neighbor tone
3. Determine if it is an upper or lower neighbor

# Context & Application
Neighbor tones create local ornamentation and interest without advancing the melodic line. They appear at all structural levels, from surface embellishment to large-scale prolongation (as neighboring chords).

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 163): In Example 7.4, F is a dissonant neighbor (P4 above the bass C) that resolves down to E (M3). A is a consonant neighbor (M6 above C) that resolves down to G (P5).

**Example 2** (p. 163): A double neighbor results from combining upper and lower neighbors in succession around a single chord tone.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Confusing neighbor tones with passing tones
  **Correction**: Neighbors return to their starting pitch; passing tones continue in one direction

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: All neighbor tones are dissonant
  **Clarification**: Consonant neighbors exist (e.g., the 5-6 motion, where both the fifth and sixth are consonant above the bass)
