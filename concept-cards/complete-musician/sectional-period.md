# Quick Definition
A period in which both phrases end on tonic (both have authentic cadences), but the second cadence is stronger than the first (e.g., IAC followed by PAC), maintaining the antecedent-consequent relationship.

# Core Definition
A sectional period has both phrases closing on tonic, but the second cadence is stronger than the first. Typically the antecedent ends with an IAC (weaker authentic cadence) and the consequent ends with a PAC (stronger). Even though both are tonic-closing, the melodic incompleteness of the antecedent (e.g., soprano ending on ^3 or ^5 instead of ^1) demands continuation. (Laitz, Ch. 20, pp. 471-472)

# Key Properties
1. Both phrases end on tonic
2. Second cadence is stronger (IAC then PAC)
3. Melodic incompleteness drives the antecedent-consequent relationship
4. Can be parallel or contrasting

# Examples
**Example 1** (p. 471): Mozart, Piano Sonata in B-flat major, K. 281 -- PSP where both phrases close on tonic but the second is stronger.

- **Confusion**: If both phrases end on tonic, there is no period
  **Clarification**: Sectional periods maintain the antecedent-consequent relationship through relative cadence strength
