# Quick Definition
The blending of chords by thirds with chords by fourths, achieved by featuring the inverted fifth (= fourth) within tertian chords to prepare quartal entry, or by highlighting thirds within quartal voicings to prepare tertian return.

# Core Definition
Quartal harmony can be blended with tertian. The interval of the fourth is given a prominent position in chords by thirds to allow chords by fourths to enter; the fifth contained in the triad is inverted to allow for oncoming fourths. In seventh chords, the inverted fifth from the root or third may be used to introduce quartal harmony. The characteristic interval of one kind of harmony is contained in any other kind and may be brought to the surface by intervallic inversion.

A fugue may be written over a cantus firmus, a hymn placed under a tone row, and quartal harmony blended with tertian -- these examples of harmonic synthesis demonstrate that acceptance of one procedure does not mean exclusion of others.

# Key Properties
1. Triad's fifth inverts to fourth, preparing quartal entrance
2. Seventh chord's fifth from root or third inverts for quartal introduction
3. Quartal voicings contain thirds that prepare tertian return
4. Both directions (tertian-to-quartal and quartal-to-tertian) are viable
5. The shared interval (inverted fifth = fourth) is the bridge
6. Best introduced well in advance of the texture change

# Construction / Recognition
- In tertian chords, voice the fifth prominently to suggest the coming fourth
- Invert the fifth (placing it as a fourth) in the voicing
- Seventh chords: use the fifth from root or third as the bridge interval
- In quartal chords, voice thirds prominently to prepare tertian return
- The transition is bidirectional and reversible

# Context & Application
Tertian-quartal integration is one of the most common texture combinations in twentieth-century music. The fifth/fourth inversional relationship makes the transition natural: every tertian chord contains fourths, and every quartal chord contains thirds. The key is voicing these shared intervals prominently before making the texture change. The technique applies equally to blended passages (both textures simultaneously) and successive passages (one following the other).

# Common Errors
- Making the transition without voicing preparation
- Treating tertian and quartal as incompatible textures
- Overlooking the fifth/fourth inversional bridge

# Common Confusions
- **Always connected**: Tertian and quartal textures share intervals and are always potentially integrated
- **Voicing is key**: The same chord can sound tertian or quartal depending on voicing; this is the basis of the transition
- **Bidirectional**: The technique works equally well from tertian to quartal and from quartal to tertian
