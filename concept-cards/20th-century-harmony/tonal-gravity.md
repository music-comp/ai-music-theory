# Quick Definition
The attractive force that pulls musical elements toward a tonal center, which can be strengthened, weakened, or eliminated through various compositional techniques.

# Formal Definition
There are many degrees of key-center gravity or of keylessness in the general area of tonality or atonality. The gravity of a tonal center is decreased by moving a new voice through notes that give an important chord new roots. Key feeling may be gradually loosened or tightened. At one extreme of the concept of key is tonality, the other extreme is atonality, and the point at which one ends and the other begins is indefinite.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Gravity spectrum:
- **Strong gravity**: Firm tonal center, clear pull toward tonic
- **Weakened gravity**: Loosened key feeling, wandering tonality
- **Absent gravity**: Atonality, no tonal pull

Methods to decrease gravity:
- Voice movement giving chord new roots
- Quick key shifts preventing establishment
- Equidistant harmony avoiding root definition
- Chromatic saturation equalizing pitch importance

Methods to increase gravity:
- Tritone-embracing scale steps
- Tonal pillar reinforcement
- Pedal points
- Cadential formulas

# Musical Context/Application
The composer can manipulate tonal gravity throughout a work, creating passages of firm tonality, ambiguous wandering, or complete keylessness. Music may hold its tonality loosely, with elements of atonality inherent from the onset. Melodic lines in a tonal framework can become so free that the implied harmony becomes evasively dissonant and key feeling is lost. Partial atonality is useful in vague introductions and transitions.

# Common Confusions
- Tonal gravity exists on a spectrum, not as binary present/absent
- The tonality/atonality boundary is indefinite
- Gravity can be manipulated locally within larger tonal contexts
