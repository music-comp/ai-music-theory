# Quick Definition

Harmony built from stacked seconds (or their inversions, sevenths), one of three fundamental categories of intervallic chord construction alongside tertian (thirds/sixths) and quartal (fourths/fifths).

# Core Definition

"There are three categories of intervallic materials from which chords may be built: thirds or sixths (tertian), fourths or fifths (quartal), and seconds or sevenths (secundal). The addition of seconds to chords by thirds or fourths, or the filling-in of the mixed intervals of compound chords for coloristic purposes, does not produce chords by seconds; these are added-note chords. But out of context such chords may be called by either name" (Persichetti, p. 120).

# Prerequisites

- **Interval** — Secundal harmony is defined by its constituent interval (the second) and requires understanding interval classification
- **Consonance-dissonance** — The graded tension of secundal types from mild to sharp dissonance is central to the system
- **Quartal harmony** — Understanding the parallel quartal system clarifies what distinguishes secundal from other non-tertian approaches

# Key Properties

1. Built from major seconds (M2) and minor seconds (m2) as structural intervals
2. The augmented second is excluded because it is tertian in effect (enharmonic to minor third)
3. Inversional equivalence: M2 inverts to m7, m2 inverts to M7
4. Chords by seconds may be rearranged in sevenths, paralleling how tertian chords rearrange in sixths and quartal chords in fifths
5. Context determines whether a given sonority is classified as secundal or added-note
6. Close-position secundal chords cramp easily and become percussive in low registers
7. Secundal harmony encompasses both true chords by seconds and clusters (which lack defined inner voice movement)

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct Secundal Harmony
1. Select major and/or minor seconds as structural intervals
2. Stack seconds to desired chord size (3, 4, 5 or more notes)
3. Choose close position (seconds) or open position (sevenths/ninths)
4. Ensure the context distinguishes the harmony from added-note chords

## To Recognize Secundal Harmony
1. Identify whether seconds are the primary structural interval
2. Verify the sonority is not an added-note modification of a tertian or quartal chord
3. Check context: surrounded by other secundal harmonies confirms identity
4. Note that out of context, the same sonority may be classified either way

# Context & Application

Secundal harmony represents the most dissonant of the three systematic chord-construction approaches. It emerged prominently in early 20th-century music and became associated with modernist and avant-garde styles, ranging from mild dissonance (major-second stacks) to extreme dissonance (chromatic clusters).

Secundal harmony provides maximum harmonic density, distinctive "crunchy" texture, strong coloristic effects, and an alternative to tertian/quartal organization. The technique spans from three-note chords through multi-note chords to clusters, each with distinct handling requirements.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Adding seconds to a tertian chord and calling it secundal harmony
  **Correction**: The addition of seconds to chords by thirds or fourths produces added-note chords, not secundal harmony (p. 120)
- **Error**: Using augmented seconds in secundal construction
  **Correction**: The augmented second is tertian in effect and is excluded from secundal chord building

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Believing that any chord containing seconds is automatically secundal
  **Clarification**: Only chords where seconds serve as the primary structural interval are secundal; context determines whether seconds function structurally or as added notes
- **Confusion**: Thinking secundal harmony and clusters are the same thing
  **Clarification**: Clusters are a specific subset of secundal harmony characterized by close position and lack of defined inner voice movement; true chords by seconds have distinct voice-leading possibilities
