# Quick Definition
Harmony built from stacked seconds (or their inversions, sevenths), one of the three fundamental categories of intervallic chord construction alongside tertian (thirds/sixths) and quartal (fourths/fifths) harmony.

# Formal Definition
There are three categories of intervallic materials from which chords may be built: thirds or sixths (tertian), fourths or fifths (quartal), and seconds or sevenths (secundal). The addition of seconds to chords by thirds or fourths, or the filling-in of mixed intervals for coloristic purposes, does not produce chords by seconds; these are added-note chords. True secundal harmony uses seconds as the primary structural interval.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Intervallic categories:
- **Tertian**: Built from 3rds/6ths
- **Quartal**: Built from 4ths/5ths
- **Secundal**: Built from 2nds/7ths

Interval types in secundal harmony:
- Major second (M2) = whole step
- Minor second (m2) = half step
- Note: Augmented second is tertian in effect (enharmonic to m3)

Inversional equivalence:
- M2 inverts to m7
- m2 inverts to M7
- Chords by seconds may be arranged in sevenths (inverted form)

# Musical Context/Application
Secundal harmony represents the most dissonant of the three systematic approaches to chord construction. It emerged prominently in early 20th-century music and became associated with modernist and avant-garde styles. The technique ranges from mild dissonance (major second stacks) to extreme dissonance (minor second clusters).

Secundal harmony provides:
- Maximum harmonic density
- Distinctive "crunchy" texture
- Strong coloristic effects
- Alternative to tertian/quartal organization

# Common Confusions
- Adding seconds to tertian/quartal chords creates added-note chords, not secundal harmony
- The augmented second sounds tertian (like m3) and is excluded from secundal construction
- Out of context, the same sonority may be called either added-note or secundal
- Secundal and added-note categories can overlap; context determines classification
