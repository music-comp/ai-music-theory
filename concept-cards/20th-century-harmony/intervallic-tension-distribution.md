# Quick Definition

The deliberate arrangement of intervals within a chord according to their tension characteristics, placing consonant and dissonant intervals in specific vertical regions.

# Formal Definition

**Intervallic tension distribution** refers to the compositional practice of organizing intervals within compound chords and other complex harmonies according to their relative consonance or dissonance. Intervals are distributed to produce specific shapes and assembled to create various consonant or dissonant areas in the bottom, middle, or top portions of the chord, thereby controlling the overall harmonic character and stability of the sonority.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation

Intervals may be classified by tension:
- **Open consonances**: Perfect fifth, octave
- **Soft consonances**: Major/minor thirds, major/minor sixths
- **Mild dissonances**: Major second, minor seventh
- **Sharp dissonances**: Minor second, major seventh, tritone

Distribution strategies:
- **Upper soft/lower open**: Soft consonant and mild dissonant intervals at top; open consonant and sharp dissonant at bottom
- **Upper sharp/lower open**: Sharp dissonances placed high with anchoring fifths or tenths below for tension and brilliance
- **Uniform distribution**: Similar tension levels throughout for consistent harmonic color

# Musical Context/Application

Effective tension distribution creates:
- Chords with stable bottom and brilliant top
- Sonorities with percussive attack when smaller intervals are placed low
- Balanced harmonic textures where tension can shift in any direction
- Clear differentiation between harmonic arrival points and transitional moments

The polyinterval texture enabled by varied tension distribution allows compound harmony to progress smoothly by managing the fluctuating density between highest and lowest voices and the shifting degrees of intervallic tension.

# Common Confusions

- **Tension vs. instability**: A dissonant chord in context may be stable if it matches the surrounding harmonic norm; tension distribution concerns intervallic quality, not functional resolution.
- **Fixed vs. contextual placement**: While certain distributions are generally effective (sharp dissonances high with consonant anchoring below), the optimal arrangement depends on musical context and intended effect.
- **Distribution vs. inversion**: Tension distribution involves planning where intervals of different types appear; inversion changes which note is in the bass but doesn't necessarily reorganize tension relationships.
