# Quick Definition
An arrangement of the seven diatonic modes from "darkest" (locrian) to "brightest" (lydian) based on accidental count, with dorian as the central norm.

# Core Definition
As working materials for composition, the modes may be arranged according to their tension relationships. The greatest number of flats that can be applied to a modal scale on a particular tone produces the "darkest" mode (locrian). Subtracting flats and then adding sharps in diatonic signature order produces an arrangement from darkest to brightest. The dorian mode is the middle point and sets the norm. Within this related order, a flexible set of modes is at the composer's disposal, and definite control of these scale formations with their inherent qualities is possible.

# Key Properties
1. **Darkest to brightest** (on any tonic):
   - Locrian (most flats): diminished 5th, minor 2nd, 3rd, 6th, 7th
   - Phrygian: minor 2nd, 3rd, 6th, 7th
   - Aeolian: minor 3rd, 6th, 7th
   - **Dorian (center/norm)**: minor 3rd, 7th; major 6th
   - Mixolydian: minor 7th; major 3rd, 6th
   - Ionian: all major (no alterations from major scale)
   - Lydian (brightest): raised 4th
2. **Dorian as center**: self-reflective mode, midpoint of the spectrum
3. **Compositional tool**: systematic movement through brightness for expressive effect

# Construction / Recognition
1. On any tonic, note how many flats or sharps each mode requires
2. Arrange from most flats (locrian) to most sharps (lydian)
3. Identify the current mode's position in the spectrum
4. Plan modal movement toward darker or brighter modes for expressive contour

# Context & Application
A melody with a crescendo in register, dynamics, rhythm, and modal materials can move systematically through the brightness spectrum. Shifting modes are effective on a stationary key center, though much melodic reference must be made to the tonic to stay within each mode. The spectrum provides a compositional tool for controlled movement through modal colors, independent of traditional major/minor associations.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking of modes as having fixed "moods" without understanding their systematic relationship. **Correction**: The brightness spectrum provides a rational ordering that enables controlled color transitions.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Equating "bright" with "happy" and "dark" with "sad." **Clarification**: Brightness refers to the sharpness/flatness quality, not emotional content; a "dark" mode can be serene and a "bright" mode can be tense.
