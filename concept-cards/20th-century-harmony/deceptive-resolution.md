# Quick Definition
A cadential progression moving to a chord whose root relation forms an interval not characteristic of the prevailing cyclical relationship, creating temporary rest on a harmonic slant that implies continuance.

# Core Definition
**Deceptive cadences** are temporary cadences that rest briefly on a harmonic slant, creating a need for harmonic, rhythmic, and melodic continuance. They imply motion from one chord to another whose root relation to the first forms an interval not characteristic of the prevailing chordal cycle. In fifth relationship, the chord of weaker impulse moves to a chord whose root lies a second above its own root. In third relationship, root movement is up a fifth. In second relationship, root movement is up a third. Deceptive progressions may also move to a chord outside the established modal or key realm, or use chromatic root movement. Other cadences are created by obliteration of all voices but one, or obliteration of all voices save a percussion note of indefinite pitch (Ch. 9, pp. 206-207).

# Prerequisites
- **Cadential devices** -- understanding how cadences function

# Key Properties
1. In fifth cycle: deception = root moves up a second (instead of expected fifth)
2. In third cycle: deception = root moves up a fifth (instead of expected third)
3. In second cycle: deception = root moves up a third (instead of expected second)
4. Movement outside established modal/key realm also creates deception
5. Chromatic root movement creates deceptive effect
6. Obliteration of voices to one, or to indefinite-pitch percussion
7. Purpose: rest briefly on harmonic slant, implying need for continuance

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the prevailing cyclical relationship
- Note when the expected root-interval movement is replaced by a different interval
- Classify the substituted interval (second, fifth, or third depending on context)
- Check for movement outside the established tonal realm

# Context & Application
Deceptive resolutions create temporary rest on a harmonic slant, freshness within established harmonic contexts, phrase extension through avoided cadence, and surprise through thwarted expectations. The technique is particularly effective when strong expectation has been established, when continuation is desired rather than closure, and when variety is needed within predictable harmonic motion.

# Common Errors
- Applying the same deceptive interval in all cyclical contexts
- Creating deception without first establishing clear expectations
- Confusing random unexpected chords with true deceptive cadences

# Common Confusions
- **Deceptive vs. merely unexpected**: True deception requires established expectation based on context or convention
- **Deceptive vs. half cadence**: A deceptive cadence substitutes for an expected resolution; a half cadence intentionally stops on dominant
- **Cycle-specific deception**: What constitutes deception depends on the prevailing cyclical relationship
