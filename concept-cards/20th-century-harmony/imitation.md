# Quick Definition
A form of repetition in which motivic elements move from voice to voice, creating textural and contrapuntal relationships.

# Formal Definition
Imitation is a form of repetition in which motivic elements move from voice to voice. The imitation may be literal or it may occur in any of the forms of extension variation (inversion, augmentation, diminution, etc.). Imitation is by nature a linear process and a valuable device in achieving meaningful part-writing and harmonic suppleness. The strictness of imitation is no measure of aesthetic evaluation, nor is free imitation necessarily a sign of inventiveness.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Imitation types and characteristics:
- **Literal imitation**: Exact reproduction in different voice
- **Imitation at interval**: Reproduction at fixed interval distance
- **Inverted imitation**: Mirror image of original
- **Augmented imitation**: Longer note values
- **Diminished imitation**: Shorter note values
- **Retrograde imitation**: Reversed order

Real canons at intervals other than the octave can imply polytonality when each voice operates in its own tonal area.

# Musical Context/Application
The success of imitative devices in a harmonic context depends largely upon the needs of the expressive climate in which they operate. In twentieth-century harmony, imitation at dissonant intervals can create polytonal textures. Real canons at intervals other than the octave can produce horizontal polytonality. Imitation serves both structural and coloristic purposes, creating textural interest while providing formal coherence.

# Examples
Harmony containing extensive imitation:
- Bela Bartok, String Quartet No. 5, p. 27 (Boosey)
- Boris Blacher, Studie im Pianissimo (orchestra), p. 28 (Bote)
- Roy Harris, Symphony No. 3, pp. 85-91 (G. Schirmer)
- Paul Hindemith, Symphonie Mathis der Maler, pp. 16-20 (Schott)
- Arnold Schoenberg, Verklarte Nacht (string orchestra), pp. 4-5 (Associated)
- Anton Webern, Symphonie Op. 21, p. 8 (Universal)
