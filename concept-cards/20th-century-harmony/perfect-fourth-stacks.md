# Quick Definition
Chords built exclusively from perfect fourths, which have a pentatonic flavor and are tonally ambiguous since any chord member can function as the root due to the equidistant intervallic structure.

# Formal Definition
Three-, four-, and five-note chords by perfect fourths have a pentatonic flavor. The five-note form contains all the steps of the diatonic pentatonic scale. Chords by perfect fourths are ambiguous in that, like all chords built by equidistant intervals (diminished seventh chords or augmented triads), any member can function as the root. The indifference of this rootless harmony to tonality places the burden of key verification upon the voice with the most active melodic line.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
**Construction:**
- 3-note: P4 + P4 (spans m7)
- 4-note: P4 + P4 + P4 (spans m10)
- 5-note: P4 + P4 + P4 + P4 (spans M13) = pentatonic collection

**Pentatonic Correspondence:**
5 perfect fourths stacked = C-F-Bb-Eb-Ab = all notes of a pentatonic scale

**Root Ambiguity:**
Like diminished seventh and augmented triads, equidistant structure means:
- No single pitch predominates as root
- Any note can serve as root
- Melodic emphasis determines perceived tonic

**Chromatic Flexibility:**
A succession of chords by perfect fourths does not fall within the intervallic make-up of any one scale. If the chords are to be made to fit a scale pattern, miscellaneous fourths (including augmented fourths) must be used. Chords by perfect fourths are therefore chromatically more supple than mixed-fourth chords.

# Musical Context/Application
The tonal ambiguity of perfect fourth stacks makes them valuable for:
1. Creating harmonic suspense without strong tonal pull
2. Supporting melodic writing that defines tonality
3. Achieving chromatic flexibility not possible with diatonic chords
4. Polytonal implications (scale tones duplicated at different levels)

Clarity is difficult to achieve in low registers; these chords flow more easily in upper voices - woodwinds or women's voices.

# Common Confusions
Students accustomed to root-based analysis may struggle with the rootlessness of perfect fourth stacks. Unlike tertian chords where the root is acoustically reinforced, perfect fourth stacks have no inherent root preference. The "root" emerges from melodic context, not from the chord itself.
