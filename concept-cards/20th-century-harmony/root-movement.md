# Quick Definition
The intervallic relationship between successive chord roots, providing directional strength independent of bass location or chord inversion.

# Core Definition
**Root movement** describes the intervallic distance and direction traveled by the conceptual root from one chord to the next. In root progression, inverted or otherwise, there are two directional factors: the action of the root and the location of the bass. The root direction of I-V, I-III, and I-II is up regardless of inversion or voice placement. The root direction of I-IV, I-VI, and I-VII is down. The uninvertible tritone is indefinite as to direction. Progressions based upon the perfect fifth between roots have strength; those based upon the third, softness; upon the second, blandness; and upon the tritone, ambiguity. These four interval types together subsume all twelve tones (Ch. 9, pp. 183-184).

# Prerequisites
- **Harmonic progression** -- understanding goal-directed chord succession

# Key Properties
1. Root movement by perfect fifth creates strength
2. Root movement by third creates softness
3. Root movement by second creates blandness
4. Root movement by tritone creates ambiguity
5. Root direction is conceptual, not dependent on bass position or voicing
6. I-V, I-III, I-II = root direction UP; I-IV, I-VI, I-VII = root direction DOWN
7. The tritone is uninvertible and indefinite as to direction
8. These four intervals subsume all twelve chromatic tones

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the root of each chord (independent of bass note)
- Determine the interval class between successive roots
- Classify the root movement quality: strength (5th), softness (3rd), blandness (2nd), ambiguity (tritone)
- Note direction: intervals from tonic upward to V, III, II are upward root motion; to IV, VI, VII are downward

# Context & Application
In twentieth-century tonal music, root distance between important chords of a phrase or cadence is usually determined by the cycle upon which the music is constructed. Music in cyclical fifth relationship is governed by a series of perfect fifths encompassing twelve different tones. In third relationship the chromatic compass is created by alternating major and minor thirds. In cyclical second relationship a series of major and minor seconds encircles the twelve tones. Understanding root movement helps the composer control harmonic strength and directional clarity, create variety through different interval relationships, and manage tonal ambiguity.

# Common Errors
- Assuming root direction matches the physical direction of the bass voice
- Treating all root-movement intervals as equivalent in harmonic effect
- Confusing the uninvertibility of the tritone with other symmetric intervals

# Common Confusions
- **Root vs. bass**: The root determines harmonic identity; the bass determines voicing -- they may be different notes
- **Root direction vs. registral direction**: Root direction is conceptual (based on interval class); registral direction is physical (actual pitch movement)
- **Tritone ambiguity**: The tritone divides the octave equally, making its directional implication genuinely ambiguous
