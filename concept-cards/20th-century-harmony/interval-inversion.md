# Quick Definition
The process of placing the lower note of an interval above the upper note (or vice versa), which changes the interval's consonant-dissonant quality due to altered spacing and register.

# Formal Definition
When intervals are inverted, their consonant-dissonant quality changes because the spacing and register have been altered. The extent of change varies with the individual interval. This transformation affects both the interval's size (inversions sum to nine) and its functional character within a harmonic context.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
- Interval + inversion = 9 (e.g., third + sixth = 9)
- Perfect intervals invert to perfect intervals
- Major intervals invert to minor intervals (and vice versa)
- Augmented intervals invert to diminished intervals (and vice versa)
- Tritone inverts to tritone (same span, different register)

Specific changes:
- Perfect fifth becomes unstable perfect fourth
- Minor second opens into wide-spreading major seventh (loses sting)
- Tritone causes pronounced register shift without distance change

# Musical Context/Application
Inverting the perfect fifth alters its basic function, transforming this solid interval into an unstable perfect fourth. When sharp dissonances are inverted, marked changes in intensity occur. The cutting minor second opens into a wide-spreading major seventh and thereby loses some of its sting. Composers use inversion to create varied textures and tension levels with related intervallic materials.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes assume inverted intervals have the same harmonic function as their original forms. Inversion fundamentally changes an interval's character, not just its size. The perfect fifth and fourth demonstrate this most clearly.
