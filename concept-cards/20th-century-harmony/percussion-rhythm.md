# Quick Definition
Rhythmic forces generated by percussion instruments or vocal sounds of indeterminate pitch, which may coincide with or oppose melodic and harmonic rhythms in various ways.

# Core Definition
Rhythmic forces may sprout from the **percussion line** of instruments or vocal sounds of indeterminate pitches. The melodic and harmonic rhythms may coincide or oppose the percussion line in various ways, creating a dimension of rhythmic counterpoint between pitched and unpitched elements (Ch. 10, pp. 215-216).

# Prerequisites
- **Meter and rhythm** -- understanding rhythmic organization

# Key Properties
1. Percussion rhythms operate independently from pitched rhythms
2. May coincide with, or oppose, melodic rhythm
3. May coincide with, or oppose, harmonic rhythm
4. Creates rhythmic counterpoint between pitched and unpitched elements
5. Various partial alignments and full independence are possible

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the percussion line and its rhythmic pattern
- Compare it with the melodic and harmonic rhythms
- Classify the relationship: coinciding, opposing, or independent

# Context & Application
Percussion rhythm adds a rhythmic dimension independent of pitch, creating layered textures. The interaction with melodic and harmonic rhythms allows for complex rhythmic counterpoint.

# Examples
**Example** (Ch. 10, Ex. 10-10, p. 215): Melodic and percussive rhythm opposing the harmonic rhythm.

Source material: Chavez, Toccata for Percussion Instruments; Holst, The Planets, p. 1; Revueltas, Sensemaya, p. 25; Varese, Density 21.5.

# Common Errors
- Treating percussion rhythm as always subordinate to pitched rhythms
- Not maintaining internal coherence of the percussion pattern

# Common Confusions
- **Percussion vs. percussive harmony**: Percussion rhythm uses unpitched instruments; percussive harmony treats pitched chords percussively
