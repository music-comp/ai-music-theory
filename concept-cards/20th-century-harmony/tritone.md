# Quick Definition
The augmented fourth or diminished fifth interval that divides the octave exactly in half, possessing an ambiguous quality that can sound either neutral or restless depending on context.

# Formal Definition
The tritone (augmented fourth or diminished fifth) is the interval that divides the octave at its halfway point, consisting of three whole tones or six semitones. It is the least stable of the intervals, sounding primarily neutral in chromatic passages and restless in diatonic passages. Its quality is determined solely by context.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
- Six semitones (three whole tones)
- Frequency ratio: approximately 45:32 (augmented fourth) or 64:45 (diminished fifth)
- Divides the octave exactly in half
- Inverts to itself (augmented fourth inverts to diminished fifth with same span)
- When spaced beyond an octave: becomes more ambiguous and veiled in chromatic contexts; less addicted to resolution in diatonic contexts

# Musical Context/Application
The tritone's classification depends entirely on musical context. In chromatic passages it sounds primarily neutral; in diatonic passages it sounds restless. Inverting the tritone causes a pronounced shift in register even though no change in intervallic distance has taken place. Chords containing a tritone tend to have a restless quality, while those without tritones have stability even when extremely dissonant.

# Common Confusions
Students often assume the tritone is always dissonant and unstable. In twentieth-century chromatic contexts, it can function as a neutral interval. The tritone's behavior depends heavily on whether the surrounding material is diatonic or chromatic.
