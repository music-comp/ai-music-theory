# Quick Definition
The vertical distribution of intervals within a chord or between voices, which significantly affects the consonant-dissonant quality, clarity, and character of harmonic structures.

# Formal Definition
When intervals are spaced more than an octave apart, their qualities transform: soft consonances (thirds and sixths) become richer; open consonances (octaves and fifths) and the consonant perfect fourth become stronger; dissonances (seconds and sevenths) become less biting yet more brilliant; the tritone becomes more ambiguous and veiled.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Effects of wide spacing (beyond one octave):
- Soft consonances (3rds, 6ths): become richer
- Open consonances (5ths, 8ves) and perfect 4th: become stronger
- Dissonances (2nds, 7ths): less biting, more brilliant
- Tritone: more ambiguous/veiled (chromatic), less resolution-driven (diatonic)

Spacing principles:
- Wide intervals at bottom: ordinary balance
- Wide intervals at top: tautness
- Even distribution: consistent tension
- Overtone-series model: wide spacing in lower register, close in upper

# Musical Context/Application
Spacing is an inseparable part of the character of a tonal structure. For ordinary balance, wide intervals are placed at the bottom of the chord; for tautness, wide intervals are placed at the top. Chordal structures are most resonant when the distances between members are similar to those in the overtone series. Closely spaced harmony crowded with small intervals clears when each chord is broken into a succession of intervals in two-part writing.

# Common Confusions
Students may focus only on which notes are in a chord without considering their spacing. In twentieth-century practice, the same pitch content can produce dramatically different effects depending on how the intervals are distributed vertically.
