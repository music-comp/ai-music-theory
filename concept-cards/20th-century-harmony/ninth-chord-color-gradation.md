# Quick Definition
A systematic ordering of the twelve ninth chord types from darkest to brightest, enabling composers to control harmonic color with precision and to create progressions based on gradual shifts in chord brightness.

# Core Definition
A workable order of the twelve ninth chords from darkest to brightest is material for a composer's craft. When color gradations of the ninth formations are made part of the composer's aural apparatus, textural control of the various ninth chords becomes possible. Ninths used with technical facility add freshness to harmonic progression. Certain chords in the gradation may be interchanged because they occupy similar positions on the color spectrum. Since any note may be considered as any position in twelve types, a single tone may be harmonized with sixty different ninths, enabling systematic color control across a melody.

- **Ninth chords**: The twelve distinct types and their construction

# Key Properties
1. Twelve ninth chords arranged from darkest to brightest
2. Darkest chords have more minor intervals (especially minor ninths)
3. Brightest chords have more major intervals (especially major ninths)
4. Adjacent chords in the gradation may be interchangeable
5. Single tone = sixty different ninth-chord possibilities
6. Color gradation enables systematic textural control
7. Effective in sequential patterns where color shifts can be organized

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify a ninth chord's position on the dark-to-bright spectrum by its intervallic content
- More minor thirds and minor ninths = darker
- More major thirds and major ninths = brighter
- Adjacent positions on the spectrum can be interchanged for variety
- Five minor-minor ninth chords on a single top-voice note (e.g., E) demonstrate the range of positions available

# Context & Application
Understanding ninth chord color gradation enables composers to create smooth progressions by moving to adjacent color positions, dramatic contrasts by jumping between distant positions, and consistent harmonic color across a melody by selecting from the sixty available harmonizations for each note. The technique is particularly useful in sequential passages where color shifts can be systematically organized.

# Common Errors
- Confusing chord quality (major, minor) with chord color (bright, dark) -- color considers total intervallic content
- Ignoring the gradation when selecting ninths, missing opportunities for systematic color control

# Common Confusions
- **Quality vs. color**: A chord with major quality can still be relatively dark if it contains other darkening intervals
- **Interchangeability**: Adjacent chords on the spectrum can be swapped without disrupting the color flow
