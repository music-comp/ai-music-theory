# Quick Definition
The compositional process of integrating different types of harmonic textures within a single work through vertical combination or successive juxtaposition.

# Formal Definition
Contemporary harmonic writing is often a composite process which may involve varying placement of the norm of dissonance, choice of a single harmonic idiom or the coalition of one with another, fusion of tonalities, singleness of sound organization or the juxtaposition of tonal and atonal aspects. The amalgamation of divergent conceptions of tonal formations is part of our harmonic language. Acceptance of one procedure does not necessarily mean the exclusion of others.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Texture combination methods:
- **Vertical combination**: Produces polychordal and compound formations
- **Successive combination**: Movement from one texture type to another
- **Heterogeneous materials**: Different harmonic types functioning side by side

Examples of texture integration:
- Fugue over cantus firmus
- Hymn under tone row
- Quartal harmony blended with tertian

Transitional techniques:
- Feature interval that, when inverted, produces characteristic interval of new harmony
- Bring characteristic interval of new harmony to surface through intervallic inversion
- Use equidistant chords to weave one fabric into another

# Musical Context/Application
The vertical combination of harmonic textures produces polychordal and compound formations, but successive combinations entail movement from one kind of texture to another and the functioning of heterogeneous harmonic materials side by side. A textural problem arises from the manipulation of transitional materials that lead from one type of harmony to another. Modern composition embraces the entire range of harmonic resources.

# Examples
Passages containing various kinds of harmonic textures:
- Alban Berg, Violin Concerto (red.), pp. 47-48 (Universal)
- Paul Hindemith, Six Chansons (II) (Associated)
- Arthur Honegger, Piano Concertino, pp. 1-4 (Senart)
- Charles Ives, Violin Sonata No. 4, p. 8 (Arrow)
- Michael Tippett, String Quartet No. 3, p. 1 (Schott)

Works containing contrasting techniques:
- Bela Bartok, Piano Concerto No. 2 (Boosey)
- Charles Ives, Piano Sonata No. 2 (Concord) (Arrow)
- Vincent Persichetti, Quintet for Piano and Strings (Elkan-Vogel)

# Common Confusions
- Texture combination is not random; it requires careful transitional handling
- Acceptance of one texture does not exclude others
- Both vertical and successive combinations are valid approaches
