# Quick Definition

A polychord of two major triads, the most consonant and resonant polychord type, with twelve combinations ordered by decreasing consonance following the cycle of fifths from the bottom six-four chord's bass note.

# Core Definition

"When it is constructed of major triads it is most consonant and resonant. A complete inventory of major-major polychords may be made by building the upper major triads on the notes of a cycle of perfect fifths that begins with the bass note of the bottom six-four chordal unit. The triads are added, in turn, to the bottom six-four chord, creating a rising order, based upon the cycle of fifths, which places the chords in a natural sequence of decreasing consonance and increasing dissonance. The last six polychords are noticeably less resonant; the sound of number 11 is almost a dull thud" (Persichetti, p. 137).

# Key Properties

1. Both units are major triads (most resonant combination)
2. Twelve combinations ordered by cycle of fifths from bottom unit bass note
3. Bottom unit in six-four (second inversion) for maximum resonance
4. Numbers 1-6 are more resonant; numbers 7-12 noticeably less so
5. Number 11 is "almost a dull thud"
6. Numbers 1 and 12 may form single tertian chords out of context but function polychordally in polychordal environment

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct Major-Major Polychords
1. Place a major triad in second inversion (six-four) as bottom unit
2. Build upper major triads on successive notes of the cycle of fifths from the bass note
3. Select the combination that provides the desired resonance level

# Context & Application

Major-major polychords are the most frequently used type for stable, tonic-like polychordal sonorities. The cycle-of-fifths ordering provides a systematic approach to selecting upper triads for specific resonance qualities. Even less resonant combinations have valid musical uses for specific dramatic or coloristic purposes.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming numbers 1 and 12 always function as polychords
  **Correction**: They form single tertian chords out of context; polychordal environment is needed
- **Error**: Avoiding less resonant combinations entirely
  **Correction**: All twelve have musical uses; less resonant types serve specific dramatic purposes

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: The ordering reflects brightness or darkness
  **Clarification**: It reflects resonance based on overtone relationships, not modal color
- **Confusion**: Only the most resonant combinations are usable
  **Clarification**: The "dull thud" of number 11 is itself a valid musical resource
