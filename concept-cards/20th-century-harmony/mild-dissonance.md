# Quick Definition
Major seconds and minor sevenths, which produce a moderately tense sound positioned between soft consonance and sharp dissonance on the tension spectrum.

# Core Definition
Mild dissonances are the major second and minor seventh intervals, occupying the middle ground between consonance and sharp dissonance. The major second (partial 9, approximately 9:8 ratio) provides moderate tension without the cutting intensity of the minor second. Like sharp dissonances, mild dissonances become less biting yet more brilliant when spaced beyond the octave. They are frequently used in twentieth-century chord construction (ninths, elevenths) without overwhelming other harmonic colors.

# Key Properties
1. **Major second**: 9:8 ratio -- moderate tension
2. **Minor seventh**: 16:9 ratio -- spread, moderate tension
3. **Middle position** on the tension spectrum between soft consonance and sharp dissonance
4. **Compound spacing**: less biting, more brilliant (same behavior as sharp dissonances)
5. **Versatility**: frequently used in extended chords without overpowering

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify intervals of 2 semitones (M2) or 10 semitones (m7)
2. Note the moderate tension -- neither warm nor cutting
3. These intervals create graduated tension transitions between consonance and sharp dissonance

# Context & Application
Mild dissonances allow composers to create subtle tension gradations. They are the workhorses of extended tertian harmony (ninths, elevenths) where they add color without the extremity of sharp dissonances. Their intermediate position makes them essential for graduated tension patterns -- moving from consonance through mild dissonance to sharp dissonance or the reverse.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Failing to distinguish mild from sharp dissonances in analysis. **Correction**: The distinction is crucial for understanding graduated tension patterns and harmonic direction.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Treating all dissonances as equivalent. **Clarification**: Mild dissonances have a fundamentally different character from sharp dissonances -- less biting, more versatile in extended harmonies.
