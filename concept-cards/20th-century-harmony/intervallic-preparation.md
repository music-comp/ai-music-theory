# Quick Definition
The technique of featuring an interval within the current harmonic texture that, when inverted, produces the characteristic interval of the target texture, providing a smooth bridge between different harmonic types.

# Core Definition
The characteristic interval of one kind of harmony is contained in any other kind of harmony and may be brought to the surface by intervallic inversion. In all textural changes, an interval may be featured that, when inverted, produces the characteristic interval of the new harmony. The interval of the fourth is given a prominent position in chords by thirds to allow chords by fourths to enter; the fifth contained in the triad is inverted to allow for oncoming fourths. In seventh chords, the inverted fifth from the root or third may be used to introduce quartal harmony. The voices of an inverted seventh chord may be arranged so that the interval of the second is emphasized and the contextual way prepared for secundal harmony. In ninth chords, the interval of the seventh from the root or third is inverted to suggest the secundal texture of clusters or added-note chords.

# Prerequisites
- **Textural transition**: The broader transitional technique

# Key Properties
1. Tertian to quartal: feature inverted fifth (= fourth) in chords by thirds
2. Seventh chords to quartal: inverted fifth from root or third
3. To secundal: emphasize seconds in seventh-chord voicing
4. Ninth chords to clusters: invert seventh from root or third (= second)
5. The characteristic interval is always already present, just hidden
6. Best introduced well in advance of the actual texture change

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the target texture's characteristic interval (3rds for tertian, 4ths for quartal, 2nds for secundal)
- Find that interval (or its inversion) within current chords
- Revoice or emphasize to bring the target interval to the surface
- Begin the preparation well before the actual change

# Context & Application
This is the primary mechanism for smooth textural transitions. By surfacing the target interval within the current texture, the ear is gradually prepared for the change. The inversional relationship between intervals means every texture contains the seeds of every other: thirds invert to sixths, fourths to fifths, seconds to sevenths. The specific voicing of chords determines which hidden intervals become audible.

# Examples

- Ex. 13-1c: Second emphasized in inverted seventh chord voicing, preparing secundal

# Common Errors
- Failing to begin preparation in advance
- Choosing voicings that obscure rather than reveal the target interval
- Making the preparation too obvious (should be organic)

# Common Confusions
- **Inversion, not substitution**: The technique highlights an interval already present in the chord, not inserting a foreign element
- **Any texture to any**: Because all intervals are contained in all chord types, this technique works for any directional transition
- **Voicing as preparation**: The choice of voicing is itself a preparatory act
