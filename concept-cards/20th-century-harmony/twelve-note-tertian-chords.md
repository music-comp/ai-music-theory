# Quick Definition
Chords containing all twelve chromatic tones arranged in superimposed thirds, with extremely limited harmonic functions (punctuation, sustained tension, contrast with thin textures) and a tendency to transform into other chord types when voices move in contrary motion.

# Core Definition
Mixed thirds may be superimposed until all twelve tones are present. Twelve-note chords containing twelve different member tones are so complex and thick that special attention must be given to register and instrumentation. This is an extremely limited species of harmony operating in a confined area; its harmonic functions are few. The thick texture lightens when the chord is placed in the upper register or when consonant portions are played by separate orchestral choirs. Parallel movement provides temporary suppleness, but contrary motion changes distances between voices, transforming the twelve-note tertian chord into chords by fourths, seconds, polychords, heterogeneous compound chords, or mirror chords.

# Prerequisites
- **Seventeenth chords**: Near-maximum tertian extension

# Key Properties
1. All twelve chromatic pitch classes present, arranged in thirds
2. Three primary functions: punctuation, quiet sustained tension, contrast with thin textures
3. Wide register span; members normally spaced in thirds
4. Parallel motion provides temporary suppleness
5. Contrary motion transforms into other chord types (quartal, secundal, polychord, mirror)
6. Texture lightens in upper register or with orchestral choir separation
7. Special effects, not normative harmonic vocabulary

# Construction / Recognition
- All twelve pitch classes arranged by superimposed mixed thirds
- Very wide register span
- Identify the limited functional contexts: accent, atmosphere, density contrast
- Watch for transformation under contrary motion

# Context & Application
Twelve-note tertian chords are extreme sonorities used sparingly. They serve as punctuating accents, atmospheric sustained-tension chords, or as maximum-density answers to unison or two-part writing. Their confined functional range makes them special-effect sonorities. The transformative property under contrary motion is a compositional resource: a twelve-note tertian chord can evolve into quartal, secundal, polychordal, or mirror structures through voice movement.

# Common Errors
- Attempting to use twelve-note chords in normal harmonic progressions (too dense for this purpose)
- Ignoring register and orchestration requirements

# Common Confusions
- **Functional limitation**: These are special-effect sonorities with only three functions, not workable harmonic vocabulary
- **Tertian vs. quartal twelve-note chords**: Same pitch content but different voicing creates different character -- the quartal version retains open, ambiguous quality
