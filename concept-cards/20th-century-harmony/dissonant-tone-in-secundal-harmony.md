# Quick Definition

The restless tone within a secundal chord that often asks for resolution, whose location varies by chord position and type, requiring conjunct approach and departure when it forms part of a sharp dissonant interval.

# Core Definition

"A dissonant tone is a restless tone that often asks for resolution. In fundamental position and in second inversion of secundal harmony of any intervallic type the dissonant tone is the second of the chord. In the first inversion it is the third of the chord, except in the minor-major first inversion, where the root is most dissonant" (Persichetti, p. 122). "In the first inversion of the major-major the root becomes the dissonant tone if it lies below the third" (p. 123).

# Key Properties

1. Fundamental position (all types): dissonant tone = the second of the chord (middle note)
2. Second inversion (all types): dissonant tone = the second of the chord
3. First inversion (general rule): dissonant tone = the third of the chord (top note)
4. First inversion exception (m-M): the root is most dissonant
5. First inversion exception (M-M): root becomes dissonant if it lies below the third
6. If the dissonant tone forms a sharp interval (m2 or M7): approach and leave conjunctly or by repetition
7. If the dissonant tone does not form a sharp interval: approach and leave freely

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify the Dissonant Tone
1. Determine the chord position (fundamental, first inversion, second inversion)
2. In fundamental or second inversion: the middle note (second of chord) is dissonant
3. In first inversion: the top note (third of chord) is dissonant, except m-M (root is dissonant) and M-M when root lies below third (root is dissonant)

## Voice-Leading Rules
1. Check whether the dissonant tone is part of a sharp dissonant interval (m2 or M7)
2. If yes: approach and depart by step or repetition (one member of the sharp interval must follow this rule)
3. If no: approach and depart freely

# Context & Application

Understanding the dissonant tone's behavior is essential for smooth voice-leading in secundal harmony. Unlike added-note chords where added tones have no resolution tendency, the dissonant tone in true secundal harmony behaves more like traditional dissonance, creating directional impetus. This enables smooth melodic lines, controlled tension and release, proper voice-leading in complex passages, and avoidance of awkward leaps.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Approaching the dissonant tone by leap when it forms a sharp interval
  **Correction**: When the dissonant tone participates in m2 or M7, approach and leave by step or repetition
- **Error**: Assuming the dissonant tone is always the same chord member regardless of inversion
  **Correction**: The dissonant tone shifts with each inversion; always re-identify it

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: "Second" and "third" refer to interval names
  **Clarification**: They refer to chord members in secundal terminology (1st note = root, 2nd note = second, 3rd note = third)
- **Confusion**: "Sharp dissonant interval" means any dissonance
  **Clarification**: It specifically means minor second or major seventh; other dissonances do not trigger the conjunct voice-leading rule
