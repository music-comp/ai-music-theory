# Quick Definition
The restless tone within a secundal chord that often asks for resolution, whose location varies by chord position and type, requiring specific voice-leading treatment when it forms part of a sharp dissonant interval.

# Formal Definition
A dissonant tone is a restless tone that often asks for resolution. In fundamental position and in second inversion of secundal harmony of any intervallic type, the dissonant tone is the second of the chord. In the first inversion it is the third of the chord, except in the minor-major first inversion where the root is most dissonant. In the first inversion of the major-major, the root becomes the dissonant tone if it lies below the third.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Dissonant tone location by position:

| Position | General Rule | Exception |
|----------|--------------|-----------|
| Fundamental | Second of chord | - |
| First Inversion | Third of chord | m-M: root is most dissonant; M-M: root if below third |
| Second Inversion | Second of chord | - |

Voice-leading rules:
- If dissonant tone is member of sharp interval (m2, M7):
  - Approach conjunctly or by repetition
  - Leave conjunctly or by repetition
  - One member of the sharp interval should follow this rule

- If dissonant tone is not member of sharp interval:
  - Approach and leave freely
  - No special treatment required

# Musical Context/Application
Understanding the dissonant tone's behavior is essential for smooth voice-leading in secundal harmony. Unlike added-note chords where added tones have no resolution tendency, the dissonant tone in true secundal harmony behaves more like traditional dissonance, creating directional impetus.

This knowledge enables:
- Smooth melodic lines in secundal texture
- Control of tension and release
- Proper voice-leading in complex secundal passages
- Avoidance of awkward melodic leaps

# Common Confusions
- The "second" and "third" refer to chord members in secundal terminology, not intervals
- The dissonant tone is not always the most chromatic pitch
- The exception in minor-major first inversion is crucial for proper voice-leading
- "Sharp dissonant interval" specifically means m2 or M7, not general dissonance
