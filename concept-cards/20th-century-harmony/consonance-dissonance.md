# Quick Definition
The relative degrees of tension or stability between intervals, which in twentieth-century music are treated as a spectrum of colors rather than a strict binary opposition.

# Formal Definition
Consonance and dissonance describe the textural characteristics of intervals as determined by their particular physical properties of sound waves and overtones. In the tempered scale, consonant intervals derive from the lower tones of the overtone series, while dissonant intervals come from upper overtones. However, this concept varies considerably from one age to another and is affected by countless factors within any given style.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Traditional classification derived from the overtone series:
- **Open consonances**: Perfect fifth, octave (partials 2, 3)
- **Soft consonances**: Major/minor thirds, major/minor sixths (partials 4, 5, 6)
- **Sharp dissonances**: Minor seconds, major sevenths (high partials)
- **Mild dissonances**: Major seconds, minor sevenths (intermediate partials)
- **Ambiguous**: Perfect fourth (context-dependent), tritone (neutral or restless)

# Musical Context/Application
In twentieth-century composition, the consonant-dissonant properties of intervals may be used to support or oppose other forces such as instrumental timbre, dynamics, and tempo. Sharp dissonances on double reeds playing loudly create a raucous effect, while the same intervals on muted strings sound introspective. In harmony comprised of strongly dissonant intervals, these dissonances often become the "consonant" norm of the musical organization.

# Examples
- When sharp dissonances are assigned to double reeds playing loudly vs. muted strings, entirely different effects result (Ex. 1-7)
- Dissonant intervals can serve as the consonant norm in a consistently dissonant passage
- Consonant intervals may sound dissonant in a passage dominated by dissonant intervals

# Common Confusions
Students often treat consonance and dissonance as absolute categories. In twentieth-century practice, what functions as "consonant" depends entirely on the surrounding harmonic context. A major triad can sound dissonant if placed within a consistently atonal texture.
