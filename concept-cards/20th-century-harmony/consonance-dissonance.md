# Quick Definition
The relative degrees of tension or stability between intervals, treated in twentieth-century music as a graduated spectrum of colors rather than a strict binary opposition.

# Core Definition
Consonance and dissonance describe the textural characteristics of intervals as determined by their physical properties of sound waves and overtones. In the tempered scale, consonant intervals derive from the lower tones of the overtone series, while dissonant intervals come from upper overtones. Persichetti classifies all twelve intervals into six categories: open consonances (P5, P8), soft consonances (M3, m3, M6, m6), mild dissonances (M2, m7), sharp dissonances (m2, M7), and two ambiguous intervals (P4, tritone). As composers' attitudes change, the concept of consonance and dissonance may change -- various degrees of tension may be accepted as consonance.

# Key Properties
1. **Open consonances**: perfect fifth and octave -- spacious, stable (lowest partials)
2. **Soft consonances**: major/minor thirds and sixths -- warm, blended (partials 4-6)
3. **Mild dissonances**: major seconds and minor sevenths -- moderate tension
4. **Sharp dissonances**: minor seconds and major sevenths -- intense, cutting
5. **Perfect fourth**: consonant in dissonant surroundings, dissonant in consonant surroundings
6. **Tritone**: neutral in chromatic passages, restless in diatonic passages
7. **Contextual relativity**: in strongly dissonant harmony, dissonances become the "consonant" norm

# Construction / Recognition
To classify intervals by tension:
1. Identify the interval by number and quality
2. Place it on the tension spectrum from open consonance to sharp dissonance
3. For P4 and tritone, assess the surrounding musical context
4. Consider how dynamics, timbre, and register modify the perceived tension
5. Note whether surrounding harmony shifts the functional role of the interval

# Context & Application
The consonant-dissonant properties of intervals may support or oppose other musical forces. Sharp dissonances on double reeds playing loudly create a raucous effect; the same intervals on muted strings sound introspective. One gives the impression of tension agreement with other compositional forces, the other disagreement. In harmony comprised of strongly dissonant intervals, these dissonances often become the "consonant" norm of the musical organization.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating consonance and dissonance as fixed, absolute categories. **Correction**: The classification shifts with style, context, and surrounding harmony.
- **Error**: Assuming all dissonances must resolve. **Correction**: In 20th-century practice, dissonances may be sustained as stable sonorities.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Equating "dissonant" with "unpleasant." **Clarification**: Dissonance is a degree of tension that serves expressive purposes; sharp dissonances on muted strings can be deeply introspective.
- **Confusion**: Thinking consonance is always "better." **Clarification**: A major triad can sound dissonant if placed within a consistently atonal texture.
