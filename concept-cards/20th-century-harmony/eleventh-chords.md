# Quick Definition
Six-note tertian chords identified as a combination of two triads a major or minor third apart, adding density but reducing suppleness, with apparent elevenths from pedal tones or ornamentation requiring careful analytical distinction.

# Core Definition
Large tertian chords form only a small portion of the harmonic palette. The multiple tones of eleventh chords add density but reduce suppleness. These six-note formations are cumbersome in harmonic progressions but useful in the general harmonic scheme. An eleventh chord is easily identified by considering the formation a combination of two triads a major or minor third apart. Eleventh sounds are often created by pedal tones or melodic ornamentation of triads and seventh chords; in these instances the apparent eleventh should be analyzed as the smallest basic chord plus ornamental tones. Eleventh chords usually contain two sharp dissonant intervals.

# Prerequisites
- **Ninth chords**: Five-note tertian extension

# Key Properties
1. Six notes built in thirds: R-3-5-7-9-11
2. Analyzed as two triads a major or minor third apart
3. Add density but reduce harmonic suppleness
4. Usually contain two sharp dissonant intervals
5. Omitting one sharp-interval tone enables suppler movement
6. If fourths predominate in spacing: sounds quartal, not tertian
7. If arrangement separates two triadic units: produces a polychord
8. Often apparent (from ornamentation) rather than structural
9. Combine well with seventh and ninth chords

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify as two triads a major or minor third apart
- Distinguish structural from ornamental elevenths (reduce to smallest basic chord)
- Omit tones forming sharp dissonant intervals for suppleness
- Check spacing: fourths predominating = quartal sound; separated triads = polychord
- The intervallic make-up often coincides with scale formations (see scale-chord-relationship)
- Seldom found in extensive part-writing; usually guided by melodic line

# Context & Application
Eleventh chords function best as rich tonic formations around which other structures function, rather than as mobile harmonic units. They combine well with seventh and ninth chords when members are omitted to reduce cumbersomeness. The chords are seldom found in extensive part-writing but excel as harmonic anchor points guided by a melodic line derived from the scale implied by the chord.

# Common Errors
- Identifying every apparent eleventh as a structural eleventh chord (many are ornamented smaller chords)
- Neglecting to reduce apparent elevenths to their basic chord for analysis

# Common Confusions
- **Structural vs. ornamental**: True eleventh chords are structural six-note formations; apparent elevenths from pedal tones or passing tones should be analyzed as smaller chords with ornamentation
- **Eleventh vs. quartal**: If fourths predominate in the spacing, the chord sounds quartal regardless of its theoretical tertian construction
