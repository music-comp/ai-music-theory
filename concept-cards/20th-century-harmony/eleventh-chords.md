# Quick Definition
Six-note tertian chords built by adding another third above the ninth, analyzed as a combination of two triads a major or minor third apart, adding density but reducing suppleness compared to smaller chord formations.

# Formal Definition
Large tertian chords, no matter how many thirds have been added, form only a small portion of the harmonic palette. The multiple tones of eleventh chords add density but reduce suppleness. These six-note formations are cumbersome in harmonic progressions but useful in the general harmonic scheme. An eleventh chord is easily identified by considering the formation a combination of two triads a major or minor third apart.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
**Construction:**
Six notes built in thirds: Root + 3rd + 5th + 7th + 9th + 11th

**Polychordal Analysis:**
An eleventh chord = two triads a major or minor third apart
- Example: C eleventh = C-E-G + D-F-A (triads a second apart when stacked)

**Sharp Dissonances:**
Eleventh chords usually contain two sharp dissonant intervals. Omitting one of the tones that forms a sharp dissonant interval lessens the dissonant content and makes relatively supple harmonic movement possible.

**Spacing Considerations:**
If chord members are arranged so that fourths predominate, the chords sound not as elevenths but as chords by fourths. If the arrangement produces two separate triadic units, a polychord results.

# Musical Context/Application
Eleventh sounds are often created by pedal tones or melodic ornamentation of triads and seventh chords. In these instances, the harmonic background is not one of eleventh chords but a smaller chord with ornamental tones. In analysis, the apparent eleventh should be broken down to the chord with the fewest notes; this is considered the basic chord, preventing ornamental tones from being mistakenly considered part of the chord.

Eleventh chords combine well with seventh and ninth chords when members are omitted to reduce cumbersomeness.

# Common Confusions
Students often identify any chord containing an eleventh above the bass as an "eleventh chord." True eleventh chords are structural six-note formations, not triads or seventh chords with passing or ornamental tones that happen to touch the eleventh. Proper analysis reduces apparent elevenths to their simplest chord form.
