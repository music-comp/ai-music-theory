# Quick Definition
The graduated levels of harmonic stress that intervals possess, which can be organized into patterns of increasing or decreasing tension for compositional effect independently of dynamics.

# Core Definition
Intervallic tension may be used to suit any design or function of the music. Intervals can follow each other in any order and may be arranged to form any pattern of tension interplay. A series of intervals may begin with little tension and end with great tension (tension crescendo), or reverse the pattern (tension diminuendo). Crucially, the consonant-dissonant properties of intervals may support or oppose other forces such as instrumental timbre, dynamics, and tempo -- a tension crescendo need not coincide with a dynamic crescendo.

# Key Properties
1. **Tension hierarchy** (least to most): open consonances, soft consonances, [P4/tritone], mild dissonances, sharp dissonances
2. **Tension crescendo**: moving from low to high tension intervals
3. **Tension diminuendo**: moving from high to low tension intervals
4. **Independence from dynamics**: tension patterns can support or oppose dynamic levels
5. **Independence from timbre**: tension can agree or disagree with instrumental character
6. **P4 and tritone quality** determined solely by context within tension patterns

# Construction / Recognition
1. Map each interval in a passage to its position on the tension hierarchy
2. Identify the overall tension contour: crescendo, diminuendo, arch, or other shape
3. Compare the tension contour to dynamic, rhythmic, and timbral contours
4. Note points of agreement (support) and disagreement (opposition)

# Context & Application
A tension arrangement might sound different in practice depending on dynamics and timbre. Sharp dissonances on double reeds playing loudly create agreement between tension and other forces; the same intervals on muted strings create disagreement, producing an introspective rather than raucous effect. This independence of tension from other parameters is a key compositional resource.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Confusing interval tension with dynamics. **Correction**: Persichetti explicitly separates these; tension patterns can move independently of dynamic levels, and their interaction or opposition creates expressive richness.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking tension patterns must follow a single direction. **Clarification**: Tension can follow any contour -- crescendo, diminuendo, arch, zigzag -- independently of other musical parameters.
