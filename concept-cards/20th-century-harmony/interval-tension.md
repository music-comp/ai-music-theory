# Quick Definition
The relative degree of harmonic instability or stress inherent in an interval, which can be organized into patterns of increasing or decreasing tension for compositional effect.

# Formal Definition
Intervallic tension refers to the graduated levels of harmonic stress that intervals possess, ranging from open consonances (least tension) through soft consonances, mild dissonances, to sharp dissonances (most tension). These tensions may be used to suit any design or function of the music and can be arranged in patterns of crescendo or diminuendo of tension.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Tension hierarchy (least to most):
1. Open consonances (perfect 5th, octave)
2. Soft consonances (major/minor 3rds, 6ths)
3. Perfect fourth (context-dependent)
4. Tritone (ambiguous)
5. Mild dissonances (major 2nd, minor 7th)
6. Sharp dissonances (minor 2nd, major 7th)

Tension patterns:
- Crescendo: moving from low to high tension
- Diminuendo: moving from high to low tension
- Design patterns independent of dynamics possible

# Musical Context/Application
Intervals can follow each other in any order and may be arranged to form any pattern of tension interplay. A series may begin with little tension and end with great tension, or reverse this pattern. The consonant-dissonant properties of intervals may support or oppose other forces such as timbre, dynamics, and tempo for various expressive purposes.

# Common Confusions
Students often confuse interval tension with dynamics. Persichetti explicitly separates these, noting that tension patterns can move independently of dynamic levels, creating rich expressive possibilities through their interaction or opposition.
