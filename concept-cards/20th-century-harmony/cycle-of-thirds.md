# Quick Definition
A harmonic relationship system where chord progressions are governed by root movements of thirds rather than fifths, with the mediant (III) and submediant (VI) serving as primary chords supporting the tonic.

# Formal Definition
In third relationship (cycle of thirds), the mediant and submediant give balanced support to the tonic from their positions a third above and below the tonic. The primary chords are I, III, and VI, with the remaining chords being secondary. The relationships III-I and VI-I dominate all others, analogous to how V-I and IV-I dominate in traditional fifth-relationship harmony.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
**Primary Chords:** I, III, VI
**Dominant Relationships:** III-I, VI-I

**Corresponding Progressions (parallel to fifth cycle):**
| Cycle of 5ths | I | III | IV | VI | V | I |
| Cycle of 3rds | I | II | VI | VII | III | I |

Root movements descend by successive intervals of the cycle relationship in force.

**In Major Key:**
- I = major
- III = minor
- VI = minor
- Secondary chords (II, IV, V, VII) create useful color tension

# Musical Context/Application
Third-relationship harmony allows composers to create convincing harmonic progressions when the cycle is confirmed by passing and cadential chordal movements. The primary chords' colors are determined by the intervallic make-up of the scale in use. In a major key, a C major chord on E-G-B can function as a "dominant" equivalent, providing refreshing color.

In modes, the primary chords of a third-cycle relationship may not coincide with natural modal primary chords. For example, in phrygian mode, a third relationship produces primary triads I, III, and VI, but these do not include the characteristic phrygian second scale step. The phrygian sound can still be captured through emphasis on secondary chords that contain the modal color.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes confuse third-relationship harmony with chromatic mediant relationships. While both involve motion by thirds, the cycle of thirds establishes III and VI as structural primary chords throughout a passage, not merely coloristic substitutions within fifth-cycle harmony.
