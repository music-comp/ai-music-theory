# Quick Definition
A harmonic relationship system where root movements of thirds govern chord progressions, with the mediant (III) and submediant (VI) serving as primary chords supporting the tonic from positions a third above and below.

# Core Definition
In third relationship (cycle of thirds), the mediant and submediant give balanced support to the tonic from their positions a third above and below the tonic. The primary chords are I, III, and VI, with the remaining chords secondary. The relationships III-I and VI-I dominate all others, functioning analogously to V-I and IV-I in traditional fifth-relationship harmony. The most natural root progressions in the cycle of thirds parallel the usual progressions in fifth-based music. In a major key, primary chords produce I major, III minor, and VI minor; the major and minor secondary chords create useful color tension.

# Key Properties
1. Primary chords: I, III, VI
2. III-I and VI-I dominate all other relationships
3. Corresponding progression to fifths: I-II-VI-VII-III-I
4. In major key: I = major, III = minor, VI = minor
5. Secondary chords provide color tension within primary third-cycle framework
6. Cyclic relationships can be constructed in any scale, synthetic or otherwise
7. In modes, third-cycle primary chords may not include the characteristic modal scale step

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify passages where root movements by third predominate
- Look for structural emphasis on I, III, and VI
- Corresponding chord functions parallel fifth-cycle functions
- In phrygian mode, third relationship produces I, III, VI but these lack the phrygian second -- secondary chords must supply modal color
- In a C major third relationship, the E-G-B "dominant equivalent" is as refreshing as the secondary G-B-D

# Context & Application
Third-relationship harmony creates convincing progressions when the cycle is confirmed by passing and cadential chordal movements. The ear accepts III-I and VI-I as structural arrivals. This system is particularly effective for creating rich, colorful harmonic progressions that avoid the strong tonal pull of fifth-cycle harmony. Modal cadences of second and third relationships find their natural habitat in passages featuring the six-four chord alongside simple quartal chords.

# Common Errors
- Confusing third-relationship harmony with chromatic mediant substitution within fifth-cycle harmony
- Assuming III and VI always include the characteristic modal flavor tone

# Common Confusions
- **Cycle of thirds vs. chromatic mediants**: The cycle of thirds establishes III and VI as structural primary chords throughout a passage; chromatic mediants are coloristic substitutions within fifth-cycle harmony
- **Modal primary chords**: In some modes, the third-cycle primary chords may not coincide with the natural modal primary chords, requiring secondary chords to supply modal color
