# Quick Definition
The theoretical property that thirteenth chords cannot be inverted in the traditional sense; any attempt at inversion produces another eleventh or thirteenth chord rather than a true inversion of the original.

# Formal Definition
The thirteenth chord is non-invertible; any attempt at inversion will produce another eleventh or thirteenth. This occurs because the thirteenth chord contains all seven notes of a diatonic scale arranged in thirds, so rearranging which note appears in the bass simply reinterprets the structure as a different large tertian chord with a different root.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
**Theoretical Basis:**
A complete thirteenth chord contains seven different pitch classes spanning the entire diatonic collection when built in thirds.

**Inversion Behavior:**
When the bass note changes:
- Third in bass: produces a new thirteenth chord (reinterpreted root)
- Fifth in bass: produces a new thirteenth chord
- Seventh in bass: produces a new eleventh or thirteenth
- Ninth in bass: produces a new eleventh or thirteenth
- Eleventh in bass: produces a new thirteenth chord
- Thirteenth in bass: produces a new eleventh chord

**Contrast with Smaller Chords:**
Triads, seventh chords, and ninth chords maintain their identity through inversions. Thirteenth chords lose their identity because every possible bass note can be reinterpreted as a root.

# Musical Context/Application
Understanding thirteenth chord non-invertibility affects compositional decisions:
1. Thirteenth chords are best used in root position or with strategic omissions
2. "Voicings" of thirteenth chords are not true inversions but redistributions
3. The concept relates to why these chords function well as tonic formations

This property also explains why thirteenth chords are cumbersome in harmonic progressions: they cannot be smoothly inverted to facilitate voice leading.

# Common Confusions
Students often believe they can invert thirteenth chords as they would triads. The issue is not practical but theoretical: while any note can be placed in the bass, the result is not an "inversion" but a reinterpretation of the chord with a different root. This distinction is important for harmonic analysis.
