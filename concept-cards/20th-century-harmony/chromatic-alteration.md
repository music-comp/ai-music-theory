# Quick Definition
The chromatic modification of chord tones to change harmonic color without changing chordal function, intensifying or lessening dissonance.

# Formal Definition
The primary effect of chordal alteration is a change of harmonic color without a change of chordal function. Dissonance may be intensified or lessened by the alteration. An alteration exists as such only when a scale area is defined or when a characteristic chord is used as a harmonic norm. The altered formation must be foreign to the scale presently in effect. Chromatic lowering causes a drop in textural tension, while chromatic raising gives a succession tonal lift.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Alteration principles:
- **Function preservation**: Chord function remains despite chromatic modification
- **Scale context**: Alteration only exists relative to established scale
- **Tension effects**: Raising increases lift; lowering decreases tension
- **Excess limitation**: Altered chords used in excess lose touch with unaltered norm

Common alteration sources (cycle of fifths):
- Major key: Borrows from keys a fifth above/below, relative minors, tonic minor
- Minor key: Borrows from keys related to both relative and parallel majors
- Cycle of thirds: Borrows from keys a major or minor third above/below

Synthetic scale alterations:
- Derived from areas reached by modulation from the synthetic scale

# Musical Context/Application
When altered chords are used in excess, they lose touch with the unaltered harmonic norm and fail to function as altered structures. In synthetic scales, common alterations may be derived from areas reached by modulation. The simultaneous sounding of altered and unaltered tones creates pungent flavor; in major-minor compound harmony this sound is firmly fixed.

# Common Confusions
- Alteration requires an established scale context to be perceived as alteration
- What is altered in one scale may be unaltered in another
- Overuse of alteration destroys the altered/unaltered distinction
