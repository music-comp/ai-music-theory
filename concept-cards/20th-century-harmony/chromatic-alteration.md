# Quick Definition
The chromatic modification of chord tones to change harmonic color without changing chordal function, where chromatic lowering drops tension and chromatic raising provides tonal lift.

# Core Definition
The primary effect of chordal **alteration** is a change of harmonic color without a change of chordal function. Dissonance may be intensified or lessened by the alteration. An alteration does not exist as such unless a scale area is defined or a characteristic chord is used as harmonic norm. The altered formation must be foreign to the scale presently in effect. Chromatic lowering causes a drop in textural tension, and chromatic raising gives a succession tonal lift. When altered chords are used in excess they lose touch with the unaltered harmonic norm and fail to function as altered structures (Ch. 11, pp. 236-240).

# Prerequisites
- **Harmonic progression** -- understanding chord function within scale context

# Key Properties
1. Changes harmonic color without changing function
2. Requires defined scale area or characteristic harmonic norm
3. Altered formation must be foreign to prevailing scale
4. Chromatic lowering = tension drop; chromatic raising = tonal lift
5. Excess alteration destroys the altered/unaltered distinction
6. Common alterations in fifth-cycle: from keys a fifth above/below, relative minors, tonic minor
7. Common alterations in third-cycle: from keys a major/minor third above/below
8. Synthetic scale alterations: from areas reached by modulation
9. Context-dependent: what is altered in one scale may be unaltered in another (e.g., Neapolitan sixth in D major vs. unaltered supertonic in D Double Harmonic)

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify tones foreign to the prevailing scale
- Verify that the chord's function remains despite the alteration
- Determine the source of the alteration (related keys, parallel minor, etc.)
- Note whether the alteration raises or lowers tension

# Context & Application
Common alterations derive from the closest related keys. The simultaneous sounding of altered and unaltered tones has a pungent flavor; when created by ornamentation, the effect is fleeting, but in major-minor compound harmony it is firmly fixed.

# Common Errors
- Using altered chords in excess, destroying the norm-deviation relationship
- Failing to establish a clear scale area before employing alterations
- Treating alterations as modulations when they preserve function

# Common Confusions
- Alteration requires an established scale context to be perceived as alteration
- What is altered in one scale may be unaltered in another
- Overuse of alteration destroys the altered/unaltered distinction
