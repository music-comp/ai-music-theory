# Quick Definition
A sustained harmonic formation of three or more tones that functions as an extended pedal while other voices move through changing harmonies, creating polychordal relationships.

# Core Definition
If three or more tones are held, a **pedal chord** evolves. This extends the pedal concept from single or double tones to complete harmonic structures. Polytonality is often suggested by triple pedal point, and the pedal chord intensifies this effect. The pedal chord maintains stability while surrounding voices progress through various harmonies, creating complex polychordal relationships (Ch. 11, pp. 240-241).

# Prerequisites
- **Pedal point** -- understanding single and double pedal function

# Key Properties
1. Minimum: three or more sustained tones
2. Functions as stable harmonic anchor
3. Creates polychordal textures with moving voices
4. Often suggests polytonal organization
5. Blurs the line between pedal function and polychordal writing

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify three or more sustained tones functioning as a harmonic unit
- Note the moving harmonies against the sustained chord
- Check for polytonal implications

# Context & Application
Pedal chords are particularly effective in polytonal contexts where the sustained chord establishes one tonal plane while moving voices operate in another. The technique provides harmonic continuity while allowing melodic voices considerable freedom.

# Common Errors
- Not recognizing when triple pedal has become a full pedal chord
- Treating pedal chord and polychordal writing as entirely separate concepts

# Common Confusions
- Pedal chord is a complete harmonic formation, not just multiple pedal tones
- The boundary between pedal chord and polychordal writing is fluid
