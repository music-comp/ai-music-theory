# Quick Definition
The culminating principle that any tone can succeed, accompany, or precede any other tone in any medium and duration, with success depending not on technical rules but on contextual and formal conditions and the skill and soul of the composer.

# Core Definition
Any tone can succeed any other tone, any tone can sound simultaneously with any other tone or tones, and any group of tones can be followed by any other group of tones, just as any degree of tension or nuance can occur in any medium under any kind of stress or duration. Successful projection will depend upon the contextual and formal conditions that prevail, and upon the skill and soul of the composer. This is the final statement of Persichetti's treatise -- the summation of the entire book's exploration of twentieth-century harmonic resources.

# Prerequisites
- **Harmonic synthesis**: The overarching integrative principle
- **Compositional resources**: The full palette of available materials

# Key Properties
1. Any melodic succession is possible
2. Any vertical combination is possible
3. Any harmonic progression is possible
4. Any dynamic range and tension level possible
5. Any medium and timbral choice applicable
6. Any duration or stress possible
7. Success depends on context and form
8. Success depends on skill and "soul" of the composer

# Construction / Recognition
- This is not a construction technique but a governing principle
- Evaluate any passage by its contextual effectiveness, not by adherence to rules
- Assess the composer's skill in managing the freedom
- Note whether formal conditions support the harmonic choices
- The quality of "soul" -- creative sensitivity -- cannot be taught but can be cultivated

# Context & Application
This principle summarizes the expansion of harmonic resources throughout the twentieth century. The removal of stylistic prohibitions does not remove the need for compositional craft -- rather, it increases the demand for sensitivity and skill. Freedom without craft produces chaos; craft without freedom produces sterility. The twentieth-century composer must balance complete resource availability with artistic judgment. This is the final, culminating principle of the entire treatise.

# Common Errors
- Interpreting freedom as absence of constraints
- Ignoring context and form in favor of abstract possibilities
- Assuming technique alone ensures quality (ignoring "soul")

# Common Confusions
- **Freedom vs. arbitrariness**: Everything is possible, but not everything is effective
- **Skill and soul**: Technical mastery is necessary but not sufficient; creative sensitivity is equally essential
- **Culminating principle**: This is not a starting point for students but the endpoint of mastering all previous chapters' resources
