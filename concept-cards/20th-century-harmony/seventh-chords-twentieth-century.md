# Quick Definition
In twentieth-century practice, seventh chords have become stable entities with their traditionally dissonant seventh not necessarily prepared or resolved, functioning with the same harmonic freedom as triads.

# Formal Definition
The seventh member of chords is traditionally a dissonant tone but has been freed of some of its former restrictions. Seventh chords have become stable entities in themselves with their dissonant tones not necessarily prepared or resolved. Like triads, seventh chords may progress within or outside any scale formation, original or traditional. Under certain formal conditions the seventh is treated as a dissonant tone needing resolution; but as independent seventh chords they have the facility of triads.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
**Seven Types of Seventh Chords (using major and minor thirds):**
1. Major-major seventh (M3 + m3 + M3)
2. Major-minor seventh (dominant seventh) (M3 + m3 + m3)
3. Minor-major seventh (m3 + M3 + M3)
4. Minor-minor seventh (m3 + M3 + m3)
5. Diminished-minor seventh (half-diminished) (m3 + m3 + M3)
6. Diminished-diminished seventh (m3 + m3 + m3)
7. Augmented-major seventh (M3 + M3 + m3)

Note: Seventh chords with augmented and diminished thirds are misspellings of other chords.

**Voicing and Inversion:**
- Any note may be doubled
- Sevenths may be inverted and spaced in any manner without destroying harmonic suppleness
- First inversion spaced in fifths is particularly useful as a key center chord

# Musical Context/Application
All types of harmonic progression possible with triads are possible with seventh chords. Seventh chords function well in relationships established by a cycle of fifths, thirds, or seconds. The augmented fourth root relationship provides fresh activity from chord to chord, particularly when the chords are dominant sevenths.

A feeling of "progression" may be created by moving voices through various forms of different seventh formations on the same root, exploiting the coloristic differences between seventh chord types.

# Common Confusions
Students trained in common-practice harmony often expect seventh chords to resolve. In twentieth-century harmony, the seventh chord can function as a consonant formation in its own right. The "dissonance" of the seventh is contextual, not absolute.
