# Quick Definition
A succession of chords that establishes a definite direction with formal function, where the goal may be reached or abandoned and a tonality fixed or forsaken.

# Core Definition
A **harmonic progression** is a succession of chords that establishes a definite direction and has formal function. Unlike a mere chord succession, a progression implies goal-directed motion. The goal may be reached or abandoned, a tonality fixed or forsaken. In root progression, inverted or otherwise, there are two directional factors: the action of the root (not necessarily in the bass) and the location of the bass. The root may fall as the sounding bass rises, or rise as the bass falls, or the factors may agree in direction. Melodic and harmonic lines of tension may be placed in variable relationships with the root movement. Although register placement of the entire tonal mass affects the direction of sound, harmony with a strong downward pull can resist a register climb (Ch. 9, p. 181-183).

# Prerequisites
- **Chordal tension** -- understanding consonance-dissonance gradation in vertical structures
- **Intervals in chords** -- knowing how intervals function within harmonic formations

# Key Properties
1. Progression requires definite direction and formal function, distinguishing it from mere succession
2. Root direction and bass location are two independent directional factors
3. Progressions by fifth have strength; by third, softness; by second, blandness; by tritone, ambiguity
4. These four interval types together subsume all twelve tones
5. Music may be based on fifth, third, or second chord relationships, or on tension between them
6. Chromatically free root movement is governed by horizontal voice motion, not by scale
7. Augmented triads, diminished sevenths, quartal chords, and whole-tone chords have indefinite roots and serve as binding chords between textures

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the root of each chord (which may differ from the bass note)
- Determine the intervallic distance between successive roots
- Classify the prevailing cyclical relationship (fifth, third, or second)
- Observe whether melodic and harmonic tension lines agree or conflict with root direction
- Note whether register placement reinforces or contradicts harmonic pull
- Test thematic passages on various tonal levels for brilliance -- transposition sheds varying light on thematic material

# Context & Application
Twentieth-century harmonic writing may be based upon fifth, third, or second relationships of chords (simple or complex), upon the tension created by moving from one kind of relationship to another, or upon the horizontal movement of voices. Chromatic freedom not governed by scale is driven by horizontal voice movement. The composer considers textural influence of characteristic intervals, the effect of melodic motifs on harmonic phrase shapes, tritone behavior in horizontal and vertical situations, placement of chords in presiding or vanishing tonal centers, and recovery of harmonic equilibrium after swift changes of compositional devices. When writing without a given line, attention should focus on outer voices.

- Chord interchange (oscillation without forward direction)
- Static harmony (single chord sustained without progression)

# Common Errors
- Confusing any chord succession with a true progression (progression requires direction and formal function)
- Assuming the root and the bass note are always the same pitch
- Neglecting register placement as a directional factor independent of root movement

# Common Confusions
- **Progression vs. succession**: A succession is any sequence of chords; a progression implies direction and formal function
- **Root vs. bass**: The root determines harmonic identity; the bass determines voicing and inversional character -- they may differ
- **Goal-directed vs. static**: Progressions imply motion toward a goal; static harmony (Ch. 10) lacks this directional quality
