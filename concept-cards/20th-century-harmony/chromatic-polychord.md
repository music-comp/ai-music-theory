# Quick Definition
Any polychord containing at least one diminished or augmented triad, which requires careful spacing when the altered triad is in the bottom position to avoid lower-register crowding.

# Formal Definition
All polychords containing at least one diminished or augmented triad are identified as chromatic polychords. When the bottom chordal unit is augmented or diminished it is advisable to spread the voices of the lower triad to avoid lower-register crowding. The most supple of the chromatics are those combining one diatonic triad with one chromatic triad.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Chromatic polychord types:
- Major-diminished
- Major-augmented
- Minor-diminished
- Minor-augmented
- Diminished-major
- Diminished-minor
- Augmented-major
- Augmented-minor
- Diminished-diminished
- Diminished-augmented
- Augmented-diminished
- Augmented-augmented

Most supple combinations (mixed diatonic/chromatic):
- One diatonic triad (major or minor)
- One chromatic triad (diminished or augmented)
- Creates balance of stability and color

Bottom unit considerations:
- Diminished bottom: spread voices, avoid crowding
- Augmented bottom: spread voices, avoid crowding
- Whole-tone content of augmented triad affects color

# Musical Context/Application
Chromatic polychords add distinctive color beyond the diatonic palette. They are valuable for:
- Increased harmonic tension
- Transitional passages
- Climactic moments
- Color contrast with diatonic polychords

The most supple chromatic polychords combine one diatonic and one chromatic triad, balancing familiar stability with chromatic color. Pure chromatic combinations (both units diminished or augmented) are more specialized.

# Common Confusions
- "Chromatic" refers to the triad type (aug/dim), not to chromatic scale usage
- Bottom unit spacing is especially critical with aug/dim triads
- The most useful chromatic polychords mix diatonic and chromatic elements
- Augmented triads bring whole-tone character; diminished bring octatonic associations
