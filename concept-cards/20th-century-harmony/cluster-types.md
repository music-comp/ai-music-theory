# Quick Definition
The classification of clusters by their scale source (diatonic, chromatic, pentatonic, synthetic) and their intervallic content, with the chromatic cluster being the least useful due to its tendency to become tiresome, while diatonic and mixed clusters offer more variety.

# Formal Definition
Clusters may be formed from one complete scale of any type or from two or more contrasting scales. The chromatic scale is the least useful because when clustered it becomes tiresome quickly. A seven-note scale may be clustered modally in seven different ways; and a two-octave scale, in fourteen.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Classification by source:
1. **Diatonic clusters**: From major, minor, or modal scales
   - Contains M2 and m2 in specific patterns
   - 7 modal positions per octave
   - More textural variety than chromatic

2. **Chromatic clusters**: All half steps
   - Uniform m2 construction
   - Becomes tiresome quickly
   - Best for brief punctuations

3. **Pentatonic clusters**: From 5-note scales
   - Contains only M2 (in standard pentatonic)
   - Milder texture
   - Gap-filled sound

4. **Synthetic scale clusters**: From constructed scales
   - Unique intervallic combinations
   - Distinctive color
   - Unlimited possibilities

Mixed-scale clusters:
- Two or more contrasting scales sounded as cluster
- Creates unique intervallic mixtures
- Enriches cluster palette

Modal clustering:
- Seven-note scale yields 7 different clusters (starting on each degree)
- Each position has different M2/m2 pattern
- Two-octave range yields 14 positions

# Musical Context/Application
The choice of cluster type directly affects the music's character. Diatonic clusters retain tonal implications while chromatic clusters create maximal density. Synthetic and mixed-scale clusters offer distinctive colors that may define a composer's personal harmonic vocabulary.

The consonant-dissonant quality of cluster harmony may oppose or parallel the quality of the outer-voice counterpoint. Consonant or dissonant clusters (diatonic or chromatic) may be used in all possible combinations with consonant or dissonant outer voices.

# Common Confusions
- "Diatonic cluster" means built from diatonic scale, not that it sounds "consonant"
- Chromatic clusters are uniform but not necessarily more dissonant in effect
- Modal position affects the cluster's character even with the same pitch content
- Mixed-scale clusters are different from polyclusters (which maintain separation)
