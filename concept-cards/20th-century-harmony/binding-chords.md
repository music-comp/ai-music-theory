# Quick Definition
Versatile chords with indefinite roots -- augmented triads, diminished seventh chords, quartal chords, whole-tone chords -- that link different textural types while acting as connectors in a synthesis of harmonic contexts.

# Core Definition
In augmented triads, diminished seventh chords, chords by perfect fourths, and chords by major seconds, roots are indefinite and vanish quickly. These versatile **binding chords** link one kind of texture to another while acting as connectors in a synthesis of harmonic contexts. Harmonic movement may be created by generous chromaticism in all voices and emphasis upon total melodic motion obliterating intervallic construction of chords. Binding chords enable the composer to navigate between disparate harmonic vocabularies without strong directional implications, facilitating harmonic synthesis (Ch. 9, pp. 184-185).

# Prerequisites
- **Harmonic progression** -- understanding chord succession and direction

# Key Properties
1. Augmented triad (M3+M3): divides octave in three equal parts
2. Diminished seventh chord (m3+m3+m3+m3): divides octave in four equal parts
3. Chords by perfect fourths (P4+P4...): equal fourth stacking
4. Chords by major seconds (M2+M2...): whole-tone collection
5. All share symmetrical structure and indefinite root function
6. Root feeling vanishes quickly, enabling harmonic ambiguity
7. Function as transitional agents between harmonic textures

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify chords built from equal interval subdivisions of the octave
- Note the absence of a clear root sensation
- Observe whether these chords appear at junctures between different harmonic types
- Check if they facilitate movement from one texture (tertian, quartal, secundal) to another

# Context & Application
Binding chords are essential in twentieth-century harmonic synthesis, where a single piece may employ tertian, quartal, secundal, polychordal, and compound harmonies. Their root ambiguity allows them to serve as neutral connectors -- they belong equally to any harmonic vocabulary. They are particularly effective in modulatory transitions, at formal junctures, and when the composer wishes to move between tonal and atonal passages.

# Common Errors
- Treating binding chords as having strong tonal function when their purpose is transitional ambiguity
- Overusing binding chords so that harmonic identity becomes permanently vague
- Failing to exploit their connective potential at textural junctures

# Common Confusions
- **Binding vs. pivot**: Pivot chords function in two keys simultaneously; binding chords connect textures through root ambiguity
- **Indefinite vs. absent root**: Binding chords may have theoretical roots that are perceptually unstable, not necessarily no root at all
- **Transitional vs. structural**: Binding chords typically serve transitional function rather than structural harmonic foundation
