# Quick Definition
Ornamental tones foreign to the prevailing chord, inherently restless due to intervallic counteraction, classified by approach and departure patterns into four general categories plus a hybrid group.

# Core Definition
Ornamental **nonharmonic tones** are inherently restless because of their intervallic counteraction to the chord. They may be either accented (occurring with the change of harmony, regardless of metric position) or unaccented. Persichetti identifies four general kinds: (1) passing and auxiliary tones (approached and left without skip); (2) changing tones (approached conjunctly, left by skip); (3) appoggiaturas (approached by skip, left conjunctly); (4) a hybrid group including suspensions (movement delayed) and anticipations (movement hastened). It is possible to skip to and from a nonharmonic tone if it becomes a member of the following chord, while implying a larger formation over the same root, or while resisting any chordal affiliation. Before resolving, the nonharmonic tone itself may be ornamented by other ornamental tones. The ornamental tone may remain unresolved, causing a change of harmonic texture (Ch. 11, pp. 229-234).

# Key Properties
1. Passing/auxiliary: approached and left by step
2. Changing tones: step approach, leap departure (opposite direction)
3. Appoggiaturas: leap approach, step departure
4. Suspensions: preparation-suspension-resolution (delayed movement)
5. Anticipations: early arrival of next-chord tone (hastened movement)
6. Accented = occurring with harmonic change, not metric accent
7. May skip to/from if tone becomes member of following chord
8. May imply larger formation or resist chordal affiliation
9. May be ornamented before resolving
10. May remain unresolved, permanently changing texture
11. May derive from scale built on chord root (each root treated as temporary tonic)
12. May derive from contrasting key centers (temporary polytonality)

# Construction / Recognition
- Classify each non-chord tone by approach and departure: step-step, step-leap, leap-step, or held
- Determine if the tone is accented (coincides with harmonic change) or unaccented
- Note any skipping to/from nonharmonic tones justified by following chord membership
- Check whether tones derive from the chord-root scale or contrasting keys

# Context & Application
In twentieth-century practice, nonharmonic tones function with greater freedom than in common-practice tonality. They may be derived from scales built upon the chord root, from contrasting key centers, or may resist any chordal affiliation. When grouped chord-wise, they form ornamental chords. They may remain permanently unresolved, changing the harmonic texture rather than returning to the original harmony.

# Common Errors
- Assuming all nonharmonic tones must resolve by step to the nearest chord tone
- Defining "accented" by metric position rather than coincidence with harmonic change
- Failing to recognize when unresolved ornamental tones have permanently altered the texture

# Common Confusions
- **"Accented"** refers to timing with harmonic change, not metric stress
- In twentieth-century practice, nonharmonic tones may remain permanently unresolved
- Nonharmonic tones can be grouped chord-wise to create ornamental chords
