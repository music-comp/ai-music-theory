# Quick Definition
A harmonic system in which chords are built by superimposing intervals of the fourth, creating a distinctly twentieth-century sound that stems from both triadic ornamentation and medieval polyphonic techniques.

# Formal Definition
Twentieth-century composers use quartal harmony (chords by fourths) as well as the tertian harmony (chords by thirds) of Classic and Romantic practice. Quartal materials stem from ornamentation of the triad and from the techniques of medieval polyphony. Chords by fourths are built by superimposing intervals of the fourth. In other spacings, most of the chord members must be placed a fourth apart in order to preserve the distinctive quartal sound; otherwise the quartal structures may sound like eleventh, thirteenth, or added-note chords.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
**Construction Principle:**
Stack perfect or augmented fourths upward from the bass:
- 3-note: Root + P4 + P4 (or variations with A4)
- 4-note: Root + P4 + P4 + P4
- 5-note: Root + P4 + P4 + P4 + P4
- Continue to 12-note chord

**Preservation of Quartal Sound:**
Most chord members must be placed a fourth apart to maintain quartal character. Wide spacing that obscures the fourths produces tertian or added-note sounds instead.

**Historical Origins:**
- Medieval polyphony (organum, early counterpoint)
- Triadic ornamentation (suspensions, appoggiaturas)

# Musical Context/Application
Quartal harmony offers composers a distinctly non-tertian harmonic vocabulary. Unlike tertian chords which have clear root functions and traditional voice-leading expectations, quartal chords are often ambiguous in root identity and more freely mobile. The open, "hollow" quality of stacked fourths contrasts with the fuller sound of thirds.

Quartal and tertian harmonies can be combined, though care must be taken to preserve the character of each. When combining, devices that emphasize the perfect fourth in tertian structures help smooth the transition.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes confuse quartal chords with inverted tertian chords or added-note formations. A true quartal chord maintains its fourth-based structure as a defining characteristic; it is not simply a tertian chord rearranged. The spacing and intervallic relationships must preserve the quartal sound.
