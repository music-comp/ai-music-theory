# Quick Definition
The arrangement of secundal chords in open position using seventh and ninth intervals to give linear freedom and room for voice activity, preventing the cramped, percussive quality that results from close-position secundal voicings in lower registers.

# Formal Definition
When chords by seconds are used in close position they cramp easily and become percussive in the low register. Spacing in intervals of the seventh and ninth gives the muscle-bound chords by seconds linear freedom and room for activity of the parts. In open position spacing, chords similar to ninths may result, but in a secundal context they remain chords by seconds.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Spacing principles:
- **Close position**: Adjacent seconds (e.g., C-D-E)
  - Problems: Cramped, percussive, muddy in low register
  - Use: Upper register, special effects, clusters

- **Open position (7ths)**: Seconds inverted to sevenths (e.g., C-B-A arranged as C-A-B)
  - Benefits: Linear freedom, clearer texture
  - Resembles ninth chord voicing

- **Open position (9ths)**: Extended spacing
  - Benefits: Maximum clarity, independent voice movement
  - May resemble larger tertian structures

Contextual identity:
- Same pitch collection may sound like:
  - Ninth chord (in tertian context)
  - Chord by seconds in open position (in secundal context)
- Surrounding harmony determines perception

# Musical Context/Application
Spacing is a primary tool for managing the inherent density of secundal harmony. Low-register secundal writing almost always requires open spacing to avoid murky textures. The "muscle-bound" quality of close-position secundal chords limits their usefulness for contrapuntal writing.

Appropriate spacing choices depend on:
- Register (lower = wider spacing needed)
- Desired texture (percussive vs. linear)
- Voice-leading requirements
- Instrumental capabilities

# Common Confusions
- Open-position secundal chords are still secundal, not tertian
- Resemblance to ninth chords does not indicate ninth chord function
- "Muscle-bound" is a texture problem, not a theoretical limitation
- Spacing choices affect perception of chord type; context clarifies identity
