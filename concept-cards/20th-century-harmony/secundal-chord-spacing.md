# Quick Definition

The arrangement of secundal chords in open position using seventh and ninth intervals to provide linear freedom and room for voice activity, preventing the cramped, percussive quality of close-position voicings in lower registers.

# Core Definition

"When chords by seconds are used in close position they cramp easily and become percussive in the low register. Spacing in intervals of the seventh and ninth gives the muscle-bound chords by seconds linear freedom and room for activity of the parts" (Persichetti, p. 121). "In the preceding example, spacing produces chords similar to ninths, but in a secundal context they are chords by seconds in open position" (p. 121).

# Prerequisites

- **Three-note secundal chord** — Spacing is a voicing strategy applied to these chords
- **Secundal chord inversions** — Seventh spacing is a product of inversional rearrangement

# Key Properties

1. Close position (adjacent seconds): cramped, percussive, muddy in low register
2. Open position (7ths): linear freedom, clearer texture; resembles ninth-chord voicing
3. Open position (9ths): maximum clarity, independent voice movement
4. Contextual identity: same pitches may sound like ninths (tertian context) or open-position seconds (secundal context)
5. Surrounding harmony determines perception of chord identity
6. Lower register demands wider spacing; upper register tolerates closer spacing

# Construction / Recognition

## To Space a Secundal Chord in Open Position
1. Begin with close-position chord (e.g., C-D-E)
2. Transfer selected notes up or down by octave to create seventh/ninth intervals
3. Verify the resulting voicing provides room for voice movement
4. Ensure surrounding context establishes secundal identity

## To Recognize Open-Position Secundal Chords
1. Identify chords with seventh or ninth intervals in an otherwise secundal passage
2. Check whether the pitch-class content reduces to stacked seconds
3. Confirm the context is secundal, not tertian

# Context & Application

Spacing is the primary tool for managing the inherent density of secundal harmony. Low-register secundal writing almost always requires open spacing to avoid murky textures. The "muscle-bound" quality of close-position secundal chords limits their usefulness for contrapuntal writing.

Appropriate spacing choices depend on register, desired texture (percussive vs. linear), voice-leading requirements, and instrumental capabilities.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using close-position secundal chords in bass register without awareness of muddiness
  **Correction**: In low registers, space seconds as sevenths or ninths for clarity
- **Error**: Analyzing open-position secundal chords as ninth chords
  **Correction**: Surrounding harmonic context determines identity; in secundal passages, these remain chords by seconds

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Open-position secundal chords have changed their fundamental nature
  **Clarification**: They remain secundal in origin and effect; the spacing is a voicing choice, not a change of harmonic category
- **Confusion**: "Muscle-bound" implies a theoretical problem
  **Clarification**: It is a practical texture problem; close position limits voice-leading possibilities, not theoretical validity
