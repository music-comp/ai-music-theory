# Quick Definition
A specific form of polytonality in which exactly two keys are combined simultaneously, producing a more transparent texture than three or more combined keys, with clarity dependent on tritone definition and registral separation.

# Core Definition
If only two keys are sounded simultaneously, the specific term bitonality may be used. Bitonality is a subset of polytonality, distinguished by the use of exactly two tonal planes. The same principles of vertical construction, key relationship, and textural clarity apply. The two-plane structure is easier to perceive and control than combinations of three or more keys.

Bitonality is effective when each tonal plane is kept within a clear scale formation. When tritone-defining tones are absent from either key, the result is ambiguously tonal rather than clearly bitonal.

# Prerequisites
- **Polytonality**: The broader category of multi-key writing

# Key Properties
1. Exactly two distinct tonal planes operating simultaneously
2. Each plane maintains its own scale formation
3. Tritone-spanning scale steps must be present in each key
4. Sufficient registral separation prevents muddiness
5. More transparent than three-or-more-key polytonality
6. Key relationship determines tension level (cycle of fifths order)

# Construction / Recognition
- Verify exactly two distinct keys are operating
- Ensure each key's tritone-defining tones (4th and 7th scale degrees) are present
- Maintain adequate registral separation between the two planes
- Assess key relationship for desired tension level
- Distinguish from ambiguous major passage (if tritone tones are missing)

# Context & Application
Bitonality provides a more transparent and manageable polytonal texture than combinations of three or more keys. Block chords against a single contrasting key line create supple bitonal patterns; shifting pedals ease the texture. The tritone relationship between keys produces the prime bitonal effect -- maximum differentiation with resonant combined sonority.

# Common Errors
- Omitting tritone-defining tones, producing ambiguity instead of bitonality
- Placing two keys too close together in register
- Confusing bitonality with polychordal writing

# Common Confusions
- **Bitonality vs. ambiguous major**: Without tritone-defining tones, two combined major keys can sound like one ambiguous major key
- **Bitonality vs. polytonality**: Bitonality is specifically two keys; polytonality is the general term
- **Registral separation**: Keys too close together produce mud, not bitonality
