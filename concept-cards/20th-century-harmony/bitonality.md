# Quick Definition
A specific form of polytonality in which exactly two keys are combined simultaneously.

# Formal Definition
If only two keys are sounded simultaneously, the specific term bitonality may be used. Bitonality is a subset of polytonality, distinguished by the use of exactly two tonal planes rather than three or more. The same principles of vertical construction, key relationship, and textural clarity apply to bitonality as to polytonality more generally.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Bitonal construction:
- Two distinct tonal planes operating simultaneously
- Each plane maintains its own scale formation
- Vertical combinations must be carefully constructed

Key relationship considerations (for two major keys):
- Consonant to dissonant order: P5, M9, M6, M3, M7, etc.
- Tritone relationship: Prime bitonal relationship
- Keys separated by tritone most clearly distinguish tonal planes

Bitonal clarity requires:
- Tritone-spanning scale steps in each key used quickly
- Sufficient registral separation between keys
- Independent melodic activity in each plane

# Musical Context/Application
Bitonality provides a more transparent polytonal texture than combinations of three or more keys. The two-plane structure is easier to perceive and control. Bitonality is effective when each tonal plane is kept within a clear scale formation; shifting modality within either plane clouds the texture or simply produces miscellaneous polychords.

# Examples
- Ex. 12-14: Two keys lacking tritone-defining tones produce ambiguous rather than clear bitonality
- Block chords against single contrasting key line create supple bitonal patterns

# Common Confusions
- Bitonality is specifically two keys, not just "a few" keys
- Keys must be clearly defined through tritone-embracing tones
- Without clear definition, result is ambiguous rather than bitonal
