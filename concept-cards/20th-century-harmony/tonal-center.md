# Quick Definition
The pitch or chord that functions as the point of gravitational focus in a musical passage, providing orientation even without traditional functional harmony, and which may be a single tone, chord, or polychord.

# Core Definition
A tonal center is the focal point around which harmonic activity is organized. In twentieth-century music, the search for tonality or key center feeling may become a creative force. The tonality of a structure may be generated from a unifying harmonic idea from which musical growth extends. Key consciousness may vanish temporarily only to make stronger an emerging key's return. In equidistant rootless harmony, any note may be made the tonal center through melodic insistence, spacing, or instrumentation.

The center may take the form of a single tone (pitch center), a chord (harmonic center), or a polychord (polytonal center). A work may be built tonally upon an initial harmony that falls cadentially, with the final cadence finding refuge far from initial tonal implications. A succession of opening chords may suggest the tonal shape of the entire work in microcosm.

# Prerequisites
- **Tonality**: Understanding of tonal organization as a continuum

# Key Properties
1. May be a single pitch, a chord, or a polychord
2. Established through melodic insistence, spacing, instrumentation, or chordal pillar relationships
3. Can vanish temporarily to strengthen its eventual return
4. In equidistant rootless harmony, any note can serve as center
5. Opening material may suggest the tonal shape of an entire work
6. An insistent chord may establish center by resisting pressures from various tonalities

# Construction / Recognition
- Listen for recurring pitch emphasis at structurally important points
- Identify chords that function as gravitational anchors
- In equidistant or rootless harmony, find the melodically insisted pitch
- Look for spacing and instrumentation cues that highlight a center
- Note whether the center is a pitch, chord, or polychord
- Track temporary disappearances and returns of center

# Context & Application
An initial major-minor compound chord may promote polychordal writing that instigates a triadic struggle between major and minor structures; the formal conflict may not be resolved until an obstinate melody note, common to both triads, anchors them in a major-minor formation transposed from the original chord. A work may be built tonally upon an initial harmony that falls cadentially; the final cadence may find refuge in a group of tones far removed from the tonal implications of the first harmonic fall. Key feeling may be gradually loosened or tightened. The gravity of a tonal center is decreased by moving a new voice through notes that give an important chord new roots.

# Common Errors
- Assuming the center must be a single pitch rather than a chord or polychord
- Overlooking non-harmonic methods of center establishment (registration, timbre)
- Missing temporary center disappearances as structural events

# Common Confusions
- **Tonal center vs. root**: A tonal center is the focus of an entire passage; a root belongs to a single chord
- **Center vs. key**: The tonal center is the gravitational pitch or chord; the key includes the full scale and functional framework
- **Temporary disappearance**: Loss of center may be intentional and structurally meaningful, not a compositional flaw
