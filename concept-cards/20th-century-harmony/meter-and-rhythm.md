# Quick Definition
The distinction between meter (a measure of rhythm with no rhythm of its own) and rhythm (the actual pattern of accents and durations), where strong and weak beats occur wherever the musical line places them regardless of meter.

# Core Definition
**Meter** is a measure of rhythm. It has no rhythm of its own; it only appears to have when rhythmic pulse coincides with the metric points. Strong and weak beats occur wherever the musical line places them, regardless of meter. Simple rhythms may be combined under one time signature. If patterns of shifted accents remain relatively consistent, the asymmetrical divisions are often indicated by compound time signatures or dotted lines. These composite meters generate asymmetrical phrases. Two patterns of varying note values coinciding at occasional points will flow without a feeling of chaos (Ch. 10, pp. 212-215).

# Prerequisites
- **Harmonic rhythm** -- understanding the temporal dimension of harmony

# Key Properties
1. Meter has no rhythm of its own -- only appears to when pulse coincides with metric points
2. Strong and weak beats are placed by the musical line, not the meter
3. Simple rhythms may combine under one time signature
4. Compound time signatures or dotted lines indicate consistent shifted accents
5. Composite meters generate asymmetrical phrases
6. Change of meter is a common means of rhythmic variety
7. Fluid bar line accommodates fractional measures
8. Rhythmic freedom of the bar line may result from vocal text demands (prose rhythm)

# Construction / Recognition
- Compare where rhythmic accents actually fall versus where the meter implies them
- Identify composite meters and asymmetrical divisions
- Note whether bar lines serve rhythmic or notational convenience
- Check for changed meters and their relationship to phrase structure

# Context & Application
Musical stress is created by pitch, intensity, color, or duration; the more factors contributing, the more complete the accent. Rhythmic pulse is most clearly defined when related melodic, contrapuntal, and harmonic functions are emphasized. The meter-rhythm distinction frees twentieth-century composers to use meter as organizational framework while creating independent rhythmic patterns.

# Common Errors
- Assuming the bar line determines where strong beats occur
- Conflating notation (time signature) with perceived rhythmic grouping
- Treating meter as inherently rhythmic rather than as a framework

# Common Confusions
- **Meter as rhythm**: Meter is not rhythm; it is a framework that may or may not align with rhythmic accent
- **Barline authority**: The barline does not determine strong beats; the musical content does
- **Notation vs. sound**: Time signature notation may not reflect perceived rhythmic grouping
