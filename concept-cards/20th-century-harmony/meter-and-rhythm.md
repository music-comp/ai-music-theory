# Quick Definition

The distinction between meter (a measure of rhythm with no inherent rhythm of its own) and rhythm (the actual pattern of accents and durations), where strong and weak beats occur wherever the musical line places them regardless of meter.

# Formal Definition

**Meter** is a measure of rhythm that has no rhythm of its own; it only appears to have rhythm when rhythmic pulse coincides with metric points. Strong and weak beats occur wherever the musical line places them, regardless of meter. This distinction enables twentieth-century composers to use meter as an organizing framework while creating rhythmic patterns that may align with, contradict, or ignore metrical expectations.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation

Relationship types:
- **Coincident**: Rhythmic stress aligns with metric strong beats
- **Contradictory**: Rhythmic stress opposes metric expectations (syncopation)
- **Independent**: Rhythm and meter operate in separate layers

Metric flexibility:
- Simple rhythms may combine under one time signature
- Shifted accent patterns may use compound time signatures or dotted bar lines
- Composite meters generate asymmetrical phrases
- Change of meter achieves rhythmic variety
- Fluid bar line accommodates fractional measures

Musical stress created by:
- Pitch level
- Intensity (dynamics)
- Tone color (timbre)
- Duration
- Multiple factors combining for complete accent

# Musical Context/Application

Understanding meter-rhythm distinction enables:
- Creating rhythmic interest through metric contradiction
- Using meter as notation convenience without rhythmic implication
- Exploiting asymmetric and changing meters
- Freeing rhythm from barline tyranny

Applications include:
- Prose rhythm in vocal settings
- Polyrhythmic layering
- Metric modulation
- Asymmetric phrase structures

# Common Confusions

- **Meter as rhythm**: Meter is not rhythm; it is a framework that may or may not align with rhythmic accent.
- **Barline authority**: The barline does not determine where strong beats occur; the musical content does.
- **Notation vs. sound**: Time signature notation may not reflect perceived rhythmic grouping.
