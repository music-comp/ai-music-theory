# Quick Definition
A transitional device involving the gradual subtraction of cluster members to leave chords suggesting the next harmonic texture, or the reverse process of gradual addition to build a cluster from simpler harmony.

# Core Definition
A common transitional harmonic device involving clusters is the gradual subtraction of cluster members, leaving chords that suggest the kind of harmony to follow. The reverse is no less effective: gradually adding tones to a simpler chord until a cluster is formed. This technique provides smooth transition between the dense secundal texture of clusters and other harmonic formations, demonstrating that clusters exist on a continuum with other chord constructions.

# Prerequisites
- **Textural transition**: The broader transitional technique
- **Tone cluster**: The secundal formation being transitioned to or from

# Key Properties
1. Subtraction: remove cluster members to reveal target harmony
2. Addition: add adjacent tones to simple chord to build cluster
3. Remaining/core tones determine harmonic direction
4. Process can accelerate or decelerate
5. Gradual transformation maintains continuity
6. Demonstrates continuity between clusters and other chord types

# Construction / Recognition
- For subtraction: begin with full cluster, remove members one at a time, noting what chord types emerge
- For addition: begin with simple chord, add adjacent tones gradually until cluster forms
- Track which tones remain to determine the harmonic direction
- Note the pace of subtraction/addition
- Identify the target texture suggested by the remaining tones

# Context & Application
Cluster transition techniques solve the problem of integrating the dense secundal mass of clusters with other harmonic textures. By gradually adding or removing tones, the composer can move smoothly between highly dissonant cluster textures and more open chord formations. The technique emphasizes the continuity between different harmonic types -- clusters are not isolated phenomena but connect to other chord constructions through shared intervallic content.

# Common Errors
- Making subtraction/addition too sudden (loses continuity)
- Removing tones randomly rather than to suggest the target harmony
- Treating clusters as isolated sonorities rather than transitional resources

# Common Confusions
- **Direction matters**: The remaining tones must suggest the target harmony, not arbitrary formations
- **Bidirectional**: Both subtraction and addition are valid and equally effective
- **Continuum**: Clusters exist on a continuum with other chord types; transitions reveal this continuity
