# Quick Definition
The distance between two pitches, sounded simultaneously or successively, forming the fundamental building block of both melodic and harmonic structures in twentieth-century composition.

# Core Definition
An interval is the sonic relationship between two tones. While its physical properties are constant, its usage and meaning change with the working context to which it belongs. In the tempered scale, the unlimited intervals of the overtone series have been reduced to twelve intervals that retain the characteristics of their acoustic counterparts. Persichetti's opening principle states that any tone can succeed any other tone, any tone can sound simultaneously with any other tone or tones, and any group of tones can be followed by any other group of tones -- successful projection depends upon context, form, and the composer's skill.

# Prerequisites
- No strict prerequisites; this is a foundational concept

# Key Properties
1. **Dual nature**: intervals may be melodic (successive) or harmonic (simultaneous)
2. **Twelve chromatic intervals** within the octave, each with a basic quality determined by physical properties of sound waves and overtones
3. **Context-dependent meaning**: the same interval can function differently depending on surrounding musical forces
4. **Tension classification**: intervals range from open consonances to sharp dissonances (see consonance-dissonance)
5. **Compositional freedom**: intervals can follow each other in any order and form any tension pattern

# Construction / Recognition
To analyze intervals in twentieth-century music:
1. Identify the two pitches and their distance in semitones
2. Classify by quality: perfect, major, minor, augmented, diminished
3. Assess the tension category: open consonance, soft consonance, mild dissonance, sharp dissonance, or ambiguous
4. Consider the contextual factors: dynamics, timbre, register, surrounding intervals

# Context & Application
In twentieth-century music, all intervals are usable materials, not limited to "consonant" ones. Intervals can be arranged into patterns of increasing or decreasing tension, and these tension patterns can support or oppose other musical forces (dynamics, timbre, tempo). The consonant-dissonant concept may shift within a piece: in strongly dissonant passages, dissonances become the "consonant" norm.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming interval qualities are absolute. **Correction**: In 20th-century practice, consonance/dissonance depends on context, orchestration, dynamics, and surrounding materials.
- **Error**: Treating intervals only as distance measurements. **Correction**: Intervals are expressive resources with tension characteristics that interact with other compositional forces.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Equating "dissonant" with "unusable." **Clarification**: Persichetti treats all twelve intervals as equally valid compositional materials; their effectiveness depends on context and skill.
