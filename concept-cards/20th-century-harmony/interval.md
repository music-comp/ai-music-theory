# Quick Definition
The distance between two pitches, forming the fundamental building block of both melodic lines and harmonic structures in twentieth-century composition.

# Formal Definition
An interval is the sonic relationship between two tones, whether sounded simultaneously (harmonic interval) or successively (melodic interval). While its physical properties remain constant, its usage and meaning change with the working context to which it belongs, making the interval's function dependent on compositional style and period.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
- Intervals are measured by the number of scale steps between pitches plus the quality (major, minor, perfect, augmented, diminished)
- The twelve chromatic intervals within an octave retain characteristics derived from the overtone series
- Interval classification:
  - Perfect fifth and octave: open consonances
  - Major and minor thirds and sixths: soft consonances
  - Minor seconds and major sevenths: sharp dissonances
  - Major seconds and minor sevenths: mild dissonances
  - Perfect fourth: consonant or dissonant (context-dependent)
  - Tritone: ambiguous (neutral or restless)

# Musical Context/Application
In twentieth-century music, intervals can follow each other in any order and may be arranged to form any pattern of tension interplay. The concept of consonance and dissonance may change as composers' attitudes and practices evolve. Various degrees of tension may be accepted as consonance, and consonant intervals may sound dissonant in a passage dominated by dissonant intervals.

# Examples
- Bartok, 44 Violin Duets: Two-part writing exploring intervallic relationships
- Schoenberg, Six Little Pieces, Op. 19, II: Passages featuring specific intervals
- Ravel, Sonate pour Violon et Violoncelle: Two-part intervallic writing

# Common Confusions
Students often assume interval qualities are fixed and universal. In twentieth-century practice, the consonant-dissonant quality of an interval depends heavily on musical context, orchestration, dynamics, and surrounding harmonic materials.
