# Quick Definition
The technique of moving triads in fundamental position with parallel fifths between root and fifth voices, a practice avoided in common-practice harmony but frequently used in twentieth-century composition.

# Formal Definition
Triads are frequently used in fundamental position while the voices containing the root and fifth (usually the two bottom voices) move in parallel fifths. This type of movement, traditionally forbidden in common-practice voice leading, is effective in twentieth-century harmony if attention is diverted from the fifths by extensive contrary motion. Periodic inversion of the triads will relieve the binding parallelism.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
**Voice Movement:**
- Root and fifth voices move in parallel (maintaining the perfect fifth)
- Third voice moves in parallel with root and fifth
- Result: All three voices move in the same direction by the same interval type

**Relief Techniques:**
- Extensive contrary motion in surrounding passages
- Periodic inversion of triads (using 6/3 or 6/4 positions)
- Strategic use of six-three chords as incomplete formations

**Six-Three Chord Function:**
A minor six-three chord can function as an incomplete major triad with an added sixth. For example, at a phrase ending, this sonority may serve as a tonic equivalent.

# Musical Context/Application
Parallel triads create a characteristic "planing" sound associated with impressionism and early twentieth-century music. The technique works particularly well with chromatic triadic progressions where traditional voice-leading rules are already suspended.

The six-four chord, with its characteristic interval of a fourth, is often heard alongside simple chords by fourths. The mild tension of the second inversion and the punctuation of recurring cadences help define the formal arc of longer triadic passages. In these surroundings, modal cadences of second and third relationships find their natural habitat.

# Common Confusions
Students trained in common-practice voice leading often struggle to accept parallel fifths as legitimate. In twentieth-century harmony, the prohibition against parallel fifths is merely one historical constraint among many that composers choose to observe or not. The key is managing the parallelism effectively through contrary motion and inversions.
