# Quick Definition
The technique of moving triads in fundamental position with parallel fifths between root and fifth voices, effective in twentieth-century harmony when relieved by contrary motion and periodic inversion.

# Core Definition
Triads are frequently used in fundamental position while the voices containing the root and fifth (usually the two bottom voices) move in parallel fifths. This type of movement is effective if attention is diverted from the fifths by extensive contrary motion. Periodic inversion of the triads will relieve the binding parallelism. The six-four chord with its characteristic interval of a fourth is often heard in the company of simple chords by fourths. The mild tension of the second inversion and the punctuation of recurring cadences help define the formal arc of longer triadic passages. In these surroundings, modal cadences of second and third relationships find their natural habitat.

# Key Properties
1. Root and fifth voices move in parallel (maintaining perfect fifth)
2. Traditionally forbidden parallel fifths are accepted in twentieth-century practice
3. Relief techniques: extensive contrary motion, periodic inversion
4. A minor six-three can function as an incomplete major triad with added sixth (tonic equivalent)
5. Six-four chords connect naturally to quartal harmony through the prominent fourth
6. Modal cadences of second and third relationships suit these textures

# Construction / Recognition
- Three voices move in parallel root-position triads (parallel fifths in outer voices)
- Periodically break the parallelism with first or second inversions
- Use contrary motion in surrounding passages to offset the binding parallelism
- The final chord may use a minor six-three as a tonic equivalent (incomplete major triad + added sixth)
- The six-four chord bridges triadic and quartal textures

# Context & Application
Parallel triads create a characteristic "planing" sound associated with impressionism and early twentieth-century music. The technique works particularly well with chromatic triadic progressions where traditional voice-leading rules are already suspended. The six-four chord's prominent fourth provides a natural connection point to quartal harmony; longer triadic passages benefit from the formal articulation provided by recurring cadences using these inversions.

# Common Errors
- Writing extended parallel triads without any relief (contrary motion or inversions)
- Ignoring the six-three chord's dual function as incomplete triad with added sixth

# Common Confusions
- **Parallel fifths**: In twentieth-century harmony, the prohibition is lifted, but the binding quality of unrelieved parallelism still needs management
- **Six-three as tonic**: A minor six-three can function as a tonic equivalent in chromatic triadic contexts, not just as an inversion
