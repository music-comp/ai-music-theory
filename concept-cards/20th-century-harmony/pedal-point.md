# Quick Definition
A tone or tones sustained, repeated, or ornamented while other voices move through a succession of chords, some of which may be foreign to the pedal.

# Formal Definition
A pedal is a tone or tones sustained, repeated, or ornamented while other voices move through a succession of chords, some of which may be foreign to the pedal. Tonic pedals have repose while non-tonic pedals have restlessness. Final pedals reinforce tonality or allow time for figuration to conclude its ornamental design. When pedals are placed a fifth or ninth below the main body of harmony, they add sheen and resonance.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Pedal characteristics:
- **Single pedal**: One sustained/repeated tone
- **Double pedal**: Two sustained tones (often tonic and dominant)
- **Triple pedal**: Three sustained tones (often suggests polytonality)
- **Pedal chord**: Three or more tones held (see separate card)

Functional types:
- **Tonic pedal**: Creates repose and stability
- **Non-tonic pedal**: Creates restlessness and tension
- **Final pedal**: Reinforces closing tonality

Registral effects:
- Fifth below harmony: Adds sheen and resonance
- Ninth below harmony: Similar resonant effect
- Middle voice: More obtrusive
- Upper voice: More melodically obstinate

# Musical Context/Application
Pedal point is a fundamental device for establishing and maintaining tonal centers in twentieth-century music. Triple pedal point often suggests polytonality. The relationship between the pedal and the moving harmonies creates varying degrees of consonance and dissonance as the progression unfolds. Pedals relate to ostinatos but differ in that pedals are typically single tones while ostinatos are melodic patterns.

# Common Confusions
- Pedal can be sustained, repeated, or ornamented - not only sustained
- Triple pedal may suggest polytonality rather than simple tonal reinforcement
- Register affects the character (sheen vs. obstruction)
