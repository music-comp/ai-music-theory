# Quick Definition

A polychord with a major bottom unit and minor upper unit (named from bottom upward), richer than the reverse because the upper minor triad receives additional support from the lower chord's overtones. Numbers 4, 5, 6, 9, and 12 are most useful.

# Core Definition

"A minor over a major triad is richer than a major over a minor; the upper triad in the first formation receives additional support from tones of the lower chord other than the bottom tone from which it is generated" (Persichetti, p. 142). "The qualities of the twelve major-minor possibilities may be observed when the upper units are added to the bottom chord in a rising cycle of fifths. Numbers 4, 5, 6, 9, and 12 are more useful because of their resonant character" (p. 141).

# Key Properties

1. Named from bottom up: major bottom, minor upper
2. Richer than minor-major because upper minor triad gets overtone support from major bottom
3. Numbers 4, 5, 6, 9, and 12 most useful due to resonant character
4. Twelve combinations arranged by cycle of fifths
5. Creates darker color than major-major while maintaining considerable resonance

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct
1. Place major triad (six-four for maximum resonance) as bottom unit
2. Place minor triad at desired transposition level as upper unit
3. Select from resonant combinations (4, 5, 6, 9, 12) for best results

# Context & Application

Major-minor polychords offer a darker color than major-major while maintaining considerable resonance. The minor upper triad creates a more somber or complex character while the major lower triad provides harmonic stability.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Naming the polychord from top to bottom
  **Correction**: Convention names from bottom upward: major-minor means major bottom, minor upper

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Major-minor and minor-major produce similar results
  **Clarification**: The resonance difference is real and significant; major bottom provides stronger overtone support for the upper unit
