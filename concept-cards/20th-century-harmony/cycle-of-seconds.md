# Quick Definition
A harmonic relationship system where chord progressions are governed by root movements of seconds, with the supertonic (II) and leading-tone chord (VII) serving as primary chords supporting the tonic.

# Formal Definition
In second relationship (cycle of seconds), the supertonic and leading-tone chords help establish the tonic center from their positions a second in either direction. Primary chords are I, II, and VII, with the remaining chords being secondary. The relationships II-I and VII-I dominate all others, functioning analogously to V-I and IV-I in traditional fifth-relationship harmony.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
**Primary Chords:** I, II, VII
**Dominant Relationships:** II-I, VII-I

**Corresponding Progressions (parallel to fifth cycle):**
| Cycle of 5ths | I | III | IV | VI | V | I |
| Cycle of 2nds | I | V | VII | IV | II | I |

Root movements descend by successive intervals of the second in second-cycle harmony.

**Modal Considerations:**
In phrygian mode, the cycle of seconds happens to include the natural primary chords of the mode (I, II, VII). The dominant equivalents II and VII contain the characteristic phrygian second scale step.

# Musical Context/Application
Second-relationship harmony is particularly well-suited to certain modes. In phrygian, the second-cycle primary chords naturally emphasize the modal character since both II and VII contain the distinctive lowered second scale degree. This alignment makes second-cycle harmony an effective way to maintain phrygian color throughout a composition.

When determining chord succession in a second relationship, progressions parallel the usual root progressions in fifth-based music. The ear accepts these relationships when they are consistently confirmed through passing chords and cadential movements.

# Common Confusions
The cycle of seconds should not be confused with step-wise voice leading or neighboring chord motion. The cycle of seconds is a structural relationship where II and VII function as primary harmonic pillars, not merely passing or neighboring chords moving by step.
