# Quick Definition
The series of upper partials generated by any sounding tone, which provides the acoustic foundation for understanding interval relationships, chord construction, and resonance in music.

# Formal Definition
Any tone generates a series of overtones or partials that reach upward indefinitely, though not all are audible. A tone has both vertical and horizontal implications; its overtones may be used simultaneously in chordal structures or consecutively in melodic lines. Basic harmonic materials may be traced to the overtone series, but only general facts concerning chordal structure and resonance are indicated by the partials.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Partial series from fundamental:
- Partial 1: fundamental (root)
- Partial 2: octave (2:1)
- Partial 3: fifth (3:2)
- Partial 4: octave (4:2)
- Partial 5: major third (5:4)
- Partial 6: fifth (6:4)
- Partial 7+: increasingly divergent from tempered scale

Chord derivations:
- Triad: partials 1-3-5
- Seventh chord: partials 1-3-5-7
- Ninth chord: partials 1-3-5-7-9
- Whole-tone chords: partials 7-11
- Chords by fourths: partials 6-8-9

# Musical Context/Application
The fifth (partial 3) is more powerful than the third (partial 5) because it is a lower partial. Resonant harmony is formed not by seeking higher overtones but by using overtones of overtones. For example, in a C-E-G-B chord, the seventh (B) is the fifth above the third (E). Chordal structures are most resonant when distances between members resemble the overtone series (wide spacing in lower register, close in upper).

# Common Confusions
Students may try to derive all chord structures directly from the overtone series. Persichetti notes that deduction beyond the 6th partial is not wholly practicable because the tempered scale does not coincide in pitch with partials 7, 11, 13, and 14. The acoustician's observations are useful only when blended with artistic intuition.
