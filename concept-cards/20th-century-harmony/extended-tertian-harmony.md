# Quick Definition
The practice of building chords by superimposing thirds beyond the traditional triad, creating seventh, ninth, eleventh, thirteenth, fifteenth, seventeenth, and ultimately twelve-note chords, with each extension adding density while reducing harmonic suppleness.

# Formal Definition
Extended tertian harmony encompasses all chords built by superimposing thirds, from simple triads through twelve-note formations. As chords grow larger, they add density but reduce suppleness. The technique treats traditionally dissonant chord members (seventh, ninth, eleventh, thirteenth) as stable entities that do not require preparation or resolution. Extended tertian chords may progress within or outside any scale formation, original or traditional.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
**Tertian Chord Progression (by size):**
| Size | Members | Notes | Character |
|------|---------|-------|-----------|
| Triad | R-3-5 | 3 | Maximum suppleness |
| Seventh | R-3-5-7 | 4 | Supple, independent |
| Ninth | R-3-5-7-9 | 5 | Rich, polychordal potential |
| Eleventh | R-3-5-7-9-11 | 6 | Dense, reduced suppleness |
| Thirteenth | R-3-5-7-9-11-13 | 7 | Very dense, non-invertible |
| Fifteenth | R-3-5-7-9-11-13-15 | 8 | Chromatic extension |
| Seventeenth | R-3-5-7-9-11-13-15-17 | 9 | Near-total chromaticism |
| Twelve-note | All pitch classes | 12 | Maximum density |

**Inversibility:**
- Triads through ninths: fully invertible
- Elevenths: partially invertible
- Thirteenths and larger: non-invertible (reinterpretation occurs)

**Managing Density:**
- Omit chord members to increase suppleness
- Avoid separated triadic units (unless polychord desired)
- Consider spacing to prevent quartal or polychordal sound

# Musical Context/Application
Extended tertian harmony is a defining feature of twentieth-century harmonic practice. The emancipation of the seventh and ninth from their traditional dissonant treatment opened vast new harmonic resources. Composers can now choose chord size based on desired density and mobility, using large formations for static moments and smaller formations for active progression.

The technique relates to scale-chord correspondence: eleventh and thirteenth chords often coincide with scale formations, functioning as rich tonic formations.

# Common Confusions
Students often approach extended tertian harmony with common-practice expectations, treating sevenths and ninths as dissonances requiring resolution. In twentieth-century practice, these chord members are emancipated and may function as consonances. The choice of chord size is a compositional decision about density, not a matter of following resolution rules.
