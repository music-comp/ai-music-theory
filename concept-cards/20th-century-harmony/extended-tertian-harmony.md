# Quick Definition
The practice of building chords by superimposing thirds beyond the triad, creating seventh through twelve-note chords, with each extension adding density while reducing harmonic suppleness, and all traditionally dissonant members freed from preparation and resolution requirements.

# Core Definition
Extended tertian harmony encompasses all chords built by superimposing thirds, from triads through twelve-note formations. As chords grow larger, they add density but reduce suppleness. Traditionally dissonant chord members (seventh, ninth, eleventh, thirteenth) are treated as stable entities not requiring preparation or resolution. The progression from maximum suppleness (triad) through increasing density to near-immobility (twelve-note chord) is the central resource of tertian chord construction. Extended tertian chords may progress within or outside any scale formation, and chord size is a compositional decision about density, not a matter of following resolution rules.

# Prerequisites
- **Triads in twentieth-century harmony**: The base formation for all extensions

# Key Properties
1. Chord sizes: triad (3), seventh (4), ninth (5), eleventh (6), thirteenth (7), fifteenth (8), seventeenth (9), twelve-note (12)
2. Inversibility: triads through ninths fully invertible; elevenths partially; thirteenths and larger non-invertible
3. Density increases and suppleness decreases with size
4. Traditionally dissonant members are emancipated from resolution requirements
5. Chord member omission manages density for larger formations
6. Voicing determines whether a chord sounds tertian, quartal, or polychordal
7. Scale-chord correspondence emerges at eleventh and thirteenth levels

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the largest tertian extension present
- Check for omissions vs. true smaller chord
- Voicing determines sonic identity (thirds = tertian; fourths = quartal; separated triads = polychord)
- Reduce apparent large chords to smallest basic formation to distinguish structural from ornamental tones
- Manage density through strategic omission and spacing

# Context & Application
Extended tertian harmony is a defining feature of twentieth-century harmonic practice. The emancipation of the seventh and ninth opened vast resources. Composers choose chord size based on desired density and mobility: triads and sevenths for active progression, ninths for rich sequential writing, elevenths and thirteenths as tonic formations, fifteenths and beyond for special effects. The technique relates to scale-chord correspondence at the eleventh and thirteenth levels.

# Common Errors
- Treating sevenths and ninths as dissonances requiring resolution in twentieth-century contexts
- Equating chord size with importance (larger is not necessarily better)

# Common Confusions
- **Emancipation, not elimination**: Seventh and ninth can still be treated as dissonances in specific formal contexts; the point is that they need not be
- **Size as choice**: Chord size is a density decision, not a complexity hierarchy
