# Quick Definition

A chord constructed by superimposing combinations of miscellaneous intervals rather than using a single organizing principle such as thirds, fourths, or seconds.

# Formal Definition

A **compound chord** is formed by superimposing mixed intervals not arranged in polychordal units, creating a simultaneous combination of heterogeneous intervals. Unlike tertian, quartal, or secundal structures that follow a single intervallic logic, compound chords draw from any combination of varied intervals. They do not include chords formed by inverting other structures, as those retain their inherent root feeling.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation

Compound chords may be organized by:
- **Intervallic tension distribution**: Consonant or dissonant areas placed in bottom, middle, or top portions
- **Graphic interval plans**: Inner construction logic rather than tension arrangement
- **Alternating patterns**: Odd- or even-numbered intervals counted in semitones
- **Pyramidal construction**: Intervals diminishing from bottom upward
- **Overtone series modeling**: Intervals arranged to reflect natural harmonic relationships

Example construction principles:
- Chromatic aggregate chords: All twelve tones with eleven symmetrically invertible intervals
- Pyramidal chord: Series of intervals diminishing from bottom (e.g., P5, P4, M3, m3, M2, m2 ascending)

# Musical Context/Application

Compound chords have little or no root significance and function as masses of sound rather than tonal entities. They are particularly effective in:
- Orchestra, band, and piano four-hands writing due to their resounding tonal intensity
- Declamatory passages, arrival points, opening and closing statements
- Percussive accentuation when smaller intervals are placed low in the structure
- Sustained backgrounds supporting independent foreground material
- Cadential treatment, usually preceded by structures of greater chordal density

The polyinterval texture of compound chords enables harmonic areas to shift easily in any direction, making them flexible for non-tonal harmonic progression.

# Common Confusions

- **Compound vs. Added-Note chords**: If a chord shows strong tendencies to progress in a tonal sphere, it is an added-note chord; otherwise it is compound. Context determines the analysis.
- **Compound vs. Inverted structures**: Inverted tertian, quartal, or secundal chords are not compound because they retain root feeling.
- **Root significance**: Compound chords typically lack root significance, unlike other chord types that can be analyzed with traditional root theory.
