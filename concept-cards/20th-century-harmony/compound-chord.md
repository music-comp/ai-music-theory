# Quick Definition

A chord constructed by superimposing combinations of miscellaneous intervals not arranged in polychordal units, lacking root significance and functioning as a mass of sound rather than a tonal entity.

# Core Definition

"Another type of chord is made by superimposing combinations of miscellaneous intervals. This simultaneous combination of mixed intervals, not arranged in polychordal units, is a compound chord. Compound harmony does not include the chords of miscellaneous intervals formed by inverting tertian, quartal, or secundal structures, because these chords retain their inherent root feeling" (Persichetti, p. 162). "Compound chords have little or no root significance and are usually handled simply as masses of sound" (p. 167).

# Prerequisites

- **Polychord** — The compound chord is distinguished from polychords
- **Secundal harmony** — One of the chord types whose inversions are excluded from compound classification
- **Quartal harmony** — Another excluded inversion source

# Key Properties

1. Superimposed combinations of miscellaneous (mixed) intervals
2. Not arranged in polychordal units (key distinction from polychords)
3. Does not include inverted tertian, quartal, or secundal structures (these retain root feeling)
4. Little or no root significance
5. Handled as masses of sound
6. No likely scale dominates the harmony
7. Any compound structure may serve as a central chord through surrounding tension relationships
8. Effective in orchestra, band, and piano four-hands
9. The polyinterval texture enables harmonic areas to shift easily in any direction

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct
1. Combine intervals of varied types (thirds, fourths, seconds, fifths, etc.)
2. Distribute intervals to create desired consonant/dissonant areas (bottom, middle, top)
3. Avoid arrangements that could be heard as polychordal units
4. Consider graphic interval plans or tension distribution strategies

## To Distinguish from Other Chord Types
1. If it shows strong tonal-sphere progression tendencies: added-note chord, not compound
2. If formed by inverting a tertian/quartal/secundal structure: retains root feeling, not compound
3. If arranged in clear chordal units from different harmonic areas: polychord, not compound
4. Context determines analysis in ambiguous cases

# Context & Application

Compound chords do not readily subordinate themselves to tonal regulation of root movement or key. Harmonic motion is created by fluctuating density and shifting degrees of intervallic tension. Compound chords are often large and complex, effective as declamations, arrival points, opening/closing statements, and percussive accentuations. The slightest miscalculation in interval placement can upset the intended balance.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Analyzing compound chords with traditional root theory
  **Correction**: Compound chords have little or no root significance; analyze through density and tension instead
- **Error**: Minor miscalculations in interval placement
  **Correction**: "The slightest miscalculation can upset the intended balance" (p. 169); precise aural attention to interval and medium color is essential

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Compound chords and polychords are the same
  **Clarification**: Polychords maintain distinct chordal units; compound chords use mixed intervals without polychordal organization
- **Confusion**: Compound chords and added-note chords are the same
  **Clarification**: If a chord shows tonal-sphere progression tendencies, it is added-note; otherwise compound. Context determines the analysis (p. 167)
- **Confusion**: Inverted tertian/quartal/secundal chords are compound
  **Clarification**: These retain their inherent root feeling and are not compound chords
