# Quick Definition
A special category of added-note chord consisting of a close-position triad in the upper voices with a note added as the bottom voice, forming a major or minor second with any triadic member, yielding 36 possible chord structures.

# Formal Definition
A triad (major, minor, diminished, or augmented) is placed in the upper voices and a major or minor second above or below any of the members is added as the bottom voice, often in octaves for balance. This creates a wide variety of chordal structures; there are thirty-six such chords.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Construction:
- Upper voices: Close-position triad (any quality)
- Bass: Note forming M2 or m2 with any triadic member
- Optional: Bass doubled at octave for balance

36 possible structures:
- 4 triad qualities (major, minor, diminished, augmented)
- 3 triad members to relate to (root, third, fifth)
- 3 interval relationships (M2 above, M2 below, m2 above, m2 below)
- Calculation: 4 x 3 x 3 = 36 (some with enharmonic equivalents)

Structural identity:
- Some structures resemble traditional chords by thirds
- In added-note context, they do not function as traditional chords
- Context determines functional interpretation

# Musical Context/Application
Progression in this type of added-note harmony is often governed by an upper melodic line. Each note of a melody (except ornamental tones) may be considered a member of a major, minor, diminished, or augmented triad. A bass line is then constructed from notes a second from any triadic member.

This technique is most effective when:
- Used in progressions consisting solely of this type
- Primary and secondary relationships are not relevant
- Melody is the governing factor
- Textural consistency is maintained by chordal spacing
- Freedom of linear movement is enabled by wide bass note selection

# Common Confusions
- These are not inversions of larger chords; they are triads with added bass notes
- The resemblance to traditional chords does not imply traditional function
- The bass note is chosen for linear reasons, not for harmonic root function
- This technique works best in isolation from traditional functional harmony
