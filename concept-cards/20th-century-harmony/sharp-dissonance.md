# Quick Definition
Minor seconds and major sevenths, which produce an intense, cutting sound quality and represent the most tension-filled intervals in the chromatic scale.

# Formal Definition
Sharp dissonances are the minor second and major seventh intervals, characterized by their intense, biting quality derived from high partials in the overtone series. These intervals create maximum harmonic tension and are fundamental to twentieth-century harmonic vocabulary as primary sources of harmonic color and intensity.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
- Minor second: approximately 16:15 frequency ratio (high partials)
- Major seventh: approximately 15:8 frequency ratio
- When inverted: minor second becomes major seventh (intensity changes but tension quality inverts)
- When spaced more than an octave apart (compound intervals), sharp dissonances become less biting yet more brilliant

# Musical Context/Application
Sharp dissonances seem consistent in double reeds playing loudly, creating a raucous effect. The same intervals assigned to muted strings create an introspective quality. A doubled dissonant tone increases the bite. When inverted, the cutting minor second opens into a wide-spreading major seventh and thereby loses some of its sting. In consistently dissonant harmony, sharp dissonances may become the "consonant" norm.

# Common Confusions
Students often assume sharp dissonances must be avoided or resolved. In twentieth-century practice, sharp dissonances are essential colors that may be sustained, emphasized, or used as stable sonorities depending on context.
