# Quick Definition
The strategic removal of specific chord tones from extended tertian structures to increase harmonic suppleness, reduce density, or modify color, with different omissions producing different effects.

# Formal Definition
If some members of eleventh and thirteenth chords are omitted, it is possible to attain a certain freedom of harmonic movement. Large tertian chords usually contain two sharp dissonant intervals. Omitting one of the tones that forms a sharp dissonant interval lessens the dissonant content and makes relatively supple harmonic movement possible. Different chord members produce different effects when omitted.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
**Ninth Chord Omissions:**
- Fifth omitted: creates richness
- Third or seventh omitted: reduces color intensity

**Eleventh/Thirteenth Chord Omissions:**
- Omit one tone forming a sharp dissonant interval: enables suppler movement
- Example: In a thirteenth chord where C-B and E-F are sharp intervals, E, B, or F are choice omissions
- Root omitted from thirteenth: produces eleventh chord
- Two or three inner tones omitted: thins texture for manipulation

**Fourth Inversion Ninth Chord:**
- Omitting the fifth: produces more suppleness
- Seventh omitted in minor dominant ninth: common in parallel harmony

**Large Chord Principle:**
For fifteenth and seventeenth chords, omit two or three inner tones for purposes of manipulation while avoiding separated triadic units.

# Musical Context/Application
Chord member omission is essential technique for working with large tertian formations. Without omission, these chords are too dense and cumbersome for most harmonic purposes. The technique allows composers to:

1. Suggest large chord sonority without full density
2. Enable voice leading that would be impossible with complete chords
3. Control dissonance level while maintaining chord identity
4. Balance between richness and mobility

# Common Confusions
Students may think omission changes chord identity. In extended tertian harmony, the identity of a chord is maintained even with omissions, as long as the root and defining upper extension remain. A thirteenth chord with omitted fifth is still a thirteenth chord, not a different chord type.
