# Quick Definition
The strategic removal of specific chord tones from extended tertian structures to increase harmonic suppleness, reduce density, or modify color, with different omissions producing predictably different effects.

# Core Definition
If some members of eleventh and thirteenth chords are omitted, it is possible to attain a certain freedom of harmonic movement. Large tertian chords usually contain two sharp dissonant intervals. Omitting one of the tones that forms a sharp dissonant interval lessens the dissonant content and makes relatively supple harmonic movement possible. For ninth chords: omit the fifth for richness, the third or seventh for less color. For the fourth inversion ninth: omitting the fifth produces more suppleness. For fifteenth and seventeenth chords: omit two or three inner tones, avoiding separated triadic units.

# Prerequisites
- **Extended tertian harmony**: The chord sizes requiring omission

# Key Properties
1. Ninth chord: fifth omitted = richness; third/seventh omitted = less color
2. Fourth inversion ninth: fifth omitted = more suppleness; seventh omitted in minor dominant = common in parallel harmony
3. Eleventh/thirteenth: omit tones forming sharp dissonant intervals
4. In thirteenth: omitting root produces eleventh; E, B, or F are choice omissions (in C thirteenth)
5. Fifteenth/seventeenth: omit two or three inner tones
6. Avoid creating separated triadic units (unless polychord desired)
7. Chord identity is maintained even with omissions

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify sharp dissonant intervals in large chords
- Choose omission targets that reduce sharp content while preserving chord identity
- Verify that omissions do not create unintended polychordal separation
- Root + defining upper extension must remain for chord identity
- A thirteenth with omitted fifth is still a thirteenth

# Context & Application
Chord member omission is essential technique for working with large tertian formations. Without omission, these chords are too dense and cumbersome for most harmonic purposes. The technique allows composers to suggest large chord sonority without full density, enable voice leading impossible with complete chords, and control dissonance level while maintaining chord identity.

# Common Errors
- Thinking omission changes chord identity (it does not, if root and defining extension remain)
- Omitting the root of a thirteenth when an eleventh is not intended

# Common Confusions
- **Identity preserved**: A thirteenth chord with omitted fifth is still a thirteenth chord
- **Root omission in thirteenth**: Produces an eleventh chord, not an incomplete thirteenth
