# Quick Definition
A harmonic tension created when a tone in one voice of a chord is chromatically altered in another voice of the following chord, with tension greater in simple harmony and barely felt in chromatic contexts.

# Core Definition
A **cross relation** exists when a tone in one voice of a chord is altered in another voice of the following chord. The tension created is greater in simple harmony and barely felt in highly chromatic harmony. In harmony that uses all twelve tones with equal frequency, no such relationship is felt. Cross relation occurs quite naturally in triads moving in chromatic third relationship. Cross relation between a chord tone and a nonharmonic tone is less noticeable. The simultaneous sounding of altered and unaltered tones has a pungent flavor; in major-minor compound harmony this sound is firmly fixed (Ch. 11, pp. 239-240).

# Prerequisites
- **Chromatic alteration** -- understanding alteration within scale context

# Key Properties
1. Same pitch class appears in different chromatic forms in adjacent chords, in different voices
2. Tension scaling: maximum in simple/diatonic harmony, minimal in twelve-tone contexts
3. Occurs naturally in chromatic third relationships (e.g., C major to E major: G vs. G#)
4. Between chord tone and nonharmonic tone: less noticeable
5. Simultaneous altered/unaltered tones: pungent flavor (fleeting in ornamentation, fixed in major-minor compound harmony)

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for a pitch class appearing in two chromatic forms across adjacent chords in different voices
- Assess the surrounding chromatic density to gauge perceptual impact
- Note whether the cross relation involves two chord tones or a chord tone and nonharmonic tone

# Context & Application
Cross relation is a natural byproduct of chromatic third-related triadic progressions and an expressive resource in diatonic contexts. Its effect diminishes as chromatic saturation increases.

# Examples
**Example 1** (Ch. 11, Ex. 11-22, p. 239): Cross relation in triads moving by chromatic thirds.
**Example 2** (Ch. 11, Ex. 11-23, p. 239): Cross relation between chord tone and nonharmonic tone (less noticeable).

# Common Errors
- Avoiding cross relations reflexively when they would add desirable tension
- Using cross relations in chromatic contexts where they have no perceptual impact

# Common Confusions
- Cross relation is between different voices in adjacent chords, not within the same chord
- The effect diminishes as chromatic saturation increases
- Cross relation with nonharmonic tones is less prominent than between chord tones
