# Quick Definition
A harmonic tension created when a tone in one voice of a chord is chromatically altered in another voice of the following chord.

# Formal Definition
A cross relation exists when a tone in one voice of a chord is altered in another voice of the following chord. The tension created by this device is greater in simple harmony and barely felt in highly chromatic harmony. In harmony that uses all twelve tones with equal frequency, no such relationship is felt at all, as the chromatic distinction loses meaning in a fully chromatic context.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Cross relation characteristics:
- **Mechanism**: Same pitch class appears in different chromatic forms in adjacent chords
- **Voice distribution**: Chromatic versions occur in different voices
- **Tension scaling**: Greater effect in diatonic contexts, less in chromatic contexts
- **Natural occurrence**: Appears quite naturally in triads moving in chromatic third relationship

Tension gradient:
- Maximum: Simple/diatonic harmony
- Moderate: Chromatic but hierarchical harmony
- Minimal: Twelve-tone equal-frequency contexts

# Musical Context/Application
Cross relation occurs quite naturally in triads moving in chromatic third relationship (e.g., C major to E major, where G natural in one voice becomes G# in another). Cross relation between a chord tone and a nonharmonic tone is less noticeable than between two chord tones. The simultaneous sounding of altered and unaltered tones has a pungent flavor, which when created by ornamentation is fleeting but in major-minor compound harmony is firmly fixed.

# Common Confusions
- Cross relation is between different voices in adjacent chords, not within the same chord
- The effect diminishes as chromatic saturation increases
- Cross relation with nonharmonic tones is less prominent than between chord tones
