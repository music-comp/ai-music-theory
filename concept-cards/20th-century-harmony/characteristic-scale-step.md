# Quick Definition
The scale degree that distinguishes one mode from major or natural minor, which must be emphasized in harmonic progressions to maintain the modal flavor.

# Core Definition
Each mode possesses a characteristic scale step that produces its principal flavor and distinguishes it from major or natural minor. The distinctive flavor of modes is exploited by employing harmonic progressions in which this characteristic step occurs often. This tone keeps the mode from becoming major or natural minor. For example, a lydian passage on D should contain a high percentage of chords that include the tone G# (raised fourth step); otherwise the lydian flavor will be lost.

# Key Properties
1. **Lydian**: raised 4th step (distinguishes from major)
2. **Mixolydian**: lowered 7th step (distinguishes from major)
3. **Dorian**: raised 6th step (distinguishes from natural minor)
4. **Phrygian**: lowered 2nd step (distinguishes from natural minor)
5. **Locrian**: diminished 5th from tonic (creates unstable tonic triad)
6. **Ionian/Aeolian**: already ARE major/natural minor, so no distinguishing step needed
7. **Frequency requirement**: chords containing the characteristic step must appear often

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the mode in use
2. Locate the scale degree that differs from the parallel major or natural minor
3. Check whether harmonic progressions include this tone frequently
4. If the characteristic step is absent from harmonies, the passage risks sounding like major or minor

# Context & Application
The characteristic step is the linchpin of modal identity. Primary chords of a mode are the tonic plus the two triads that include this step -- these serve as "dominant equivalents." Without frequent emphasis on the characteristic step, the ionic power of the tritone will pull the music into major. Melodic circling or chant-like repetition of the tonic tone and frequent cadences also help maintain modal identity.

# Examples
- D Lydian: characteristic G# should appear in chords I, II, and VII frequently
- D Mixolydian: characteristic C-natural defines primary chords I, V, VII
- D Dorian: characteristic B-natural appears in primary chords I, II, IV
- D Phrygian: characteristic Eb appears in primary chords I, II, VII

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Writing modal passages without emphasizing the characteristic step. **Correction**: The characteristic tone must appear frequently, especially in primary chords, or the music will sound like major or minor.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking any non-diatonic tone creates modal flavor. **Clarification**: Only the specific characteristic step of each mode creates its distinctive flavor; other alterations may suggest different modes or chromaticism.
