# Quick Definition
The scale degree that distinguishes one mode from another, which must be emphasized in harmonic progressions to maintain the modal flavor.

# Formal Definition
Each mode possesses a characteristic scale step that produces its principal flavor and distinguishes it from major or natural minor. The primary chords of a mode are the tonic plus the two major or minor triads that include this characteristic step. Frequent use of chords containing this tone maintains the modal identity.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Characteristic steps by mode:
- Lydian: raised 4th step (distinguishes from major)
- Mixolydian: lowered 7th step (distinguishes from major)
- Dorian: raised 6th step (distinguishes from natural minor)
- Phrygian: lowered 2nd step (distinguishes from natural minor)
- Locrian: diminished 5th from tonic (creates unstable tonic triad)

Primary chord identification:
- Tonic (I) + two triads containing the characteristic step
- These become the "dominant equivalents" of modal harmony

# Musical Context/Application
The distinctive flavor of modes is exploited by employing harmonic progressions in which the characteristic scale step occurs often. This tone keeps the mode from becoming major or natural minor. For example, a lydian passage on D should contain a high percentage of chords that include G# (raised fourth step); otherwise the lydian flavor will be lost.

# Examples
- D Lydian: characteristic G# should appear in chords I, II, and VII frequently
- D Mixolydian: characteristic C-natural defines primary chords I, V, VII
- D Dorian: characteristic B-natural appears in primary chords I, II, IV
- D Phrygian: characteristic Eb appears in primary chords I, II, VII

# Common Confusions
Students may write modal passages without emphasizing the characteristic step, resulting in music that sounds like major or minor rather than the intended mode. The characteristic tone must appear frequently, especially in primary chords, to maintain modal identity.
