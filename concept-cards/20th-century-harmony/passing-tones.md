# Quick Definition
Nonharmonic tones that provide linear stepwise motion between chord tones, approached and left by step in the same direction.

# Formal Definition
Passing tones belong to the first category of nonharmonic tones: those approached and left without skip. They create linear connection between chord tones, moving stepwise in a consistent direction. In twentieth-century practice, passing tones may be diatonic, chromatic, or drawn from synthetic scale sources including scales built upon the chord root.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Passing tone characteristics:
- **Approach**: By step from chord tone
- **Departure**: By step to chord tone (same direction)
- **Direction**: Ascending or descending, but consistent
- **Scalar source**: May be diatonic, chromatic, or from synthetic scales
- **Grouping**: May be grouped chord-wise to form passing chords

When multiple passing tones occur simultaneously, they create passing chords that ornament the harmonic progression as complete vertical units.

# Musical Context/Application
In twentieth-century harmony, passing tones operate with expanded scalar resources. Ornamental tones may be derived from a scale built upon the root of the chord, with each root embellished as though it were a tonic. Passing tones may also be derived from contrasting key centers, creating temporary polytonality within the ornamental figuration. When grouped into chords, passing formations create smooth transitions between harmonic pillars.

# Common Confusions
- Passing tones move through, not around, the space between chord tones
- In 20th-century practice, passing tones need not come from the prevailing diatonic scale
- When grouped chord-wise, passing tones form complete passing chords
