# Quick Definition
The reinforcement of chord tones by duplication, and the arrangement of chord members so that specific interval types monopolize the texture, including the use of intervallic strands in contrary motion.

# Core Definition
Any kind of chord has its own natural doubling (such as root doubling in major triads), but in twentieth-century practice any note may be doubled, tripled, or omitted for specific textural purposes. Doubling enriches simple chords, points up characteristic part-writing, or strengthens certain parts of a chord. A doubled major third adds color; a doubled dissonant tone increases the bite. Excessive coupling (all voices doubled) produces percussive-sounding harmony. Chord members may be arranged so that specific interval kinds monopolize the pattern, and the chosen interval may form separate intervallic strands that move in contrary motion.

# Key Properties
1. **Root doubling**: traditional stability
2. **Third doubling**: adds color
3. **Dissonant tone doubling**: increases bite
4. **Excessive coupling** (all voices doubled): percussive effect
5. **Intervallic monopoly**: arranging chord members so one interval type dominates
6. **Contrary-motion strands**: the chosen interval forms lines moving in opposite directions
7. **Freedom of choice**: any note may be doubled, tripled, or omitted for textural purposes

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify which chord members are doubled and at what intervals (unison, octave)
2. Note whether specific interval types dominate the voicing
3. Look for intervallic strands moving in contrary motion
4. Assess whether doubling serves enrichment, part-writing clarity, or percussive effect

# Context & Application
The choice of which tone to double is made for textural and expressive purposes rather than following rigid common-practice rules. Closely spaced harmony crowded with small intervals clears when each chord is broken into a succession of intervals in two-part writing. Both cloudy and clear chordal materials are essential ingredients in musical composition.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Following rigid common-practice doubling rules in 20th-century contexts. **Correction**: Any chord member may be doubled for specific textural purposes; the choice is based on desired color and effect.
- **Error**: Always avoiding parallel motion in doubled voices. **Correction**: Parallel and contrary motion in intervallic strands are both valid textures.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking excessive coupling is always undesirable. **Clarification**: Percussive-sounding harmony from excessive coupling is a deliberate textural choice, not an error.
