# Quick Definition
The reinforcement of chord tones by duplicating them at the unison or octave, which can enrich simple chords, highlight part-writing, strengthen specific elements, or add percussive quality.

# Formal Definition
Any kind of chord has, under "normal" conditions, its own natural doubling, such as the doubling of roots in major triads. However, any note may be doubled, tripled, or omitted for specific textural purposes. Doubling may be used to enrich simple chords, to point up characteristic part-writing, or to strengthen certain parts of a chord.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Doubling effects:
- Root doubling: traditional stability
- Third doubling: adds color
- Fifth doubling: strengthens foundation
- Dissonant tone doubling: increases bite
- Excessive coupling (all voices doubled): percussive effect

Types of doubling:
- At unison: reinforcement
- At octave: enrichment and spread
- Multiple octaves: orchestral weight

# Musical Context/Application
A doubled major third adds color and a doubled dissonant tone increases the bite. Excessive coupling (all voices doubled) produces percussive-sounding harmony. In twentieth-century practice, the choice of which tone to double is made for textural and expressive purposes rather than following rigid rules about root doubling.

# Common Confusions
Students trained in common-practice rules may avoid doubling thirds or leading tones. In twentieth-century harmony, any chord member may be doubled for specific textural purposes, with the choice based on desired color and effect rather than avoidance rules.
