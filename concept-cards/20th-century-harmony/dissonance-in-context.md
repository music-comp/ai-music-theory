# Quick Definition
The principle that any combination of tones is relatively consonant if there is a more dissonant combination nearby, and that dissonance exists only where a norm of consonance is implied or stated.

# Core Definition
Any combination of simultaneously sounding tones is relatively consonant if there is a more dissonant combination in the surrounding area. An abundance of dissonance can result in the feeling of a consonant texture because harmonic tension often relaxes in chords of the same values. The juxtaposition of chords of different values creates strong harmonic tension. A chord is dissonant only in relation to an overall harmonic scheme, and at times the "consonant" chord is the restless one. Because of melodic pull and chordal relationships, a "consonant" chord may have strong urge to move while a "dissonant" chord remains unflustered. Dissonance exists only where there is a norm of consonance implied or stated (Ch. 9, pp. 193-195).

# Key Properties
1. Consonance and dissonance are relative, not absolute
2. Uniform dissonance levels can feel consonant through consistent texture
3. Juxtaposition of different tension levels creates strong harmonic tension
4. A traditionally consonant chord may be restless in dissonant context
5. Consonant-dissonant relationship may be reversed: start/end with dissonance as norm
6. Sequential patterns, strong melodic lines, and characteristic intervals keep extreme dissonance in check
7. A dissonant chord may represent a tonality and generate ideas for a large work

# Construction / Recognition
- Evaluate each chord's tension relative to its immediate context, not in isolation
- Identify the normative harmonic tension level of the passage
- Note whether "consonant" chords create more restlessness than "dissonant" ones
- Observe whether increasing/decreasing dissonance contributes to phrase shape and cadential design

# Context & Application
Increasing and decreasing consonance or dissonance contributes to phrase shaping, cadential progressions, and sectional articulation. Consonant-dissonant relationship may be reversed by starting and ending with a dissonant chord as the norm; consonance then resolves to dissonance. Extremely dissonant chords are kept in check by sequential patterns, overbearing melodic lines, and characteristic melodic intervals. A dissonant chord has special uses: it sometimes ends a phrase when used to begin the next phrase, or is repeated so dynamically and intensely that the need for resolution is eradicated.

# Common Errors
- Evaluating dissonance in isolation rather than in relation to surrounding context
- Assuming more dissonance always means more tension
- Neglecting the possibility of reversed consonance-dissonance relationships

# Common Confusions
- **Absolute vs. relative dissonance**: Intervals have inherent acoustical properties, but their effect depends on context
- **Dissonance as error**: In twentieth-century harmony, dissonance is a resource, not a mistake to be resolved
- **More dissonance = more tension**: Context determines tension; uniform dissonance may feel stable
