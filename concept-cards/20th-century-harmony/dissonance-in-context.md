# Quick Definition

The understanding that any combination of tones is relatively consonant if there is a more dissonant combination nearby, and that a chord is dissonant only in relation to an overall harmonic scheme.

# Formal Definition

**Dissonance in context** refers to the principle that consonance and dissonance are relative rather than absolute qualities in twentieth-century harmony. Any combination of simultaneously sounding tones is relatively consonant if there is a more dissonant combination in the surrounding area. An abundance of dissonance can result in the feeling of a consonant texture because harmonic tension often relaxes in chords of the same values. A chord is dissonant only in relation to an overall harmonic scheme, and at times the "consonant" chord may be the restless one due to melodic pull and chordal relationships.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation

Contextual principles:
- **Relative consonance**: Any chord is consonant relative to a more dissonant surrounding
- **Tension relaxation**: Uniform dissonance levels can feel consonant through consistent texture
- **Contextual restlessness**: A traditionally consonant chord may be restless in dissonant context
- **Normative dissonance**: Dissonance exists only where consonance is implied or stated as norm

Reversal technique:
- Start and end with dissonant chord as norm
- Consonance then resolves to dissonance
- Traditional tension-resolution relationship inverted

# Musical Context/Application

Understanding contextual dissonance enables:
- Effective use of extreme dissonance within appropriate context
- Control of tension through changing harmonic norms
- Phrase shaping through increasing/decreasing consonance-dissonance
- Cadential design using relative tension relationships

Dissonance management techniques:
- Sequential patterns contain extreme dissonance
- Strong melodic lines overshadow dissonant tension
- Characteristic melodic intervals provide coherence
- Dissonant chord may represent tonality and generate form

# Common Confusions

- **Absolute vs. relative dissonance**: Intervals have inherent acoustical properties, but their effect depends on context.
- **Dissonance as error**: In twentieth-century harmony, dissonance is a resource, not a mistake to be resolved.
- **More dissonance = more tension**: Context determines tension; extreme dissonance in uniformly dissonant context may feel stable.
