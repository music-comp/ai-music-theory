# Quick Definition
The tonic plus two "dominant equivalent" triads that include the characteristic scale step of each mode, forming the harmonic foundation of modal writing.

# Formal Definition
The primary chords in modal harmony are the tonic plus two dominant equivalents. These double dominants are those major or minor triads that include the characteristic scale step which produces the principal flavor of the mode. In each mode there lurks a diminished triad that must be handled carefully because its tritone tends to suggest the dominant seventh of the relative major key.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Primary chords by mode:
- Lydian: I, II, VII (secondary: III, V, VI; diminished: IV)
- Mixolydian: I, V, VII (secondary: II, IV, VI; diminished: III)
- Dorian: I, II, IV (secondary: III, V, VII; diminished: VI)
- Phrygian: I, II, VII (secondary: III, IV, VI; diminished: V)

The diminished triad in each mode:
- Creates restless tendency due to tritone
- Often chromatically altered for stability
- Located on different scale degrees in each mode

# Musical Context/Application
The selection and distribution of primary and secondary chords within a given segment of music helps give it individual sound. A pure modal passage harmonizes modal melody with chords from the same mode on the same tonal center. Triads, sevenths, and ninths progress easily from one to another while remaining in the same mode.

# Common Confusions
Students may apply major/minor harmonic patterns (I-IV-V-I) to modal writing. Modal harmony requires emphasizing the chords containing the characteristic step, which often excludes or de-emphasizes the traditional dominant function.
