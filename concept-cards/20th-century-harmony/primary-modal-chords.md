# Quick Definition
The tonic plus two "dominant equivalent" triads that include the characteristic scale step of each mode, forming the harmonic foundation of modal writing.

# Core Definition
The primary chords in modal harmony are the tonic plus two dominant equivalents. These double dominants are those major or minor triads that include the characteristic scale step which produces the principal flavor of the mode. In each mode there lurks a diminished triad; this is a difficult chord because its diminished fifth tends to suggest the dominant seventh of the major key with the same number of sharps or flats as the mode. The selection and distribution of primary and secondary chords within a given segment of music helps give it individual sound.

# Key Properties
1. **Lydian**: I, II, VII (secondary: III, V, VI; diminished: IV)
2. **Mixolydian**: I, V, VII (secondary: II, IV, VI; diminished: III)
3. **Dorian**: I, II, IV (secondary: III, V, VII; diminished: VI)
4. **Phrygian**: I, II, VII (secondary: III, IV, VI; diminished: V)
5. **Diminished triad in each mode**: restless due to tritone, often chromatically altered
6. **Chord succession**: triads, sevenths, and ninths progress easily within the same mode
7. **Harmonic rhythm**: the distribution of primary vs. secondary chords shapes the music's character

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the mode and its characteristic scale step
2. Find the tonic triad (I)
3. Identify the two other major or minor triads containing the characteristic step
4. These three are the primary chords; remaining triads are secondary
5. Locate the diminished triad and note its position for careful handling

# Context & Application
A pure modal passage harmonizes modal melody with chords from the same mode on the same tonal center. The primary chords carry the modal flavor; secondary chords function within their gravitation. The diminished triad's tritone threatens to suggest the relative major's dominant seventh, potentially destroying modal identity.

# Examples

- Ex. 2-3: D Mixolydian -- primary (I, V, VII), secondary (II, IV, VI), diminished (III)
- Ex. 2-4: D Dorian -- primary (I, II, IV), secondary (III, V, VII), diminished (VI)

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Applying major/minor harmonic patterns (I-IV-V-I) to modal writing. **Correction**: Modal harmony requires emphasizing chords containing the characteristic step, which often excludes or de-emphasizes traditional dominant function.
- **Error**: Using the diminished triad freely. **Correction**: Its tritone suggests the relative major's dominant seventh; handle carefully or alter chromatically.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming "dominant equivalents" function like tonal dominants. **Clarification**: They sustain modal flavor rather than creating tension-resolution patterns; they are "dominant" in importance, not in harmonic function.
