# Quick Definition
Triads derived from synthetic scales, which often contain augmented or diminished thirds that sound like fourths or seconds, usually requiring alteration to one of the four basic triad types (major, minor, diminished, augmented).

# Core Definition
Cyclic relationships may be constructed in any scale, synthetic or otherwise. Synthetic scales often contain triads that have augmented or diminished thirds. These are awkward because they sound like fourths or seconds and usually result in misspelling a chord. Such chords are often altered to one of the four basic triads -- major, minor, diminished, or augmented -- in which only major and minor thirds are present. The colors of primary chords in any cyclic relationship are determined by the intervallic make-up of the scale in force and will vary considerably across different synthetic constructions.

# Key Properties
1. Synthetic scales may produce triads with augmented or diminished thirds
2. Augmented third = enharmonically equivalent to perfect fourth (sounds quartal)
3. Diminished third = enharmonically equivalent to major second (sounds secundal)
4. These awkward triads are usually altered to standard major, minor, diminished, or augmented
5. All four basic triads use only major and minor thirds
6. Primary chord colors vary by the intervallic make-up of the scale

# Construction / Recognition
- Build triads in thirds from synthetic scale degrees
- Identify any thirds that are augmented or diminished
- An augmented-third triad will sound like a quartal formation, not tertian
- A diminished-third triad will sound like a secundal formation, not tertian
- Alter problematic intervals to the nearest major or minor third
- Maintain the synthetic scale's melodic character while correcting chordal spelling

# Context & Application
When working with synthetic scales, composers face a choice: maintain the precise intervallic content (producing unusual triads with augmented or diminished thirds) or alter these triads to standard forms. Most often, composers alter to standard triads to maintain clear tertian sonority while preserving the melodic character of the synthetic scale. The decision depends on whether the quartal or secundal quality of the unusual triads serves the musical purpose.

# Common Errors
- Building triads strictly from synthetic scale degrees without considering the sonic result
- Failing to recognize when an augmented third produces a quartal sound

# Common Confusions
- **Spelling vs. sound**: A triad with an augmented third is spelled as a triad but sounds like a quartal formation
- **Alteration vs. distortion**: Altering awkward thirds to standard forms preserves the scale's melodic identity while correcting the chordal sonority
