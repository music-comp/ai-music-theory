# Quick Definition
Seven-note tertian chords built by adding another third above the eleventh, analyzed as three simultaneous triads with tones in common at two points, and notably non-invertible since any inversion produces another eleventh or thirteenth.

# Formal Definition
The multiple tones of thirteenth chords add density but reduce suppleness. These seven-note formations are cumbersome in harmonic progressions but useful in the general harmonic scheme. A thirteenth chord may be identified by considering the formation a simultaneous combination of three triads with tones in common at two points. The thirteenth chord is non-invertible; any attempt at inversion will produce another eleventh or thirteenth.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
**Construction:**
Seven notes built in thirds: Root + 3rd + 5th + 7th + 9th + 11th + 13th

**Polychordal Analysis:**
A thirteenth chord = three triads with common tones at two points
- Example: C thirteenth = C-E-G + E-G-B + G-B-D (overlapping triads)

**Non-Invertibility:**
Any attempt to invert a thirteenth chord produces another eleventh or thirteenth formation, not a true inversion of the original.

**Omission for Suppleness:**
Thirteenth chords usually contain two sharp dissonant intervals. Choice notes of omission include those forming sharp intervals (e.g., in a C thirteenth with C-B and E-F as sharp intervals, E, B, or F are good omissions). Omitting the root produces an eleventh chord.

# Musical Context/Application
The intervallic make-up of eleventh and thirteenth chords often coincides with various scale formations. The chords become excellent six- and seven-note tonic formations around which polychords and compound structures may function. These formations are usually guided by a melodic line derived from the scale implied by the rich tonic chord; they are seldom found in extensive part-writing.

Thirteenth sounds, like elevenths, are often created by pedal tones or melodic ornamentation. In analysis, apparent thirteenths should be reduced to the basic chord with fewest notes.

# Common Confusions
Students frequently attempt to invert thirteenth chords as they would smaller chords. The non-invertibility of thirteenth chords is a fundamental property: rearranging the bass note simply produces a different eleventh or thirteenth, not an inversion of the original chord. This is a key theoretical distinction.
