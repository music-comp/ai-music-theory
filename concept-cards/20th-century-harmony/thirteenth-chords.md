# Quick Definition
Seven-note tertian chords identified as three simultaneous triads with tones in common at two points, non-invertible (any inversion produces another eleventh or thirteenth), functioning best as rich tonic formations.

# Core Definition
A thirteenth chord may be identified by considering the formation a simultaneous combination of three triads with tones in common at two points. The thirteenth chord is non-invertible; any attempt at inversion will produce another eleventh or thirteenth. These seven-note formations are cumbersome in harmonic progressions but useful in the general harmonic scheme. Thirteenth chords usually contain two sharp dissonant intervals. The intervallic make-up often coincides with various scale formations, making them excellent seven-note tonic formations around which polychords and compound structures may function.

# Prerequisites
- **Eleventh chords**: Six-note tertian extension

# Key Properties
1. Seven notes built in thirds: R-3-5-7-9-11-13
2. Analyzed as three triads with common tones at two points
3. Non-invertible: any bass-note change produces another eleventh or thirteenth
4. Contains all seven notes of a diatonic scale when diatonic
5. Usually contains two sharp dissonant intervals
6. Choice omissions: tones forming sharp intervals (not root, which would produce an eleventh)
7. Intervallic make-up often coincides with scale formations
8. Seldom found in extensive part-writing
9. Guided by melodic line derived from the implied scale

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify as three overlapping triads
- Test non-invertibility: any bass change reinterprets the chord
- Omit tones forming sharp intervals for suppleness (E, B, or F in a C thirteenth where C-B and E-F are sharp)
- Check if intervallic content matches a known scale formation
- Distinguish from ornamented smaller chords (reduce to fewest notes)
- Avoid spacing that separates triadic units (polychord) or emphasizes fourths (quartal)

# Context & Application
Thirteenth chords excel as tonic formations around which polychords and compound structures function. Their seven-note completeness matches entire scale formations, providing both vertical and horizontal coherence. They combine well with seventh and ninth chords. Due to density, they are seldom found in extensive part-writing but serve as harmonic anchor points.

# Common Errors
- Attempting to invert thirteenth chords (produces different eleventh or thirteenth, not a true inversion)
- Mistaking ornamented smaller chords for structural thirteenths

# Common Confusions
- **Non-invertibility**: Changing the bass note does not create an "inversion" but a reinterpretation with a different root
- **Scale correspondence**: A diatonic thirteenth chord contains all seven scale tones; it IS the scale arranged in thirds
