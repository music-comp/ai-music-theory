# Quick Definition
The various methods by which a sense of key or tonal center is created in twentieth-century music, extending beyond traditional dominant-tonic relationships to include persistent dissonance, converging tonalities, recurring tones, and non-harmonic means.

# Core Definition
Key feeling may be created by forces other than the tonal pull of chordal pillars. Key consciousness may arise from an extremely dissonant chord refusing to become consonant, from a succession of simple chords resisting complex harmonic forces, from two culminating tonalities merging finally as a dominating polychord, from a group of important tones recurring at decisive points, or from atonal music searching for a key at cadential points. Key consciousness may vanish temporarily only to make stronger an emerging key's return.

In equidistant rootless harmony, any note may be made the tonal center through melodic insistence, spacing, or instrumentation. The search for tonality or key center feeling may become a creative force in music. Tonality may be established through contradictory tonal elements or through a streamlined drive to the tonic.

# Prerequisites
- **Tonality**: The organizational principle being established
- **Tonal center**: The target focal point
- **Tonal pillars**: The traditional method of establishment

# Key Properties
1. Traditional: three center-affirming chords (above, below, tonic) with tritone emphasis
2. Persistent dissonance: dissonant chord refusing consonance
3. Simple resistance: simple chords resisting complex forces
4. Convergence: two tonalities merging as polychord
5. Recurrence: important tones at decisive points
6. Cadential search: atonal music finding key at cadences
7. Melodic insistence in equidistant harmony
8. Spacing and registration emphasis
9. Instrumental timbre highlighting

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify which method is being used in a given passage
- Look for cadential points where key emerges
- Note persistent chords that resist resolution or change
- Track recurring pitch groups at structural moments
- In equidistant harmony, listen for melodic, registral, or timbral emphasis
- Observe whether key feeling strengthens after temporary absence

# Context & Application
The tonality of a structure may be generated from a unifying harmonic idea from which musical growth extends. An initial major-minor compound chord may promote polychordal writing that instigates a triadic struggle between major and minor structures; the formal conflict may not be resolved until an obstinate melody note, common to both triads, anchors them in a major-minor formation transposed from the original. A work may be built tonally upon initial harmony falling cadentially. A succession of chords at an opening may, in microcosm, suggest the tonal shape of the entire work. An insistent chord may establish a center by resisting pressures from various tonalities.

# Common Errors
- Assuming key must be established through V-I motion
- Overlooking non-harmonic establishment methods
- Treating key establishment as instantaneous rather than processual

# Common Confusions
- **Traditional vs. non-traditional**: Both are valid; twentieth-century music uses many methods simultaneously
- **Absence and return**: Temporary loss of key can be a deliberate structural strategy that strengthens the returning center
- **Equidistant harmony**: In rootless contexts, melodic, registral, or timbral emphasis alone can create key feeling
