# Quick Definition
The various methods by which a sense of key or tonal center is created in twentieth-century music, extending beyond traditional dominant-tonic relationships.

# Formal Definition
Key feeling may be created by forces other than the tonal pull of chordal pillars. Key consciousness may arise from an extremely dissonant chord refusing to become consonant, from a succession of simple chords resisting complex harmonic forces, from two culminating tonalities merging finally as a dominating polychord, from a group of important tones recurring at decisive points, or from atonal music searching for a key at cadential points.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Traditional establishment:
- Three center-affirming chords (above, below, and tonic)
- Tritone-embracing scale steps
- Dominant-tonic relationships

Non-traditional establishment:
- Persistent dissonant chord
- Simple chords resisting complexity
- Converging tonalities forming polychord
- Recurring important tones
- Atonal music finding key at cadences

Additional methods:
- Melodic insistence
- Registration and spacing
- Instrumentation and timbre
- Pedal point persistence
- Ostinato repetition

# Musical Context/Application
In equidistant rootless harmony, any note may be made the tonal center through melodic insistence, spacing, or instrumentation. The search for tonality or key center feeling may become a creative force in music. The tonality of a structure may be generated from a unifying harmonic idea from which musical growth extends. Key consciousness may vanish temporarily only to make stronger an emerging key's return.

- Tonality created by non-traditional means in works by Fricker, Henze, Hindemith, Honegger, Poulenc, Shapero, Stravinsky

- Key can be established without traditional V-I motion
- Multiple methods can operate simultaneously
- The absence and return of key can be structurally significant
