# Quick Definition
A six-note symmetrical scale dividing the octave into six equal whole steps, which offers limited harmonic resources but valuable contrast when combined with other materials.

# Formal Definition
The whole-tone hexatonic scale offers a limited basis for extended musical expression. When the scale is mirrored there is no change except in register. A second whole-tone scale lies a half step above the first, but any attempt to produce more results in transposition and duplication. Modal versions result in exact transposition of original forms. Its equidistant intervallic make-up deprives the scale of perfect fourth, fifth, and leading tone.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Structure and properties:
- Six whole steps: W-W-W-W-W-W (all intervals equal)
- Only two distinct whole-tone collections exist (C and C#)
- No modes possible (all rotations are transpositions)
- Mirror = retrograde (reflectively identical)
- No perfect 4th, perfect 5th, or leading tone

Chord resources:
- All triads are augmented (only 2 distinct, others are inversions)
- One type of seventh chord
- One type of ninth chord
- One category of chords by seconds

# Musical Context/Application
A real feeling of tonality must be established by harmony outside the whole-tone category. The true value of the whole-tone scale lies in the contrast it provides when combined with other scales and techniques. Various devices prolong harmonic interest: contrary motion, alternating the two scales, sounding all six tones together, changing density and spacing, and using both scales simultaneously.

# Common Confusions
Students may overuse whole-tone scales without recognizing their severe limitations. Pure whole-tone writing quickly becomes monotonous; its value is primarily in contrast and combination with other harmonic materials.
