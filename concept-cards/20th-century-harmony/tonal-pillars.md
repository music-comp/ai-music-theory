# Quick Definition
The three primary chordal supports that establish and maintain a tonal center: one built on a scale step above the tonic, one below, and the tonic itself, with secondary scale roots decorating these pillars.

# Core Definition
Usually three basic chords are needed to produce a feeling of tonality: one built upon a scale step above the tonic, one below the tonic, and the tonic itself. Harmony with tritone gravitation is helpful in establishing the center. The remaining secondary scale roots decorate the three tonal pillars in each case. Tonality may be established by using tones of a scale as chordal roots in varying degrees of support of the central tonic.

# Prerequisites
- **Tonality**: The broader principle of tonal organization
- **Tonal center**: The focal pitch that the pillars support

# Key Properties
1. Three chords required: above tonic, below tonic, and tonic
2. Multiple possible configurations of the above/below pair
3. Tritone gravitation reinforces pillar effectiveness
4. Secondary scale roots decorate (not replace) the pillars
5. Pillars provide balanced support around the tonic
6. In polytonal contexts, pillar relationships apply to combined tonics

# Construction / Recognition
- Primary configuration (V-IV-I): Subdominant below, dominant above, tonic
- Mediant-based (III-VI-I): Submediant below, mediant above
- Semitone-based (II-VII-I): Leading tone and supertonic framing tonic
- Tritone-embracing: Scale steps spanning the tritone
- Identify which three chords carry the tonal weight in a passage
- Secondary chords fill in between pillar appearances

# Context & Application
In twentieth-century practice, the concept of tonal pillars extends beyond traditional I-IV-V relationships. Any three scale-based chords that frame the tonic can function as pillars. The tritone-embracing pillars are particularly effective because the tritone's gravitational pull reinforces the center. Polytonal resonance also depends upon the resonance of the over-all tonic formation; passing secondary textures are maneuvered around the most resonant polychords that form the structural pillars of the particular key combination.

# Common Errors
- Assuming tonal pillars must be I-IV-V
- Treating secondary chords as pillars
- Ignoring tritone-embracing configurations

# Common Confusions
- **Pillars vs. functional harmony**: Pillars are a broader concept than tonic-subdominant-dominant; any above-below-tonic configuration can serve
- **Secondary chords**: These decorate the pillars but are not themselves pillar-level structural supports
- **Polytonal pillars**: The concept extends to polytonal contexts where combined tonics have their own pillar relationships
