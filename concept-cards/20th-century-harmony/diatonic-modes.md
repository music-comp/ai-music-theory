# Quick Definition
Seven scale patterns distinguished by their unique whole-step/half-step arrangements, each with a special character that twentieth-century composers exploit as independent scalar resources.

# Core Definition
A central tone to which other tones are related establishes tonality; the manner in which these other tones are placed around the central tone produces modality. Seven scale patterns stand apart because of their whole-half-step order: Ionian, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, and Locrian. While they bear medieval names, the resemblance to medieval usage is one of construction, not usage. Ionian is the familiar major scale; aeolian is the natural minor. The four distinctive modes are: Lydian (major with raised 4th), Mixolydian (major with lowered 7th), Dorian (natural minor with raised 6th), and Phrygian (natural minor with lowered 2nd). Locrian, infrequently used, is unmistakable because of its diminished tonic triad.

- **interval** -- modes are defined by their interval patterns

# Key Properties
1. **Seven modes** with distinct whole-half patterns:
   - Ionian: W-W-H-W-W-W-H (major)
   - Dorian: W-H-W-W-W-H-W (minor with raised 6th)
   - Phrygian: H-W-W-W-H-W-W (minor with lowered 2nd)
   - Lydian: W-W-W-H-W-W-H (major with raised 4th)
   - Mixolydian: W-W-H-W-W-H-W (major with lowered 7th)
   - Aeolian: W-H-W-W-H-W-W (natural minor)
   - Locrian: H-W-W-H-W-W-W (diminished tonic triad)
2. **Any tone** may serve as the tonic starting point
3. **Tonality vs. modality**: tonality = central tone; modality = arrangement around it
4. **Each mode has a characteristic scale step** that must be emphasized to maintain flavor
5. **Twentieth-century usage** differs fundamentally from medieval practice

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify the tonic (central tone)
2. Map the whole-step/half-step pattern
3. Compare to the seven standard patterns
4. Identify the characteristic scale step (the tone distinguishing the mode from major or natural minor)
5. Check whether harmonic progressions emphasize chords containing the characteristic step

# Context & Application
The distinctive flavor of modes is exploited by employing harmonic progressions in which the characteristic scale step occurs often. This tone keeps the mode from becoming major or natural minor. In twentieth-century music, key signatures are seldom used because tonal centers and modality shift rapidly and because atonality is often present. The enharmonic choice of spelling is determined by readability.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Thinking of modes as "scales starting on different notes of C major." **Correction**: In 20th-century practice, modes are independent collections with their own tonic, harmonic vocabulary, and expressive character.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming medieval and 20th-century modal practice are the same. **Clarification**: The resemblance is one of construction, not usage; 20th-century modal writing has its own harmonic and voice-leading conventions.
