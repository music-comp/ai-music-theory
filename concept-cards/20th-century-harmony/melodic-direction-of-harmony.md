# Quick Definition
The use of melodic lines, whether inner or outer voices, as directional guides for harmonic progression, where predominant intervals and durations must be aurally retainable.

# Core Definition
A melodic line, whether an inner or outer voice, often acts as a directional guide for harmonic progression. When a complex melody is the motivating force, the success of motivic development depends upon the ability of the ear to retain melodic elements. Predominant intervals and durations must be aurally retainable, and elements having special significance for the formal plan should be made melodic and harmonic characteristics. Melodies imply tonal inflection, harmonic punctuation, and rhythmic movement, and any one of these elements may be featured (Ch. 9, pp. 184-186).

# Key Properties
1. Any voice (inner or outer) can guide harmonic progression melodically
2. Predominant intervals and durations must be aurally retainable
3. Melodies imply tonal inflection, harmonic punctuation, and rhythmic movement
4. Formally significant elements should become both melodic and harmonic characteristics
5. When writing without a given line, focus on outer voices
6. Transposition sheds varying lights on thematic material
7. The composer is constantly aware of culminating points, dynamics, predominant interval textures, and medium characteristics

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the melodic line that appears to guide harmonic direction
- Check whether predominant intervals are aurally retainable
- Note whether melodic characteristics are reflected in harmonic construction
- Observe the relationship between melodic culmination points and harmonic events

# Context & Application
The composer considers textural influence of characteristic intervals, effect of melodic motifs on harmonic phrase shapes, tritone behavior in both horizontal and vertical situations, chord placement relative to tonal centers, and recovery of equilibrium after swift compositional changes. A vital thematic passage should be tested on various tonal levels for brilliance. When undecided about upper voice motion, work a few notes ahead in the bass, and vice versa.

# Common Errors
- Using melodies with intervals too complex to be retained aurally as harmonic guides
- Neglecting to make formally significant melodic elements into harmonic characteristics
- Failing to test thematic material on different tonal levels

# Common Confusions
- **Melody-first vs. harmony-first**: Both approaches are valid; melodic direction emphasizes melody as generating force
- **Outer vs. inner voice melody**: Any voice can direct; outer voices are most prominent but inner voices also function as guides
- **Retention requirement**: Complex melodic direction requires aurally retainable elements; overly complex melodies may not effectively guide harmony
