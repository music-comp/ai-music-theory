# Quick Definition

The use of melodic lines, whether inner or outer voices, as directional guides for harmonic progression, with complex melodies acting as the motivating force for chord movement.

# Formal Definition

**Melodic direction of harmony** refers to the principle that a melodic line, whether an inner or outer voice, often acts as a directional guide for harmonic progression. When a complex melody is the motivating force, the success of motivic development depends upon the ear's ability to retain melodic elements. Predominant intervals and durations must be aurally retainable, and elements having special significance for the formal plan should become melodic and harmonic characteristics.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation

Melodic implications for harmony:
- **Tonal inflection**: Melody suggests key areas and tonal direction
- **Harmonic punctuation**: Melodic arrival points suggest harmonic cadences
- **Rhythmic movement**: Melodic rhythm influences harmonic rhythm

Composer awareness required:
- Culminating points and general design
- Articulation and phrasing
- Dynamics and rhythmic meaning of each note
- Predominant interval textures
- Medium characteristics

When writing without a given line:
- Focus on outer voices
- Work ahead in bass when undecided about upper voice
- Work ahead in upper voice when undecided about bass

# Musical Context/Application

Melodic-harmonic integration involves:
- Melodic intervals becoming harmonic characteristics
- Motifs influencing harmonic phrase shapes
- Tritone behavior considered both horizontally and vertically
- Placement of chords in relation to presiding or vanishing tonal centers
- Recovery of equilibrium after swift compositional changes

The technique enables:
- Organic relationship between melody and harmony
- Motivic saturation of both dimensions
- Formal coherence through shared intervallic content

# Common Confusions

- **Melody-first vs. harmony-first**: Both approaches are valid; melodic direction of harmony emphasizes melody as the generating force.
- **Outer vs. inner voice melody**: Any voice can serve as directional guide; outer voices are most prominent but inner voices can also direct.
- **Retention requirement**: Complex melodic direction requires aurally retainable elements; overly complex melodies may not effectively guide harmony.
