# Quick Definition

A compound chord formed by fusing two different triads in open position so that two notes are shared, resulting in a four-note formation with distinct polychordal flavor.

# Formal Definition

A **fused chord** is a special category of compound harmony derived from combining a triad with added seconds placed in the bass. When two different types of triads are written in open position and fused so that two of the six notes become duplications in the middle voices, a unique four-note compound harmony results. These chords are labeled from the bottom triad upward and retain a distinct polychordal flavor while functioning as single compound sonorities.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation

Construction requirements:
- Two triads of different quality (e.g., major + minor, major + diminished)
- Open position arrangement
- Two notes common to both triads, appearing in middle voices
- Result: four-note chord with mixed intervals

**The six primary fused chords:**
There exist only six compound chords of this variety; other combinations of fused triads form seventh or triadic harmony rather than compound structures. These six fused chords are completely interchangeable and free to move to and from each other in any order.

**Secondary chords:**
The secondary chords in this category include seventh, ninth, and eleventh chords (in root position or inversion) that contain a sharp dissonant interval serving as a blending agent.

# Musical Context/Application

Fused harmony characteristics:
- Flows freely in four-part writing
- Completely interchangeable among the six primary formations
- Provides polychordal color without full polychordal complexity
- Functions well in chamber music and ensemble writing
- Creates a hybrid between added-note chords and polychords

The blending agent (sharp dissonant interval) in secondary fused formations helps unify the compound structure, preventing it from separating into distinct chordal layers.

# Common Confusions

- **Fused vs. polychord**: A polychord maintains audible separation between its component triads; a fused chord creates a single sonority where the triads merge through shared notes.
- **Limited inventory**: Only six true fused chord formations exist; other triad combinations produce different chord types (sevenths, standard triads).
- **Labeling convention**: Fused chords are named from the bottom triad upward, which may differ from how polychords are labeled.
