# Quick Definition
The decoration of harmonic structures through ornamental tones and figures, including both chord-tone based and non-chord-tone based embellishments.

# Formal Definition
Harmonic embellishment encompasses all the techniques used to ornament vertical harmonic structures. Ornamental harmonic tones (repeated chord tones and broken-chord tones) create no intervallic resistance to the harmony. When broken-chord figuration occurs in two or more voices simultaneously, the individual parts gain freedom. Harmonic ornamentation may increase the space between chord changes and relax harmonic rhythm. Members of a broken chord sounded by a single voice may imply meaningful harmonic progression.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Embellishment types:
- **Harmonic embellishment**: Using chord tones (no dissonance)
  - Repeated chord tones
  - Broken-chord (arpeggiated) figures
  - Implied progressions through broken chords
- **Nonharmonic embellishment**: Using foreign tones (creates dissonance)
  - Passing tones, auxiliary tones, etc.
  - Creates intervallic counteraction

Effects on harmonic rhythm:
- Can extend time between chord changes
- Can relax harmonic rhythm
- Can imply additional harmonic content

# Musical Context/Application
In twentieth-century practice, harmonic embellishment operates with the full range of available chord types. Simple chords may be embellished so that one chord penetrates another, creating polychordal sounds where they meet. Embellishment may derive from scales built on chord roots, from contrasting key centers, or from other scalar sources. The ornamental tone may remain unresolved, becoming part of a permanent texture change.

- Harmonic embellishment (chord tones) vs. nonharmonic embellishment (foreign tones)
- Embellishment can create complexity from simple harmonies
- Broken chords can imply progression beyond stated harmony
