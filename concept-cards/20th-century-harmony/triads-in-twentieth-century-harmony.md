# Quick Definition
Triads in twentieth-century music move freely through cycles of thirds and seconds as well as the traditional cycle of fifths, with the choice of cycle determining which chords serve as primary harmonic pillars.

# Core Definition
While eighteenth- and nineteenth-century triadic harmony revolves around tonal pillars (tonic, dominant, subdominant) governed by the cycle of fifths, twentieth-century composers employ triads in relationships governed by cycles of thirds or seconds in addition to the traditional cycle of fifths. In the cycle of fifths, V-I and IV-I dominate; in the cycle of thirds, III-I and VI-I dominate from positions a third above and below the tonic; in the cycle of seconds, II-I and VII-I dominate from positions a second in either direction. These three cyclic relationships provide complete freedom of root movement when interchanged. The dominant-subdominant balance that gives classical keys their identity can be replaced by analogous balancing on either side of the tonic by the intervals of the operative cycle.

# Prerequisites
- **Triads**: The basic three-note tertian chord types (major, minor, diminished, augmented)
- **Harmonic progression**: Understanding of root-movement principles and tonal function

# Key Properties
1. Three cyclic relationships are possible: fifths, thirds, and seconds
2. Each cycle defines its own set of primary and secondary chords
3. Cycle of fifths: primary chords I, IV, V
4. Cycle of thirds: primary chords I, III, VI
5. Cycle of seconds: primary chords I, II, VII
6. Corresponding progressions can be constructed across all three cycles
7. Colors of primary chords are determined by the intervallic make-up of the scale in force
8. In a major key under third relationship, I is major, III is minor, VI is minor

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the governing cycle by observing which root relationships dominate the passage
- Corresponding progressions across cycles: Cycle of 5ths: I-III-IV-VI-V-I; Cycle of 3rds: I-II-VI-VII-III-I; Cycle of 2nds: I-V-VII-IV-II-I
- Root progressions descend by successive intervals of the cycle relationship in force
- In modes, the primary chords of a cyclic relationship may not coincide with the natural modal primary chords
- Ornamental tones may be derived from any scale inferred from the chord treated as a local "tonic"

# Context & Application
Twentieth-century triadic practice grants composers complete freedom of root movement by interchanging the three cyclic relationships within a single passage. Every intervallic root progression becomes available because movements of the second, third, and fifth are identical to those of the seventh, sixth, and fourth by inversion. The whole twelve-tone compass of intervallic root progression is automatically available. Triads may also progress chromatically outside any scale formation, with root movements of seconds and thirds occurring more often than fifths (which strongly establish modality). A diatonic melody can bring chromatic triadic progressions into clear focus.

# Common Errors
- Assuming triadic harmony must follow traditional dominant-subdominant patterns
- Applying only cycle-of-fifths analysis to twentieth-century triadic passages
- Forgetting that primary chord colors vary by scale/mode in each cycle

# Common Confusions
- **Cycle vs. chromatic progression**: Cyclic relationships operate within a scale system; chromatic triadic progression operates outside any scale
- **Primary chords across modes**: In modes, the primary chords of a given cycle may not include the characteristic modal scale step (e.g., third relationship in phrygian lacks the phrygian second)
- **Root movement vs. voice leading**: The cycle governs root relationships, not the stepwise motion of individual voices
