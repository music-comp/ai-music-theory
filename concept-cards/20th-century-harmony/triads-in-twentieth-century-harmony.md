# Quick Definition
Triads in twentieth-century music are used in ways not emphasized in earlier periods, freed from traditional dominant-subdominant relationships and able to move through various cyclic relationships including thirds and seconds.

# Formal Definition
While eighteenth- and nineteenth-century triadic harmony revolved around tonal pillars (tonic, dominant, subdominant) with gravity created by the anticipation of harmonic arrival, twentieth-century composers employ triads in relationships governed by cycles of thirds or seconds in addition to the traditional cycle of fifths. The dominant-subdominant relationships (V-I and IV-I) that dominated earlier music may be replaced by mediant-submediant relationships (III-I and VI-I) in third-cycle harmony, or supertonic-leading-tone relationships (II-I and VII-I) in second-cycle harmony.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
**Traditional Fifth Relationship:**
- Primary chords: I, IV, V
- V-I and IV-I dominate all others
- Secondary chords provide variety

**Third Relationship (Cycle of Thirds):**
- Primary chords: I, III, VI
- III-I and VI-I dominate
- Mediant and submediant support tonic from positions a third above and below

**Second Relationship (Cycle of Seconds):**
- Primary chords: I, II, VII
- II-I and VII-I dominate
- Supertonic and leading-tone support tonic from positions a second in either direction

# Musical Context/Application
In twentieth-century practice, triads function with complete freedom of root movement when the three cyclic relationships are interchanged. Every intervallic root progression becomes possible because movements of the second, third, and fifth are identical to those of the seventh, sixth, and fourth by inversion. This provides the whole twelve-tone compass of intervallic root progression.

When triads progress chromatically outside any scale formation, root movements of major and minor seconds and thirds occur more often than fifths, as these intervals are less likely to define any one scale. The perfect fifth root movement has a strong tendency to establish a definite modality and tonality.

# Examples
- Samuel Barber, *Dover Beach* (red.), p. 12 - triads moving by third relationship
- Samuel Barber, *Adagio for Strings*, p. 3 - triads moving by second relationship
- Serge Prokofiev, *March from L'Amour des Trois Oranges* - third relationship
- Serge Prokofiev, *Classical Symphony*, p. 51 - second relationship
- Aaron Copland, *Clarinet Concerto* (red.), p. 2 - second relationship
- William Schuman, *Symphony No. 4*, pp. 34-35 - third relationship

# Common Confusions
Students often assume triadic harmony must follow traditional dominant-subdominant patterns. In twentieth-century practice, any triad may follow any other triad when music lacks a definite scale or modality, and the choice of cyclic relationship fundamentally alters which chords serve as primary harmonic pillars.
