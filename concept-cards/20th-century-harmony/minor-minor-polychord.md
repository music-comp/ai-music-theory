# Quick Definition
A polychord constructed from two minor triads, which is the least resonant of the diatonic triad combinations, with numbers 1, 5, and 9 being most frequently used due to their relatively greater resonance.

# Formal Definition
When a polychord is constructed of major triads it is most consonant and resonant, becoming progressively less so as minor, then augmented, and finally diminished triads are included. In minor-minor polychords, the resonant numbers 1, 5, and 9 are most frequently used.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Resonance hierarchy of diatonic triad polychords:
1. Major-Major (most resonant)
2. Major-Minor
3. Minor-Major
4. Minor-Minor (least resonant of diatonic types)

Most useful minor-minor combinations: 1, 5, and 9
- Selected from cycle-of-fifths ordering
- Have strongest remaining overtone relationships
- Other combinations significantly less resonant

Acoustic considerations:
- Both units generate weaker overtone series (due to minor quality)
- Less natural reinforcement between units
- Darker overall color

Character:
- Somber, dark quality
- Less blend than other diatonic combinations
- Requires careful spacing for clarity

# Musical Context/Application
Minor-minor polychords create the darkest diatonic polychordal sonority. Their reduced resonance makes them suitable for:
- Dark, somber passages
- Textural contrast with brighter polychords
- Specific coloristic effects

The limited resonance requires attention to:
- Spacing (wider preferred)
- Orchestration (contrasting timbres help)
- Duration (sustained use can become opaque)

Numbers 1, 5, and 9 provide workable options; other combinations risk excessive muddiness.

# Common Confusions
- Minor-minor is diatonic but least resonant of diatonic types
- Chromatic polychords (with aug/dim) form a separate category
- Limited usefulness is about resonance, not about the sonority being "wrong"
- Spacing and orchestration are especially critical for minor-minor
