# Quick Definition

The simultaneous combination of two or more chords from different harmonic areas, with component parts called chordal units, whose clear grouping is requisite for polyharmonic perception.

# Core Definition

"A polychord is the simultaneous combination of two or more chords from different harmonic areas. The segments of the polychord are referred to as chordal units. The beginnings of polychords may be traced to double and triple pedal point, where hints of bitonality, caused by the relationship of the passing chords to the pedal chord, lie in the passing harmony" (Persichetti, p. 134). "Clear grouping of the chordal units is a requisite of polyharmony, and rearranging the tones of these units can destroy the polychordal organization" (p. 136).

# Prerequisites

- **Consonance-dissonance** — Polychord resonance and character depend on tension relationships

# Key Properties

1. Simultaneous combination of two or more chords from different harmonic areas
2. Component chords are called chordal units
3. Clear grouping of units is requisite; rearranging tones destroys polychordal organization
4. Historical origin in double/triple pedal point
5. Polyharmony is seldom polytonal; polytonality requires key adherence
6. Non-polytonal polychords are more flexible and versatile
7. If upper unit forms chord by thirds with bottom unit, it sounds non-polychordal out of context but can function polychordally when surrounded by polyharmony

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct a Polychord
1. Select two or more chords from different harmonic areas
2. Voice each chord as a clear chordal unit (maintain registral grouping)
3. Place units in distinct registral zones
4. Use contrasting instrumentation to define harmonic zones
5. Consider resonance based on overtone relationships

## To Recognize a Polychord
1. Identify distinct chordal units in the voicing
2. Verify they come from different harmonic areas
3. Check that rearranging the tones would destroy the polychordal organization
4. Consider surrounding context (polychordal environment confirms ambiguous cases)

# Context & Application

Polychords became a defining technique of 20th-century composition, particularly in works of Stravinsky, Milhaud, and Bartok. They allow complex, rich sonorities while maintaining structural clarity through unit separation. Polychords that are not polytonal are considerably more flexible; harmonic areas of both units shift often. Prolonged passages of pure polychords can result in tiring mass resonance; ornamentation, unison interruptions, and note omission revitalize the texture.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Scrambling the voicing of chordal units
  **Correction**: Clear registral grouping of units is requisite; rearranged tones destroy polychordal perception
- **Error**: Assuming all polychords are polytonal
  **Correction**: Most polyharmony is not polytonal; polytonality requires adherence to separate keys

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: The same pitches are always polychordal or always non-polychordal
  **Clarification**: Voicing and context determine whether a pitch collection is heard as polychord; the same notes rearranged may sound like a single chord
- **Confusion**: Polychord equals compound chord
  **Clarification**: Polychords require distinct chordal units from different harmonic areas; compound chords use mixed intervals without polychordal organization
