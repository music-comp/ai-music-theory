# Quick Definition
Triadic progressions that move freely outside any scale formation, with roots progressing most often by seconds, thirds, and the tritone rather than by fifths, allowing any triad to follow any other triad.

# Core Definition
In music that lacks a definite scale or modality, any triad may be followed by any other triad and any sequence of key centers may be used. Root movements of major and minor seconds and major and minor thirds occur more often than fifths because they are less likely to define any one scale. The root of one triad moves up or down to the root of another more often in intervals of seconds and thirds than in fifths or the tritone. Both triads involved may be of any type but are usually major or minor. Root movement of the augmented fourth adds variety to the basic second-third relationship. At any point, any note in the strongest voice may be considered the root, third, or fifth of a major or minor triad, giving each melody note six triadic possibilities.

# Prerequisites
- **Triads in twentieth-century harmony**: Understanding that triads can operate outside cyclic systems

# Key Properties
1. Any triad may follow any other triad
2. Root movements by second and third are most common (less likely to establish a key)
3. Perfect fifth root movement strongly establishes modality -- therefore seldom used
4. Tritone root movement adds variety to second-third relationships
5. Triads are usually major or minor (less often diminished or augmented)
6. Any melody note has three major and three minor triadic possibilities
7. An underlying tonal center may emerge from melodic emphasis without a governing mode
8. A diatonic melody can bring chromatic triadic wandering into clear focus

# Construction / Recognition
- Look for root movements predominantly by seconds and thirds rather than fifths
- Check for absence of a governing scale or mode
- A prominent melody note may be harmonized as root, third, or fifth of six different triads
- Even an unadorned scale line produces potential chordal variety through this technique
- Tonal center may be indisputable even without prevailing mode (e.g., clearly F-centered without modal governance)
- Each voice should have purpose and design for the progression to have harmonic meaning

# Context & Application
Chromatic triadic progression often creates harmonic and tonal restlessness. This restlessness can be focused by ensuring each voice has purpose and design. When chromatic triads wander harmonically and make vague phrase-shapes, a diatonic melody may bring the chromaticism into clear focus. The technique enables tonality without modality -- a passage can center unmistakably on a pitch without being governed by any preconceived tonal framework.

# Common Errors
- Assuming chromatic triadic progression is random or atonal
- Using too many perfect-fifth root movements, which establish unwanted tonal gravity
- Letting all voices wander without melodic purpose

# Common Confusions
- **Free vs. random**: While chromatic triadic progression operates outside scale constraints, effective writing maintains musical logic through voice-leading purpose
- **Tonality without modality**: A passage can be unmistakably centered on a pitch without being governed by any specific scale or mode
- **Tritone root movement**: Adds variety to the second-third framework but is less common than seconds and thirds
