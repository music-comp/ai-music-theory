# Quick Definition
The use of symmetrical chords (augmented triads, diminished seventh chords) to weave between different harmonic textures, exploiting their rootless ambiguity.

# Formal Definition
The equidistant chord has neither root nor power to resist homogeneous chordal formations and can, by chromatic motion, weave one harmonic fabric into another. These symmetrical structures (chords built from equal intervals) lack the hierarchical pull of root-based harmony, making them ideal pivot points between different harmonic textures. Their ambiguity becomes a compositional resource.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Equidistant chord properties:
- Built from equal intervals (aug. triads: M3s; dim7: m3s)
- No definable root
- No resistance to other chord types
- Symmetrical pitch content

Transitional function:
- Chromatic voice motion from equidistant chord
- Can resolve to any texture type
- Connects otherwise incompatible harmonies
- Masks the transformation process

Related techniques:
- Rough tritone relationships divert attention from transformations
- Prominent hidden fifths also mask harmonic changes
- Sequential patterns drive varied chords over barriers

# Musical Context/Application
Equidistant chords serve as neutral territory between harmonic regions. Their lack of root definition means they pull toward nothing specifically, allowing the composer to direct resolution toward any desired texture. The augmented triad (dividing the octave into three equal parts) and diminished seventh chord (dividing into four) are the most common equidistant formations used for transitional purposes.

# Common Confusions
- Equidistant chords lack roots, not just ambiguous roots
- Their neutrality is a resource, not a weakness
- Chromatic motion from equidistant chords can lead anywhere
