# Quick Definition
The unified organization of musical material through aural retention of motifs, achieved by repetition, variation, or mutation, essential regardless of harmonic language and especially critical when traditional tonal relationships cannot provide structural unity.

# Core Definition
The compositional process is meaningless unless thematic statements are identifiable, for continuity and coherence are effected through aural retention of motifs. If chromatic chordal mixtures accumulate, formal coherence is achieved by repetition, variation, or mutation of the chromatic sound groups. Thematic meaningfulness can arise only from thematic unity and purpose; if a theme is not nourished by its motivic constituents it will lack significance. In atonal music, the various elements are tightly knit by extreme motivic concentration with constant reference to previous material.

# Prerequisites
- **Thematic conception**: The identifiable material providing coherence
- **Thematic transformation**: The developmental process maintaining identity

# Key Properties
1. Aural retention of motifs is the mechanism of coherence
2. Thematic statements must be identifiable
3. Three coherence methods: repetition, variation, mutation
4. In chromatic/atonal contexts, coherence through chromatic sound group manipulation
5. Extreme motivic concentration in atonal music
6. Constant reference to previous material
7. Thematic unity and purpose are prerequisites for meaningfulness

# Construction / Recognition
- Verify that thematic statements are identifiable by ear
- Track repetition, variation, and mutation of motivic material
- In chromatic contexts, identify the sound groups being manipulated
- Note the degree of motivic concentration
- Assess whether cross-references to previous material are audible

# Context & Application
Formal coherence is essential regardless of harmonic language. In highly chromatic or atonal contexts, traditional tonal relationships cannot provide coherence, so motivic relationships become even more critical. Even in the most diverse textural combinations -- the full range of harmonic synthesis -- coherence depends on perceptible relationships. The challenge increases as harmonic variety increases: more diverse resources demand tighter motivic integration.

# Examples
Works demonstrating formal coherence through diverse means:
- Bela Bartok, Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta (Boosey)
- Alban Berg, Lyric Suite (Universal)
- Elliott Carter, String Quartet No. 2 (Associated)
- Roy Harris, Symphony No. 3 (G. Schirmer)
- Anton Webern, Five Pieces for String Quartet Op. 5 (Universal)

# Common Errors
- Relying on tonal relationships for coherence in atonal contexts
- Creating material that cannot be aurally retained
- Allowing harmonic diversity to outstrip motivic integration

# Common Confusions
- **Aural retention**: Coherence depends on what the listener can hear and remember, not on abstract structural relationships
- **Chromatic contexts**: When tonal coherence is unavailable, motivic coherence must compensate
- **Diversity vs. coherence**: More harmonic variety demands more (not less) motivic discipline
