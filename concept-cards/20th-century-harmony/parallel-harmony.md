# Quick Definition
Harmonic motion where all voices move in the same direction, functioning as an expanded textural equivalent of a melodic line, including both strict parallel motion (identical chord construction) and similar motion (changing chords).

# Core Definition
When all voices in a succession of chords move in the same direction, **parallel harmony** occurs. The term includes both strict parallel motion (all chords identical in construction) and similar motion (chords change as voices move freely but in the same direction). **Real parallel harmony** (exact transposition) tends to sever connections with any one key and may be used as a means of entering and leaving atonality, functioning freely in modulatory transitions and thematic statements where tonality is meant to be obscure. **Tonal parallel harmony** (intervallic changes determined by the scale in force) tends to preserve a modality. Parallel harmony is an expanded textural equivalent of a melodic line; its direction is governed primarily by melodic considerations and its intervallic construction by the texture demanded by dramatic form. In parallel harmony, fourths and fifths are as liquid as thirds and sixths, and intervals of the second and seventh find horizontal freedom (Ch. 9, pp. 197-198).

# Prerequisites
- **Chord connection** -- understanding voice-leading between chords

# Key Properties
1. All voices move in the same direction
2. Real (exact transposition): severs key connections, enables entering/leaving atonality
3. Tonal (scale-determined intervals): preserves modality
4. Fourths and fifths are as liquid as thirds and sixths
5. Seconds and sevenths find horizontal freedom
6. Direction governed by melodic considerations
7. Extended similar motion tires quickly even with complex chords
8. Momentary parallel harmony effectively accentuates melodic rises/falls or slips into fresh keys

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify passages where all voices move in the same direction
- Determine whether the parallel motion is real (exact transposition) or tonal (scale-adjusted)
- Note the intervallic construction of the parallel chords
- Check whether the passage is momentary or extended (extended requires renewal techniques)

# Context & Application
Parallel harmony is found in eighteenth-century six-three successions and nineteenth-century diminished seventh chord successions. In twentieth-century practice, it serves to accentuate melodic rises or falls, slip into fresh key areas, and create thick, unified textural blocks. Extended similar motion risks monotony and requires renewal devices (see parallel-harmony-renewal). Two sets of parallel chords in contrary motion may or may not create reflective texture.

# Common Errors
- Extending parallel motion without employing renewal techniques, causing monotony
- Failing to distinguish between real and tonal parallel harmony when the distinction matters for tonal coherence
- Neglecting that fourths, fifths, seconds, and sevenths are freed from traditional restrictions in parallel contexts

# Common Confusions
- **Parallel vs. similar motion**: Strict parallel keeps identical intervals; similar motion has all voices in same direction but changing intervals
- **Real vs. tonal**: Real exactly transposes all intervals; tonal adjusts intervals to stay within scale/mode
- **Liberation of intervals**: All intervals gain horizontal freedom in parallel harmony, not just thirds and sixths
