# Quick Definition

Harmonic motion where all voices in a succession of chords move in the same direction, functioning as an expanded textural equivalent of a melodic line (also called planing or chordal melody).

# Formal Definition

**Parallel harmony** occurs when all voices in a succession of chords move in the same direction. The term includes both strict parallel motion (all chords identical in construction) and similar motion (chords change as voices move freely but in the same direction). Parallel harmony, also known as planing or chordal melody, is an expanded textural equivalent of a melodic line, with its direction governed primarily by melodic considerations and its intervallic construction determined by the texture demanded by dramatic form.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation

Two types:
1. **Real parallel harmony**: Exact transposition of chord structure
   - Tends to sever connections with any one key
   - Useful for entering/leaving atonality
   - Functions in modulatory transitions
   - Creates tonal obscurity

2. **Tonal parallel harmony**: Intervallic changes determined by scale in force
   - Tends to preserve modality
   - Chord quality changes to stay within scale
   - Reinforces key center

In parallel harmony:
- Fourths and fifths flow as liquidly as thirds and sixths
- Seconds and sevenths find horizontal freedom
- Traditional voice-leading prohibitions are suspended

# Musical Context/Application

Parallel harmony serves:
- Accentuating melodic rises or falls
- Slipping into fresh key areas
- Creating thick, unified textural blocks
- Obscuring or reinforcing tonality (depending on type)

Extended parallel motion risks monotony; renewal techniques include:
- Contrary motion in one voice against parallel current
- Converting tonal to real parallel motion
- Changing strict parallel to similar motion
- Moving roots opposite to the moving mass

# Common Confusions

- **Parallel vs. similar motion**: Strict parallel keeps identical intervals; similar motion has all voices moving same direction but with changing intervals.
- **Real vs. tonal**: Real exactly transposes all intervals; tonal adjusts intervals to stay within scale/mode.
- **Liberation of intervals**: Parallel harmony frees all intervals from traditional restrictions, not just thirds and sixths.
