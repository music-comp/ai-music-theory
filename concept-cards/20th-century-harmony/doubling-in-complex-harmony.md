# Quick Definition
The practice of doubling chord tones in twentieth-century multi-voice harmony, where doubling considerations shift from securing maximum sound to achieving textural variety and managing consonance-dissonance relationships.

# Core Definition
The spacing concern in three- and four-part harmony is often one of securing maximum sound, but in harmony of more than four parts, doubling and spacing considerations are directed to securing variety of texture. Various colors and weights are obtainable through omission and doubling of both dissonant and consonant chord members. Doubling of the dissonant tone raises harmonic tension, and such doubling enables a simple chord to function easily with complex formations. The quality of the dissonant tone can be softened by coupling it with consonant intervals. To soften doubled dissonant tones, the voices involving the dissonance move in contrary motion, or one doubled tone may move before the other resolves (Ch. 9, pp. 196-197).

# Key Properties
1. Three-/four-part: focus on maximum sound through doubling
2. Five+ parts: focus on textural variety through selective doubling
3. Doubling consonant members adds weight safely
4. Doubling dissonant members raises harmonic tension
5. Doubled dissonance enables simple chords to function with complex formations
6. Softening: contrary motion between doubled dissonant tones, or staggered resolution
7. Coupling dissonant tone with consonant intervals softens its quality
8. At orchestral consonant climaxes within dissonant contexts, extensive doubling avoids sound loss

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify which chord members are doubled in multi-voice textures
- Note whether dissonant or consonant members are doubled
- Observe softening techniques for doubled dissonances
- Check climactic points for adequate doubling to maintain sound

# Context & Application
The density and concentration of tonal sound varies under different dramatic conditions. In an orchestral arrival of dissonant material upon a consonant climax, extensive doubling of all members of the consonant chord may be necessary to avoid loss of sound. The technique is essential for orchestral and large-ensemble writing where balance between textural variety and sonic presence is critical.

# Common Errors
- Applying traditional doubling rules (avoid leading-tone doubling, etc.) rigidly in twentieth-century contexts
- Neglecting to provide extensive doubling at consonant climactic arrivals
- Doubling dissonant tones without employing softening techniques when smoothness is desired

# Common Confusions
- **Traditional vs. contemporary doubling**: Common-practice restrictions may not apply in twentieth-century contexts
- **Doubling vs. spacing**: Doubling concerns which tones are duplicated; spacing concerns where all tones are placed
- **Tension management**: Doubling dissonance increases tension; this is sometimes the desired effect
