# Quick Definition
A compositional procedure in which two or more keys are combined simultaneously, creating multiple tonal planes that maintain their own organizational centers while the bass usually determines the over-all tonic structure.

# Core Definition
Polytonal writing is a procedure in which two or more keys are combined simultaneously. If only two keys are sounded, the specific term bitonality may be used, but polytonality has come generally to imply the use of more than one tonal plane at the same time. The scales forming different tonic centers may be intervallically identical or contrasting, traditional or synthetic.

Great concern must be given the vertical factor; if tonal planes are arbitrarily combined the harmonic result will be haphazard. The music must be conceived in polytonal context, not cloaked in accidental texture. Each melodic line should retain its own individuality yet the harmonic total must have direction of flow, tension design, and textural consistency. Although each tonal plane has its own organizational center, a single over-all tonic structure is usually felt from the bass. From this basic polychordal tonic stems the polytonal texture.

Polytonality is a specific kind of tonal organization, a means of moving groups of voices within confined harmonic register areas. Polychordal writing is often tonal, and when each group of voices within a chordal unit functions within the confines of a stable scale zone, polytonality occurs.

# Prerequisites
- **Tonality**: The organizational principle operative in each layer
- **Tonal pillars**: Each key needs its own center-establishing chords
- **Polychord**: The vertical sonority from which polytonality may emerge

# Key Properties
1. Two or more keys combined simultaneously
2. Scales may be identical or contrasting, traditional or synthetic
3. Vertical factor must be carefully considered
4. Each line retains individuality; harmonic total must have direction
5. Over-all tonic usually felt from the bass
6. Music must be conceived in polytonal context, not accidental
7. Each tonal plane kept within clear scale formation
8. Tritone-spanning scale steps brought into play quickly for clarity

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify distinct tonal planes operating simultaneously
- Verify that each plane maintains a clear scale formation
- Check for the over-all tonic felt from the bass
- Assess vertical sonority for coherent direction and tension design
- Ensure tritone-defining tones are present in each key area
- Distinguish from miscellaneous polychords (which lack key progression)

# Context & Application
Polytonality is effective as such only when each tonal plane is kept within a clear scale formation. Shifting of modality through chromatic alteration clouds the texture or simply produces miscellaneous polychords. For maximum clarity in the projection of different tonalities, one key is introduced and as the next key is added, the preceding key, having been established, becomes less obvious. Independent lines should not be placed at random without understanding polytonal construction. The fundamental quality of polytonal texture is determined by the key relationship set up by the tonics.

# Common Errors
- Arbitrarily combining tonal planes without vertical design
- Failing to establish each key's identity before combining
- Cramping polytonal areas into too narrow a register

# Common Confusions
- **Polytonality vs. polychords**: Polychords are static vertical combinations; polytonality involves progression within distinct key zones
- **Over-all tonic**: The bass usually determines the over-all tonic, not an averaging of the combined keys
- **Chromatic alteration**: Shifting modality within a polytonal plane clouds the texture or produces mere polychords, destroying the polytonal effect
