# Quick Definition
The various positions of ninth chords created by placing different members in the bass, with the fourth inversion (ninth in bass) creating a distinctive hard texture and the ninth below the root reducing agility.

# Core Definition
When the ninth of the chord is below the root the chord is less agile; fluent part-writing helps the progression. The fourth inversion (ninth in the bass) creates a hard texture and is effective in rough-hewn passages. Omitting the fifth in fourth inversion produces more suppleness. The minor dominant ninth in fourth inversion is often found with its seventh omitted in parallel harmony. In fourth inversion the interval of the minor ninth is sometimes spelled as a diminished octave to facilitate reading.

# Key Properties
1. Five positions: root position through fourth inversion
2. Fourth inversion (ninth in bass) = hard, rough texture
3. Ninth below root = reduced agility, requires fluent voice leading
4. Omitting fifth in fourth inversion = more suppleness
5. Minor dominant ninth, fourth inversion, seventh omitted = common in parallel harmony
6. Minor ninth sometimes spelled as diminished octave for reading ease
7. Each inversion has distinctly different character and handling requirements

# Construction / Recognition
- Root position: root in bass (most standard)
- First inversion: third in bass
- Second inversion: fifth in bass
- Third inversion: seventh in bass
- Fourth inversion: ninth in bass (hard texture, sharp dissonance against bass)
- When ninth appears below root in any position: chord loses agility

# Context & Application
The choice of inversion significantly affects ninth chord character and mobility. Root position and early inversions maintain suppleness. Fourth inversion creates a distinctive harsh sonority effective in loud, aggressive passages. Composers select inversions based on desired textural effect: fourth inversion for rough-hewn passages, other inversions for more supple contexts. The fourth inversion ninth chord is particularly useful as a bridge to quartal harmony, since its root-on-top arrangement (spanning two fourths in the seventh) acts as a binder between tertian and quartal categories.

# Common Errors
- Treating fourth inversion as just another voicing rather than a fundamentally different sonority
- Freely inverting ninth chords without considering the different properties of each inversion

# Common Confusions
- **Fourth inversion is not just a voicing**: It creates a categorically different sonority requiring different compositional handling
- **Spelling convenience**: The diminished octave spelling (for minor ninth) is for reading ease, not a different interval
