# Quick Definition
Major and minor thirds and sixths, which produce a warm, blended sound derived from the middle region of the overtone series.

# Core Definition
Soft consonances are the major and minor thirds and sixths, characterized by their warm, blending quality derived from partials 4, 5, and 6 of the overtone series. These intervals form the foundation of triadic harmony and provide harmonic color without the spaciousness of open consonances or the tension of dissonances. When spaced more than an octave apart, soft consonances become richer. Orchestral color may rob the triad of its soft consonance through particular timbral choices.

# Key Properties
1. **Major third**: 5:4 ratio (partial 5)
2. **Minor third**: 6:5 ratio (partial 6)
3. **Major sixth**: 5:3 ratio
4. **Minor sixth**: 8:5 ratio
5. **Wide spacing**: become richer (not just louder) beyond the octave
6. **A doubled major third adds color** to chordal textures

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify intervals of 3, 4, 8, or 9 semitones
2. Note the warm, blended quality
3. Observe how they become richer with compound spacing
4. Note how timbre can alter their perceived warmth

# Context & Application
Soft consonances provide harmonic warmth characteristic of triadic writing. In 20th-century practice, orchestral color may transform or negate the expected warmth of thirds and sixths. A doubled major third adds color. The third (partial 5) is weaker than the fifth (partial 3), which explains why thirds provide color while fifths provide structural power.

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Assuming soft consonances always sound "pleasant." **Correction**: In 20th-century contexts, soft consonances can acquire unexpected tension when surrounded by dissonant materials or when orchestration alters their quality.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Thinking thirds and sixths are interchangeable. **Clarification**: While both are soft consonances, they have distinct characters; sixths are more spread and open, thirds more compact and warm.
