# Quick Definition
Major and minor thirds and sixths, which produce a warm, blended sound quality due to their position in the middle region of the overtone series.

# Formal Definition
Soft consonances are the major and minor thirds and sixths, characterized by their warm, blending quality derived from partials 4, 5, and 6 of the overtone series. These intervals form the foundation of triadic harmony and provide color without the spaciousness of open consonances or the tension of dissonances.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
- Major third: 5:4 frequency ratio (partial 5)
- Minor third: 6:5 frequency ratio (partial 6)
- Major sixth: 5:3 frequency ratio
- Minor sixth: 8:5 frequency ratio
- When spaced more than an octave apart, soft consonances become richer

# Musical Context/Application
Soft consonances provide the harmonic warmth characteristic of triadic writing. In twentieth-century practice, orchestral color may rob the triad of its soft consonance through particular timbral choices. A doubled major third adds color to chordal textures. When soft consonances are expanded beyond the octave, they gain richness without losing their essential blending quality.

# Common Confusions
Students may assume soft consonances always sound "pleasant." In twentieth-century contexts, soft consonances can acquire unexpected tension when surrounded by more dissonant materials or when orchestration alters their quality.
