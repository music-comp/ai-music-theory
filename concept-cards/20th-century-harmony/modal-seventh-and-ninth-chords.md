# Quick Definition
Extended tertian chords built within modal scales, where tritone-free constructions are preferred to avoid suggesting major-key dominant function.

# Formal Definition
Modal chords by thirds, other than triads, need special attention because the tritone present in many seventh and ninth chords implies the dominant seventh of a major scale. The chord may then easily lose its tonic feeling and slip into a major scale. Useful seventh and ninth chords in the modes (excepting ionian) are those involving no tritone.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Tritone-free seventh types:
- Major seventh: no tritone (stable)
- Minor seventh: no tritone (stable)
- Major-minor seventh: contains tritone (use carefully)
- Half-diminished seventh: contains tritone (use carefully)

Modal chord progression principle:
- Triads, sevenths, and ninths progress easily within same mode
- Avoid creating tritone implications that suggest major-key function
- Non-tritone chords maintain modal identity

# Musical Context/Application
Useful seventh and ninth chords in the modes are those involving no tritone. Triads, sevenths, and ninths progress easily from one to another while remaining in the same mode. When a tritone is present, it may pull the harmony toward major tonality, undermining the modal character.

# Common Confusions
Students often build seventh chords on every scale degree without considering tritone content. In modal writing, chords containing tritones risk suggesting major-key dominant function and should be avoided or handled carefully to maintain modal identity.
