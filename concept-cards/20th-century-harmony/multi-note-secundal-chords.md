# Quick Definition

Four- and five-note chords built from seconds that move contrapuntally with difficulty, best combined with three-note secundal chords or quartal chords to allow linear motion.

# Core Definition

"Four- and five-note chords by seconds move contrapuntally with difficulty. Linear motion can be obtained by including in the progression three-note chords by seconds or chords by fourths. The sevenths (inverted seconds) that are present in chords by fourths blend with the seconds in chords by seconds and leave space for movement of the parts" (Persichetti, p. 123). "A single chord by seconds can be enlarged to encompass an entire scale, diatonic, chromatic, or original. It may or may not be a cluster depending upon the type of harmony that surrounds it" (p. 124).

# Prerequisites

- **Three-note secundal chord** — The multi-note chord extends this fundamental unit
- **Quartal harmony** — Quartal chords provide the blending sevenths that enable linear motion

# Key Properties

1. Four-note secundal: three stacked seconds (e.g., C-D-E-F)
2. Five-note secundal: four stacked seconds (e.g., C-D-E-F-G)
3. Dense texture restricts voice movement and independent voice activity
4. Quartal chords blend well because their sevenths (inverted fourths) complement seconds
5. Can extend to encompass entire scales (diatonic, chromatic, synthetic)
6. Whether a large secundal chord functions as a cluster depends on surrounding context
7. Best combined with polychordal or compound harmony before the texture "hardens"

# Construction / Recognition

## To Manage Multi-Note Secundal Progressions
1. Write the multi-note secundal chord at desired position
2. Include three-note secundal chords at phrase-breath points
3. Alternate with quartal chords to provide space for voice movement
4. Combine with polychordal or compound harmony to prevent textural rigidity

## To Distinguish from Clusters
1. Check for defined inner voice movement (present in true chords by seconds, absent in clusters)
2. Check the surrounding harmonic context
3. Note whether the voicing is predominantly close (uninverted) position

# Context & Application

Multi-note secundal chords represent the transition point between true chords by seconds and clusters. When allowed to "harden" without relief from lighter textures, they become static and lose harmonic vitality. Effective use requires mixing with lighter textures, combination with polychordal or compound harmony, strategic introduction and departure, and attention to phrase-breath points.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Writing extended passages of pure four- or five-note secundal chords
  **Correction**: Include three-note secundal or quartal chords to provide linear motion and prevent textural hardening
- **Error**: Assuming any large secundal chord is a cluster
  **Correction**: Context and inner voice movement determine the distinction

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: The contrapuntal difficulty of multi-note secundal chords is merely a technical limitation
  **Clarification**: It is a structural consequence of dense second-stacking; the close intervals physically prevent independent voice movement
- **Confusion**: Quartal chords are unrelated to secundal harmony
  **Clarification**: The sevenths in quartal chords (inverted fourths) blend naturally with seconds and provide the space needed for voice motion
