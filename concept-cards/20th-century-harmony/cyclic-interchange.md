# Quick Definition
The practice of shifting between different harmonic cycles (fifths, thirds, seconds) within a single passage, affording complete freedom of root movement within a scale.

# Formal Definition
Interchange of the three cyclic relationships (second, third, and fifth) allows complete freedom of root movement within a scale. Every intervallic root progression is possible because movements of the second, third, and fifth are identical to those of the seventh, sixth, and fourth by inversion. Since the intervals between moving roots appear as major, minor, perfect, and augmented (depending upon the scale in use), the whole twelve-tone compass of intervallic root progression is automatically available through cyclic interchange.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
**Interval Equivalences by Inversion:**
- Second (2nd) = Seventh (7th) inverted
- Third (3rd) = Sixth (6th) inverted
- Fifth (5th) = Fourth (4th) inverted

**Available Root Movements:**
Through cyclic interchange, all possible root intervals become accessible:
- Major and minor seconds
- Major and minor thirds
- Perfect, augmented, and diminished fourths
- Perfect, augmented, and diminished fifths
- Major and minor sixths
- Major and minor sevenths

**Ornamental Tone Derivation:**
Any chord in a progression may be considered a "tonic" of any scale (synthetic or otherwise), and ornamental tones may be derived from that inferred scale.

# Musical Context/Application
Cyclic interchange liberates harmonic progression from the constraints of any single cyclic system. Composers can begin a phrase in fifth-relationship harmony, shift to third-relationship harmony, and conclude in second-relationship harmony, all while maintaining coherent harmonic logic. This technique is particularly useful for creating harmonic variety without abandoning tonal coherence.

The technique also enables sophisticated ornamental writing, as any chord can be treated as a local tonic with its own implied scale providing ornamental tones.

# Common Confusions
Cyclic interchange should not be confused with random root movement. While all root intervals become available, the technique works best when each shift between cycles is motivated by phrase structure or melodic considerations, maintaining musical logic even as cyclic allegiances change.
