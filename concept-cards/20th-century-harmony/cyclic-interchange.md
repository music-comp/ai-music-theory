# Quick Definition
The practice of shifting between the three harmonic cycles (fifths, thirds, seconds) within a single passage, affording complete freedom of root movement within a scale by making the entire twelve-tone compass of intervallic root progression available.

# Core Definition
Interchange of the three cyclic relationships (second, third, and fifth) allows complete freedom of root movement within a scale. Every intervallic root progression is possible because movements of the second, third, and fifth are identical to those of the seventh, sixth, and fourth by inversion. Since the intervals between moving roots appear as major, minor, perfect, and augmented (depending upon the scale in use), the whole twelve-tone compass of intervallic root progression is automatically available. Any chord in a progression may be considered a "tonic" of any scale (synthetic or otherwise), and ornamental tones may be derived from that inferred scale.

# Prerequisites
- **Cycle of thirds**: Understanding III-I and VI-I as primary relationships
- **Cycle of seconds**: Understanding II-I and VII-I as primary relationships
- **Triads in twentieth-century harmony**: The three-cycle framework

# Key Properties
1. All three cycles (fifths, thirds, seconds) may be combined within a single passage
2. Every root interval becomes available: M2, m2, M3, m3, P4, A4, P5, m6, M6, m7, M7
3. Interval equivalences by inversion: 2nd = 7th, 3rd = 6th, 5th = 4th
4. Ornamental tones derived from any scale implied by the local chord
5. Phrase structure or melodic considerations motivate the shift between cycles
6. Works in any scale, including synthetic scales

# Construction / Recognition
- Observe root movements that shift between second, third, and fifth intervals
- A passage may begin in fifth-relationship, shift to third-relationship, and conclude in second-relationship
- Each chord can be treated as a local tonic with its own implied ornamental scale
- Cyclic shifts are most effective when motivated by phrase shape or melodic direction
- The technique is not random root movement but purposeful shifting between organized systems

# Context & Application
Cyclic interchange liberates harmonic progression from the constraints of any single cyclic system while maintaining coherent harmonic logic. It also enables sophisticated ornamental writing, as each chord can be treated as a local tonic with its own implied scale providing ornamental tones. The technique is essential for twentieth-century composers who wish to maintain tonal coherence while exploiting the full range of root-movement possibilities.

# Common Errors
- Treating cyclic interchange as random or arbitrary root movement
- Failing to maintain melodic or phrase-structural motivation for cycle shifts

# Common Confusions
- **Cyclic interchange vs. random root movement**: While all root intervals become available, the technique works best when each shift is motivated by phrase structure or melodic considerations
- **Cyclic interchange vs. chromatic triadic progression**: Cyclic interchange operates within a scale system by combining cycles; chromatic triadic progression operates outside any scale formation
