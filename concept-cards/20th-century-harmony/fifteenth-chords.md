# Quick Definition
Eight-note tertian chords built by adding another third above the thirteenth, accommodating more than seven different chord tones as the tonal system expanded to include chromatic extensions.

# Formal Definition
As the tonal system expanded into one that accommodated more than seven different chord tones, thirds were added to the thirteenth chord as a fifteenth without duplicating chord members. The fifteenth chord contains eight different pitch classes, extending beyond the seven-note diatonic collection to incorporate chromatic alterations.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
**Construction:**
Eight notes built in thirds: Root + 3rd + 5th + 7th + 9th + 11th + 13th + 15th

**Interval Span:**
The fifteenth spans two octaves plus a second (compound second)

**Chromatic Extension:**
Unlike thirteenth chords that can be purely diatonic, fifteenth chords necessarily include at least one chromatic tone to achieve eight different pitch classes.

**Manipulation:**
For purposes of manipulation, the texture of these large chords should be thinned by omitting two or three inner tones. Separated triadic units must be avoided unless polychordal structure is desired.

# Musical Context/Application
Fifteenth chords represent an expansion of the harmonic palette beyond the seven-note diatonic system. They are effective in:
1. Parallel harmony
2. Harmonic punctuations

Due to their density, fifteenth chords are best used sparingly and with strategic omissions to maintain textural clarity. The extreme register span requires careful orchestration.

# Common Confusions
Students may confuse fifteenth chords with doubled thirteenth chords. A true fifteenth chord contains eight distinct pitch classes; a thirteenth with doublings still contains only seven. The fifteenth requires chromatic extension beyond the diatonic collection.
