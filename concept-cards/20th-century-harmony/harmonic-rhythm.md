# Quick Definition
The underlying rhythm created by the rate of harmonic change, which controls and stabilizes musical flow -- quick changes create restlessness, widely spaced changes create breadth.

# Core Definition
**Harmonic rhythm** is the underlying rhythm that plays a large part in controlling and stabilizing musical flow. Harmony is always felt in its relation to rhythmic structure, and not until chords evolve in a rhythmic form does harmony become wholly articulate. If the harmonic changes are quick, there is an undercurrent of restlessness; if widely spaced, there is breadth. Three forces important to harmonic progression are: the linear motion and outline of outer voices, the pull of harmonic or tonal centers and chord relationships, and the time duration of stressed and unstressed chords. Harmonic rhythm does not move when chords are repeated (Ch. 10, pp. 211-213).

# Prerequisites
- **Harmonic progression** -- understanding chord succession

# Key Properties
1. Quick harmonic changes create restlessness
2. Widely spaced changes create breadth
3. Harmonic rhythm does not move when chords are repeated
4. Three forces: outer voice motion, tonal center pull, stress/unstress duration
5. Additional forces: graded tensions of texture/pitch, phrasing/bowing/tonguing rhythm
6. Tempo can cause chord changes to sound ornamental
7. Simple chords at high speed may create complex sounds
8. Various combinations of fluctuating melodic and harmonic rhythms give creative potential

# Construction / Recognition
- Mark each point where the harmony changes
- Note the rhythm created by these change points
- Compare harmonic rhythm with melodic rhythm and metric framework
- Identify whether changes are regular or irregular
- Observe the effect of tempo on perceived harmonic rhythm

# Context & Application
Harmonic rhythm interacts with melodic rhythm to create varied textures. When both coincide, clarity results; when they conflict, complexity and tension arise. The composer manipulates harmonic rhythm to create breadth, restlessness, drive, relaxation, and formal articulation. Tempo is a determining factor: quick tempo may cause chord changes to sound ornamental rather than structural.

**Example** (Ch. 10, Ex. 10-1, p. 212): Quick tempo causing chord changes to sound ornamental; simple chords at high speed creating complex sounds; repeated chords not moving harmonic rhythm.

# Common Errors
- Confusing chord repetition with harmonic rhythm movement
- Ignoring the effect of tempo on harmonic rhythm perception
- Maintaining uniform harmonic rhythm without variation

# Common Confusions
- **Harmonic rhythm vs. melodic rhythm**: Harmonic rhythm concerns chord change rate; melodic rhythm concerns note durations and patterns
- **Chord change vs. movement**: Repeating a chord does not create harmonic rhythm movement
- **Speed vs. complexity**: Fast harmonic rhythm can make simple chords sound complex
