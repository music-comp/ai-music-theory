# Quick Definition
Nonharmonic tones that ornament a chord tone by stepping to an adjacent pitch and returning, also called neighbor tones.

# Formal Definition
Auxiliary tones (neighbor tones) belong to the first category of nonharmonic tones: those approached and left without skip. Unlike passing tones which connect different chord tones, auxiliary tones ornament a single chord tone by moving to an adjacent pitch (upper or lower) and returning to the original tone. They may be diatonic or chromatic.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Auxiliary tone characteristics:
- **Approach**: By step from chord tone
- **Departure**: By step back to same chord tone
- **Direction**: Returns to starting pitch (unlike passing tones)
- **Types**: Upper auxiliary (step above) or lower auxiliary (step below)
- **Grouping**: May be grouped chord-wise to form auxiliary chords

The auxiliary tone creates a brief departure from the chord tone while the harmonic context remains stable, providing ornamental interest without linear progression.

# Musical Context/Application
In twentieth-century practice, auxiliary tones may be derived from various scalar sources including synthetic scales built upon the chord root. They can create momentary dissonance against simple chords or add subtle color to complex harmonies. When grouped chord-wise, auxiliary tones form auxiliary chords that ornament the principal harmony. The auxiliary motion may be combined with other ornamental patterns before the chord tone returns.

# Common Confusions
- Auxiliary tones return to their starting pitch; passing tones continue in one direction
- "Neighbor tone" is another term for auxiliary tone
- The step size (half or whole) depends on the scale context being employed
