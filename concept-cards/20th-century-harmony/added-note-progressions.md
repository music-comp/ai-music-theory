# Quick Definition
The movement of added-note chords in succession, governed by the underlying basic harmony (not the added notes), requiring that a clear harmonic progression be either implied or previously established -- without this, complex added-note chords lose identity and merge into compound harmony.

# Core Definition
The progressive motion of added-note chords is governed by the basic harmony to which added notes are attached. Added-note chords are effective as such only when a clear and definite harmonic progression is implied by the basic chords or is previously established by chords without added notes. This is particularly true of chords containing many notes spread over a wide range. Notes may be added to complex basic chords if the structures are first clearly stated without the color tones. If notes are added to a complex basic chord without textural preparation, a chord of a larger basic formation -- not of added notes -- is heard. A large compound chord and a complex added-note chord are often identical; unless a firm harmonic progression is felt, compound harmony will prevail.

# Key Properties
1. Basic harmony governs motion (not added notes)
2. Clear progression must be implied or previously established
3. Complex chords need textural preparation (stated without color tones first)
4. Without preparation: complex added-note chord sounds like compound chord
5. Added-note and compound chords often identical in pitch content
6. Perceptual distinction depends on felt harmonic progression
7. Traditional devices (cadences, sequences, formulas) protect basic chords
8. Added-note chords may be secundal in appearance

# Construction / Recognition
- Verify a clear harmonic progression underlies the added-note surface
- For complex chords: establish the basic progression first without added notes
- Then add color tones to the established structures
- If no progression is felt: the result is compound harmony, not added-note

# Context & Application
This principle is crucial for maintaining harmonic clarity in dense textures. Without recognizable harmonic progressions, added-note chords lose identity. Sequences, cadences, and periodic unadorned statements of basic chords anchor the added-note texture. Added-note chords that are secundal in appearance require contextual differentiation from true secundal harmony.
