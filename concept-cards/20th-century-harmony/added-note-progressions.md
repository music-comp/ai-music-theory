# Quick Definition
The movement of added-note chords in succession, governed by the basic harmony to which added notes are attached, requiring clear harmonic progressions or previous establishment of the basic chords without added notes.

# Formal Definition
The progressive motion of added-note chords is governed by the basic harmony to which added notes are attached. Added-note chords are effective as such only when a clear and definite harmonic progression is implied by the basic chords or is previously established by chords without added notes. This is particularly true of chords containing many notes spread over a wide range.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Progression principles:
1. Basic harmony governs motion (not the added notes)
2. Traditional devices protect from "crowding seconds":
   - Cadences
   - Sequences
   - Traditional harmonic formulas

Textural preparation requirement:
- Complex basic chords need textural preparation
- State structures first without color tones
- Then add color tones to prepared structures

Identification rule:
- If notes added to complex chord without preparation -> heard as larger basic formation
- If firm progression felt under structure -> heard as added-note chord
- Large compound chord and complex added-note chord often identical in pitch content

# Musical Context/Application
This principle is crucial for maintaining harmonic clarity in densely textured passages. Without the anchor of recognizable harmonic progressions, added-note chords lose their identity and merge into undifferentiated compound harmony.

Devices for maintaining progression clarity:
- Use of sequences with added-note chords
- Periodic cadences revealing basic harmony
- Introduction of unadorned basic chords before adding color
- Traditional root movements underlying the added-note surface

# Common Confusions
- Added notes do not create new progressions; they color existing ones
- The same pitch collection can be added-note or compound depending on context
- Lack of preparation makes complex added-note chords sound like compound chords
- The distinction is perceptual, not absolute - context determines category
