# Quick Definition
A polychord with a minor triad as the bottom unit and a major triad as the upper unit, which is less rich than the major-minor combination because the upper major triad receives less support from the overtones of the minor bottom chord.

# Formal Definition
A minor over a major triad is richer than a major over a minor; the upper triad in the first formation receives additional support from tones of the lower chord other than the bottom tone from which it is generated. Of the minor-major polychords, numbers 1, 5, and 10 have wider use because they are more resonant.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Naming convention: Bottom quality - Upper quality
- "Minor-major" = minor bottom, major upper

Acoustic explanation:
- Minor triad generates fewer supportive overtones than major
- Upper major triad has less harmonic reinforcement
- Result: Less rich, more exposed upper unit

Most useful minor-major combinations: 1, 5, and 10
- These have strongest overtone relationships
- Selected from cycle-of-fifths ordering
- Other combinations less resonant

Character comparison:
- Minor-major: Brighter upper unit, darker foundation
- Major-minor: Darker upper unit, brighter foundation
- Minor-major generally less blended than major-minor

# Musical Context/Application
Minor-major polychords create a distinctive color where the brightness of the upper major triad contrasts with the darker minor bottom. The reduced blend compared to major-minor can be exploited for effect or compensated through spacing and instrumentation.

Numbers 1, 5, and 10 are most frequently used due to their greater resonance. The choice depends on:
- Desired degree of blend
- Melodic/voice-leading requirements
- Overall harmonic context

# Common Confusions
- Naming is from bottom up, not top down
- Less resonant does not mean less useful, but requires more care in use
- The acoustic difference from major-minor is real, not merely theoretical
- Spacing and orchestration can compensate for reduced natural resonance
