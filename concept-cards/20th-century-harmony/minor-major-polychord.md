# Quick Definition

A polychord with a minor bottom unit and major upper unit, less rich than major-minor because the upper major triad receives less overtone support from the minor bottom chord. Numbers 1, 5, and 10 have wider use.

# Core Definition

"A minor over a major triad is richer than a major over a minor; the upper triad in the first formation receives additional support from tones of the lower chord other than the bottom tone from which it is generated. Of the minor-major polychords, numbers 1, 5, and 10 have wider use because they are more resonant" (Persichetti, p. 142).

# Prerequisites

- **Polychord** — The minor-major is a subtype of polychord
- **Polychord resonance** — Resonance ranking determines usefulness

# Key Properties

1. Named from bottom up: minor bottom, major upper
2. Less rich than major-minor because minor bottom generates weaker overtones
3. Numbers 1, 5, and 10 most useful due to greater resonance
4. Brighter upper unit (major) over darker foundation (minor)
5. Less blended than major-minor; requires more care in spacing and instrumentation

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct
1. Place minor triad as bottom unit
2. Place major triad as upper unit at desired transposition
3. Select from resonant combinations (1, 5, 10)
4. Compensate for reduced resonance through wider spacing and contrasting instrumentation

# Context & Application

Minor-major polychords create a distinctive color where the brightness of the upper major triad contrasts with the darker minor bottom. The reduced blend compared to major-minor can be exploited for effect or compensated through spacing and instrumentation.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Expecting minor-major to blend as well as major-minor
  **Correction**: The acoustic difference is real; wider spacing and contrasting instrumentation compensate

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Less resonant means less useful
  **Clarification**: Reduced resonance creates a distinctive character that has its own expressive value
