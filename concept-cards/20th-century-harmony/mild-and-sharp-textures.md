# Quick Definition
Two general texture categories for added-note chords: mild type (no sharp dissonance -- m2, M7) which tends to stop harmonic flow through cadential paralysis, and sharp type (at least one m2 or M7) which maintains tension; mixing both types freely sustains harmonic flow.

# Core Definition
In any kind of added-note chord, two general textures are possible: a mild type having no sharp dissonance and a sharp type having at least one sharp dissonance. Added-note chords of the mild type often stop harmonic flow by their tendency to form a cadence; when used extensively they cause progressions to become paralyzed. A flow can be maintained by mixing freely the mild and sharp types. The progressive motion of added-note chords is governed by the basic harmony to which added notes are attached. Traditional devices (cadences, sequences, harmonic formulas) help protect basic chords from the debilitating effect of crowding seconds.

# Prerequisites
- **Added-note chord**: The formations exhibiting these two textures

# Key Properties
1. Mild = no m2, M7, or compounds; sharp = at least one m2, M7, or compound
2. Mild chords tend toward cadence (flow-stopping)
3. Extensive mild chords = harmonic paralysis
4. Sharp chords maintain tension and forward motion
5. Mix mild and sharp freely for maintained flow
6. Basic harmony governs progressive motion (not the added notes)
7. Traditional devices (sequences, cadences) protect against crowding

# Construction / Recognition
- Check for m2, M7, or compound intervals
- None present = mild; any present = sharp
- Monitor flow: too many mild chords in succession = paralysis
- Mix textures for sustained motion

# Context & Application
The mild/sharp distinction is fundamental to controlling harmonic rhythm in added-note harmony. Composers balance textures to avoid cadential stagnation (too mild) or excessive tension (too sharp). Traditional harmonic devices serve as structural scaffolding beneath the added-note surface.

# Common Errors
- Using mild chords exclusively (causes harmonic paralysis)
- Confusing "mild" with "consonant" (mild still contains added dissonances)

# Common Confusions
- **"Mild" does not mean "consonant"**: Mild chords still contain added dissonances; they lack sharp dissonances specifically
- **"Sharp" = specific intervals**: Refers to m2 and M7, not to overall harshness
- **Paralysis from mild chords**: Comes from cadential tendency, not from lack of interest
