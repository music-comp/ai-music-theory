# Quick Definition
The structural principles governing atonal music, including motivic concentration, rhythmic asymmetry, and intervallic rather than tonal relationships.

# Formal Definition
Atonal organization operates within a syntax that favors dissonant formations, with structure based upon shifting intervallic tension or an order of tones. The various elements in atonal music are tightly knit by extreme motivic concentration, and reference is constantly made to previous material. There is little regular rhythmic stamping and no continuous chain rhythms; the rhythmic patterns are asymmetrical and the meters irregular and often complicated.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Organizational elements in atonal music:
- **Intervallic basis**: Shifting intervallic tension rather than tonal hierarchy
- **Motivic concentration**: Extreme focus on motivic development
- **Constant reference**: Continuous relation to previous material
- **Rhythmic asymmetry**: No regular patterns or chain rhythms
- **Metric irregularity**: Irregular and complicated meters

Structural resources:
- Repetition of chromatic sound groups
- Variation of motivic material
- Mutation of chromatic formations
- Vertical structures as contributing element (not governing force)

# Musical Context/Application
When the controlling principle of scalar tonality is abandoned, chordal root organization of the twelve tones ceases to exist, and form and unity are created by melodic and rhythmic development. Although harmonic factors are dependent upon melodic relationships, vertical structures are often manipulated as a contributing element in atonal composition. The tight motivic concentration provides coherence without tonal hierarchy.

# Examples
- Atonal compositions featuring extreme motivic concentration and asymmetrical rhythm
- Works by Schoenberg, Berg, Webern showing tight motivic relationships

# Common Confusions
- Atonal organization is organization, just not tonal organization
- Harmony remains a contributing element even when not governing
- Motivic concentration replaces tonal hierarchy as unifying force
