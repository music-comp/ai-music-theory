# Quick Definition
The structural principles governing atonal music: extreme motivic concentration, constant reference to previous material, asymmetrical rhythmic patterns, irregular meters, and shifting intervallic tension as the organizational basis.

# Core Definition
The various elements in atonal music are tightly knit by extreme motivic concentration, and reference is constantly made to previous material. There is little regular rhythmic stamping and no continuous chain rhythms; the rhythmic patterns are asymmetrical and the meters irregular and often complicated. Atonality operates within a syntax that favors dissonant formations, and its organization is based upon shifting intervallic tension or an order of tones.

When chromatic chordal mixtures accumulate, formal coherence is achieved by repetition, variation, or mutation of the chromatic sound groups. Although the harmonic factors are dependent upon melodic relationships, these vertical structures are often manipulated as a contributing element in atonal composition.

# Prerequisites
- **Atonality**: The musical context being organized

# Key Properties
1. Extreme motivic concentration as primary unifying force
2. Constant reference to previous material
3. Asymmetrical rhythmic patterns (no regular stamping)
4. No continuous chain rhythms
5. Irregular and complicated meters
6. Shifting intervallic tension as organizational basis
7. Formal coherence through repetition, variation, or mutation of chromatic sound groups
8. Vertical structures as contributing (not governing) element

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the motivic kernel and track its transformations
- Note the absence of regular rhythmic patterns
- Observe shifting intervallic tension rather than tonal hierarchy
- Look for repetition, variation, and mutation of chromatic formations
- Assess whether vertical structures contribute to or govern the organization
- Track constant cross-references to previous material

# Context & Application
When the controlling principle of scalar tonality is abandoned, chordal root organization of the twelve tones ceases to exist, and form and unity are created by melodic and rhythmic development. The tight motivic concentration provides coherence without tonal hierarchy. Harmony, even when secondary, is an important consideration in linear music -- atonal composition does not ignore vertical sonority.

# Examples
- Arnold Schoenberg, Pierrot Lunaire: extreme motivic concentration
- Alban Berg, Wozzeck: formal coherence through motivic development
- Carl Ruggles, Evocations for Piano: tight motivic relationships
- Anton Webern, Five Pieces for String Quartet Op. 5: asymmetrical rhythm and motivic concentration

# Common Errors
- Assuming atonal organization is random or arbitrary
- Overlooking the role of vertical structures
- Imposing tonal analytical frameworks on atonal music

# Common Confusions
- **Organization exists**: Atonality has structure -- just not tonal structure
- **Harmony's role**: Vertical combinations are a contributing element in atonal composition, not absent
- **Motivic vs. tonal**: Motivic concentration replaces tonal hierarchy as the primary unifying force
