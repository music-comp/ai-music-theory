# Quick Definition
The Chapter 13 synthesis-context treatment of the transformation from polychordal succession to polytonal texture, where harmonic units in a tonal polychordal progression meet at a point where each progresses in a separate and consistent key zone, positioned on the continuum toward atonality.

# Core Definition
The harmonic units of a tonal polychordal succession may meet at a point where each progresses in a separate and consistent key zone. The polychordal progression then becomes polytonal. Atonality may be entered through florid writing when the individual lines are given such freedom of chromatic movement that the implied harmony becomes indefinite and the feeling of key disappears. This positions the transformation on a continuum: polychords to polytonality to atonality, each step representing increasing independence and chromatic freedom of the component layers.

# Prerequisites
- **Polychord approach**: How polychords are entered and exited
- **Polytonality**: The multi-key result

# Key Properties
1. Polychordal succession may reach a point of independent key-zone progression
2. At that point, the texture becomes polytonal
3. Further chromatic freedom can dissolve polytonality into atonality
4. Represents a continuum: polychord -> polytonality -> atonality
5. Each step involves greater independence of component layers
6. Chapter 13 places this in the context of texture combination

# Construction / Recognition
- Begin with a polychordal succession
- Give each unit's component progressions independent key-zone identities
- The texture becomes polytonal when each layer maintains a consistent key
- Further chromatic freedom may dissolve key feeling entirely
- Track the level of layer independence: vertical (polychordal), independent (polytonal), free (atonal)

# Context & Application
In Chapter 13's synthesis framework, this transformation is positioned as one pathway within the broader toolkit of texture combination. The polychordal-to-polytonal transformation demonstrates that these texture types are not discrete categories but points on a continuum of layer independence. The further step toward atonality shows the full range of this continuum. This understanding allows the composer to control the degree of layer independence precisely, moving fluidly along the continuum as the music requires.

# Common Errors
- Treating polychords, polytonality, and atonality as discrete categories
- Failing to control the degree of layer independence
- Making the transition too abrupt

# Common Confusions
- **Continuum, not categories**: Polychordal, polytonal, and atonal are points on a continuum of layer independence
- **Ch12 vs. Ch13**: Chapter 12 treats the transformation within polytonality; Chapter 13 positions it within the broader synthesis framework
- **Layer independence**: The key variable is how independently each layer progresses, not the specific harmonic content
