# Quick Definition
Compositional techniques for developing and extending harmonic material through motivic transformation and voice-to-voice repetition, where the transformative character of elements gives a work its personal identity.

# Core Definition
The many motivic cells that make up a theme are the concern of the composer when extending harmonic material. Repetition is one of the most important devices, emerging as **sequence**, **imitation**, **variation**, **ostinato**, and in various other guises. Of the many techniques, none is more trying than literal repetition. Development involves motivic metamorphosis; the life of a structural unit depends upon the transformative character of its elements. **Imitation** is a form of repetition where motivic elements move from voice to voice -- by nature a linear process and valuable for achieving meaningful part-writing and harmonic suppleness. The strictness of imitation is no measure of aesthetic evaluation, nor is free imitation necessarily a sign of inventiveness (Ch. 11, pp. 234-236).

# Prerequisites
- **Harmonic progression** -- understanding harmonic material to be extended

# Key Properties
1. Repetition: the most important compositional device (and most difficult when literal)
2. Extension techniques: sequence, diminution, augmentation, intervallic expansion/contraction, order change, inversion, hidden tone-relationships, filling out, dismemberment, retrogression, retrograde inversion, rhythmic transformation
3. Ascending sequence adds dynamic tension; descending relaxes
4. Diminution promotes drive; augmentation lessens movement tension
5. Sequences soften extremely dissonant passages
6. Imitation: linear process, voice-to-voice, strict or free
7. Strictness of imitation is no measure of aesthetic quality
8. Success depends on the expressive climate

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify motivic cells and trace their transformations
- Classify extension technique: sequence, diminution, augmentation, inversion, etc.
- Check for imitative entries and their degree of strictness
- Note whether the extension serves the expressive climate

# Context & Application
Variants of the motif include retrogression, retrograde inversion, and rhythmic transformation; although original identity is often obscured, unified musical expression may unfold. Imitation is valuable for achieving meaningful part-writing and harmonic suppleness. Removing certain notes creates hidden tone-relationships; filling out with ornaments produces additional segments for further extension.

# Common Errors
- Overusing literal repetition without sufficient timing and taste
- Assuming strict imitation is superior to free imitation
- Extending material without maintaining connection to original motifs

# Common Confusions
- Strictness of imitation is not a measure of quality
- Literal repetition is the most "trying" (difficult) technique to use effectively
- Extension concerns both melodic and harmonic dimensions
