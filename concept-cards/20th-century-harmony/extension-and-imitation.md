# Quick Definition
Compositional techniques for developing and extending harmonic material through motivic transformation and voice-to-voice repetition.

# Formal Definition
Extension refers to the techniques composers use to develop and expand harmonic material through motivic transformation. Imitation is a specific form of extension in which motivic elements move from voice to voice, creating repetition across different registers or timbres. Both techniques concern the manipulation of the many motivic cells that make up a theme, and the transformative character of elements gives a structural unit its life.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Extension techniques include:
- **Sequence**: Melodic, harmonic, rhythmic, or combined (ascending adds tension, descending relaxes)
- **Diminution**: Shortening rhythmic values (promotes drive)
- **Augmentation**: Lengthening rhythmic values (lessens movement tension)
- **Intervallic expansion/contraction**: Widening or narrowing melodic intervals
- **Order change**: Rearranging motivic elements
- **Inversion**: Revealing fresh melodic and harmonic aspects
- **Hidden tone-relationships**: Removing certain notes
- **Filling out**: Adding ornaments to thematic material
- **Dismemberment**: Omitting parts for later expansion
- **Retrogression, retrograde inversion**: Reversed or mirror-reversed forms
- **Rhythmic transformation**: Altering durational patterns

# Musical Context/Application
Imitation is by nature a linear process and valuable for achieving meaningful part-writing and harmonic suppleness. The strictness of imitation is no measure of aesthetic evaluation, nor is free imitation necessarily a sign of inventiveness. The success of linear devices in a harmonic context depends upon the expressive climate in which they operate. Development of thematic material involves motivic metamorphosis.

# Examples
Harmony containing extensive imitation:
- Bela Bartok, String Quartet No. 5, p. 27 (Boosey)
- Boris Blacher, Studie im Pianissimo (orchestra), p. 28 (Bote)
- Roy Harris, Symphony No. 3, pp. 85-91 (G. Schirmer)
- Paul Hindemith, Symphonie Mathis der Maler, pp. 16-20 (Schott)
- Arnold Schoenberg, Verklarte Nacht (string orchestra), pp. 4-5 (Associated)
- Anton Webern, Symphonie Op. 21, p. 8 (Universal)

# Common Confusions
- Strictness of imitation is not a measure of quality
- Literal repetition is the most "trying" (difficult) technique to use effectively
- Extension concerns both melodic and harmonic dimensions
