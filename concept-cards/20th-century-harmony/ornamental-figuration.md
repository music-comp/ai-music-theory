# Quick Definition
Melodic embellishments that decorate vertical harmonic structures, consisting of either harmonic (chord-derived) ornamental tones or nonharmonic (chord-foreign) ornamental tones.

# Core Definition
Vertical structures form a harmonic skeleton upon which melodic figuration may rest. These melodic embellishments may be **harmonic** or **nonharmonic** ornamental tones. Ornamental harmonic tones consist of repeated chord tones and broken-chord tones and create no intervallic resistance to the harmony. When ornamental figuration is created by chords broken in two or more voices, the individual parts gain freedom. Harmonic ornamentation may increase the space between chord changes and relax harmonic rhythm. Members of a broken chord sounded by a single voice may imply meaningful harmonic progression. Simple chords may be embellished so that one chord penetrates another, creating polychordal sounds where they meet (Ch. 11, pp. 229-234).

# Prerequisites
- **Harmonic progression** -- understanding the harmonic skeleton being ornamented

# Key Properties
1. Harmonic ornamental tones: repeated chord tones and broken-chord tones (no intervallic resistance)
2. Nonharmonic ornamental tones: inherently restless (intervallic counteraction)
3. Multi-voice broken chords give individual parts freedom
4. Harmonic ornamentation can relax harmonic rhythm
5. Single-voice broken chord may imply harmonic progression
6. Embellishment can create polychordal sounds at chord-meeting points
7. Ornamental tones may derive from scale built on chord root
8. Ornamental patterns may derive from contrasting key centers (temporary polytonality)
9. Ornamental tone may remain unresolved, changing harmonic texture

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the harmonic skeleton (chord tones)
- Classify each non-chord tone as harmonic ornament (from the chord) or nonharmonic (foreign)
- Note whether embellishment creates polychordal overlaps
- Check if ornamental tones derive from the root's scale or from contrasting keys

# Context & Application
In twentieth-century practice, ornamental figuration serves multiple purposes: relaxing harmonic rhythm, implying progression through arpeggiation, creating polychordal textures through embellishment overlap, and changing harmonic texture through unresolved ornamental tones. Relatively simple harmony or held chords may be spiced by dissonant and free ornamentation.

# Common Errors
- Failing to distinguish between harmonic ornamentation (no resistance) and nonharmonic ornamentation (resistance)
- Not recognizing when embellishment has permanently changed the harmonic texture

# Common Confusions
- **Harmonic ornamentation** (using chord tones) does not create dissonance; nonharmonic ornamentation does
- **"Accented"** refers to coinciding with harmonic change, not metric accent
- Simple embellishment can create complex polychordal sounds at overlap points
