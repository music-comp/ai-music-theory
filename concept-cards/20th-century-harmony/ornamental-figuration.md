# Quick Definition
Melodic embellishments that decorate vertical harmonic structures, consisting of either harmonic (chord-derived) or nonharmonic (chord-foreign) tones.

# Formal Definition
Ornamental figuration refers to the melodic embellishments that rest upon the harmonic skeleton formed by vertical structures. These ornamental tones divide into two categories: harmonic ornamental tones (repeated chord tones and broken-chord tones) which create no intervallic resistance to the harmony, and nonharmonic ornamental tones which are inherently restless due to their intervallic counteraction to the chord. Harmonic ornamentation may increase the space between chord changes and relax harmonic rhythm.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Ornamental figuration operates through:
- **Harmonic ornamentation**: Chord tones repeated, arpeggiated, or broken across voices
- **Nonharmonic ornamentation**: Tones foreign to the current chord creating intervallic tension
- **Accented vs. unaccented**: Nonharmonic tones are accented when they occur with change of harmony, regardless of metric position
- **Multi-voice broken chords**: When broken in two or more voices, individual parts gain freedom

# Musical Context/Application
In twentieth-century practice, ornamental figuration serves multiple purposes: it can relax harmonic rhythm by extending time between chord changes, it can imply harmonic progression through broken-chord patterns in a single voice, and it can create polychordal sounds when simple chords are embellished in ways that cause one chord to penetrate another. The ornamental tone may remain unresolved, causing a change of harmonic texture.

# Common Confusions
- Harmonic ornamentation (using chord tones) does not create dissonance, while nonharmonic ornamentation does
- "Accented" refers to coinciding with harmonic change, not metric accent
- Simple embellishment can create complex polychordal sounds at points of overlap
