# Quick Definition
The registral placement of chordal units within a polychord, where wider spacing allows maximum tonal vibration, smaller intervals in upper register and wider intervals in lower register create the best resonance, and contrasting instrumentation clarifies harmonic zones.

# Formal Definition
The density of the polychordal structure is determined by spacing. Placing chordal units well apart allows for maximum tonal vibration. A polychord gains resonance if smaller intervals are placed in the upper register and wider intervals are placed in the lower register. Contrasting instrumentation of the chordal units clearly defines the contrasting harmonic zones.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Spacing principles:
1. **Wide spacing**: Units well apart
   - Maximum tonal vibration
   - Clear definition of harmonic zones
   - Less muddiness

2. **Close spacing**: Units near each other
   - Cloudiness, nebulousness
   - Valuable for fluctuating tension
   - Risk of harmonic zones "running together"

Register distribution:
- Upper register: smaller intervals acceptable
- Lower register: wider intervals preferred
- Follows natural overtone spacing

Additional clarity techniques:
- Contrasting instrumentation between units
- Common tones help blend units
- Doubling consonant intervals gives strength

Outer-voice relationship:
- Consonant outer voices spread effectiveness throughout
- Dissonant outer voices similarly spread their quality
- Choice affects overall character

# Musical Context/Application
Careful attention to spacing distinguishes effective polychordal writing from muddy or unclear textures. Close spacing with monochrome scoring produces cloudiness; wide spacing with contrasted instrumentation produces clarity.

When the lowest note of the polychord is placed somewhere below the bass clef low F, the harmony becomes muddy unless open-position chords are used. The "muddy" polychord is nevertheless a valuable structure under appropriate dramatic conditions.

As a polychord is transposed upward it loses body and resonance but gains brilliance. Drastic complexion changes occur in lower register transpositions.

# Common Confusions
- Close spacing is not always bad; it creates specific effects
- "Muddy" is sometimes the desired result
- Spacing affects perceived identity (polychord vs. non-polychord)
- Register transposition changes character, not just pitch
