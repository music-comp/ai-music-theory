# Quick Definition
Scales spanning two octaves where tones in the upper octave are not duplications of the lower octave, providing extended intervallic color for complex chordal formations.

# Formal Definition
New scales may be so built with similar or dissimilar tetrachords that the tonic is not repeated at the first octave. When the octave is missed and the tetrachords are continued, a multi-octave scale may evolve. A second type of two-octave scale is built by combining two different one-octave scales with common tonics.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation
Two types of two-octave scales:
1. Extended tetrachords: continuing tetrachord pattern past the octave
2. Combined scales: two different one-octave scales sharing a tonic

Characteristics:
- Tones 9-15 are not duplications of tones 2-8
- Modal versions are impractical due to tremendous intervallic span
- 15th and 17th of a chord by thirds are not duplications of root and third

# Musical Context/Application
The tremendous span of intervallic color makes shifting of modal versions impracticable. The principal chords of two-octave scales tend to be large, complex structures that clearly define the two-octave compass. These scales serve as a unifying element in music of complex chordal formations.

# Common Confusions
Students may assume all scales repeat at the octave. Two-octave scales provide unique resources for extended harmony where upper extensions (15ths, 17ths) have different pitch content than their lower counterparts.
