# Quick Definition
Harmonic writing employing all twelve chromatic tones, ranging from chromatic embellishment of diatonic progressions to fully chromatic textures with or without tonal centers.

# Core Definition
Chromatic harmony encompasses five distinct approaches: (1) chromatic figuration of non-chromatic harmony, (2) chromatic harmony with diatonic melodic writing, (3) chromatic melody generated by chromatic harmony, (4) mixed chordal structures formed by the chromatic motion of parts, and (5) chromatic harmony generated by chromatic melodic writing. When characteristic groups of melodic notes are used vertically, chords are formed by intervals from the horizontal motif. Composition with twelve notes can generate this kind of harmony.

# Key Properties
1. **Five types of chromatic writing**:
   - Chromatic figuration of non-chromatic harmony (decorative)
   - Chromatic harmony with diatonic melody (harmonic chromaticism)
   - Chromatic melody over chromatic harmony (full chromaticism)
   - Mixed structures from chromatic voice motion (part-writing-generated)
   - Harmony derived from chromatic melodic motifs (motif-generated)
2. **Motif-to-chord derivation**: horizontal melodic groups used vertically
3. **Bidirectional relationship**: melody can generate harmony, or harmony can generate melody
4. **Compound harmony**: chromatic harmony often contains miscellaneous intervallic building materials

# Construction / Recognition
1. Identify whether chromatic elements are in melody, harmony, or both
2. Classify the relationship: is one generating the other, or are they independent?
3. Check for motivic connections between horizontal and vertical elements
4. Determine whether a tonal center is present, shifting, or absent

# Context & Application
The five types represent a spectrum from chromatic decoration of essentially diatonic music to fully chromatic composition. The most advanced type -- harmony generated from melodic motifs -- anticipates twelve-tone composition, where the horizontal set generates all vertical structures. This approach creates deep unity between melodic and harmonic dimensions.

# Examples
- Stravinsky, *Symphony of Psalms*, p. 2 (Boosey): chromatic figuration of non-chromatic harmony
- Harris, *Little Suite*, p. 5 (C. Fischer): chromatic harmony with diatonic melody
- Berg, *Funf Orchester-Lieder*, Op. 4, p. 5 (Universal): chromatic harmony with chromatic melody
- Ex. 2-53: mixed chordal structures from chromatic part motion
- Sessions, *Piano Concerto*, p. 34 (Marks): full chromatic harmony
- Scriabine, *Piano Sonata No. 9*, p. 1 (Leeds): chromatic harmony

# Common Errors
- **Error**: Treating all chromatic harmony as equivalent. **Correction**: The five types have different compositional implications; distinguishing them reveals the relationship between melody and harmony.

# Common Confusions
- **Confusion**: Assuming chromatic = atonal. **Clarification**: Many types of chromatic harmony maintain tonal centers; atonality is one possibility, not the default.
