# Quick Definition

Perfect fifths or octaves approached by similar motion, which give coupled two-part harmony textural strength and thirdless harmony freedom of movement in twentieth-century practice.

# Formal Definition

**Hidden fifths and octaves** (also called direct fifths and octaves) occur when two voices approach a perfect fifth or octave by similar motion. While traditional counterpoint restricted these progressions, twentieth-century practice recognizes their value: hidden fifths and octaves give coupled two-part harmony textural strength and thirdless harmony a freedom of movement not available when such motion is prohibited.

# Musical/Mathematical Formulation

Hidden fifth/octave:
- Two voices move in same direction
- Arrive at perfect fifth or octave
- Neither voice moves by step to the perfect interval (distinguishing from some traditional exceptions)

Traditional concern:
- Similar motion to perfect consonance weakens voice independence
- Voices tend to fuse rather than maintain separate identity

Twentieth-century value:
- Fusion is desirable in coupled textures
- Thirdless harmony benefits from motion freedom
- Textural strength outweighs independence concerns in specific contexts

# Musical Context/Application

Hidden fifths and octaves are valuable for:
- Coupled two-part writing requiring unified texture
- Quartal and quintal harmony where thirds are absent
- Reinforcing melodic contours with harmonic support
- Creating bold, open sonorities

The technique exploits:
- Voice fusion for textural unity
- Freedom from third-based voice-leading restrictions
- Parallel-fifth and parallel-octave vocabulary
- Open, archaic, or folk-like character

# Common Confusions

- **Hidden vs. parallel**: Hidden fifths/octaves approach by similar motion; parallel fifths/octaves maintain the interval through parallel motion.
- **Prohibited vs. resource**: Traditional prohibition focused on voice independence; modern practice uses the fusion effect as a resource.
- **Context-dependent value**: Hidden fifths/octaves are valuable in specific textures (coupled, thirdless) rather than universally applicable.
