# Quick Definition
The organization of musical elements around a central tone or chord, implied through harmonic articulation and the tension-relaxation relationship of surrounding chords, existing in many degrees and established by many means in twentieth-century music.

# Core Definition
Tonality does not exist as an absolute. It is implied through harmonic articulation and through the tension and relaxation of chords around a tone or chord base. The tonal meaning of an isolated chord is indefinite; it may be a crucial or ornamental chord of many keys, or it may belong to no key. When surrounded by other chords its meaning may be restricted to a single tonality, to two or more wavering tonalities, or made obviously atonal. A particular style or period is not always limited to a single kind of tonality. Twentieth-century music makes use of many degrees of tonality and employs many means for establishing them.

In a strong tonal context, all elements of progression are subordinate to the pull of the tonal center and a drive toward cadential realization. Traditional tonality depends upon scale and chord relationships for its organization. Usually three basic chords are needed to produce a feeling of tonality: one built upon a scale step above the tonic, one below the tonic, and the tonic itself. Harmony with tritone gravitation is helpful in establishing the center.

# Key Properties
1. Tonality is not absolute but exists on a continuum of degrees
2. Three basic chords needed: one above tonic, one below, and tonic itself
3. Tritone gravitation helps establish the center
4. Key feeling may be created by forces other than chordal pillar pull
5. Key consciousness may vanish temporarily to strengthen an emerging key's return
6. The search for tonality may become a creative force in music
7. In equidistant rootless harmony, any note may serve as tonal center through melodic insistence, spacing, or instrumentation
8. At one extreme is tonality, the other atonality; the boundary is indefinite

# Construction / Recognition
- Identify the tonal center through recurring chord emphasis and cadential motion
- Look for the three tonal pillars: chords above, below, and on the tonic
- Check for tritone-embracing scale steps that reinforce the center
- Note non-traditional establishment: persistent dissonant chords, converging tonalities, recurring important tones
- Assess whether key feeling is firm, loose, or absent
- In equidistant harmony, look for melodic insistence, registration, or instrumentation to locate center

# Context & Application
Music may hold its tonality loosely, elements of atonality being inherent from the onset. There are many degrees of key-center gravity or keylessness in the general area of tonality or atonality. Melodic lines in a tonal framework can become so free that the implied harmony becomes evasively dissonant and key feeling is lost. Partial atonality is useful in vague introductions and transition passages, and when preparing for a returning tonal thematic idea. The tonality of a structure may be generated from a unifying harmonic idea from which musical growth extends. An initial major-minor compound chord may promote polychordal writing that instigates a triadic struggle resolved only at the final cadence.

# Common Errors
- Assuming tonality requires traditional V-I motion
- Treating tonality as binary (present/absent) rather than as a continuum
- Overlooking non-traditional means of establishing key feeling

# Common Confusions
- **Tonality vs. key**: Tonality is the broader principle; key is a specific instance
- **Tonality vs. modality**: Modal music has tonality but not traditional major/minor key organization
- **Boundary with atonality**: The point where tonality ends and atonality begins is indefinite, not a clear dividing line
