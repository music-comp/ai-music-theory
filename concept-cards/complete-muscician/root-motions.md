# Quick Definition
The three fundamental types of root movement in tonal harmony: descending fifth (D5), ascending second (A2), and descending third (D3), which can be combined in infinite ways to create diatonic progressions.

# Formal Definition
Diatonic harmonies tend to follow one another by one of three root motions: (1) Descending fifth (D5) or rising fourth -- the strongest root motion, seen in V-I, ii-V, vi-ii, iii-vi. (2) Ascending second (A2) -- seen in IV-V, vi-V, I-ii. (3) Descending third (D3) -- seen in I-vi, vi-IV, iii-I. These root motions can be combined in unlimited ways. For example, descending thirds (I-vi-IV) followed by ascending seconds (IV-V) and a deceptive motion (V-vi), then descending fifths (vi-ii-V-I) yields the progression I-vi-IV-V-vi-ii-V-I.

# Musical Context
Understanding the three root motions provides a practical framework for composing chord progressions and analyzing harmonic motion. With the completion of all diatonic harmonies (I through vii-diminished), these root motions describe how any chord can connect to any other within the key.

# Examples
- D5: V-I (authentic cadence), ii-V, vi-ii, iii-vi
- A2: IV-V (pre-dominant to dominant), I-ii
- D3: I-vi (descending harmonic arpeggiation), vi-IV

# Common Confusions
- These are root motions, not bass motions; the bass may move by different intervals depending on chord inversions
- Not all three root motions are equally strong; D5 is the most powerful, A2 and D3 are supplementary
- Ascending thirds and descending seconds are possible but much less common in standard tonal progressions
