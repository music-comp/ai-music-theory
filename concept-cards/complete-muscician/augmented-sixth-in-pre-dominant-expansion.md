# Quick Definition
Augmented sixth chords frequently combine with other pre-dominant harmonies (iv, ii6, bVI) to expand the pre-dominant function, typically appearing as the last event before the dominant.

# Formal Definition
Given that two of its voices are strongly goal-directed toward ^5, the augmented sixth chord is usually the last event before the dominant in a PD expansion, following either iv(6) or bVI. Common PD expansion patterns include: iv to It6 or Ger6/5 (via chromatic voice exchange), iv through a passing 6/4 to an augmented sixth chord, and bVI converted to Ger6/5 (since the two chords share three of four scale degrees). The augmented sixth chord also appears frequently in chromaticized bass descents as the final step before V.

# Musical Context
Pre-dominant expansions involving augmented sixth chords are a hallmark of dramatic cadential writing from the Classical period onward. The expansion creates a buildup of harmonic tension that intensifies the drive toward the dominant and tonic resolution.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 13, op. 27, no. 1 (Example 30.8): The passage moves from iv6 to Ger6/5 repeatedly, with the Ger6/5-V motion reiterated seven times and marked sf.
- Example 30.6: bVI is converted to Ger6/5 by changing only one pitch (the doubled ^1 becomes #^4), enabling bVI to move smoothly to V without voice-leading problems.

# Common Confusions
When bVI appears followed by an augmented sixth chord, students may analyze them as separate harmonic events. In many cases, the augmented sixth chord is a chromatically altered form of bVI, with only one pitch changed. Recognizing this connection helps explain the smooth voice leading.
