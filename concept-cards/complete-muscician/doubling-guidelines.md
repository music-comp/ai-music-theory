# Quick Definition
In four-voice writing, one triad member must be doubled. Generally double the root; the fifth may be doubled if it improves voice leading; never double the leading tone (^7).

# Formal Definition
Since four voices must accommodate three-note triads, one member must be doubled. The root is the preferred doubling because it is the most stable member. Doubling the fifth is acceptable if it makes for smoother voice leading; doubling the third is a last resort. Tendency tones must never be doubled: this includes the leading tone (^7), chordal dissonances, and chromatically altered tones. Never double the third in V because it is the leading tone. Doubling the third emphasizes the least stable member of the triad, which does not require reinforcement.

# Musical Context
Proper doubling choices are essential for good voice leading and proper resolution of tendency tones. The root provides the strongest harmonic foundation when doubled. In first-inversion chords, doubling rules are modified: scale degrees ^1, ^4, ^5, ^2 are preferred for doubling.

# Common Confusions
Students often default to always doubling the root, even when it creates awkward voice leading. The primary goal is smooth voice leading, and doubling the fifth (or even the third, for non-tendency-tone degrees) is acceptable when it achieves smoother motion. The absolute prohibition is against doubling the leading tone.
