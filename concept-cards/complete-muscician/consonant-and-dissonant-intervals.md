# Quick Definition
Consonant intervals sound stable and at rest (unisons, thirds, perfect fifths, sixths, octaves); dissonant intervals sound unstable and create tension requiring resolution (seconds, perfect fourths, sevenths, all augmented and diminished intervals).

# Formal Definition
Consonant intervals are divided into two categories: perfect consonances (P1, P5, P8), which are the most stable, and imperfect consonances (M3, m3, M6, m6), which are moderately stable yet more fluid. Dissonant intervals include seconds, sevenths, the perfect fourth (in most contexts), and all augmented and diminished intervals. Consonance and dissonance exist on a continuum but are placed into these two categories for practical purposes. Inversionally related intervals share similar stability characteristics.

# Musical Context
The interplay of consonance and dissonance is the engine of musical motion in tonal music. Composers create movement by circulating through consonances and dissonances. Perfect consonances provide stability at beginnings and endings; imperfect consonances drive the music forward; dissonances create tension that demands resolution. The perfect fourth is a special case: usually dissonant in two-voice counterpoint, its status depends on context.

# Common Confusions
The perfect fourth is the most commonly misunderstood interval. It is classified as a dissonance in two-voice counterpoint and when formed with the bass, but it can sound consonant in other contexts (e.g., within upper voices of a chord). Students should treat it as dissonant by default until learning the specific contexts where it behaves differently.
