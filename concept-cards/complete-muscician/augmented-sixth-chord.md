# Quick Definition
An augmented sixth chord is a chromatic pre-dominant harmony built on b^6 in the bass, containing the characteristic interval of an augmented sixth between the bass (b^6) and an upper voice (#^4), both of which resolve outward by half step to ^5.

# Formal Definition
The augmented sixth chord derives from the Phrygian cadence (iv6-V) by chromatically raising ^4 to #^4 to create the strongly directed interval of an augmented sixth above the bass. The bass b^6 falls by half step to ^5 while #^4 ascends by half step to ^5, creating powerful contrary-motion voice leading toward the dominant. The augmented sixth chord functions primarily as a pre-dominant, occurs more frequently in minor-mode pieces (where b^6 is diatonic), and comes in three varieties: Italian (It6), German (Ger6/5), and French (Fr4/3).

# Musical Context
Augmented sixth chords are among the most dramatic chromatic pre-dominants in tonal music. They appear from the Baroque through the Romantic era and contain more linear drive toward the dominant than any other pre-dominant harmony. Their appearance after a pivot chord is particularly helpful in securing a new key during modulations.

# Examples
- Schubert, Waltz in G minor, D. 146 (Example 30.1A): The augmented sixth chord replaces the expected Phrygian cadence (iv6-V) with a chromatic variant.

# Common Confusions
Students often confuse the three types of augmented sixth chords. The basic framework (b^6 in bass, #^4 in upper voice, doubled ^1) is the same for all three. The difference lies in the fourth pitch: It6 doubles ^1; Ger6/5 substitutes b^3 (perfect fifth above bass); Fr4/3 substitutes ^2 (augmented fourth above bass).
