# Quick Definition
A neighbor tone that occurs on a metrically stressed beat, creating greater dissonance and expressive emphasis than its unaccented counterpart.

# Formal Definition
The accented neighbor tone (AN) is a three-note figure identical to the unaccented neighbor but occurring on an accented beat. Just as unaccented upper neighbors are more common than lower neighbors, accented upper neighbors are more common than accented lower neighbors. The accented chromatic neighbor (ACN) is the chromatic variant, using a nondiatonic pitch on the strong beat. The displacement of a chord tone by an accented neighbor can create apparent harmonies that do not truly function as independent chords.

# Musical Context
Accented neighbor tones occur with particular frequency in nineteenth-century music, adding color and expressiveness. Performers should recognize them to avoid misinterpreting the harmony and to shape their dynamic and articulative responses appropriately.

# Examples
- When a soprano's accented neighbor displaces a chord tone in a V6 chord, the resulting vertical sonority may look like a vii6/7 chord, but context reveals the soprano pitch as a nonchord tone

# Common Confusions
- An accented neighbor can create an apparent chord that does not actually function as that harmony -- always interpret based on context
- Accented upper neighbors are more common than accented lower neighbors
- Do not confuse accented neighbors with appoggiaturas: neighbors return to the original pitch, appoggiaturas resolve to a different pitch
