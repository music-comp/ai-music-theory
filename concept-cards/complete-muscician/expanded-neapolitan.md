# Quick Definition
The Neapolitan can be prolonged beyond a single chord through chordal leaps, transposed subphrases, tonicization by its own dominant (V/bII), or extended tonicization with applied chords.

# Formal Definition
Although the Neapolitan often appears as a single bII6 chord, it can be expanded in several ways: (1) by chordal leaps in the bass between root position and first inversion; (2) by restating a subphrase transposed up a half step to bII, creating a root-position Neapolitan; (3) by tonicizing bII with its own dominant, which is enharmonically equivalent to VI in minor (or bVI in major); (4) through extended tonicization using multiple applied chords. No matter how extended, the Neapolitan almost always functions as a pre-dominant within the overall progression.

# Musical Context
Extended Neapolitans are particularly characteristic of middle-period Beethoven and Romantic composers. The technique allows the Neapolitan to become a structural key area rather than a fleeting coloring, creating dramatic tonal departures that are ultimately resolved through the pre-dominant-dominant-tonic progression.

# Examples
- Beethoven, String Quartet in E minor, op. 59, no. 2 (Example 29.6): The root-position Neapolitan emerges when the opening tune is literally restated a half step higher in m. 6, creating a large-scale i-bII-V-i motion.
- Chopin, Nocturne in B minor, op. 9, no. 1 (Example 29.9): The Neapolitan is contrapuntally prepared by a 5-6 motion from I, creating V6/5/bII. Chopin spells the Neapolitan enharmonically as D major rather than Eb major.

# Common Confusions
When the Neapolitan is extended through tonicization, the resulting string of roman numerals can appear confusing. Use brackets to identify the extended tonicization of bII, and always refer to the second-level analysis to show that the expanded Neapolitan still functions as a pre-dominant within the larger progression.
