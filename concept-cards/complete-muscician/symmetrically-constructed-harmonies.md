# Quick Definition
Symmetrically constructed harmonies are chords that partition the octave into equal intervals, such as the diminished seventh chord (four minor thirds) and the augmented triad (two major thirds), creating inherent tonal ambiguity.

# Formal Definition
A symmetrically constructed harmony divides the octave into intervals of the same size. The tonal system is predicated on asymmetrical structures (major and minor triads, containing different-sized thirds), which create functional hierarchy. Symmetrical harmonies, by contrast, make all component intervals equivalent, eliminating the unequal relationships that create tonal direction. Key examples: (1) The diminished seventh chord divides the octave into four minor thirds; only three distinct diminished seventh chords exist. (2) The augmented triad divides the octave into three major thirds (two major thirds spanning an augmented fifth); only four distinct augmented triads exist. (3) The whole-tone scale divides the octave into six major seconds; only two whole-tone collections exist.

# Musical Context
Symmetrical harmonies became increasingly important in nineteenth-century music as tools for creating tonal ambiguity. Their equal internal divisions make them inherently directionless, lacking the asymmetry that creates functional gravity in tonal music. Composers exploited this ambiguity for dramatic and expressive purposes.

# Common Confusions
Symmetry in harmony is different from symmetry in form or melody. Harmonic symmetry refers to equal intervallic division within a chord or progression, which creates tonal ambiguity. Formal symmetry (as in a rondo) creates balance and predictability. The two concepts produce opposite effects.
