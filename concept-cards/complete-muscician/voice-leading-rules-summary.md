# Quick Definition
A comprehensive set of rules and guidelines governing how voices connect between chords, organized into three categories: minimal motion, voice independence, and logical chord construction.

# Formal Definition
The voice-leading rules are organized in three groups: (1) Move voices as little as possible: RULE 1 -- resolve tendency tones (^7 and chordal dissonances) by step; Guidelines 1-4 cover common tones, stepwise motion, avoiding augmented leaps, and checking simultaneous leaps. (2) Maintain voice independence: *RULE 2 -- no parallel or contrary-motion unisons, octaves, or perfect fifths (absolute rule); *RULE 3 -- do not double tendency tones (absolute rule); RULE 4 -- keep adjacent upper voices within an octave; Guidelines 5-6 cover voice crossings, overlaps, and direct fifths/octaves. (3) Construct chords logically: Guideline 7 -- use complete chords (may omit fifth); Guideline 8 -- prefer root doubling. Tips: write outer voices first, use contrary motion to bass, begin in close position.

# Musical Context
These rules remain in effect throughout the entire study of harmony. Only two rules are absolute (marked with asterisks): no parallel perfect intervals and no doubling of tendency tones. All other guidelines may be bent when voice-leading smoothness requires it. The outer voices control everything -- writing them first is the most important procedural tip.

# Examples
RULE 2 (absolute): Moving from C-G (P5) to D-A (P5) between any pair of voices is forbidden. RULE 3 (absolute): Doubling ^7 (the leading tone) results in parallel octaves when both resolve correctly. Guideline exception: ^7 may fall to ^5 in an inner voice rather than rising to ^1, to create a complete triad.

# Common Confusions
Students often treat all rules as equally binding. Only two are absolute: no parallel perfect intervals and no doubled tendency tones. Other guidelines (common tones, complete chords, spacing) may be adjusted for smoother voice leading. The most common procedural error is not writing the outer voices first.
