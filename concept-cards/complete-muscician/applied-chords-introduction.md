# Quick Definition
Chords that temporarily function as the dominant of a diatonic chord other than tonic, introduced here as V/III to prepare the mediant in minor keys.

# Formal Definition
Applied chords (to be explored fully in Chapter 24) are chords that function as the dominant of a chord other than the tonic. In the context of Chapter 19, V/III is introduced: in minor keys, the major triad built on raised ^7 functions as the dominant of III (the mediant). The label "V/III" indicates that this chord is "the dominant (V) of III," reflecting the V-I sound of the progression. This is more analytically accurate than the label "VII," which fails to capture the chord's dominant function. V/III can appear in root position, first inversion (V6/III, with the temporary leading tone in the bass), or with a seventh (V7/III).

# Musical Context
V/III is introduced here because III in minor is so often preceded by its own dominant, which is diatonic in the minor key. Applied chords intensify motion to their target chord by creating a temporary V-I relationship.

# Examples
- In C minor: the B-natural major triad functions as V/III, leading to Eb major (III)
- V6/III places the temporary leading tone in the bass, intensifying the motion to III
- V7/III adds a seventh for even stronger motion to III

# Common Confusions
- V/III is not a modulation; it is a local dominant that temporarily points toward III
- In minor keys, V/III is diatonic (built from natural scale degrees with raised ^7); it is not borrowed or chromatic
- The label "VII" is avoided because it does not capture the dominant function of this chord
