# Quick Definition
A perfect authentic cadence (PAC) is the strongest cadence type, with the bass moving ^5-^1 (V-I in root position) while the soprano moves ^2-^1 or ^7-^8.

# Formal Definition
A perfect authentic cadence (PAC) occurs when a root-position dominant resolves to a root-position tonic with the soprano moving either ^2-^1 or ^7-^8 (^1). Both conditions must be met: the bass must move ^5-^1 and the soprano must arrive on ^1. This cadence provides the strongest harmonic closure in tonal music.

# Musical Context
The PAC is the ultimate goal of tonal phrases and the primary means of establishing finality. Most tonal pieces end with a PAC. Within a piece, PACs at the ends of phrases create strong moments of closure, while earlier phrases may end with weaker cadences (IAC, HC) to maintain forward momentum.

# Examples
In Mozart's Rondo in C major K. 144, the second phrase (m. 8) ends with a PAC: the bass moves from G (^5) to C (^1) while the soprano firmly states ^1. The harmonic rhythm speeds up approaching this cadence, with chord changes every half note in m. 7.

# Common Confusions
A PAC requires both conditions: root-position V to root-position I in the bass AND ^2-^1 or ^7-^1 in the soprano. If either condition is not met, the cadence is something else (IAC if the soprano is on ^3 or ^5; contrapuntal cadence if the bass is not ^5-^1).
