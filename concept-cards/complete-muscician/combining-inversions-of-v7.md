# Quick Definition
Extended tonic prolongations often combine two or more inversions of V7, creating complex embellishing patterns such as double neighbors or combined passing-neighboring motions.

# Formal Definition
Extended tonic prolongations commonly use two or more inverted V7 chords in combination. For example, V43 and V65 can create a double-neighbor pattern in the bass (^2 below ^1 and ^7 above, or vice versa). V43 and V42 can combine to extend tonic through both passing and neighboring functions. The order of inversions matters: moving from a less active bass tone (^2 in V43) to a more active one (^7 in V65) is preferred over the reverse. Multiple-pitch swappings between voices create part exchanges, extending the concept of voice exchange.

# Musical Context
These combinations are the building blocks of sophisticated tonal phrases. Composers from all common-practice periods use them to create extended, harmonically rich tonic prolongations. The patterns created by combined inversions form harmonic paradigms that recur across centuries of tonal music.

# Examples
In Donizetti's Lucrezia Borgia (Example 11.6A), V43 and V65 create double neighbors, linking two-measure subphrases. In Mozart's Presto K. 280 (Example 11.6B), V43 and V42 expand tonic with part exchanges -- the two-note pairs Bb-A and G-F swap between voices while tracing a falling fifth C-F. In Beethoven's Fifth Symphony (Example 11.6C), i-V43-i6-V65-i creates a dramatic tonic expansion with a diminished-fourth bass leap.

# Common Confusions
Students may analyze each inverted V7 independently rather than recognizing them as parts of a larger prolongational pattern. The second-level analysis should show a single tonic harmony being extended through multiple embellishing chords. Also, part exchanges (multiple-pitch swaps) are an extension of voice exchanges (single-pitch swaps).
