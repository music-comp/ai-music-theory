# Quick Definition
Tonal ambiguity is the deliberate obscuring of the tonic's location through techniques such as modal mixture, semitonal voice leading, the reciprocal process, and off-tonic beginnings.

# Formal Definition
Tonal ambiguity is an important aesthetic in nineteenth-century music, involving the deliberate obscuring of which pitch serves as the tonal center. Several techniques produce ambiguity: (1) The plagal relation weakens the dominant and substitutes pre-dominant harmonies at cadences. (2) The reciprocal process creates confusion among tonic, pre-dominant, and dominant functions. (3) Semitonal voice leading connects distantly related chords through half-step motion, suspending tonal function. (4) Off-tonic beginnings postpone tonal clarity. (5) Enharmonic reinterpretation of diminished seventh chords and augmented sixth chords creates tonal misdirection. These techniques range from brief surface-level colorings to deep structural ambiguities.

# Musical Context
Tonal ambiguity became a central compositional aesthetic from the late Classical period through the end of the nineteenth century. Composers such as Mozart, Beethoven, Schubert, Chopin, Brahms, Wagner, and Scriabin progressively extended ambiguity to deeper structural levels, ultimately leading to the dissolution of tonality in the early twentieth century.

# Examples
- Chopin, Etude in C minor, op. 10, no. 12 (Example 34.1): The final section treats the major tonic as V/iv, creating the reciprocal process where the closing tonic sounds like a dominant.
- Beethoven, Symphony no. 4, op. 60, Adagio (Example 34.6): The introduction takes 39 measures to establish Bb major, using b^6 to displace ^5 and create extended tonal uncertainty.

# Common Confusions
Tonal ambiguity is not the same as atonality. In ambiguous passages, the listener cannot immediately determine the tonic, but the music still operates within a tonal framework. The ambiguity creates tension that is typically resolved through eventual tonal clarification.
