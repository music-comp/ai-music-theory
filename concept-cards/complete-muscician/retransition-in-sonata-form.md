# Quick Definition
The retransition (RTr) is the final area of the development section, where the dominant is secured and extended to prepare the tonic's return at the recapitulation.

# Formal Definition
The retransition occupies the end of the development section and prepares the return of the tonic at the recapitulation. It typically features a dominant pedal point or an extended prolongation of the dominant, creating anticipation for the tonic's arrival. In major-mode sonata forms, the dominant is often secured earlier (in the STA) and implicitly prolonged through the development; the retransition makes this dominant explicit. In minor-mode sonata forms, the retransition may need to establish the dominant more forcefully. The retransition culminates in the harmonic interruption that precedes the recapitulation.

# Musical Context
The retransition creates one of the most dramatically charged moments in sonata form. The extended dominant suspension builds tension that is released by the recapitulation's tonic arrival. Some retransitions are brief (a few measures of dominant pedal), while others are substantial passages involving thematic liquidation and harmonic intensification.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 19, op. 49, no. 1 (Example 33.2): The retransition extends the dominant beginning around m. 54 with the interruption before the recapitulation.
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C major, Hob. XVI.35 (Example 33.9): The pedal on E (V/vi) in m. 94 begins a circle of fifths (E-A-D-G) that constitutes the retransition leading to the recapitulation in m. 104.

# Common Confusions
The retransition is part of the development section, not a separate formal section. Students sometimes label the retransition as the beginning of the recapitulation, but the recapitulation starts only when the tonic returns with FTA material. Also, a dominant pedal does not automatically indicate a retransition; it could also be part of a tonic expansion in the FTA.
