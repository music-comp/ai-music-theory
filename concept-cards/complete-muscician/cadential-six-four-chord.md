# Quick Definition
An accented six-four chord over the dominant bass that temporarily postpones the chordal fifth and third of V with a dissonant sixth and fourth, creating a powerful double suspension or double accented passing tone at cadences.

# Formal Definition
The cadential six-four chord (cad 6/4) is a root-position dominant harmony whose chordal fifth and third are temporarily postponed by a dissonant fourth and sixth. These nonharmonic tones are prepared and resolved downward by step. Preparation occurs either by common tone (as double suspensions, when preceded by I or IV) or by step (as accented passing tones, when preceded by a pre-dominant such as ii6). The cad 6/4 always occurs on a metrically accented beat (beat 1 or beat 3 in 4/4; beat 1 or beat 2 in 3/4), and its resolution occurs on weaker beats.

# Musical Context
The cadential six-four is regularly heard in Classical-period music from the early eighteenth through late nineteenth centuries. It participates in both half cadences (intensifying V) and authentic cadences. It can lead to V (in HC or AC) or V7 (in AC with 8-7 motion). The bass should always be doubled.

# Examples
- Mozart, Concerto in Eb for Horn, K. 447: cad 6/4 at both HC (m. 4) and PAC (m. 8)
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in G minor, Hob. XVI/44: cad 6/4 within a phrase and at the cadence

# Common Confusions
- The cad 6/4 is NOT a tonic chord in second inversion; it is a dominant harmony with nonharmonic tones
- It must occur on a strong beat; unaccented six-four chords have different functions (pedal, passing, arpeggiating)
- Do not precede the cad 6/4 with another dominant-function chord (vii6, vii7), as this weakens its impact
- Approach and leave all six-four chords by step or common tone
