# Quick Definition
An altered dominant seventh chord chromatically raises or lowers ^2 (the chordal fifth of V7), creating either an augmented sixth with ^4 (raised ^2) or an augmented sixth with ^7 (lowered ^2), intensifying the chord's resolution.

# Formal Definition
Two types of altered V7 exist: (1) When ^2 is raised (#^2), it forms an augmented sixth interval with ^4 (the chordal seventh) and must rise to ^3, resulting in a doubled third in the tonic chord. (2) When ^2 is lowered (b^2), it forms an augmented sixth with ^7 (the leading tone). In second inversion, this produces a chord identical to a French augmented sixth (Fr4/3). This creates a reciprocal process: the chord sounds like it participates in a half cadence (Fr4/3 to V) but actually functions as an authentic cadence (altered V4/3 to I). This type is labeled FrV4/3.

# Musical Context
Altered dominants became increasingly important in late-nineteenth-century music as composers sought new harmonic colors and tonal ambiguities. The reciprocal process between the French augmented sixth and the altered dominant is particularly significant for composers such as Brahms and Scriabin.

# Examples
- Brahms, Symphony no. 4, op. 98, Allegro energico e passionato (Example 35.15): The altered V4/3 (with b^2 in the bass) is identical to a Fr4/3 in A minor. The Picardy third makes the chord sound like a half cadence rather than an authentic cadence, creating the reciprocal process.
- Example 35.13: The raised ^2 creates an augmented sixth with ^4, requiring resolution upward to ^3.

# Common Confusions
The altered V7 with lowered ^2 (FrV4/3) sounds identical to a French augmented sixth chord. The difference is function: FrV4/3 resolves to I (dominant function), while the regular Fr4/3 resolves to V (pre-dominant function). Only context and resolution can distinguish them.
