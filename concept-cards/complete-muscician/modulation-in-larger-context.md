# Quick Definition
Modulations participate in a single overall harmonic motion within a composition; the series of keys visited creates a large-scale harmonic progression in the home key.

# Formal Definition
Very few compositions end in a different key from the one in which they begin. Modulations never permanently displace the tonic; instead, they create large-scale harmonic progressions. For example, a piece in C minor might modulate through E-flat major and F minor before returning to C minor, creating the large-scale progression i-III-iv-V-i. When analyzing modulations, the new key should be identified by its relationship to the original tonic using roman numerals (e.g., a modulation from C major to G major is I to V). Determining whether a tonal shift is a tonicization or modulation is often a matter of personal interpretation.

# Musical Context
Understanding modulations within their larger context is essential for analyzing complete movements and multi-section works. The series of keys forms a tonal plan that creates large-scale tension and resolution.

# Examples
- Robert Schumann, Davidsbundler Tanze, op. 6, no. 7: C minor moves through III (E-flat major) and v (G minor), with the overall tonal trajectory i-III-v
- A piece modulating from C major to G major: the modulation represents a large-scale I-V motion

# Common Confusions
- Modulations are always subordinate to the home key's overall tonal structure
- Whether a tonal shift is a tonicization or modulation can be debated; the distinction depends on cadential strength and duration
