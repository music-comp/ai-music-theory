# Quick Definition
Augmented intervals are a half step larger than major or perfect intervals; diminished intervals are a half step smaller than minor or perfect intervals.

# Formal Definition
Augmented intervals result from increasing the size of a major or perfect interval by one half step (by raising the upper note or lowering the lower note). Diminished intervals result from decreasing the size of a minor or perfect interval by one half step. The transformation chain for perfect intervals is: diminished - perfect - augmented. For major/minor intervals: diminished - minor - major - augmented. Major and minor intervals can never become perfect, and vice versa. The abbreviations are: A (or +) for augmented, d (or degree sign) for diminished.

# Musical Context
Augmented and diminished intervals are dissonant and occur in specific diatonic contexts. The most important are the augmented fourth (tritone, occurring between ^4 and ^7 in major) and the diminished fifth (between ^7 and ^4). In harmonic minor, the augmented second between ^6 and raised ^7 is a characteristic dissonance that should be avoided in melody.

# Examples
- Eb up to A natural: not a P5 (Ab is in Eb major, not A natural), so it is a half step larger -- a diminished fifth (d5) (Example 3.11A).
- C up to G#: a half step larger than P5, so an augmented fifth (A5) (Example 3.11B).
- D to B#: a half step larger than M6 (D to B natural), so an augmented sixth (A6) (Example 3.11D).
- Diatonic augmented and diminished intervals: A4 between ^4-^7 in major; d5 between ^7-^4 in major; A2 between ^6-^7 in harmonic minor.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes confuse augmented/diminished intervals with their enharmonic equivalents. An augmented second (C to D#) sounds identical to a minor third (C to Eb) but is spelled differently and functions differently. The distinction matters for voice-leading and harmonic context. Also remember: you cannot have an augmented interval that is also major, or a diminished interval that is also minor.
