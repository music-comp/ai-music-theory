# Quick Definition
A diminished seventh chord or diminished triad that functions as a dominant substitute tonicizing a diatonic harmony other than tonic, labeled vii-7/X or vii-6/X.

# Formal Definition
Applied leading-tone chords (vii-6, vii-7, and their inversions) serve as applied dominant substitutes that tonicize major and minor diatonic triads. They function identically to vii-7 and vii-6 in relation to tonic but are applied to other scale degrees. With the introduction of applied leading-tone chords, diminished seventh chords built on pitches other than ^7 of the minor mode become available. These chords are labeled vii-7/X, vii-6/5/X, vii-4/3/X, or vii-4/2/X, where X is the target chord. They follow the same voice-leading rules as their diatonic counterparts.

# Musical Context
Applied leading-tone chords provide an alternative to applied dominant chords for tonicizing diatonic harmonies. They are particularly useful as passing and neighboring chords in contrapuntal expansions of non-tonic harmonies, due to their smooth voice-leading properties.

# Examples
- In C major: vii-7/ii = C#-E-G-Bb, resolving to D minor
- Beethoven, String Quartet no. 9, op. 59, no. 3, Menuetto: multiple applied chords including vii-7 forms tonicizing various harmonies
- Using vii-6/5/ii, vii-6/ii, and vii-4/3/ii to extend the tonicization of ii over multiple beats

# Common Confusions
- Applied leading-tone chords can appear in any inversion, not just root position
- They use the same analytical arrow notation as applied dominants (curved arrow pointing to target chord)
- The same voice-leading rules apply: do not double the leading tone or seventh, resolve appropriately
