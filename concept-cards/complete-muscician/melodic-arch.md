# Quick Definition
A melodic arch is a contour shape in which the melody ascends to a climax and then descends, or (inverted) descends and then ascends, creating a balanced, satisfying phrase shape.

# Formal Definition
Many phrases exhibit archlike contours in which ascending and descending structural lines combine. The standard arch ascends from a stable starting point (often ^1 or ^3) to a melodic high point (climax), then descends to a point of repose. Inverted arches (descent followed by ascent) also appear. Both types create balanced, goal-oriented melodic shapes. The arch contour often spans an entire phrase, with the ascent building tension and the descent releasing it. Composers may parallel the melodic arch in the bass to create an integrated contrapuntal structure.

# Musical Context
The arch is one of the most natural and satisfying melodic shapes. It appears across centuries and styles. The rising portion often arpeggiates the tonic triad, while the falling portion often moves by step through the descending scale. The climactic high point typically coincides with heightened harmonic tension.

# Examples
In "Clementine," the first phrase creates an arch: the melody ascends via arpeggiation of the tonic triad (F4-A4-C5) to the melodic high point on C5 (m. 3), then quickly descends to G4 (m. 4). In Mozart's Jupiter Symphony Andante (Example 7.18A), each member of an ascending fourth line appears on accented downbeats, followed by a clear descent. Chopin's Etude in Ab (Example 7.18B) shows a descent-then-ascent (inverted arch).

# Common Confusions
Not all melodies form arches -- some exhibit only ascending or descending structural lines, or more complex contours. The arch describes the underlying structural contour, not necessarily the surface contour, which may contain many small leaps and changes of direction.
