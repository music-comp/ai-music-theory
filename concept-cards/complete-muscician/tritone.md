# Quick Definition
The tritone is an interval of three whole steps (six half steps), encompassing both the augmented fourth and the diminished fifth, which are enharmonically equivalent and form the most unstable diatonic interval.

# Formal Definition
The tritone is named for its three whole-step span (tri-tone). It appears in two enharmonic forms: the augmented fourth (A4, e.g., F up to B) and the diminished fifth (d5, e.g., B up to F). Both contain exactly six half steps and divide the octave exactly in half. The tritone is the only interval pair where inversion produces the same number of half steps (6 + 6 = 12). In major keys, the tritone occurs between ^4 and ^7. In minor keys, it appears between ^2 and ^6 and between raised ^7 and ^4.

# Musical Context
The tritone is the most dissonant and unstable diatonic interval, historically called "diabolus in musica" (the devil in music). Its instability makes it a powerful driver of harmonic motion, particularly within the dominant seventh chord (V7), where it occurs between ^7 and ^4 and resolves by contrary motion to a third or sixth. The tritone's resolution is one of the fundamental voice-leading events in tonal music.

# Examples
- In C major, the tritone occurs between F (^4) and B (^7).
- The A4 and d5 are the only inversional pair of intervals that contain the same number of half steps (Example 3.14).
- Both forms are dissonant and typically resolve: ^7 resolves up to ^1, and ^4 resolves down to ^3.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes think "tritone" refers only to the augmented fourth. Both the augmented fourth and the diminished fifth are called tritones because they share the same number of half steps (six), even though the d5 technically spans five letter names rather than three whole tones.
