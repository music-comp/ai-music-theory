# Quick Definition
The Neapolitan chord (bII) is a major triad built on the lowered second scale degree, functioning as a powerful chromatic pre-dominant that pulls strongly toward V.

# Formal Definition
The Neapolitan is a major triad built on b^2 (e.g., in A minor: Bb-D-F; in C major: Db-F-Ab). It most commonly appears in first inversion as the Neapolitan sixth chord (bII6), with ^4 in the bass. Named after Baroque composers from Naples who frequently used the sonority, the Neapolitan functions as a pre-dominant harmony. It occurs more often in minor-mode pieces; in major, it arises as a product of modal mixture (requiring both b^2 and b^6). When doubling, prefer the bass (^4); ^6 may be doubled if necessary, but never double b^2.

# Musical Context
The Neapolitan is one of the most expressive chromatic pre-dominants, creating a striking harmonic color through its lowered root. It appears frequently at cadences but can also be used with embedded phrase models (EPMs). The chord became increasingly important from the Baroque through the Romantic era, and its role expanded from a simple pre-dominant to a harmony that could be prolonged, tonicized, and used as a pivot for modulation.

# Examples
- Schubert, "Der Muller und der Bach," Die schone Mullerin, D. 795 (Example 29.1): The Neapolitan appears in mm. 8, 16, and 25, each time as a chromatic pre-dominant creating a poignant harmonic coloring.
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 14 ("Moonlight"), op. 27, no. 2 (Exercise 29.8): The Neapolitan appears within a typical cadential context in C# minor.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes confuse the Neapolitan with a first-inversion major triad on ^4. Check the root: bII6 has b^2 as its root with ^4 in the bass. Also, avoid the augmented second between ^3 and b^2 in major mode by using the soprano line ^1-b^2 or filling the diminished third (b^2-^7) with a passing ^1.
