# Quick Definition
Chromatic contrary motion extends a harmony (typically the dominant seventh) by moving the outer voices in half steps in opposite directions while the inner voices remain relatively stationary.

# Formal Definition
Chromatic contrary motion is a technique for prolonging an underlying harmony by moving the bass and soprano in opposite directions by half step. The inner voices remain mostly stationary, providing harmonic stability while the outer voices create chromatic passing chords. A dissonant prolongation can span a third or a tritone. The intervals with an even number of half steps (unison, M2, M3, tritone, m6, m7) are best harmonized in contrary-motion chromatic lines. If the initial interval has an odd number of half steps, one voice can be suspended by a half step to adjust. The nonfunctional Ger7 and Ger6/5 chords often appear as passing chords within these progressions.

# Musical Context
Chromatic contrary motion is a powerful tool for extending dominant harmony in nineteenth-century music. It creates dense chromaticism while maintaining clear harmonic function at a deeper level. The technique can prolong tonic, dominant, or pre-dominant harmony depending on which portion of the contrary-motion pattern is used.

# Common Confusions
Chromatic contrary motion may look complex on the surface, but it serves a simple prolongational function. The key to analysis is identifying which underlying harmony is being extended. The chromatic passing chords are nonfunctional and should not receive independent roman numerals.
