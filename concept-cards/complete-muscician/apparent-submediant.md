# Quick Definition
A vi6 chord that arises as a byproduct of voice leading (5-6 motion) rather than functioning as an actual submediant harmony.

# Formal Definition
An apparent submediant occurs when a vi6 chord results from a 5-6 contrapuntal motion above the bass in progressions whose roots lie a second apart (such as I moving to ii). The 5-6 motion avoids parallel fifths: as the bass rises from ^1 to ^2, the upper voice shifts from the fifth above the bass to the sixth, creating a momentary vi6 sonority. This is a voice-leading chord, not a functional chord change, analogous to the IV-ii complex where a 5-6 motion creates an apparent ii6.

# Musical Context
Apparent submediants appear in any progression where roots move by ascending second and the 5-6 technique is employed to avoid parallels. They are found throughout common-practice music and require contextual analysis to distinguish from functional submediant harmonies.

# Examples
- Wagner, "Mogst du mein Kind," from The Flying Dutchman: apparent vi6 as byproduct of voice leading in I-ii motion

# Common Confusions
- Not every vertical sonority that looks like vi actually functions as vi; context determines whether it is a voice-leading byproduct
- The 5-6 motion that creates apparent submediants is the same technique seen in the IV-ii complex
- An apparent vi6 should not receive an independent harmonic label at the second level of analysis
