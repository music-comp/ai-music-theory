# Quick Definition
Nonchord tones are pitches in a melody that do not belong to the underlying harmony, creating dissonance that resolves to chord tones.

# Formal Definition
Nonchord tones are melodic pitches that are not members of the prevailing chord. They appear between and connect stable chord tones, functioning as passing tones, neighbor tones, or other embellishments. Since these pitches are not part of the underlying harmony (such as the tonic triad implied by a sustained bass note), they create tension that requires resolution by chord tones.

# Musical Context
Nonchord tones pervade virtually all tonal melody. They appear in both accompanied textures, where the underlying harmony is explicit, and in solo instrumental music, where the harmony is implied. Composers carefully clarify the consonant or dissonant status of melodic pitches through harmonic context.

# Examples
In a C-major context with a sustained C bass, the scale degrees ^7, ^6, ^4, and ^2 (B, A, F, D) function as nonchord tones that connect the stable pitches C, E, G (members of the tonic triad). In Vivaldi's Sonata in C minor, the oboe's F4 in m. 1 is a nonchord tone (neighbor) against the C-minor triad, while D on beat 3 is a nonchord tone (passing tone) between Eb5 and C5.

# Common Confusions
The term "nonharmonic tone" is sometimes used interchangeably with nonchord tone, but Laitz prefers "tones of figuration" as the generic term because some embellishments (like chordal leaps and arpeggiations) are consonant members of the underlying harmony and therefore are chord tones, not nonharmonic.
