# Quick Definition
A seven-part rondo follows the plan A1-B1-A2-C-A3-B2-A4, creating a symmetrical arch form with four refrain statements, two B episodes, and a central C episode.

# Formal Definition
The seven-part rondo adds another episode and refrain to the five-part rondo, creating a symmetrical formal arch: A1-B1-A2-C-A3-B2-A4. Typical key plans: In major, B episodes appear in V, i, or IV; C appears in i, IV, or vi. In minor, B is in III or I; C is in iv, IV, III, VI, or I. The C section is often longer and more distinct from the flanking ABA sections, making the seven-part rondo resemble a giant three-part ternary (ABA-C-ABA). Sections typically contain nested rounded binary forms. After A1 and B1, composers often omit repeats or write out ornamented versions.

# Musical Context
The seven-part rondo was common in Classical chamber music and symphonies. Its symmetrical construction creates a satisfying sense of balance and return, while the greater number of episodes provides more opportunity for tonal and thematic variety than the five-part rondo.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in D major, op. 33, no. 6, Finale (Example 32.4): A seven-part rondo where A2 is a written-out, ornamented version of A1, occupying twice as many measures.
- The seven-part rondo's typical key plan: A1(I)-B1(V)-A2(I)-C(iv)-A3(I)-B2(I)-A4(I).

# Common Confusions
The seven-part rondo is easily confused with a large ternary form, since the outer ABA groups can sound like enlarged A sections flanking a central B section. Distinguishing criteria: consider the period (rondos favor the eighteenth century), tempo (rondos are typically fast), and the presence of nested smaller forms.
