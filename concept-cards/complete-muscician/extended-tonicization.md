# Quick Definition
A tonicization that employs multiple applied chords to expand a single diatonic harmony over several beats or measures, creating a brief but substantial key area.

# Formal Definition
An extended tonicization expands a diatonic harmony using the same techniques used to expand tonic: passing chords, neighboring chords, and contrapuntal expansions, all within the temporary key. Analysis method: (1) bracket where the tonicization occurs, (2) label the temporary tonic chord under the bracket, (3) label all chords within the bracket as if they were in the temporary new key. Extended tonicizations do not disrupt the feeling of the home key and do not include strong cadences in the new key. The expanded harmonies retain their function within the overall home-key progression.

# Musical Context
Extended tonicizations appear throughout common-practice music, particularly in passages where a pre-dominant or other diatonic harmony is expanded with multiple applied chords. They can create large-scale arpeggiations of the tonic triad through successive tonicizations.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in E-flat major, op. 27, no. 1: extended tonicization of ii (F minor) lasting several measures, expanding the PD function
- Robert Schumann, "Sangers Trost": brief tonicizations of G minor, B-flat major (III), and D minor (v) creating a large-scale arpeggiation of the tonic G-minor triad
- Clara Schumann, "Andante espressivo," op. 15, no. 3: extended tonicization of ii following a back-relating dominant

# Common Confusions
- Extended tonicizations use the same expansion techniques as tonic prolongation but in a temporary key
- An A-minor chord functioning as iv within an extended tonicization of ii in D major is not a "minor v" in the home key
- Extended tonicizations are still subordinate to the home-key progression
