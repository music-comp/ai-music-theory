# Quick Definition
A harmonic progression in which roots descend by fifths (or ascend by fourths), creating the strongest chain of root motion in tonal music: iii-vi-ii-V-I.

# Formal Definition
The descending-fifths progression extends the fundamental V-I root motion into a longer chain: iii-vi-ii-V-I. Each chord's root lies a fifth above the next chord's root, creating the most goal-directed harmonic motion in tonal music. The mediant can extend this chain back one more step (iii-vi-ii-V-I), and the submediant fits naturally into the chain as well (I-vi-ii-V-I). Both the mediant and submediant participate in descending-fifths progressions, providing additional harmonic richness within the phrase.

# Musical Context
Descending-fifths progressions are among the most common and powerful harmonic patterns in tonal music. They create strong forward motion and clear tonal direction. Individual segments of the chain (such as ii-V-I or vi-ii-V-I) are extremely common; the full chain from iii is less frequent but appears in notable works.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Violin Sonata no. 5 ("Spring"), op. 24: vi-ii-V-I as part of descending-fifths progression in both EPM and structural cadence

# Common Confusions
- The descending-fifths progression is about root motion, not necessarily bass motion; inversions may alter the bass line
- Not every pair of chords a fifth apart constitutes a descending-fifths progression; the pattern must be sequential
- The circle-of-fifths progression can be extended beyond diatonic limits through applied chords (covered later)
