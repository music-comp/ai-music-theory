# Quick Definition
The smallest formal unit of musical organization, a short recurring pitch and/or rhythmic pattern that provides substance, logic, coherence, and dramatic energy to a composition.

# Formal Definition
A motive is defined as the smallest formal unit of musical organization. From the Latin motus ("motion"), motives are short patterns (one beat in slow tempo to one or more measures in fast tempo) composed of characteristic pitch contours and/or rhythms. They must be audible, possessing a clear identity and profile. Motives are given a prominent position at the beginning of a work. Pieces usually contain more than one motive. Motives are distinct from figures: a figure is an undifferentiated recurring pattern (like a broken-chord accompaniment) that becomes background, while a motive is a distinctive event set in relief against other material.

# Musical Context
Motives are building blocks that combine to form phrase members, which combine into phrases, which combine into periods and larger structures. They can represent characters or events (Wagner's Leitmotiven). Two types exist: independent motives (self-standing, generating much of the piece) and thematic motives (components of longer themes).

# Examples
- Beethoven, String Quartet in F major, op. 18 no. 1: self-standing four-beat motive (turn figure followed by falling skip) set off by rests
- Beethoven, Symphony no. 6 ("Pastorale"): three motives (X, Y, Z) combine as thematic components, each occupying one measure, sharing length, contour, and underlying third-intervals
- Bach, Prelude in C major, WTC Book I: the recurring broken-chord pattern is a figure, not a motive

# Common Confusions
- A motive is not the same as a figure: motives are distinctive and set in relief; figures are undifferentiated background patterns
- Multiple motives in a theme must relate to one another (e.g., shared contour, length, or intervals) for the theme to be coherent
- Motives need not be entire themes; they are the building blocks from which themes are constructed
