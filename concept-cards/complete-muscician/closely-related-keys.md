# Quick Definition
Keys whose key signatures differ by no more than one sharp or flat from the home key, determined by building consonant diatonic triads on each scale degree.

# Formal Definition
Closely related keys are determined in two ways: (1) build a diatonic triad on each scale degree of the home key; all consonant (major and minor) triads form tonics for closely related keys; (2) identify major and minor keys whose key signatures differ by no more than one accidental from the home key. For C major, the closely related keys are D minor (ii), E minor (iii), F major (IV), G major (V), and A minor (vi). For C minor (using natural minor), they are E-flat major (III), F minor (iv), G minor (v), A-flat major (VI), and B-flat major (VII). The most common modulation targets are: major keys to V, vi, and iii; minor keys to III, v, and VI.

# Musical Context
Most modulations in tonal music move to closely related keys. This principle governs the tonal plans of binary forms, sonata expositions, and other large-scale formal structures throughout the common-practice period.

# Examples
- C major closely related keys: A minor, G major, E minor, F major, D minor
- C minor closely related keys: E-flat major, F minor, A-flat major, G minor, B-flat major

# Common Confusions
- Although modulation to any key is possible, most common-practice modulations target closely related keys
- Minor keys use the natural minor scale (not harmonic or melodic) for determining closely related keys
- The order of frequency matters: V and III are the most common targets in major and minor respectively
