# Quick Definition
Melodic mixture is a type of modal mixture that changes the quality of a chord by altering one or more of its upper members (third or fifth) while keeping the root unchanged.

# Formal Definition
Melodic mixture occurs when b^6 from the parallel minor mode is imported into a major-mode chord as the third or fifth, changing the chord's quality but not its root. The most common results are: ii becomes ii° (diminished triad), ii7 becomes ii-half-diminished-7, and IV becomes iv (minor subdominant). These altered pre-dominants are labeled with roman numerals as if they were in a minor key. Melodic mixture works well in contrapuntal expansions of the tonic, especially as embedded phrase models (EPMs).

# Musical Context
Melodic mixture is the most common form of mixture because b^6 is the scale degree least likely to undermine the integrity of the home key. It permits a strong half-step motion to the dominant, colors all pre-dominant harmonies, and is the only scale degree outside the tonic triad that can be consonantly supported by a harmony with ^1 in the bass.

# Examples
- Schumann, "Ich grolle nicht," Dichterliebe, op. 48, no. 7 (Example 27.3): The Ab in m. 3 creates a ii-half-diminished-7 chord in C major, musically representing the poet's broken heart ("Herz"). The Ab continues to intensify the pain by forming a minor ninth when sustained over the dominant.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes confuse melodic mixture with harmonic mixture. In melodic mixture, the altered pitch is the third or fifth of the chord (the root stays the same). In harmonic mixture, the root itself is altered (e.g., bVI, bIII). The roman numeral labeling also differs: melodic mixture uses lowercase or diminished symbols (iv, ii°), while harmonic mixture uses a flat before the numeral (bVI).
