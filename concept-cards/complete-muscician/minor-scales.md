# Quick Definition
The minor mode uses three scale forms -- natural, harmonic, and melodic -- that share a lowered ^3 (compared to major) and vary in their treatment of ^6 and ^7 to balance smooth melodic motion with goal-directed harmonic function.

# Formal Definition
The natural minor scale has half steps between ^2-^3 and ^5-^6, with both ^6 and ^7 in their lowered forms. The harmonic minor scale raises ^7 to create a leading tone, producing a half step between ^7 and ^1 but introducing an augmented second between ^6 and ^7. The melodic minor scale raises both ^6 and ^7 when ascending (for smooth stepwise motion toward ^1) and lowers both when descending (to enhance motion toward ^5). These three forms should be viewed as variations of a single nine-pitch-class minor collection rather than as three separate scales.

# Musical Context
Composers draw freely from all three forms of minor within a single passage based on melodic context. Raised ^7 gravitates toward ^1; lowered ^6 gravitates toward ^5. The augmented second between lowered ^6 and raised ^7 rarely occurs within a single melodic line because ^6 and ^7 arise from different melodic contexts.

# Examples
- D natural minor: D-E-F-G-A-Bb-C-D (Example 1.15)
- D harmonic minor: D-E-F-G-A-Bb-C#-D (Example 1.16)
- D melodic minor ascending: D-E-F-G-A-B-C#-D; descending: D-C-Bb-A-G-F-E-D (Example 1.16)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D major, K. 284 (Example 1.17A) shows both forms of ^7 and ^6 in a chromatic descending line.
- Bach, "Verleih' uns Frieden" (Example 1.17C) shows raised ^7 arising from ascent through raised ^6, while lowered ^6 gravitates toward ^5.

# Common Confusions
The three minor scale forms are not three different scales but three aspects of a single flexible minor collection. The "augmented second problem" between ^6 and ^7 in harmonic minor is largely academic, since these pitches rarely appear adjacent in a single melodic line. Also, key signatures for minor keys reflect only the natural minor form; raised ^6 and ^7 appear as accidentals.
