# Quick Definition
A miniature T-PD-D-T progression occurring within the tonic expansion of a larger phrase, which is tonally closed but does not function as a structural cadence.

# Formal Definition
An embedded phrase model (EPM) is a mini T-PD-D-T progression that occurs within the initial tonic part of a phrase. Although these mini-progressions end with tonic (making them tonally closed), they do not act as points of arrival in the way cadences do. At the second level of analysis, the overall phrase has just one structural T-PD-D-T progression with a cadence; earlier EPMs expand the tonic. Composers distinguish EPMs from actual cadences by weakening the PD and D harmonies through inversions, making them sound more contrapuntal.

# Musical Context
EPMs are common within tonic expansions, where they provide harmonic activity and forward motion without creating premature closure. They can occur anywhere within the initial tonic portion of a phrase. Four important EPM settings are shown: (1) dominant in weak inversion; (2) ii4/2 with seventh in bass creating neighboring motion; (3) bass leap of third with stepwise return; (4) same in minor with raised ^6 and ^7.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Concerto no. 4 in G major, op. 58: EPM within tonic expansion, distinguished from the structural cadence later in the phrase

# Common Confusions
- EPMs are NOT cadences; they do not function as structural arrivals
- At the second level, EPMs fall under "T" (tonic), not as independent PD-D-T progressions
- EPMs use inversions to weaken harmonic motion; root-position V-I within a tonic expansion would sound too cadential
