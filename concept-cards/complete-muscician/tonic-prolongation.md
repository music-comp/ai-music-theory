# Quick Definition
Tonic prolongation extends the control of the tonic harmony over multiple beats or measures through subordinate chords (I6, V6, viio6, IV6, and inversions of V7) that embellish without changing the underlying harmonic function.

# Formal Definition
Tonic prolongation is the extension of tonic harmonic function beyond a single chord through contrapuntal means. Moving between I and I6 is analogous to revoicing -- no harmonic change occurs, only an extension of a single harmony made more dramatic by the bass change. Common prolongation techniques include: chordal leaps (I-I6), neighboring motion (I-V6-I), passing motion (I-viio6-I6), arpeggiating bass (I-IV6-I6), and combinations of these. It is much more common to extend tonic with its first inversion than to extend dominant similarly.

# Musical Context
Tonic prolongation is essential for creating phrases of any length. Without it, tonic would occupy only a single beat or chord. Extended tonic prolongations often fill the opening portion of phrases before the music moves to the dominant area. The T-PD-D-T phrase model depends on tonic prolongation in both the opening T and closing T sections.

# Examples
In Handel's "Comfort Ye" from Messiah, tonic is prolonged from I through a descending arpeggiation (^1-^5-^3) with a voice exchange, arriving at I6. In Telemann's flute sonata, tonic is expanded using V6 (incomplete neighbor) and viio6 (passing). Chopin's Etude op. 10/8 prolongs tonic for eight measures using only a neighboring V65.

# Common Confusions
Students may not realize that chords like V6 and viio6 within a tonic prolongation are not structural dominants -- they are subordinate embellishments. A structural V is progressional (moving the harmony forward); a V6 within a tonic prolongation is prolongational (extending the tonic).
