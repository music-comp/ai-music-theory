# Quick Definition
A musical genre in which an initial theme is followed by a series of variations that alter one or more musical elements while retaining characteristic features of the theme.

# Formal Definition
A variation set begins with a theme from which a series of variations unfolds. Variations may be independent pieces or members of larger multimovement works. The two common types are continuous variations (short theme, seamless flow between variations) and sectional variations (theme in binary form, clear separations between variations). Composers balance unity with variety by retaining characteristic elements (melody, harmony, form, proportions) while altering others (texture, figuration, register, dynamics). Strategies for creating larger-scale coherence include rhythmic crescendo (ever-smaller note values), expanded textures and registers, and grouping variations into larger units (e.g., major-minor-major groupings).

# Musical Context
Variation sets appear across all instrumental combinations and periods, from Baroque passacaglias to Classical piano variations to Romantic orchestral works. They explore the fundamental compositional challenge of balancing repetition with change.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Thirty-two Variations in C minor, WoO 80: continuous variations on a harmonic ostinato
- Beethoven, Six Variations on "Nel cor piu non mi sento," WoO 70: sectional variations on a binary theme
- Brahms, Symphony no. 4, finale: continuous variations combining ground bass and chaconne

# Common Confusions
- One musical element (melody, harmony, form) usually remains stable while others change; if everything changes, the variation loses connection to the theme
- The form and proportions of the theme tend to be preserved even when other elements are substantially altered
