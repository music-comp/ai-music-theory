# Quick Definition
The viio6 chord functions as a passing chord between I and I6, serving as a "dominant substitute" because it shares three pitches with V7 and has an inherently unstable diminished sonority.

# Formal Definition
The first-inversion diminished triad on ^7 (viio6) is the standard passing chord for expanding tonic harmony. It harmonizes bass ^2 between ^1 (I) and ^3 (I6). viio6 is called a "dominant substitute" because it shares three pitches with V7 (^7, ^2, ^4). It is a perfect passing chord because: (1) its diminished sonority is unstable, (2) it shares notes with V/V7, and (3) being in first inversion makes it naturally suited for a contrapuntal role. In minor, ^7 must be raised (creating a leading tone) above the bass of viio6.

# Musical Context
viio6 is one of the most common chords in tonal music, appearing in nearly every phrase that contains a tonic expansion with stepwise bass motion. It typically falls on a metrically weak beat between root-position I and first-inversion I6 (or the reverse).

# Examples
In Handel's Flute Sonata in B minor, viio6 passes between I and I6, harmonizing bass C# (^2) with the diminished triad C#-E-G (with raised leading tone A#). Common soprano settings include parallel tenths with the bass (^1-^2-^3 in soprano over ^1-^2-^3 in bass).

# Common Confusions
Students may try to use ii (root position) instead of viio6 for harmonizing bass ^2. The ii chord is too structurally strong and draws attention away from the tonic prolongation. viio6 is preferred precisely because of its instability. Voice leading: never double ^7 (the leading tone); prefer doubling ^2 (the third of the chord), which can move to either ^1 or ^3.
