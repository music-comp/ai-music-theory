# Quick Definition
The supertonic (ii in major, ii-diminished in minor) is the most common pre-dominant chord, combining strong root motion, modal contrast, and powerful melodic resolution at cadences.

# Formal Definition
The supertonic is the most common pre-dominant for three reasons: (1) ii-V proceeds by descending-fifth motion, the strongest root motion in tonal music; (2) ii introduces modal contrast -- minor in major keys, diminished in minor keys; (3) ii-V-I often sets ^2-^7-^1 in the soprano, creating a powerful double-neighbor motion encircling ^1. In minor, composers almost always use ii-diminished-6 (first inversion) rather than root-position ii-diminished, because the exposed tritone between bass and upper voice in root position is unsettling.

# Musical Context
First-inversion supertonic (ii6) embodies the best attributes of both ii and IV: modal contrast from ii, descending-fifth root relation of ii-V, and smooth bass voice leading of ^4-^5 from IV. Double the bass (^4) when writing ii6. Root-position ii is used freely in major but avoided in minor.

# Examples
- The progression ii6-V combines modal contrast with smooth ^4-^5 bass motion
- Chopin, Nocturne in F# minor, op. 48, no. 2: stepwise ascending bass ^1-^2-^3-^4-^5 through tonic expansion to pre-dominant

# Common Confusions
- Root-position ii-diminished is avoided in minor due to the exposed tritone; use ii-diminished-6 instead
- ii6 is not the same chord as IV even though both have ^4 in the bass -- ii6 retains ii's modal contrast and root motion
- When writing in minor, approach the leading tone (^7) in the V chord from above
