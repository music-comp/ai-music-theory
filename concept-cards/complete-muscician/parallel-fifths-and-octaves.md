# Quick Definition
Parallel fifths and octaves occur when two voices move from one perfect fifth (or octave) to another perfect fifth (or octave) in parallel motion, creating the effect of one voice "dropping out" of the texture.

# Formal Definition
Parallel motion by perfect consonances (unisons, fifths, and octaves) between any pair of voices is forbidden in common-practice voice leading. This is an absolute rule that cannot be broken. Parallels occur only when a pair of voices moves from one perfect interval to the same perfect interval on different pitches. A sustained perfect interval (where neither voice moves) does not constitute a parallel. Direct (or hidden) fifths and octaves -- where two voices arrive at a perfect interval by similar motion -- should also be avoided between soprano and bass unless the soprano moves stepwise.

# Musical Context
Parallel perfect intervals weaken the independence of voices, creating the auditory effect of one voice duplicating another and thus reducing the perceived number of independent parts. This is one of only two absolute voice-leading rules (the other being never to double tendency tones).

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes flag sustained perfect intervals as parallels. Parallels require motion -- both voices must move to a new perfect interval of the same type. Also, contrary-motion octaves and fifths (moving from one perfect interval to the same type in contrary motion) are also forbidden. Unequal fifths (P5 to d5 or vice versa) have different rules.
