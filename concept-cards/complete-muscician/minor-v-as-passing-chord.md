# Quick Definition
In minor keys, a first-inversion minor v chord (using lowered ^7) that serves as a passing chord between i and iv6, avoiding the augmented second that raised ^7 would create.

# Formal Definition
First-inversion minor v (v6) and root-position minor v do not function as dominants in tonal music. Instead, they serve as passing chords or voice-leading chords, most commonly in the step-descent bass progression i-v6-iv6-V. The lowered ^7 in the bass avoids the augmented second between raised ^7 and lowered ^6 that would occur if V6 were used in this context. Minor v is exclusively a contrapuntal, nonfunctional chord.

# Musical Context
Minor v6 appears specifically in step-descent bass progressions in minor keys. It connects tonic to iv6 through smooth stepwise bass motion (^1-^7-^6). This is one of the few contexts where lowered ^7 appears harmonized in minor-mode music.

# Examples
- Schutz, "Nacket bin ich von Mutterleibe kommen": i-v6-iv6-V with minor v6 as passing chord
- General step-descent bass pattern: ^1 (i) - ^7 (v6, passing) - ^6 (iv6, PD) - ^5 (V)

# Common Confusions
- Minor v does NOT function as a dominant; it is purely a contrapuntal chord
- Minor v uses lowered ^7, not raised ^7; this is essential to avoid the augmented second
- Do not confuse minor v6 with major V6, which IS a functional dominant
