# Quick Definition
Extended tonic prolongations often use combinations of first-inversion chords (I6, V6, viio6, IV6) to create complex bass patterns including double-neighbor figures and mixed passing/neighboring motions.

# Formal Definition
First-inversion chords can be combined in various ways to create extended prolongations of tonic. For example, a neighboring V6 and a passing viio6 can create a double-neighbor figure in the bass (^1-^7-^1-^2-^3). An incomplete neighboring V6 can be followed by a passing viio6 (^3-^7-^1-^2-^3). These combinations produce sophisticated bass melodies while maintaining the control of a single underlying harmony.

# Musical Context
These combinations are the building blocks of real compositional practice. Composers freely combine contrapuntal devices -- chordal leaps, neighbors, passing tones -- to create rich, directed phrases. The summary chart (Example 10.19) organizes all the ways tonic and dominant can be expanded through first-inversion chords.

# Common Confusions
Students may try to analyze each first-inversion chord as an independent harmonic event. The key is to recognize that these chords work together as parts of a single prolongational process. Their individual Roman numerals are less important than their collective function of extending a structural harmony.
