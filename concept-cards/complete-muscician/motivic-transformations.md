# Quick Definition
The various techniques for altering a motive while maintaining its identity, including transposition, inversion, retrograde, augmentation, diminution, embellishment, fragmentation, and interpolation.

# Formal Definition
Motivic transformations include: Transposition -- restating the entire motive at a different pitch level (tonal transposition maintains generic intervals within the key; real transposition maintains exact interval quality). Inversion (mirror inversion) -- projecting intervals in the opposite direction. Retrograde -- reversing the order of pitches. Retrograde inversion -- performing the inverted form backwards. Augmentation -- proportionally increasing note durations. Diminution -- proportionally decreasing note durations. Embellishment -- adding pitches (passing tones, neighbors). Change of interval -- altering specific intervals while maintaining contour and rhythm. Octave displacement -- shifting a portion of the motive by an octave. Fragmentation -- developing only a portion of the motive, often the "head."

# Musical Context
These transformations allow motives to withstand extensive development while remaining recognizable. Rhythm often provides the most important cue for recognition -- pitch change over a recurring rhythmic pattern preserves identity better than rhythmic change over recurring pitches. Composers often combine multiple transformations simultaneously for "motivic saturation."

# Examples
- Beethoven, String Quartet in Eb major, op. 127: four-note motive transposed by thirds, then inverted in viola
- Haydn, "Menuet al rovescio": palindromic retrograde structure
- Mahler, Symphony no. 5, Adagietto: motive in violin augmented by factor of 2 in cello
- Brahms, "Mein wundes Herz": simultaneous augmentation, diminution, stretto, transposition

# Common Confusions
- Tonal transposition stays within the key (generic intervals maintained); real transposition maintains exact interval qualities (may leave the key)
- Retrograde is rarely used because reversed contour and pitches are hard to recognize aurally
- Octave displacement of a single pitch can weaken motivic identity more than transposing the entire motive
