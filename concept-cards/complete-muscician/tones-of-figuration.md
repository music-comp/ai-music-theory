# Quick Definition
Tones of figuration is a generic term for all melodic embellishments, both consonant (chordal leaps, arpeggiations) and dissonant (passing tones, neighbor tones).

# Formal Definition
Tones of figuration encompass all melodic embellishments, whether consonant or dissonant. This term is preferred over "nonharmonic tone" because some embellishments (chordal leaps and arpeggiations) are consonant members of the underlying harmony. The prefix "consonant" or "dissonant" can be added depending on function within the specific musical context. Types include: passing tones, neighbor tones (complete, incomplete, double), chordal leaps, and arpeggiations.

# Musical Context
Tones of figuration are the surface ornaments that elaborate the deeper structural pitches of a melody. By identifying and removing them (reduction), one can discover the underlying skeletal structure of a piece. Conversely, adding them to a skeletal framework (embellishment) creates a richly ornamented musical surface.

# Examples
In Vivaldi's Sonata in C minor, the oboe melody contains dissonant tones of figuration (the neighbor F4 and passing tone D) as well as consonant tones of figuration (chordal skips within the C-minor harmony). The same pitches (Eb5, C5) can shift from being chord tones to dissonant tones of figuration when the underlying harmony changes (from C minor to dominant).

# Common Confusions
The term "nonharmonic tone" is commonly used but misleading because it implies all embellishments are dissonant. Some tones of figuration (chordal leaps, arpeggiations) are consonant members of the underlying harmony. The broader term "tones of figuration" avoids this confusion.
