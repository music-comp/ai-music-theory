# Quick Definition
A symmetrical sequential progression is a large-scale tonal plan in which entire musical sections are transposed to key areas at equal intervals, dividing the octave symmetrically into minor thirds, major thirds, or other equal divisions.

# Formal Definition
Symmetrical sequential progressions extend the principle of sequences from the harmonic surface to the structural level of an entire piece or large section. Entire phrases, periods, or sections are transposed to new key areas at consistent intervals that equally divide the octave. When the interval of transposition divides the octave into equal parts (minor thirds = 4 divisions; major thirds = 3 divisions; tritones = 2 divisions; whole steps = 6 divisions), the resulting large-scale key scheme is symmetrical and inherently nondirectional. These progressions evolved from the chromatic third relations that arose from mixture in the late eighteenth century, representing the ultimate extension of symmetrical thinking to large-scale tonal planning.

# Musical Context
Symmetrical sequential progressions became increasingly important in the nineteenth century, particularly in the music of Beethoven, Schubert, Liszt, and Wagner. They represent one of the primary means by which nineteenth-century composers pushed beyond traditional fifth-related tonal structures. The technique bridges tonal and post-tonal practice.

# Examples
- Beethoven, "Waldstein" Sonata, op. 53: The development explores key areas related by major thirds (C-Ab-E), dividing the octave into three equal parts.
- Schubert, Wanderer Fantasy, D. 760: Key areas progress by minor thirds (C#-E-G-Bb), dividing the octave into four equal parts.
- Scriabin, Prelude, op. 39, no. 2 (Example 37.12): The second half transposes the first half's material down a major second.

# Common Confusions
A symmetrical sequential progression is not the same as a harmonic sequence within a single key. It operates at the level of key areas, with entire musical units transposed to equidistant keys. Also, not every modulation scheme creates a symmetrical sequential progression; the key areas must divide the octave into equal parts.
