# Quick Definition
The development is the freest section of a sonata form, analogous to the digression in binary form, where exposition material is transformed through fragmentation, sequence, and exploration of remote harmonic areas.

# Formal Definition
The development section occupies the middle of the sonata form, between the exposition and recapitulation. It is analogous to the digression in binary form. Composers may transform material from the exposition, introduce new themes, explore remote harmonic areas, and develop thematic and motivic material through fragmentation and sequence. The development typically features irregular phrasing and absence of periodicity. The retransition (RTr) is the final area of the development, where the dominant prepares the return of the tonic in the recapitulation. Despite its seemingly chaotic surface, a logical unfolding of tonal and melodic events underlies the development.

# Musical Context
Developments are often the most complex and dramatic sections of a sonata movement. They provide the arena for a composer's most imaginative and unpredictable musical thinking. The tonal instability of the development creates tension that is resolved by the return of the tonic at the recapitulation.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 19, op. 49, no. 1 (Example 33.2): The development begins with a variation of the STA theme, introduces a new melody in Eb major (VI), and uses sequence to build tension toward the retransition.
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in Bb major, K. 333 (Example 33.14): The development projects the opening motive (descending fifth-plus-neighbor) over the entire bass line, using keys related by ascending fifths (F-C-G-D).

# Common Confusions
The development is not simply a free fantasia. Despite its apparent freedom, it typically follows a logical tonal plan and draws on exposition material. Also, the retransition is part of the development, not a separate section; it prepares the dominant that resolves at the recapitulation.
