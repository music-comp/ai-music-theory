# Quick Definition
Direct (or hidden) fifths and octaves occur when two voices arrive at a perfect fifth or octave by similar motion. They should be avoided between soprano and bass unless the soprano moves stepwise.

# Formal Definition
Direct fifths and octaves (also called hidden fifths and octaves) occur when two voices approach a perfect fifth or perfect octave by similar motion (both ascending or both descending). These are problematic because similar motion to a perfect interval weakens the independence of the two voices. The guideline is to avoid direct fifths and octaves between the soprano and bass unless the soprano moves by step. When the soprano moves stepwise, the stepwise motion mitigates the effect of the similar motion. Direct intervals between inner voices or between an inner voice and an outer voice are less problematic.

# Musical Context
Direct fifths and octaves are a voice-leading guideline (not an absolute rule like parallel fifths/octaves). They are most objectionable between the outer voices because the soprano and bass are the most audible. Careful attention to the outer-voice counterpoint -- particularly ensuring contrary motion or stepwise soprano motion -- prevents this issue.

# Examples
If the soprano leaps from E5 to G5 while the bass leaps from A3 to C3 (both ascending), and the resulting interval is a perfect fifth, this is a direct fifth. If instead the soprano steps from F5 to G5 while the bass leaps to C3, the stepwise soprano motion makes the direct fifth acceptable.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes confuse direct fifths/octaves with parallel fifths/octaves. Parallel motion involves moving from one perfect interval to the same type in parallel; direct motion involves arriving at a perfect interval by similar (but not parallel) motion. Direct intervals are a guideline, not an absolute rule, and they are acceptable when the soprano moves by step.
