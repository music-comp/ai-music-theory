# Quick Definition
A three-key exposition is a tonal strategy in major-mode sonata forms where the STA moves to a diatonic third-related key (iii or III) before reaching the traditional dominant, bisecting the fifth motion from I to V.

# Formal Definition
The three-key exposition is a nineteenth-century innovation that supplants the traditional I-V tonal polarity with a progression through a third-related key. In major-mode works, the STA moves first to a mediant-related key (iii, III, or bIII) before eventually reaching V by the end of the exposition. This creates a I-iii-V or I-III-V progression in the exposition and a corresponding I-vi-IV or I-vi-I in the recapitulation. The motivation may derive from the arguably more dramatic three-key structure already present in minor-mode works (i-III-V).

# Musical Context
Three-key expositions are characteristic of early nineteenth-century works, particularly by Beethoven. They represent the expansion of tonal possibilities in sonata form and the growing influence of third-related key areas in the Romantic period.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in G major, op. 31, no. 1 (Example 33.7): The STA arrives first in B major (III) before quickly shifting to B minor (iii) for the remainder of the exposition.
- Beethoven, "Waldstein" Sonata, op. 53 (Example 33.8): The exposition moves from C major to E major (III) and remains there. The dominant is only secured at the retransition.
- Bruckner, Symphony no. 6 in A major: The exposition moves I-iii-V, with the recapitulation following I-vi-IV before returning to tonic.

# Common Confusions
A three-key exposition is not the same as a modulating development. In a three-key exposition, the mediant key area is a structural part of the exposition, presented with its own thematic material and formal closure. The traditional dominant is still reached by the end of the exposition in most cases.
