# Quick Definition
A cadential motion from IV to I, also known as the "amen cadence," which is much weaker than the authentic cadence and usually follows one.

# Formal Definition
The plagal cadence is the two-chord motion IV-I, often called the "amen cadence" because of its frequent appearance in church music. It is considerably weaker than the authentic cadence because IV-I lacks the goal-directed descending-fifth root motion of V-I and the strong melodic resolution of ^7 to ^1. The plagal cadence has only a descending fourth in the bass and a static common tone (^1) above. Therefore, IV is peripheral to the harmonic motion and extends tonic through double upper-neighbor motion. A strong authentic cadence usually precedes the plagal cadence.

# Musical Context
The plagal cadence is often a cadence in name only, typically appended to the end of a piece after a definitive authentic cadence. It is characteristic of hymn endings and sacred music but appears in secular music as well.

# Examples
- Dykes, "Holy, Holy, Holy": plagal cadence immediately following an authentic cadence to close the hymn

# Common Confusions
- The plagal cadence is not a strong structural cadence; an authentic cadence usually precedes it
- IV in a plagal cadence functions more as an embellishing chord extending tonic than as a true harmonic goal
- Do not confuse the plagal cadence with IV functioning as a pre-dominant (where IV leads to V, not I)
