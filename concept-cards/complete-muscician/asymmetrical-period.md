# Quick Definition
A period comprising an uneven number of phrases (three, five, or seven), where all phrases except the last close with cadences weaker than the final cadence.

# Formal Definition
An asymmetrical period contains an odd number of phrases, each closing with a cadence weaker than the final phrase's cadence. Composers distribute the unequal phrases in three ways: (1) immediate repetition of the antecedent or consequent (e.g., aab or abb), maintaining deeper-level two-phrase symmetry; (2) new material in each phrase (e.g., abc), where the asymmetry is clearly perceived; (3) combination of repeated and new material (e.g., aabbc). A series of phrases with only weak cadences and no strong closing cadence is not a period but a phrase group.

# Musical Context
Asymmetrical periods appear in folk songs, Classical themes, and Romantic character pieces. They create formal variety by departing from the standard two-phrase period model.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in C minor ("Pathetique"), op. 13, Rondo: antecedent-consequent-consequent (abb) structure maintaining deeper-level two-phrase symmetry
- Gruber, "Silent Night": three four-measure sections (abc), with only the first and third containing cadences; interpretable as an asymmetrical three-phrase period
- Haydn, String Quartet "Emperor," op. 76, no. 3: five four-measure phrases (aabbc) with underlying three-phrase structure

# Common Confusions
- A phrase group (phrase chain) is not an asymmetrical period; in a phrase group, none of the cadences is strong enough to provide closure
- The abb type maintains a deeper two-phrase symmetry; the repeated consequent is more of an echo than an independent entity
