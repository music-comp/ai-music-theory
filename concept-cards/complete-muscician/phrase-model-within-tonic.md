# Quick Definition
A miniature T-PD-D-T progression embedded within the tonic prolongation of a larger phrase, using inversions to weaken the cadential effect and avoid confusion with the structural cadence.

# Formal Definition
Composers often incorporate the T-PD-D-T model within the tonic prolongation itself, creating a mini-progression that does not serve as the structural cadence. To prevent the listener from hearing this as the actual cadence, composers weaken the progression through inversions. For example, the strong V7-I motion is undermined by placing the seventh in the bass (V4/2-I6), delaying closure. A common technique follows IV or ii6 with V4/2, maintaining ^4 in the bass which must fall to ^3. This concept is later formalized as the Embedded Phrase Model (EPM) in Chapter 17.

# Musical Context
This technique allows for richer harmonic activity within the tonic area of a phrase without creating premature closure. It extends phrases and creates more complex harmonic narratives while maintaining the primacy of the structural cadence.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in G minor, Hob. XVI/44: six-measure phrase extended by evading the HC in m. 4; V sustained, bass becomes seventh, resolving to i6 and extending tonic for two more measures before cadencing

# Common Confusions
- The mini T-PD-D-T within tonic is not a cadence; it is a tonic expansion
- Inversions are crucial: root-position V-I would create a premature cadence
- This concept foreshadows the more formal treatment of EPMs in Chapter 17
