# Quick Definition
A complete V7 (all four members present) resolves to an incomplete I (missing the fifth); an incomplete V7 (missing the fifth) resolves to a complete I. The leading tone may fall to ^5 in an inner voice to allow both chords to be complete.

# Formal Definition
When all four members of V7 are present (complete chord), the resolution to I will be incomplete -- the tonic will lack the fifth and have a tripled root. This is because the strict resolution of all tendency tones (^7 up to ^1, ^4 down to ^3, ^2 to ^1 or ^3) forces the doubling. An incomplete V7 (missing the fifth, with doubled root) resolves to a complete tonic. There is one exception: when the leading tone appears in an inner voice, it may skip down a third to ^5 instead of ascending to ^1, permitting both V7 and I to be complete chords.

# Musical Context
This interplay between complete and incomplete voicings is a practical necessity of four-voice writing. Understanding it prevents students from writing incorrect doublings or unresolved tendency tones. The inner-voice exception for the leading tone is an important flexibility.

# Common Confusions
Students often try to make both V7 and I complete, which forces voice-leading errors. The inner-voice exception (^7 falling to ^5) is the only way to achieve both complete chords. The fifth is the only member that may be omitted from either chord.
