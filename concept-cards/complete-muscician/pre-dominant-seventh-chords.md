# Quick Definition
Seventh chords built on pre-dominant harmonies (ii7, ii-diminished-7, IV7, iv7), classified as nondominant seventh chords, which add color and intensify the pre-dominant function.

# Formal Definition
Pre-dominant seventh chords are formed by adding a diatonic seventh to ii and IV triads. In major: ii7 and IV7. In minor: ii-diminished-7 and iv7. These belong to the broad category of nondominant seventh chords -- chords that do not have dominant (major-minor) or leading-tone (diminished-diminished) quality. The seventh must be prepared (by common tone or step from above) and resolved (step down). The ii6/5 chord is the most common type and inversion of nondominant seventh chords. Root-position seventh chords can omit the fifth (doubling the root) to avoid parallels; inverted seventh chords should be complete.

# Musical Context
Nondominant seventh chords provide color and contrast throughout the common-practice period. The tension of ii7 can make V7 sound like a resolution rather than a dissonance. IV7 often resolves to V7 or the cadential six-four (not directly to V triad, to avoid parallel fifths).

# Examples
- Chopin, Nocturne in B major, op. 62, no. 1: cadential progression ii7-V7-I with abrupt, unprepared entrance of ii7 creating tension that V7 resolves

# Common Confusions
- The seventh of nondominant seventh chords must be prepared AND resolved; unlike V7, these sevenths cannot enter freely
- A suspension over ii can create an apparent iv7 chord; context determines whether the dissonant pitch is a chord tone or a nonchord tone
- IV7 to V risks parallel fifths; resolve IV7 to V7 or to the cadential six-four instead
