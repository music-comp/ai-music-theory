# Quick Definition
The triad built on ^6 (vi in major, VI in minor), providing modal contrast and serving as a bridge between tonic and pre-dominant, a tonic substitute, or occasionally as a pre-dominant itself.

# Formal Definition
The submediant harmony (vi in major, VI in minor) provides color contrast: it is minor in a major key and major in a minor key. Its harmonic contexts are tied to the three basic root motions of tonal music: descending fifth, ascending second, and descending third. The submediant acts in two seemingly contradictory ways: (1) as an extension of tonic (sharing two common tones with I, arising from a contrapuntal 5-6 motion), and (2) as a pre-pre-dominant chord (preparing and preceding PD). At the second level, vi is generally analyzed under "T" (tonic) with an arrowhead showing its bridging function.

# Musical Context
The submediant introduces the last major new color to the diatonic palette. In major keys, vi provides welcome minor-mode contrast; in minor keys, VI provides major-mode contrast. It participates in some of the most characteristic progressions in tonal music, including the deceptive motion V-vi.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Violin Sonata no. 5 ("Spring"), op. 24: vi appears in both an EPM and the structural cadence, each time as part of a descending-fifths progression
- Schubert, "Fruhlingstraum," Winterreise: vi-ii6 progression combines descending-fifths motion with melodic bass arpeggiation ^1-^6-^4

# Common Confusions
- vi can extend tonic or precede PD (or both simultaneously); the interpretation depends on context
- An apparent vi6 can be a voice-leading byproduct (5-6 motion) rather than a true submediant harmony
- At the second level, vi usually falls under "T" even when it precedes PD
