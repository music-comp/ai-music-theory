# Quick Definition
The closing section (Cl) follows the STA's conclusive cadence in a sonata form exposition, reinforcing the new key through multiple cadential figures and sometimes introducing new thematic material.

# Formal Definition
The closing section (Cl) follows the appearance of contrasting thematic material in the STA and a conclusive cadence of that material. Its purpose is to reinforce the new key (V in major, III in minor) through multiple cadential figures cast in two or more subsections that may contain new thematic material. The closing section is often longer than the STA, which may occupy eight or fewer measures. A double bar or repeat sign usually marks the end of the exposition after the closing section. An optional codetta may follow. In the recapitulation, the closing section returns in the tonic, fulfilling the sonata principle.

# Musical Context
The closing section serves a structural role analogous to the confirmation of a new key in binary form. Its cadential nature provides a sense of arrival and stability after the tonal tension created by the transition and STA. The closing section is an important but often overlooked component of sonata form.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 19, op. 49, no. 1 (Example 33.2): The Cl follows a PAC in III at m. 29, with cadential material reinforcing Bb major.
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C major, Hob. XVI.35 (Example 33.9): The Cl (mm. 46-62) begins with a descending-fifth figure and is longer than the STA itself (mm. 36-45).

# Common Confusions
Students often neglect the closing section or merge it with the STA. The closing section begins after the STA's conclusive cadence and serves a different function: reinforcing the new key rather than presenting new thematic material in it. However, the closing section may contain its own thematic material.
