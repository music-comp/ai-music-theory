# Quick Definition
The reciprocal process occurs when the listener loses tonal grounding because a harmony simultaneously implies two conflicting functions, such as when the tonic sounds like the dominant of another key.

# Formal Definition
The reciprocal process is a type of tonal ambiguity in which conflicting tonal implications confuse the three harmonic functions (T, PD, and D). It occurs when a chord simultaneously functions plausibly as tonic in one key and as dominant (or pre-dominant) of another. For example, when a major tonic is treated as V7/iv, the listener cannot determine whether the chord is a tonic resolution or a dominant preparation. This ambiguity is especially powerful when it occurs at cadences, where tonal clarity is expected.

# Musical Context
The reciprocal process is favored by Romantic composers, particularly Schumann and Brahms. It reflects the nineteenth-century fascination with tonal ambiguity and the blurring of functional boundaries. The technique often serves dramatic or poetic purposes in Lieder, where harmonic ambiguity mirrors textual ambiguity.

# Examples
- Chopin, Etude in C minor, op. 10, no. 12 (Example 34.1): The coda treats the C-major tonic as V/iv, so the ending sounds more like a dominant of F minor than a tonic resolution in C.
- Schumann, "Auf einer Burg," Liederkreis, op. 39, no. 7 (Example 34.2): The song appears to end in E major, but the final E-major chord functions as V leading to the next song's A-minor opening.

# Common Confusions
The reciprocal process is not simply a deceptive cadence or an applied chord. It is a deeper ambiguity where the entire tonal orientation is in question. The listener genuinely cannot determine whether a chord is a tonic or a dominant, and both interpretations are simultaneously valid.
