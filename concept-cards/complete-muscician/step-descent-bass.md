# Quick Definition
A bass line that descends stepwise from ^1 to ^5 (direct) or from ^1 through ^5 to ^4 and back (indirect), commonly harmonized with i-v6-iv6-V in minor.

# Formal Definition
Step-descent basses descend from tonic to dominant through stepwise motion. The direct step-descent bass falls ^1-^7-^6-^5 and is typically harmonized as i-v6 (passing)-iv6-V. In minor, this is called a lament bass due to its association with melancholy texts. The half step ^6-^5 creates a powerful gravitational pull. Minor v6 (with lowered ^7) is used to precede minor iv6, avoiding the augmented second between raised ^7 and lowered ^6. The indirect step-descent bass extends beyond ^5 to ^4 (harmonized by iv or ii6), then returns to ^5: ^1-^7-^6-^5-^4 back to ^5. VI usually converts to iv6 via a 5-6 motion to avoid parallels before moving to V.

# Musical Context
Step-descent basses have been used since the early seventeenth century (Monteverdi). When repeated as an ostinato, they create ground basses or chaconnes. The lament bass pattern underlies famous works spanning centuries, from Purcell's "Dido's Lament" to popular songs like "Hit the Road, Jack" and "Erie Canal."

# Examples
- Schutz, "Nacket bin ich von Mutterleibe kommen": diatonic descent i-v6-iv6-V
- Purcell, "Dido's Lament" (Dido and Aeneas): lament bass as ground bass
- Bach, "Crucifixus," Mass in B minor: chromatic lament bass
- Rachmaninoff, Prelude in G minor, op. 23, no. 5: lament bass pattern

# Common Confusions
- Minor v6 in a step-descent bass is a passing chord, NOT a functional dominant; it uses lowered ^7 to avoid the augmented second
- Root-position VI usually cannot lead directly to V due to parallels; convert to iv6 via 5-6 motion first
- The first ^5 in an indirect descent is harmonized with a passing chord (not structural V) to avoid premature arrival on dominant
