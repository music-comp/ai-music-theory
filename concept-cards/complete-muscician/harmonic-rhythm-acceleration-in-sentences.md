# Quick Definition
A characteristic feature of sentence structure where the rate of harmonic change speeds up in the B section, even as the melodic units combine into a longer span.

# Formal Definition
In sentence structure, the B section (the second half, which is twice as long as each A statement) typically features accelerated harmonic rhythm compared to the A and A' sections. This creates a musical paradox: as the initial two-measure melodic units combine and lengthen into a single four-measure unit, the harmonic changes become more frequent, fragmenting the longer unit internally. The acceleration drives toward the cadence and contributes to the sense of culmination at the end of the sentence.

# Musical Context
This harmonic acceleration is one of the defining characteristics that distinguishes sentence structure from a simple repeated phrase. It provides the rhetorical intensification that makes the sentence form compelling.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 1 in F minor, op. 2: mm. 1-4 have slow harmonic rhythm (two measures of tonic followed by two measures of neighboring dominant); mm. 5-8 accelerate with chord changes every measure or more frequently

# Common Confusions
- The paradox of sentence form: the melodic units get longer (combining into B) while the harmonic rhythm gets faster
- This acceleration is typical but not absolutely required in every sentence
