# Quick Definition
An intervallic cell is a small, malleable motivic structure defined by its intervallic content and contour rather than by its melodic design, capable of development in both melodic and harmonic domains.

# Formal Definition
Intervallic cells (or simply cells) are short pitch collections defined by their component intervals and overall contour rather than by specific pitches. Unlike traditional motives, which are characterized by rhythm and melodic shape, cells are more abstract and defined by intervallic relationships. Cells can be developed through transposition, inversion (reversing contour), interpolation (adding pitches between cell members), rhythmic diminution, and rhythmic augmentation. They appear early in a composition and can be transformed at various structural levels, from the surface to the deepest harmonic level. The emphasis on intervallic rather than melodic identity was a central focus of atonal music between 1900 and 1920.

# Musical Context
Cells became increasingly important in the nineteenth century because of their capacity for diverse harmonization and their inherent ambiguity. They represent a subtle shift from periodic, singable melodic constructions to more abstract pitch collections. This development bridges tonal and atonal compositional practice.

# Examples
- Brahms, Intermezzo in A minor, op. 118, no. 1 (Example 37.6): A three-note descending cell (C6-Bb5-A5) is transposed, inverted, and expanded across multiple structural levels.
- Chopin, Prelude in A minor, op. 28, no. 2 (Example 37.8): A three-note cell (fourth + third, containing a second) appears in the melody, ostinato, bass, and even at the deepest structural level of the piece.

# Common Confusions
A cell is not the same as a motive. Motives are defined by specific rhythmic and pitch profiles; cells are defined by intervallic content and can appear in varied rhythmic, registral, and contour configurations. A cell's identity is preserved when its intervals are maintained even if its specific pitches, rhythm, or direction change.
