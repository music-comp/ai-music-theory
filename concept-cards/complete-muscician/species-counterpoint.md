# Quick Definition
Species counterpoint is a pedagogical system of five progressively complex exercises for learning to combine voices, beginning with note-against-note writing and advancing through increasingly elaborate rhythmic and melodic interactions.

# Formal Definition
Species counterpoint, codified by Johann Fux in Gradus ad Parnassum (1725), presents five species (steps) for learning to compose voices against a cantus firmus: First species (1:1): one note against each CF note, using only consonances. Second species (2:1): two notes against each CF note, introducing weak-beat dissonance (unaccented passing tones). Third species (4:1): four notes per CF note, allowing more dissonance types. Fourth species: syncopated counterpoint with suspensions. Fifth species (florid): combines techniques from all previous species. Each species isolates specific aspects of voice interaction in a controlled environment.

# Musical Context
Species counterpoint has been the standard pedagogical approach to voice-leading since the eighteenth century. Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, and Brahms all studied it intensively. The principles learned through species counterpoint -- controlling consonance and dissonance, maintaining voice independence, avoiding parallel perfect intervals -- apply directly to all tonal composition and voice leading.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes view species counterpoint as an abstract academic exercise disconnected from "real" music. In fact, the principles it teaches are the foundation of all tonal voice leading. Excerpts from real music throughout the text demonstrate that species counterpoint principles are directly observable in compositions from the Renaissance through the Romantic period (Examples 4.4A-F).
