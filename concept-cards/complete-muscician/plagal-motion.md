# Quick Definition
Plagal motion is a cadential progression in which a harmony other than the dominant (typically iv, ii-half-dim-6/5, or bVI) moves directly to the tonic, bypassing the dominant function entirely.

# Formal Definition
During the nineteenth century, the dominant's cadential role gradually diminished as composers explored novel harmonic combinations. Mixture harmonies, with their dramatic effects, became popular substitutes for the dominant at cadences. Because b^6 strongly desires resolution by half step to ^5, iv can move directly to the tonic. The supertonic (ii-half-dim-6/5) and submediant (bVI) may also resolve directly to tonic. These progressions are labeled "PL" (plagal) at the second level of analysis. The Hollywood cadence is a specific type: a chromatically embellished plagal cadence where a contrapuntal 5-6 motion and mixture transform IV into ii-half-dim-6/5 before resolution to I.

# Musical Context
Plagal motions became increasingly common in the nineteenth century as composers developed a distaste for commonplace V7-I formulas. They are a hallmark of Brahms, Schumann, and later Romantic composers. The Hollywood cadence (ii-half-dim-6/5 to I) is frequently heard in popular music of the 1920s-1950s and in film scores today.

# Examples
- Brahms, Symphony no. 3 in F major, op. 90, Andante (Example 27.12): The passage features the complete absence of dominant harmony, even at the final cadence. bVI and minor iv resolve to the tonic in a plagal cadence.

# Common Confusions
Plagal motion is not merely IV-I (the traditional plagal cadence). It refers more broadly to any non-dominant harmony resolving directly to tonic at a cadence, particularly mixture chords like iv, ii-half-dim-6/5, or bVI. The term "plagal relation" (Chapter 34) extends this concept to a broader structural phenomenon in nineteenth-century music.
