# Quick Definition
Applied chords follow the same voice-leading rules as V7: do not double the leading tone or seventh, resolve the leading tone up in outer voices, and resolve the seventh down.

# Formal Definition
Applied dominant chords contain two chromatic tendency tones: (1) the third of the chord, which is often chromatically raised and acts as a temporary leading tone, and (2) the seventh of the chord, which may need to be chromatically lowered. Voice-leading rules: do not double the temporary leading tone or the chordal seventh; the temporary leading tone must resolve upward when in an outer voice; the seventh always resolves downward. Root-position applied dominant chords can be complete or incomplete. Prepared chromaticism (preceding a chromatic tone with its diatonic version in the same voice) is always preferred.

# Musical Context
Proper voice leading of applied chords is essential for smooth chromatic writing. Applied chords typically appear on metrically weak beats, resolving to structural chords on stronger beats.

# Examples
- In V7/vi in C major: G-sharp is the temporary leading tone (resolves up to A), and the chord follows standard V7 resolution rules
- Prepared chromaticism: G-G#-A creates a smooth chromatic line in the soprano

# Common Confusions
- The temporary leading tone and chordal seventh are tendency tones that must resolve, just like in a regular V7 chord
- When a chromatic tone cannot be prepared in the same voice, a cross relation results (acceptable between inner voices or between an outer and inner voice, not between bass and soprano)
