# Quick Definition
Da capo form is a ternary structure realized by the instruction "Da Capo al fine" at the end of the B section, directing the performer to return to the beginning and play to the fermata or fine marking.

# Formal Definition
Da capo form creates an ABA structure through performance instruction rather than written-out repetition. The marking "Da Capo" (literally "from the head") tells the performer to return to the beginning and play until the fermata sign or "fine" marking. The da capo instruction saves space and ink while creating the final balancing A section. The da capo aria is arguably the most important ternary structure in the Baroque period, typically punctuated by instrumental interludes called ritornellos. Da capo form can be found in almost all genres, style periods, and instrumental combinations.

# Musical Context
While the da capo aria is the primary form of Baroque vocal music (exemplified by Bach, Handel, and Vivaldi), da capo form also appears in instrumental works across periods. The form provides opportunities for ornamentation and improvisation on the return of A, a practice expected in Baroque performance.

# Examples
- Haydn, Divertimento in G major, Hob. XVI.11, Presto (Example 31.5): A full sectional ternary form realized through the da capo instruction.
- Bach, "Ich will Dir mein Herze schenken," St. Matthew Passion, BWV 244 (Example 31.7): A da capo aria with ritornellos punctuating the formal sections. The A part stays in the tonic while the B part is more transitory and sequential.

# Common Confusions
Da capo form is not a separate form type from ternary; it is a method of realizing ternary form through performance instruction. The formal analysis is the same as for any ternary form; the difference is merely notational.
