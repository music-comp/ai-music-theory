# Quick Definition
The whole-tone scale is a symmetrical scale dividing the octave into six equal whole steps, producing complete tonal ambiguity since every scale step is equally stable (or unstable).

# Formal Definition
The whole-tone scale consists entirely of whole steps, creating a symmetrical division of the octave into six equal parts. Only two distinct whole-tone collections exist: one beginning on C (C-D-E-F#-G#-A#) and one beginning on C# (C#-D#-F-G-A-B). The scale lacks the asymmetrical half-step/whole-step patterns that create leading tones and tonal gravity in major and minor scales. Consequently, no pitch has greater tendency toward any other, and goal-directed motion disappears. The augmented triad and the French augmented sixth chord (and its enharmonic equivalent, the altered dominant seventh) are chords whose pitches all belong to a single whole-tone collection.

# Musical Context
The whole-tone scale appeared in tonal music primarily through chromatic sequences (particularly the D2 (-P4/+m3) sequence, which traces a whole-tone path in the bass) and through the augmented triad. By the late nineteenth century, Debussy and other composers used the whole-tone scale as an independent harmonic and melodic resource, divorced from functional tonality.

# Examples
- The chromatic D2 (-P4/+m3) sequence carves out a whole-tone path (Example 36.2), dividing the octave into six major seconds (G-F-Eb-Db-Cb-Ab-G).
- Scriabin, Prelude, op. 39, no. 2 (Example 37.12): The opening sonority (F#-A#-C-E) contains only intervals belonging to a single whole-tone track, demonstrating the sonority's symmetrical properties.

# Common Confusions
The whole-tone scale is not simply "a scale with whole steps." Its significance lies in its symmetry: because all intervals are equal, it creates no tonal hierarchy, no leading tones, and no functional harmony. This makes it fundamentally different from major and minor scales, which derive their tonal power from asymmetrical interval patterns.
