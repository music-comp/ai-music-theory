# Quick Definition
In minor keys, ^7 must be raised (creating a leading tone) to form a major dominant triad and provide the half-step resolution to ^1 essential for tonal cadences.

# Formal Definition
In the natural minor scale, ^7 is a whole step below ^1 and does not function as a leading tone. To create the essential half-step pull toward ^1 that defines tonal music, ^7 must be raised chromatically, producing the harmonic minor scale. This raised ^7 appears in V (making it a major triad), V7, viio6, viio7, and all their inversions. The raising of ^7 is a necessary chromatic alteration throughout minor-key music whenever dominant function is expressed.

# Musical Context
The raised leading tone in minor is so fundamental that it is not typically considered a chromatic alteration but rather a standard feature of the minor mode. In figured bass notation, a sharp, natural, or other accidental may be needed below viio6 to indicate the raised sixth above the bass (which is ^7). When ^7 descends to ^6 (as in ^1-^7-^6-^5), it is not a leading tone and should not be raised.

# Examples
In A minor, G# (raised ^7) creates the leading tone for the dominant triad E-G#-B. In viio6, the raised ^7 appears as the sixth above the bass -- e.g., in A minor, viio6 = B-D-G#, where G# must be specifically indicated in figured bass. In minor-key cadences, the dominant must always be a major triad through this raised ^7.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes forget to raise ^7 in minor, particularly in viio6 and viio7 chords where the leading tone appears above the bass rather than as the bass note itself. Also, ^7 should only be raised when it functions as a leading tone (ascending to ^1), not when it descends as part of the natural minor scale (^1-^7-^6-^5).
