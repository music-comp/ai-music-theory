# Quick Definition
Voice overlap occurs when a voice moves beyond the previous note of an adjacent voice; voice crossing occurs when two voices swap registers so that the lower voice sounds higher than the upper voice (or vice versa).

# Formal Definition
Voice overlap occurs when a voice moves to a pitch that is beyond (above or below) the immediately preceding note of an adjacent voice -- for example, the alto moving above the soprano's previous note. Voice crossing occurs when voices exchange registers -- for example, the alto sounds below the current tenor note. Both problems result from excessive leaping and registral shifting within voices, compromising each voice's independence and musical territory.

# Musical Context
These errors undermine the independence of voices, creating confusion about which voice is which. Maintaining each voice's specific register is essential for clear four-part writing. Occasional voice crossings appear in masterworks for specific dramatic or contrapuntal reasons, but they should be avoided in student writing.

# Examples
In Example 8.10B, the alto intrudes into the soprano's register by hopping up a sixth to C5, extending beyond the soprano's established A4 (voice overlap). On beat 1 of m. 3, the alto appears below the tenor C4 (voice crossing). In the last chord of m. 4, the tenor C4 is higher than the alto A3 (another voice crossing).

# Common Confusions
Voice overlap and voice crossing are related but distinct. Crossing means two voices are simultaneously in the wrong registers. Overlap means a voice has moved past where the adjacent voice was on the immediately preceding beat, even if they are not currently crossed.
