# Quick Definition
Sonata form is a large-scale musical process based on a three-part tonal strategy: stating material in the tonic, restating material in a contrasting key, and finally restating all material in the tonic.

# Formal Definition
Sonata form arises from the combination of balanced and rounded continuous binary forms. It consists of three main sections: the exposition (presenting material in tonic and a contrasting key), the development (transforming and exploring material in remote keys), and the recapitulation (restating all material in the tonic). The exposition divides into the first tonal area (FTA) in the tonic and the second tonal area (STA) in the contrasting key (usually V in major, III in minor). The sonata principle requires that material presented in the contrasting key returns in the tonic in the recapitulation. Sonata form is not a rigid mold but a flexible compositional process.

# Musical Context
Since the 1780s, sonata form has been featured in virtually all important genres of art music: symphonies, concertos, operas, and instrumental sonatas. The terms "sonata-allegro form" and "first-movement form" are misnomers, since sonata form may appear in any tempo and any movement. It remained the dominant large-scale formal type through the nineteenth century.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 19 in G minor, op. 49, no. 1 (Example 33.2): A clear sonata form with exposition (i to III), development (VI to V), and recapitulation (i throughout).
- Haydn, Piano Sonata no. 48 in C major, Hob. XVI.35 (Example 33.9): Features a monothematic sonata form with detailed motivic development of the descending fifth.

# Common Confusions
Sonata form is not merely a "form" but a dynamic process. It is an organic structure where each part depends on every other part, unlike ternary and rondo, which are additive composite forms whose sections could theoretically be omitted without jeopardizing structural integrity. Also, the STA is defined by key, not by theme; a monothematic sonata uses the same theme in both tonal areas.
