# Quick Definition
The antecedent phrase opens a period with a weaker cadence, creating expectation; the consequent phrase closes the period with a stronger cadence, providing resolution.

# Formal Definition
In a period, the antecedent is the first phrase, which ends with a harmonically or melodically incomplete cadence (typically HC or IAC). The consequent is the second phrase, which ends with a more conclusive cadence (typically PAC). Together they form a question-and-answer relationship. The antecedent establishes tonal material and creates a sense of incompleteness, while the consequent restates or develops the material and provides satisfying closure.

# Musical Context
This pairing is the basis of period construction across all tonal music. The antecedent-consequent relationship drives formal continuity by exploiting listener expectations for resolution after an incomplete cadence.

# Examples
- In a PIP: the antecedent ends with HC, and the consequent restarts on tonic and pushes through to PAC
- In a CCP: the antecedent ends with HC or IAC, and the consequent begins on a non-tonic harmony (often PD), creating a single harmonic sweep to PAC

# Common Confusions
- Not every pair of phrases is antecedent-consequent; the second phrase must provide closure stronger than the first
- A phrase ending with PAC cannot serve as an antecedent in an interrupted period (though it can in a sectional period if the second PAC is stronger)
