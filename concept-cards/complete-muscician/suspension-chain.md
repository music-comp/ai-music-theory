# Quick Definition
A patterned series of interlocking suspensions derived from a series of falling 6/3 chords, where each resolution becomes the preparation for the next suspension.

# Formal Definition
A suspension chain is a series of suspensions that interlock, with each suspension's resolution serving as the preparation for the following suspension. Chains are commonly derived from parallel six-three chords: the resulting 7-6 suspensions disguise the underlying parallel fifths. The motions are more contrapuntal and melodic than harmonic, often framed by outer-voice parallel tenths descending by step.

# Musical Context
Suspension chains create forward momentum and sustained tension over extended passages. They are particularly effective in sequential passages and cadential approaches. Composers may break the chain pattern at the end to create a stronger cadential chord (e.g., substituting V7 for what would have been a vii-diminished chord).

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata no. 37 in E major, Hob. XVI/22: 7-6 suspension chain beginning in m. 63, with outer-voice parallel tenths descending by step; Haydn breaks the pattern in m. 67 with a change of bass to create V7 for a stronger cadence on G major

# Common Confusions
- The underlying structure of a suspension chain is often parallel six-three chords, not a standard harmonic progression
- The chain is a contrapuntal device that disguises parallel fifths
- Breaking the chain at the end for a stronger cadence is a common and deliberate compositional choice
