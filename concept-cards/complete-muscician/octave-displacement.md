# Quick Definition
A motivic transformation in which a portion of the motive is shifted up or down by an octave, altering contour while preserving pitch-class content.

# Formal Definition
Octave displacement shifts a portion of a motive up or down by one or more octaves. This can dramatically alter the contour and intervallic profile: for example, a descending third becomes an ascending sixth (its inversion). While the pitch classes remain the same, the registral shift can transform the motive's character. Octave displacement can make a motive unrecognizable if applied carelessly but can also reveal hidden relationships between surface intervals and their compound or inverted forms.

# Musical Context
Octave displacement is a favorite technique of Brahms and other composers who valued organic motivic unity. It allows a single-interval motive (such as a falling third) to appear as its inversion (a rising sixth) while maintaining the same pitch-class relationship, creating subtle unity across seemingly different thematic ideas.

# Examples
- Clementi, Sonata in F minor, op. 13, no. 6: incomplete neighbor motive subjected to octave displacement, combined with augmentation, creating dramatic effect at the first crescendo
- Brahms, Symphony no. 4: falling third chain (E-C-A-F#-D#-B-G-E) includes one ascending sixth (B to G) due to range limitations; the first movement theme uses sixths and thirds interchangeably through octave displacement

# Common Confusions
- Displacing a single pitch by an octave can weaken motivic identity more than transposing the entire motive
- Octave displacement changes interval size but preserves interval class (third/sixth, second/seventh, etc.)
- A rising sixth created by octave displacement is not "inversion" in the traditional sense; it is registral manipulation
