# Quick Definition
A triad is a three-note chord built by stacking two thirds above a root, creating the fundamental harmonic unit of tonal music.

# Formal Definition
A triad comprises three distinct pitches arranged in thirds: the root (lowest pitch when stacked in thirds), the third (a third above the root), and the fifth (a fifth above the root). A chord is any combination of three or more different pitches; a triad is specifically a chord stacked in thirds. Four types of triads exist, based on the quality of their component thirds and the interval of the fifth: major (M3 + m3 = P5), minor (m3 + M3 = P5), diminished (m3 + m3 = d5), and augmented (M3 + M3 = A5). Major and minor triads are consonant (spanning a P5); diminished and augmented triads are dissonant.

# Musical Context
Triads are the basic units of harmonic organization in tonal music. Only major, minor, and diminished triads function as independent harmonies in common-practice music; the augmented triad results from contrapuntal voice leading rather than serving as an independent sonority. Understanding triad construction is prerequisite to harmonic analysis.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes confuse the terms "chord" and "triad." Every triad is a chord, but not every chord is a triad. A triad specifically has three distinct pitches stacked in thirds. Also, when a triad is inverted, the root is no longer the lowest note, but it remains the root; students must learn to identify the root regardless of voicing.
