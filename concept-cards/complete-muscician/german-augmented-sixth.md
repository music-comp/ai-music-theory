# Quick Definition
The German augmented sixth chord (Ger6/5) substitutes a perfect fifth above the bass (b^3) for one of the doubled pitches in the Italian sixth, creating a four-note chord containing b^6, ^1, b^3, and #^4.

# Formal Definition
The German augmented sixth chord (Ger6/5) contains b^6, ^1, b^3 (a perfect fifth above the bass), and #^4. The figured bass is 6/5/3. It sounds identical to a complete dominant seventh chord, a property exploited in enharmonic modulations. The Ger6/5 should move first to a cadential 6/4 rather than directly to V, in order to avoid parallel fifths between b^3-^1 and ^2-^5. In major mode, b^3 must be lowered (requiring an additional accidental beyond b^6). Composers sometimes notate b^3 enharmonically as #^2 to show the chromatic ascent to ^3, producing a "doubly augmented sixth chord" (sometimes called "Swiss sixth").

# Musical Context
The German sixth is the most commonly used augmented sixth chord in practice. Its enharmonic equivalence with V7 makes it a powerful tool for modulation, particularly to bII (the Neapolitan key area). The Ger6/5 frequently appears in extended pre-dominant expansions, often as the final chord before V, following iv or bVI.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 13 in Eb major, op. 27, no. 1 (Example 30.8): The Ger6/5-V motion is reiterated seven times in nine measures, highlighted by sf markings.
- Haydn, String Quartet in A major, op. 55, no. 1 (Example 30.15): A Ger6/5 in the key of E (V) secures the modulation in a contrasting progressive period.

# Common Confusions
The Ger6/5 sounds exactly like a dominant seventh chord but functions as a pre-dominant. The difference lies in voice leading: the augmented sixth chord's outer voices expand outward to ^5, while V7's seventh descends and the bass leaps. When the Ger6/5 occurs in major mode, the "doubly augmented sixth" spelling (#^2 instead of b^3) is visually awkward but shows correct voice leading.
