# Quick Definition
Unequal fifths occur when a perfect fifth moves to a diminished fifth (or vice versa) between two voices. P5 to d5 is permissible as long as the d5 resolves to a third; d5 to P5 is generally not permitted.

# Formal Definition
Unequal fifths are the progression from a perfect fifth to a diminished fifth, or the reverse, between a pair of voices. These are not parallel perfect fifths because the two fifths are of different quality. Moving from a perfect fifth (in I) to a diminished fifth (in V7) is permissible as long as the diminished fifth resolves to a third. The reverse motion -- diminished fifth to perfect fifth -- is generally not permitted because it contradicts the natural tendency of the diminished fifth to contract to a third.

# Musical Context
Unequal fifths commonly arise in the progression I to V7, where the perfect fifth between two upper voices in I becomes a diminished fifth in V7. Understanding this distinction prevents students from incorrectly flagging acceptable voice leading as parallel fifths.

# Common Confusions
Students often mistake unequal fifths for parallel fifths. The key distinction: parallel fifths involve two perfect fifths (or two diminished fifths) in succession, while unequal fifths involve a change from one type of fifth to another. Only P5 to P5 or d5 to d5 are prohibited parallels.
