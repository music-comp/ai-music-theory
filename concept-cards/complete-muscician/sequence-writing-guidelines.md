# Quick Definition
Three essential principles for writing sequences: correct voice leading in the model, soprano-bass contrary motion, and at least one imperfect consonance between outer voices.

# Formal Definition
Three guidelines govern sequence writing: (1) Ensure correct voice leading in the first three chords (model plus first chord of copy), since copies replicate the model exactly and any errors will propagate. (2) Start with bass and soprano voices, incorporating contrary step motion between outer voices. (3) Include at least one imperfect consonance (3rd, 6th, or 10th) between outer voices in the model. Within sequences, normally forbidden voice-leading situations (doubled leading tones, dissonant leaps, diminished triads in root position) are acceptable because of the overall repetition pattern.

# Musical Context
These guidelines apply to all sequence types and ensure that the resulting sequential passages sound musical and avoid cumulative voice-leading errors.

# Examples
- D2 (-5/+4) outer-voice patterns may use 8-6, 10-8, 8-10, or 10-10 intervallic patterns, each incorporating at least one imperfect consonance
- The 10-10 pattern (nonstop tenths) appears in popular music such as "Fly Me to the Moon" and "All the Things You Are"

# Common Confusions
- Do not use accidentals within diatonic sequences
- Parallel fifths and octaves that arise from the sequential pattern are acceptable
- For D2 (-5/+4), write a complete diatonic cycle back to tonic; for other types, stop on a pre-dominant chord
