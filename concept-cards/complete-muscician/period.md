# Quick Definition
A period is a formal structure of two or more phrases in which the final phrase provides closure to something left harmonically or melodically open in the first phrase.

# Formal Definition
A period consists of an antecedent phrase that ends with a weaker cadence (typically HC or IAC) followed by a consequent phrase that ends with a stronger cadence (typically PAC). The antecedent creates expectation through incompleteness, and the consequent fulfills that expectation through closure. The period is labeled with three terms describing its melodic relationship (parallel or contrasting), harmonic structure (interrupted, continuous, sectional, or progressive), and the word "period."

# Musical Context
Periods are one of the most fundamental formal structures in tonal music, appearing in themes, melodies, dance movements, and as building blocks of larger forms. They appear throughout the common-practice period and are essential to Classical-era composition in particular.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in B-flat major, K. 333: Parallel interrupted period (PIP) with HC in m. 4 and PAC in m. 8
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 11 in B-flat major, op. 22, Menuetto: Contrasting continuous period (CCP)
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in B-flat major, K. 281: Parallel sectional period (PSP) where both phrases close on tonic but the second cadence is stronger
- Schubert, "Standchen" from Schwanengesang: Parallel progressive period (PPP) where the second phrase closes in III

# Common Confusions
- Not every pair of phrases forms a period; the second cadence must be stronger than the first to create the antecedent-consequent relationship
- A single eight-measure phrase with no internal cadence is not a period, even if it contains a caesura or break (as in Beethoven's Klavierstuck, WoO 82)
- Sectional periods can still be periods even though both phrases close on tonic, because the melodic incompleteness of the first phrase demands continuation
