# Quick Definition
An occasional usage where vi directly precedes V and functions as the pre-dominant, producing the bass motion ^6-^5 and strong 10-5-10 outer-voice counterpoint.

# Formal Definition
Although vi most commonly functions as a bridge between tonic and pre-dominant or as a tonic substitute, it can occasionally serve directly as the pre-dominant chord, immediately preceding the dominant. In this role, it allows for the motive ^6-^5 in the bass. The progression I-vi-V produces strong 10-5-10 outer-voice counterpoint that makes it effective despite its relatively uncommon usage.

# Musical Context
The vi-V progression as a pre-dominant is less common than ii-V or IV-V but appears in notable works. Composers use it when the ^6-^5 bass motion serves a specific motivic or expressive purpose.

# Examples
- Mahler, Symphony no. 2 ("Resurrection"), "Urlicht": vi functions as pre-dominant, allowing ^6-^5 bass motion
- Chopin, Scherzo no. 2 in Bb minor, op. 31: same vi-V pre-dominant usage, 70 years before Mahler, in the same key

# Common Confusions
- vi as PD is uncommon; in most contexts vi bridges T and PD rather than serving directly as PD
- Do not automatically label vi as PD; it is usually analyzed under T at the second level
- The vi-V-I progression should not be confused with the deceptive motion V-vi (different direction and function)
