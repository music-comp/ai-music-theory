# Quick Definition
The occurrence of a motive at both the musical surface and at deeper structural levels, where it shapes both melody and the underlying harmonic/contrapuntal framework.

# Formal Definition
Motivic parallelism is the phenomenon whereby transformed repetitions of a motive and its harmonization occur at different structural levels simultaneously. A surface melodic gesture can control the larger melodic path and even influence bass motion and harmonic choices at a deeper level. When the initial motive occurs within the longer, more structural version, the parallelism is also nested (motivic nesting). This demonstrates that motives are not merely decorative but serve as generative forces shaping both melody and harmony.

# Musical Context
Motivic parallelism is one of the most profound analytical findings, revealing how a small surface gesture can determine the entire trajectory of a passage or movement. It connects the foreground (what we hear directly) with the background (the underlying structure).

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Fantasy in D minor, K. 397: double-neighbor figure appears both as a tiny surface melody (m. 1, beats 1-3) and as a large gesture controlling the melodic and bass paths of the entire first phrase (mm. 1-4)
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in Ab major, op. 110: the interval of a third operates as both a surface motive and a structural force controlling the outer-voice counterpoint across multiple measures, with connections even between the first and last movements

# Common Confusions
- Motivic parallelism is not mere repetition; it shows the same motive operating at different hierarchical levels simultaneously
- Identifying motivic parallelism requires analysis beyond the musical surface, examining the underlying counterpoint and harmony
- Not every recurring interval is motivic parallelism; the structural and surface appearances must genuinely relate
