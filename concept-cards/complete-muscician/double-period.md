# Quick Definition
A 16-measure formal structure consisting of four phrases arranged so that the antecedent and consequent segments each subdivide into smaller antecedent-consequent phrase pairs.

# Formal Definition
A double period is a structure in which the large-scale antecedent (typically mm. 1-8) and consequent (mm. 9-16) each contain two internal phrases with their own cadences. The cadences in mm. 4 and 12 are weaker than those in mm. 8 and 16, and the cadence in m. 8 (typically HC) is weaker than the final cadence in m. 16 (typically PAC). The internal phrase pairs may suggest antecedent-consequent relationships at the smaller level, but the true period relationship is at the larger level. A common type is the parallel interrupted double period, where the large-scale antecedent ends with HC and the consequent ends with PAC.

# Musical Context
Double periods appear in Classical-era piano sonatas, string quartets, and theme-and-variations sets. They create hierarchical nesting of formal relationships across 16 measures.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in A-flat major, op. 26: 16-measure parallel interrupted double period with cadences at mm. 4 (HC), 8 (HC), 12 (HC), and 16 (PAC)
- Common cadence formula: mm. 1-4 IAC, mm. 5-8 HC, mm. 9-12 IAC, mm. 13-16 PAC

# Common Confusions
- A double period is not simply two periods back-to-back; the cadence at m. 8 must be inconclusive enough to require the PAC at m. 16 for closure
- The internal cadences at mm. 4 and 12 do not set up true antecedent-consequent relationships at the smaller level because of the overarching need for closure at m. 16
