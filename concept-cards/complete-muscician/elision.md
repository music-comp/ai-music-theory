# Quick Definition
A technique for hiding the seam between two independent phrases by converting a cadential dominant to V4/2, creating one large continuous phrase.

# Formal Definition
Elision is a compositional technique in which a composer hides the seams between two independent phrases to create one large phrase. The most common method converts a cadential six-four chord to V4/2 by adding a passing seventh in the bass. Since V4/2 must resolve to I6 (a weak resolution), the potential cadence is averted, and the music flows continuously into what would have been the beginning of a second phrase.

# Musical Context
Elision is distinct from cadential extension: elision conceals phrase boundaries, while cadential extension prolongs and intensifies a cadence through repetition. Both techniques use evaded cadences but serve different formal purposes.

# Examples
- Mozart, Symphony in A major, K. 114: cad 6/4 in m. 12 converted to V4/2, covering the seam between mm. 12 and 13 to create a single eight-measure phrase

# Common Confusions
- Elision hides phrase boundaries; cadential extension repeats cadential material -- these are different purposes for evading a cadence
- The elided phrase is perceived as one continuous unit, not two separate phrases
- Elision typically involves V4/2-I6, not a deceptive motion (V-vi)
