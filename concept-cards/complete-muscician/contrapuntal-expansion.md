# Quick Definition
Contrapuntal expansion (or prolongation) uses embellishing chords -- derived from melodic motions like passing tones, neighbors, and chordal leaps in the bass -- to extend a single structural harmony over time.

# Formal Definition
Contrapuntal expansion is the process by which a structural harmony (typically tonic or dominant) is extended in duration through subordinate chords that arise from melodic motions in the bass and soprano. These embellishing chords are not independent harmonic events but rather byproducts of contrapuntal voice leading. The three primary types of contrapuntal motion that generate expansions are: chordal leaps (I to I6 or V to V6), neighboring motion (I-V6-I), and passing motion (I-viio6-I6). Embellishing chords are usually in first inversion and fall on metrically weak beats, while the structural harmonies they extend are typically in root position and on strong beats.

# Musical Context
Contrapuntal expansion is one of the most important concepts in tonal theory. It explains how tonal music achieves harmonic variety and forward motion while maintaining coherence around a few structural harmonies (primarily I and V). Without contrapuntal expansion, music would consist only of root-position I and V chords, creating a heavy, static sound.

# Examples
In Brahms's "Vom verwundeten Knaben" (Example 10.3C), the same I and V chords used in a static root-position harmonization are transformed into a buoyant, directed phrase through first-inversion chords. The bass becomes melodic, creating a sixth descent from C to E and an octave ascent to the half cadence. Telemann's Scherzi Melodichi draws exclusively on I, I6, V, V6, and V7.

# Common Confusions
Students often treat all chords as equally structural. The key insight is that many chords in first inversion are not independent harmonic events but rather extensions of a single underlying harmony. Their function is melodic/contrapuntal, not harmonic. Second-level analysis reveals this distinction.
