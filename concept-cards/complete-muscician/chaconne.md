# Quick Definition
A type of continuous variation built on a repeating harmonic pattern rather than (or in addition to) a repeating bass line.

# Formal Definition
A chaconne is constructed over a repeating harmonic progression that serves as the foundation for each variation. Unlike a ground bass (which emphasizes the repeating bass line), the chaconne emphasizes the repeating harmonic framework. In practice, however, the distinction between chaconne and passacaglia is often artificial, as composers frequently use both a repeating bass and a repeating harmonic pattern simultaneously. Chaconnes may begin in mid-measure to maintain continuity between variations, and variations often overlap with dovetailed endings.

# Musical Context
Chaconnes are particularly associated with Baroque instrumental music. Bach's famous Chaconne from the D-minor Partita for solo violin is one of the most celebrated examples, using a repeating harmonic ostinato as the foundation for extensive variations.

# Examples
- Bach, Chaconne from Partita no. 2 in D minor: repeating harmonic pattern beginning in mid-measure; only the theme is not overlapped
- Brahms, Symphony no. 4, finale: combines chaconne (harmonic pattern) with ground (upper-voice ascent)

# Common Confusions
- Chaconne and passacaglia are often used interchangeably; strict differentiation is debated among theorists
- The harmonic pattern may be varied or enriched in later variations while maintaining its structural identity
