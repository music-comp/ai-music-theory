# Quick Definition
Major and minor triads are consonant because they span a perfect fifth; diminished and augmented triads are dissonant because they span a diminished or augmented fifth.

# Formal Definition
Triad consonance and dissonance are determined by the interval of the fifth between root and fifth. Major triads (M3 + m3 = P5) and minor triads (m3 + M3 = P5) are consonant because the perfect fifth is a consonant interval. Diminished triads (m3 + m3 = d5) are dissonant because the diminished fifth is a dissonant interval. Augmented triads (M3 + M3 = A5) are dissonant because the augmented fifth is a dissonant interval. Inversion also affects stability: root-position triads are most stable; first-inversion triads less so; second-inversion triads are considered dissonant due to the perfect fourth with the bass.

# Musical Context
The distinction between consonant and dissonant triads governs their usage in tonal music. Consonant triads (major and minor) serve as stable harmonic pillars. The diminished triad appears most commonly in first inversion (where the tritone does not involve the bass), and the augmented triad results from contrapuntal motion rather than serving as an independent harmony. Second-inversion triads of any quality are used only in restricted contexts.

# Common Confusions
Students are often surprised that second-inversion major and minor triads are considered dissonant, since the individual intervals (P4 and M/m6) may not seem harsh. The dissonance arises specifically from the P4 formed with the bass, which creates instability in the harmonic foundation.
