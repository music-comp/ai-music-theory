# Quick Definition
Motivic connections that link different sections or movements of a multi-part work, unifying the composition through transformed versions of shared motivic material.

# Formal Definition
Composers unify multisection and multimovement works through motivic connections, in which a motive migrates from one section or movement to another in a transformed state. For a motive to migrate successfully, it must be highly malleable, capable of fitting into contrasting musical environments while retaining recognizable features. Intersection connections link contrasting sections within a single movement. Intermovement connections link separate movements. These connections may involve transformed pitch content, rhythmic profiles, or both.

# Musical Context
Intermovement connections were common in the nineteenth century but also occur in Classical and Baroque music. They reveal deep structural relationships between movements that might appear unrelated on the surface, providing organic unity across large-scale works.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in Bb: ^6-^5 incomplete upper-neighbor motive at the opening migrates to the contrasting theme in F major, transformed with sixteenth-note figuration
- Chopin, Fantasie Impromptu, op. 66: ascending tonic-triad arpeggiation with turn figures in the fast opening section reappears as the slow lyrical melody in the contrasting Db major section
- Corelli, Trio Sonata in F major, op. 2, no. 7: dotted-note arpeggiating motive connects Allemande, Corrente, Gigue, and Prelude movements
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in Ab major, op. 110: first and last movement themes share identical pitch content despite contrasting rhythm and meter

# Common Confusions
- Intermovement connections may be concealed by dramatic changes in rhythm, tempo, and character
- Shared pitch-class content between movements may not be obvious without stripping away rhythmic and metric differences
- Not every recurring interval or contour constitutes a genuine intermovement connection; the relationship must be structurally significant
