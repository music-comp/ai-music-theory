# Quick Definition
A period in which the consequent phrase closes with an authentic cadence in a different key from the opening tonic, creating a modulation within the period structure.

# Formal Definition
In a progressive period, the antecedent phrase typically ends with a HC or IAC in the home key. The consequent phrase then closes with an authentic cadence in a new tonal area, such as the relative major (III) in minor keys, or the dominant (V) in major keys. This technique of moving to a new harmonic area is called modulation. Progressive periods can be parallel (PPP, common) or contrasting (CPP, common).

# Musical Context
Progressive periods are common in tonal music, especially in pieces that need to move to a new key area early in their formal structure. They frequently appear in the opening sections of binary forms and sonata expositions.

# Examples
- Schubert, "Standchen" from Schwanengesang, D. 957: first phrase ends with a cadence in the home key, second phrase closes with an AC in III (relative major)

# Common Confusions
- A progressive period is not the same as a tonicization within a phrase; it involves a genuine cadential arrival in a new key
- Progressive periods are distinct from continuous periods, which maintain one harmonic sweep but stay in the home key
