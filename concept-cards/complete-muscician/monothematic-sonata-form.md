# Quick Definition
A monothematic sonata form uses the same theme (often varied) in both the FTA and STA, defining the tonal areas by key rather than by contrasting themes.

# Formal Definition
In a monothematic sonata form, the opening theme from the FTA reappears in the STA, sometimes varied but recognizably the same. This technique, frequently used by Haydn, demonstrates that sonata form is defined by tonal structure rather than thematic contrast. The lack of differentiation between sections based on theme poses no problem for analysis as long as tonal areas are identified by key. Themes are still labeled by tonal area (1a for FTA, 2a for STA) even when they share the same melodic material.

# Musical Context
Monothematic sonata forms are especially associated with Haydn, who frequently used this technique. They highlight the primacy of tonal structure over thematic contrast in defining sonata form. The technique also demonstrates the compositional principle of unity through varied repetition.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in A major, op. 55, no. 1 (Example 33.3): The FTA theme reappears in the STA transposed to the dominant. Despite using the same theme, the two areas are clearly distinguished by key.

# Common Confusions
In a monothematic sonata, students may struggle to identify where the STA begins because the theme sounds the same. Focus on key: the STA begins when the new key is established. The transition's modulatory function becomes especially important as the primary signal of the shift from FTA to STA.
