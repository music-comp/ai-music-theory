# Quick Definition
Melodic fluency refers to the underlying scalar patterns that support the variety of melodic embellishments on the music's surface, creating coherent long-range stepwise lines.

# Formal Definition
Melodic fluency describes underlying stepwise pitch patterns that emerge when surface embellishments are stripped away. These subsurface step motions connect accented pitches across a phrase or passage, creating coherent descents, ascents, or archlike contours. Composers aurally "mark" these structural lines through various types of accent: duration, metric placement, dynamics, articulation, harmonic changes, and contour changes. These goal-oriented motions are especially powerful when accented musical events coordinate with hierarchical pitch structures such as the tonic triad.

# Musical Context
Most tonal melodies exhibit melodic fluency, whether they were written in 800 or 1990. Melodies typically rise from a stable initiating point (often ^1) supported by the tonic triad, ascend to a climax, and descend to repose. The structural stepwise lines may ascend, descend, or form arch shapes. Both "Clementine" and "God Save the King" share the same deep structure: first phrases arpeggiate the tonic triad F4-A4-C5, and second phrases bring C5 back to ^1 through stepwise descents.

# Examples
In Donizetti's Lucrezia Borgia, the melody slowly descends a fifth from B to E despite many ascending surface gestures. The structural pitches B-A-G#-F#-E are marked by duration, metric placement, and harmonic support. In Beethoven's Piano Trio op. 1/2, a slow structural ascent E-F#-G# spans four measures, embellished by various thirds.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes assume that melodic fluency means the surface melody moves by step. Rather, it refers to deeper-level stepwise connections between structurally important (accented) pitches, even when the surface melody contains many leaps. The structural line may unfold across several measures.
