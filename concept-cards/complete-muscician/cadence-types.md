# Quick Definition
Cadences are points of arrival at phrase endings. The two main types are authentic cadences (V-I) and half cadences (ending on V). Authentic cadences are further classified as perfect (PAC) or imperfect (IAC) based on soprano scale degree and bass motion.

# Formal Definition
A cadence is a point of arrival usually occurring at the end of a phrase, where melody, harmony, counterpoint, rhythm, and meter all participate. The two structural cadence types are: (1) Authentic cadences (AC), closing V-I in root position, subdivided into perfect authentic cadences (PAC), where the soprano moves ^2-^1 or ^7-^8 over bass ^5-^1, and imperfect authentic cadences (IAC), where the soprano closes on ^3 or ^5. A contrapuntal cadence is a special IAC with bass motion other than ^5-^1 (such as ^7-^1 or ^2-^1). (2) Half cadences (HC), closing on a root-position dominant, usually with ^2 in the soprano.

# Musical Context
Cadences define phrases and create the punctuation of musical discourse. PACs provide the strongest closure, IACs provide weaker closure, and HCs create an open, questioning effect that demands continuation. The half cadence creates the effect of a musical question, while the authentic cadence provides the answer.

# Examples
In Mozart's Rondo in C major K. 144, the first four-measure phrase closes on V (HC) with soprano arriving on ^2, and the second phrase closes on I (PAC) with soprano on ^1. In minor keys, ^7 must be raised to create a leading tone for authentic cadences. A Picardy third raises the third of the final tonic chord to major in minor-key pieces.

# Common Confusions
Students often confuse IAC and PAC. The critical distinction is the soprano: PAC requires ^2-^1 or ^7-^1 in the soprano with ^5-^1 in the bass. If the soprano ends on ^3 or ^5, it is an IAC regardless of the bass. Also, a half cadence is not defined by what comes before V -- only that the phrase ends on a root-position V.
