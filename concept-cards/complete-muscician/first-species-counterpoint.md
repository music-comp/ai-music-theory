# Quick Definition
First-species (1:1) counterpoint places one note in the contrapuntal voice against each note of the cantus firmus, using only consonant harmonic intervals and following strict rules of melodic motion and voice independence.

# Formal Definition
In first-species counterpoint, the contrapuntal voice adds one pitch for every pitch of the cantus firmus (note-against-note). Rules: (1) All harmonic intervals must be consonant (P1, P5, P8, M3, m3, M6, m6); the P4 is dissonant. (2) No parallel perfect intervals. (3) Approach perfect consonances by contrary motion (except when the upper voice steps to P5 or P8). (4) Begin on ^1 (or ^5 if the counterpoint is above the CF). (5) End on ^1 in both voices, approached stepwise by contrary motion (^2 and ^7 in the penultimate measure). (6) In minor, use lowered ^6 and ^7 within the exercise; raise ^7 only at the cadence. Guidelines: use predominantly step motion; favor contrary motion; use imperfect consonances over perfect consonances.

# Musical Context
First-species counterpoint isolates the fundamental relationship between melody and harmony. It teaches the essential voice-leading principles that govern all tonal music: the prohibition of parallel perfect intervals, the preference for contrary motion, and the cadential approach to ^1 by stepwise motion.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes forget that the P4 is dissonant in two-voice counterpoint and try to use it as a consonance. Also, the distinction between rules (absolute requirements) and guidelines (aesthetic suggestions) is important: breaking a rule is an error; not following a guideline results in less elegant but technically correct counterpoint.
