# Quick Definition
The recapitulation restates the exposition material with the crucial change that both the FTA and STA now appear in the tonic, resolving the tonal conflict established in the exposition.

# Formal Definition
The recapitulation repeats many events of the exposition but with crucial changes. Most importantly, both the FTA and STA material return in the tonic key, fulfilling the sonata principle. The transition is often altered since it no longer needs to modulate; it may be compressed or harmonically varied. Composers may further alter the recapitulation by compressing FTA material, introducing modal mixture, or reversing theme order. A coda may follow the recapitulation, confirming the tonic through cadential material, often incorporating FTA or STA themes over a pedal point. Codas frequently emphasize the subdominant for a large plagal motion.

# Musical Context
The recapitulation provides the large-scale resolution of the tonal conflict that drives the entire sonata form. It is not merely a repetition of the exposition but a recontextualized statement in which material heard in the contrasting key is now "corrected" to the tonic.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C major, Hob. XVI.35 (Example 33.9): The recapitulation begins with theme 1a an octave lower, then introduces modal mixture (parallel minor) before compressing the transition.
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 19, op. 49, no. 1 (Example 33.2): The recapitulation's transition is altered with more activity and quicker harmonic motion, and a coda follows using STA cadential material.

# Common Confusions
The recapitulation is not an exact repeat of the exposition. The transition is almost always altered (it must stay in the tonic rather than modulating), and other changes are common. A false recapitulation occurs when FTA material appears in the wrong key during the development; the real recapitulation restores the tonic.
