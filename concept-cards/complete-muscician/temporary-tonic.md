# Quick Definition
A diatonic chord that is momentarily treated as a tonic through the use of applied chords, while retaining its original function in the home key.

# Formal Definition
A temporary tonic is any major or minor diatonic harmony that is tonicized by one or more applied chords. During the tonicization, this chord briefly assumes the role of tonic in its own key area, with applied chords functioning as its dominants, pre-dominants, and other subordinate harmonies. Despite this temporary tonic status, the chord retains its function within the home key's phrase model. For example, ii tonicized by V/ii and vii-6/ii still functions as the pre-dominant in the home key. Any consonant (major or minor) diatonic triad can serve as a temporary tonic; diminished triads (vii in major, ii in minor) cannot be tonicized.

# Musical Context
Understanding temporary tonics is essential for analyzing and composing passages with applied chords and extended tonicizations. The concept explains how chromatic enrichment coexists with diatonic structure.

# Examples
- In C major, V/ii makes D minor a temporary tonic while D minor continues to function as ii (PD) in C major
- In G minor, V/III makes B-flat major a temporary tonic while it functions as III in G minor

# Common Confusions
- A temporary tonic is not a modulation; it does not establish a new key with cadential confirmation
- Diminished triads cannot serve as temporary tonics because they are dissonant
- The temporary tonic retains its home-key function even while being tonicized
