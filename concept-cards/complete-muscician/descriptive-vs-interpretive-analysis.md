# Quick Definition
Descriptive analysis labels each chord with a Roman numeral. Interpretive analysis goes deeper, determining which harmonies are structural and which are embellishing, revealing the underlying harmonic progression.

# Formal Definition
First-level (descriptive) analysis identifies each sonority with Roman numerals -- a necessary but insufficient step that merely describes the surface. Second-level (interpretive) analysis reflects the functions of those sonorities: some are structural (progressional, keeping their Roman numerals) while others are subordinate (prolongational, labeled by melodic function such as N, P, CS). Interpretive analysis is a creative, thought-provoking activity whose goal is to penetrate into the musical structure and reveal correspondences between the surface and the underlying structure. An analysis limited to Roman numerals is "mechanical and dull" and "doomed to fail."

# Musical Context
This distinction transforms harmonic analysis from a labeling exercise into a meaningful interpretive activity. It directly informs performance: a performer who understands which harmonies are structural will shape phrases differently from one who treats all chords equally.

# Examples
A passage labeled i-V6-i-viio6-i6-V-i at the descriptive level becomes, at the interpretive level: i (prolonged through neighbor and passing chords) leading to a structural V-i cadence. In Corelli's sonatas, the harmonies are byproducts of melodic figures transferred between instruments -- understanding this changes how one interprets and performs the music.

# Common Confusions
Many students believe that placing Roman numerals beneath every chord constitutes analysis. This is description, not analysis. True analysis requires interpretive decisions about harmonic hierarchy. These decisions are not arbitrary but are informed by meter, rhythm, voice leading, and the broader musical context.
