# Quick Definition
The plagal relation is the nineteenth-century structural weakening of dominant function, in which pre-dominant harmonies such as iv and ii°6/5 substitute for the dominant at cadences, resolving directly to the tonic.

# Formal Definition
The plagal relation describes the broader nineteenth-century tendency for the dominant to lose its cadential primacy. Through modal mixture, harmonies such as iv, ii°6/5, and bVI gained enough harmonic weight to substitute for the dominant at structural cadences, moving directly to the tonic. This weakened the dominant's role while paradoxically making it more important as a signal of where the tonic should be. The plagal relation contributed to tonal ambiguity: if pre-dominant harmonies can close a piece, the distinction between tonal functions becomes blurred. The plagal relation extends the concept of plagal motion (Chapter 27) to a large-scale structural phenomenon.

# Musical Context
The plagal relation is a hallmark of nineteenth-century harmonic practice. It reflects the general Romantic tendency to explore alternatives to conventional harmonic formulas, particularly the V7-I cadence. Composers such as Brahms, Wagner, and Mahler frequently ended phrases, sections, or entire movements with plagal rather than authentic cadences.

# Examples
- Chopin, Etude in C minor, op. 10, no. 12 (Example 34.1): The final section treats the tonic as V/iv, effectively replacing authentic cadential function with plagal relations.
- The nineteenth-century compression from 24 major and minor keys to 12 "major-minor" keys through free modal interchange facilitated the plagal relation by making minor-mode pre-dominants readily available in major keys.

# Common Confusions
The plagal relation is not merely a IV-I cadence. It describes a broader historical trend in which pre-dominant harmonies (especially chromatic ones from mixture) take on structural weight traditionally reserved for the dominant. The reciprocal process is a specific manifestation of the plagal relation.
