# Quick Definition
Three main categories of motivic repetition based on degree of change: strict (exact), modified (varied), and developmental (significantly transformed).

# Formal Definition
Strict repetition preserves both pitch and rhythm of the original motive. It is relatively rare because of its limitations, but works well at the opening of a piece when listeners must process many stimuli simultaneously. Modified repetition varies a motive's pitches and/or rhythms while maintaining a recognizable connection to the original. It is the most common and varied category. Developmental repetition involves significant transformation of pitch and/or rhythmic content, often proving the most important for a piece's unfolding; it includes interpolation, fragmentation, and hidden repetitions.

# Musical Context
Composers use these repetition types strategically. Strict repetition reduces cognitive load at the beginning. Modified repetition provides the balance between unity and variety that sustains musical interest. Developmental repetition allows motives to operate at different structural levels and to generate new material.

# Examples
- Loeillet, Sonata for Oboe in A minor: strict repetition of a two-measure motive
- Grieg, "Erotikon": modified repetition through embellishment -- added pitches leave contour unaffected
- Schubert, "Die Nebensonnen": reharmonization -- same pitches in a new harmonic context

# Common Confusions
- Strict repetition is much less common than modified repetition; most motivic returns involve some transformation
- Modified repetitions can usually be traced back to the original motive without difficulty
- Developmental repetitions may not be immediately audible but often have the most far-reaching structural consequences
