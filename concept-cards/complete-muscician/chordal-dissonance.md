# Quick Definition
Chordal dissonance occurs when a dissonant interval (such as a seventh) is a structural member of a chord, creating an active sonority that seeks resolution.

# Formal Definition
Chordal dissonance refers to dissonant intervals that are incorporated as members of a chord (as opposed to melodic dissonances like passing tones). Just as dissonant intervals resolve to consonant ones, dissonant chords are active sonorities that seek resolution and create tension in tonal music. The seventh in V7 is the primary chordal dissonance studied initially. The chordal seventh can be approached (prepared) in various ways -- by step is preferred, by common tone is acceptable, by leap is uncommon and should be avoided. However, it can only be left (resolved) by descending step.

# Musical Context
Chordal dissonance is a key expressive resource. The dominant seventh is the most common chordal dissonance, but seventh chords on other scale degrees also contain chordal dissonances. The preparation and resolution of chordal dissonance follow strict voice-leading protocols.

# Examples
In V7, the interval of the seventh (between root ^5 and seventh ^4) is a chordal dissonance. The figured bass notation 8-7 shows the voice leading when the octave above the bass moves to the dissonant seventh by step. In Schubert's "Wasserfluth," C#5 (octave above bass C#) descends to B4 (the dissonant seventh), which must then resolve down to A4.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes confuse chordal dissonance (a dissonant interval built into the chord structure) with melodic dissonance (a passing tone or neighbor that is not a chord member). Both require resolution, but chordal dissonance is part of the harmony itself. Also, while preparation of the seventh can vary, resolution must always be by descending step.
