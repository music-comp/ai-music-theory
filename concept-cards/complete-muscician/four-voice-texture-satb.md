# Quick Definition
The standard four-voice texture (soprano, alto, tenor, bass) provides the framework for tonal harmony, accommodating triads and seventh chords while allowing independent voice lines.

# Formal Definition
Tonal music of the common-practice era was generally conceived in four voices: soprano, alto, tenor, and bass (SATB). This framework underlies compositions of all sizes, from solo works (where it is implicit) to large orchestral scores. The four voices are notated in three formats: open/full score (each voice on its own staff), short score (SA on treble staff, TB on bass staff, with soprano/tenor stems up and alto/bass stems down), and keyboard style (SAT in treble, B in bass). Each voice has a specific range and tessitura (optimal singing range) roughly spanning a twelfth, with practical writing limited to about a sixth or seventh.

# Musical Context
The four-voice texture represents a balance between accommodating triadic/seventh-chord harmony and maintaining melodic independence of parts. It is the standard medium for chorale-style writing and harmonic exercises, though real compositions often vary the number of sounding voices.

# Examples
Bach chorales are the quintessential four-voice SATB texture. Beethoven's Symphony no. 9 slow movement presents a four-voice texture divided between orchestral choirs. The three upper voices should generally be kept within an octave of each other; the bass may be more than an octave from the tenor.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes assume every piece literally uses four voices at all times. The four-voice framework is an underlying model; actual compositions frequently have more or fewer voices, with the four-part voice leading implicit rather than explicit.
