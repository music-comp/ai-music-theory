# Quick Definition
Chromatic descending second sequences come in two principal forms: D2 (-P4/+m3), which descends by whole step through back-relating dominants tracing a whole-tone path, and D2 (-P5/+P4), which descends by whole step through exclusively perfect fifths.

# Formal Definition
Two chromatic D2 sequences exist: (1) The D2 (-P4/+m3) sequence, related to the diatonic D3 (-4/+2) "descending 5-6" pattern, descends by major second with all major triads. It carves out a whole-tone path, skipping over the dominant and creating tonal ambiguity. It contains back-relating dominants and never lands on the structural V chord. To restore tonal focus, the sequence must break off and lead to the pre-dominant. (2) The D2 (-P5/+P4) sequence uses exclusively perfect fifths and is more goal-directed because descending fifths move naturally toward tonal goals. A complete statement requires 12 chromatic steps. Composers avoid sequential stasis by using partial statements or three-chord models (reducing repetitions from twelve to four). A variant, D2 (-P5/+A4), descends by minor seconds using alternating perfect fifths and tritones.

# Musical Context
These sequences are central to the chromatic harmonic language of the nineteenth century. The D2 (-P4/+m3) sequence, with its whole-tone bass path, is particularly associated with tonal ambiguity and features prominently in the music of Schubert and Liszt.

# Common Confusions
Both D2 sequences descend by major second overall, but they differ in their internal motion: D2 (-P4/+m3) moves down a fourth then up a minor third; D2 (-P5/+P4) moves down a fifth then up a fourth. The first is ambiguous (back-relating dominants), while the second is more goal-directed (forward-moving fifths).
