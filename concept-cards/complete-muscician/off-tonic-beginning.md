# Quick Definition
An off-tonic beginning opens a piece or section on a harmony other than the tonic, postponing tonal stability to create ambiguity and heightened expectation.

# Formal Definition
In an off-tonic beginning, a piece or important subsection begins on a harmony other than the tonic, such as a pre-dominant (ii, IV) or an applied dominant. The tonic is established only after a period of harmonic wandering. Classical-period slow introductions approximate this by opening with a weakly stated tonic that is quickly displaced by chromaticism. In the nineteenth century, off-tonic beginnings became more explicit, with composers like Schumann, Brahms, Wolf, and Mahler beginning directly on non-tonic harmonies. The technique was also common in twentieth-century popular music.

# Musical Context
Off-tonic beginnings are a hallmark of nineteenth-century harmony, reflecting the general trend toward tonal ambiguity and delayed gratification. They are intimately connected to the plagal relation, since the growing emphasis on pre-dominant harmony made it natural to begin pieces on these harmonies rather than the tonic.

# Examples
- Brahms, "Parole," op. 7, no. 2 (Example 34.7): The song begins on what sounds like a C-major tonic but turns out to be a Ger6/5 in E minor; the true key is only revealed at the cadence in m. 9.
- Pinkard, "Sweet Georgia Brown" (Example 34.14): The G-major refrain begins with a series of applied dominants (E7-A7-D7) that only arrive at the tonic G in m. 33.

# Common Confusions
An off-tonic beginning is not an error in key identification. The piece genuinely starts away from the tonic. Determine the true key by examining cadences and the key in which the piece closes. Also, do not confuse an off-tonic beginning with a slow introduction; the former starts on a non-tonic harmony, while the latter may begin on the tonic but quickly move away from it.
