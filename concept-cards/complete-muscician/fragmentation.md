# Quick Definition
A developmental technique that isolates and develops only a portion of a motive, often the opening "head," frequently repeating fragments sequentially.

# Formal Definition
Fragmentation is a developmental motivic technique that develops only a portion of a motive, such as a neighbor gesture or a single interval. Often the opening or "head" of the motive is fragmented, given its prominent placement and memorability. Composers frequently repeat fragments sequentially, creating forward momentum from reduced motivic material. Fragmentation is a type of developmental repetition that significantly transforms the original motive.

# Musical Context
Fragmentation is a primary tool of motivic development, used extensively in development sections of sonata forms and in sequential passages. By reducing a motive to its essential kernel, composers can generate new material while maintaining motivic unity.

# Examples
- Bach, Invention in Bb major: the opening thirty-second-note figure is fragmented in m. 4, stated in both original and inverted forms, then sequenced down by step

# Common Confusions
- Fragmentation uses only part of the motive; embellishment adds to the whole motive -- these are opposite processes
- The "head" of a motive is most commonly fragmented because it is the most memorable portion
- Fragments are often treated sequentially to maintain forward motion
