# Quick Definition
Augmentation proportionally increases the duration of motivic notes; diminution proportionally decreases them, allowing a motive to appear at different temporal scales.

# Formal Definition
Augmentation is a proportional increase in the overall duration of motivic repetitions (e.g., doubling all note values). Diminution is a proportional decrease (e.g., halving all note values). Both transformations can occur simultaneously in different voices, and they may be combined with other transformations such as inversion, transposition, and stretto, creating what amounts to "motivic saturation." Augmentation and diminution are particularly effective because they maintain the motive's intervallic and contour profile while dramatically changing its temporal character.

# Musical Context
These rhythmic transformations are common in contrapuntal works (fugues, inventions) and in developmental passages. Augmentation slows a motive to create a broader, more structural version; diminution accelerates it for increased urgency. Simultaneous use of both creates rich textural relationships.

# Examples
- Mahler, Symphony no. 5, Adagietto: main motive in violin is augmented by factor of 2 in cello
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in Ab major, op. 110, fugue: perfect fourth motive subjected to simultaneous augmentation and diminution

# Common Confusions
- Augmentation and diminution are proportional -- all note values change by the same ratio
- A motive in augmentation retains its identity despite sounding very different rhythmically
- Simultaneous augmentation in one voice and diminution in another creates motivic nesting
