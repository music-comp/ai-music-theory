# Quick Definition
The major scale is a seven-note diatonic scale with a specific pattern of whole and half steps (W-W-H-W-W-W-H) that creates the bright, stable sound characteristic of the major mode.

# Formal Definition
The major scale is constructed from a fixed sequence of intervals ascending from the tonic: whole step, whole step, half step, whole step, whole step, whole step, half step. Half steps occur between ^3 and ^4 and between ^7 and ^1. The major scale contains three primary triads (I, IV, V) that are all major, contributing to the mode's characteristic brightness. The major scale serves as the reference point for identifying all interval qualities.

# Musical Context
The major mode dominates Western tonal music and is used in the majority of common-practice compositions. It serves as the default reference scale for interval identification: intervals measured from ^1 upward in the major scale are either perfect (P1, P4, P5, P8) or major (M2, M3, M6, M7).

# Examples
- The C major scale (C-D-E-F-G-A-B-C) contains no sharps or flats and serves as the prototype for all major scales.
- D major scale (D-E-F#-G-A-B-C#-D) has two sharps (Example 1.15).
- Each major key generates a unique key signature based on the circle of fifths (Example 1.18).

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes confuse the major scale pattern with that of other modes. The major scale is equivalent to the Ionian mode. The critical distinction from minor is the placement of half steps: in major, half steps fall between ^3-^4 and ^7-^1; in natural minor, between ^2-^3 and ^5-^6.
