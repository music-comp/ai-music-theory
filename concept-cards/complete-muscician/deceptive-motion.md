# Quick Definition
The progression V-vi (or V-VI in minor), where the listener expects tonic but hears the submediant instead, creating harmonic surprise and dramatic suspense.

# Formal Definition
Deceptive motion (also called evaded cadence or deceptive cadence) occurs when V moves to vi instead of the expected I, with the bass moving from ^5 to ^6 by ascending second. In major keys, ^7 in V may either descend to ^6 or ascend to ^1 (resulting in a doubled third in vi). In minor keys, ^7 must ascend to ^1 (since falling to ^6 would create an augmented second), always resulting in a doubled third in VI. After a deceptive motion, the phrase model typically "rewinds" to the pre-dominant or tonic, followed by an authentic cadence.

# Musical Context
Deceptive motions create dramatic suspense and extend phrases by thwarting expected closure. They are called "deceptive motions" rather than "deceptive cadences" because they do not constitute actual cadences -- vi is not an independent arrival point but a member of the underlying progression leading back to PD and D.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in D minor ("Quinten"), op. 76, no. 2: dramatic deceptive motion intensified by a fermata and florid ascent to a high register
- Mozart, Trio in Eb major, K. 498: deceptive motion in m. 12 followed by return to tonic and varied repetition leading to PAC

# Common Confusions
- Deceptive motion is not a true cadence; it prolongs the phrase and leads back to PD-D-T
- In minor, ^7 in V must ascend to ^1 in VI to avoid an augmented second; this always doubles the third
- V-vi early in a phrase (not at a cadential moment) is a voice-leading motion, not a deceptive cadence
