# Quick Definition
The triad built on ^3 (iii in major, III in minor), the least common diatonic harmony but structurally significant in minor keys, providing color contrast and serving in arpeggiations and descending-fifth progressions.

# Formal Definition
The mediant harmony (iii in major, III in minor) occurs less frequently than any other diatonic harmony within the phrase, yet in minor-key pieces its structural significance is great -- second only to tonic and dominant. Like the submediant, it provides color contrast: minor in major keys, major in minor keys. Within phrases, the mediant usually appears in ascending-bass arpeggiations (^1-^3-^5, where iii substitutes for I6) and descending-fifth progressions (iii-vi-ii-V-I). Voice leading guidelines: move upper voices in contrary motion to the bass; use soprano ^1-^7-^6 when iii supports passing tone ^7.

# Musical Context
The mediant divides the fifth from ^1 to ^5 in the bass, creating a tonic extension. In minor, III is often preceded by V/III (an applied dominant), which is the diatonic major triad built on raised ^7. The mediant completes the diatonic spectrum of harmonies available for phrase construction.

# Common Confusions
- iii and I6 share the same bass note (^3) but have different roots and functions; iii is a root-position chord, I6 is a tonic inversion
- In minor, III is major and often preceded by its own dominant (V/III); this is an applied chord, not a modulation
- iii is the least common diatonic harmony; do not overuse it in analysis or composition
