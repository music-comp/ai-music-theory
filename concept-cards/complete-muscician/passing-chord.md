# Quick Definition
A passing chord is created by harmonizing a bass passing tone, connecting two positions of the same harmony (e.g., viio6 passes between I and I6 by harmonizing bass ^2).

# Formal Definition
A passing chord arises when a bass passing tone between two chord members is harmonized with consonant upper voices. The most common passing chord is viio6, which harmonizes bass ^2 passing between ^1 (I) and ^3 (I6). Just as with neighboring chords, passing chords fall on metrically weak beats and are subordinate to the structural harmonies they connect. Passing chords can also expand the dominant: IV6 can pass between V and V6 (harmonizing bass ^6 between ^5 and ^7).

# Musical Context
Passing chords create smooth stepwise bass lines between root-position and first-inversion forms of the same harmony. They are one of the three primary means of contrapuntal expansion and generate linear bass motion that adds melodic interest while maintaining harmonic coherence.

# Examples
In Handel's Flute Sonata in B minor (Example 10.10), viio6 harmonizes the bass passing tone C# (^2) connecting I (^1) and I6 (^3), with a voice exchange between bass and flute. In Example 10.15, IV6 passes between V and V6, expanding dominant harmony.

# Common Confusions
Students may wonder why viio6 is preferred over ii for harmonizing bass ^2. The viio6 is ideal as a passing chord because: (1) it is unstable (diminished sonority), (2) it shares three notes with V and V7 (making it a "dominant substitute"), and (3) it is in first inversion, making it naturally suited to a contrapuntal role. The ii chord is too strong and draws attention away from the tonic being prolonged.
