# Quick Definition
A major triad or dominant seventh chord that functions as the dominant of a diatonic chord other than tonic, creating a brief tonicization of that chord.

# Formal Definition
An applied dominant chord (also called a secondary dominant chord) must be a major triad (V) or a dominant seventh chord (V7) and must resolve to its temporary tonic. Any major or minor diatonic harmony can be preceded by its applied dominant. The applied dominant is labeled V/X or V7/X, where X is the target chord (e.g., V/V, V7/ii). Applied dominants that are already diatonic major triads (e.g., I functioning as V/IV in major) typically require the added seventh to clarify their applied function. Applied dominants and their target chords share the same harmonic function in the second-level analysis, meaning they do not change the underlying T-PD-D-T progression.

# Musical Context
Applied dominants are among the most common chromatic chords in tonal music. They enrich harmonic progressions by creating momentary tonicizations that intensify motion toward diatonic goals. The dominant and pre-dominant are the most commonly tonicized harmonies.

# Examples
- In C major: V/V = D major (resolves to G major); V7/ii = A7 (resolves to D minor); V/vi = E major (resolves to A minor)
- V/IV in major is diatonic (it is the I chord), so the seventh must be added (V7/IV) to clarify its applied function
- In minor: V/III is diatonic (built on ^7), requiring the seventh for clarity

# Common Confusions
- IV in major cannot be altered to create V/vii because vii is diminished (cannot be tonicized)
- Similarly, VI in minor cannot create V/ii because ii is diminished in minor
- Applied dominants are embellishing chords and do not change the underlying diatonic harmonic function
