# Quick Definition
A D2 (-5/+4) sequence enriched by adding diatonic seventh chords, either to every other chord (alternating) or to every chord (interlocking).

# Formal Definition
Seventh chords may be added to the D2 (-5/+4) sequence in two ways. In alternating sevenths, every other chord receives a seventh, creating a pattern of triads alternating with seventh chords. In interlocking sevenths, every chord contains a seventh, with two voices sharing the seventh preparation and resolution in an interlocking pattern: as one voice resolves its seventh, another prepares the next. The critical voice-leading rule is that each chordal seventh must be prepared by common tone and resolved down by step. With interlocking sevenths, every other chord will be incomplete (omitted fifth, doubled root). Four different seventh-chord qualities appear in the diatonic sequence (mm, MM, dm, Mm).

# Musical Context
Adding sevenths to sequences significantly enriches the harmonic color and intensifies the goal-directed motion. This technique is common from the Baroque period onward.

# Examples
- Francois Couperin, "Les Nations," Chaconne: D2 sequence with diatonic seventh chords on every chord except tonic
- In alternating sevenths: each resolution pitch simultaneously serves as the next preparation pitch
- In interlocking sevenths: soprano and alto have interlocking seventh patterns above the bass

# Common Confusions
- Alternating seventh chords may be complete; interlocking seventh chords require every other chord to be incomplete
- Each seventh must be prepared by common tone and resolved down by step regardless of type
- Third-inversion (4/2) seventh chords in sequences resolve to first-inversion triads or seventh chords
