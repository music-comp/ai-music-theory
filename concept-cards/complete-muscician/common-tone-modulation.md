# Quick Definition
A common-tone modulation connects two keys by sustaining one or more pitches shared between the final chord of the old key and the first chord of the new key, often without a traditional pivot chord.

# Formal Definition
Common-tone modulation is a technique in which the connection between two key areas is achieved through shared pitch classes rather than through a chord functioning simultaneously in both keys. The common tone is typically sustained in one voice while other voices move chromatically to the new harmony. This technique is especially useful for modulations involving chromatic third relations (e.g., I to bVI or I to III), where the two triads share one common tone but no traditional pivot chord is available.

# Musical Context
Common-tone modulations became increasingly important in the nineteenth century as composers sought smooth connections to chromatically remote keys. The technique is characteristic of Schubert, Brahms, and Wagner, who used sustained tones to create seamless transitions between distant tonal areas. It is particularly effective when combined with semitonal voice leading.

# Examples
- In a modulation from C major to Ab major, the pitch C is common to both triads (C-E-G and Ab-C-Eb) and can be sustained while other voices shift chromatically.
- Schubert frequently uses common tones to connect keys a third apart, sustaining the shared pitch while other voices slide by half step.

# Common Confusions
A common-tone modulation is distinct from a pivot-chord modulation. In a pivot-chord modulation, an entire chord has dual function in both keys. In a common-tone modulation, only a single pitch (or pitches) serves as the link; there need not be a chord that functions in both keys simultaneously.
