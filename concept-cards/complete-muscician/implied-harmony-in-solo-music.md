# Quick Definition
Solo instrumental music consisting of a single melodic line often unfolds within a strong implied harmonic context, allowing listeners to distinguish chord tones from tones of figuration even without explicit accompaniment.

# Formal Definition
Even single-line melodies in solo instrumental music often project an implied harmonic framework. Listeners can deduce the underlying harmony from arpeggiations, chordal leaps, patterns of consonance and dissonance, and the rate of implied harmonic change (harmonic rhythm). The principles of harmonic analysis -- distinguishing structural chord tones from embellishing tones of figuration -- apply to unaccompanied melodies just as they do to fully harmonized textures. Good continuation (maintaining an established harmonic rhythm) and metric accent provide standards for determining where implied harmonies change.

# Musical Context
Solo works by Bach (violin partitas, cello suites), Telemann, and other Baroque composers frequently rely on implied harmony. The performer must understand the underlying harmonic structure to shape the phrase correctly, even though only one voice is sounding.

# Examples
In Bach's Presto in G minor (Example 7.6), a single violin line implies specific harmonies: G minor for mm. 1-5, V7 in mm. 6-7, tonic return in m. 8, with the harmonic rhythm accelerating. In Bach's Solo Violin Sonata no. 3 in C major (Example 7.19), two harmonic changes per measure are implied, with a chromatic rising line (C-C#-D-D#-E) traceable across multiple measures.

# Common Confusions
Students may assume that solo unaccompanied music has no harmonic structure to analyze. In fact, strong harmonic implications are usually present, and the analytical method is the same as for harmonized textures: identify controlling harmonies, determine the harmonic rhythm, and distinguish structural from embellishing pitches.
