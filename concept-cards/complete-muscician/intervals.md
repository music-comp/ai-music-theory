# Quick Definition
An interval is the distance between two pitches, measured by generic size (number of letter names spanned) and specific quality (the precise number of half steps).

# Formal Definition
An interval is the distance between two pitches in a dyad. Intervals are named in two parts: the generic name (a number derived from the count of letter names spanned, inclusive of both endpoints) and the specific quality (perfect, major, minor, augmented, or diminished, determined by the exact number of half steps). Generic intervals are identified by counting staff positions. Specific quality is determined by whether the upper pitch belongs to the major or minor scale of the lower pitch.

# Musical Context
Intervals are the basic building blocks of tonal music. They form the substance of both melody (successive intervals) and harmony (simultaneous intervals). Understanding intervals is prerequisite to understanding chords, counterpoint, and voice leading.

# Examples
- G4 up to E5 spans six letter names (G-A-B-C-D-E), so it is a generic sixth (Example 3.2A).
- Accidentals do not change generic size: G to E, G to Eb, G# to E# are all sixths.
- A single beat of Handel's Concerto Grosso in C minor (Example 3.1) contains six intervals between different voices.

# Common Confusions
Students often forget to count both the starting and ending pitch when determining generic size. The interval from C to E is a third (C-D-E = 3 letter names), not a second. Also, accidentals affect quality but never generic size.
