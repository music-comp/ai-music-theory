# Quick Definition
A triad is inverted when a chord member other than the root appears in the bass: first inversion has the third in the bass (figured bass: 6 or 6/3); second inversion has the fifth in the bass (figured bass: 6/4).

# Formal Definition
Root position occurs when the root is the lowest-sounding pitch (figured bass: 5/3, usually omitted). First inversion places the third in the bass (figured bass: 6/3, abbreviated to 6). Second inversion places the fifth in the bass (figured bass: 6/4, always written in full). The distribution of upper voices does not affect the inversion; only the bass pitch determines position. Root-position major and minor triads are stable (P5 with the bass); first-inversion triads are less stable (sixths with the bass); second-inversion triads are considered dissonant because the P4 with the bass drives the harmony. Second-inversion triads occur only in restricted contexts.

# Musical Context
Root position and first inversion are common and generally interchangeable. Second-inversion triads are special and require specific contrapuntal justification (cadential, passing, or pedal contexts, covered in Chapter 14). First-inversion diminished triads are actually more stable than their root-position form because the tritone does not involve the bass.

# Common Confusions
The most critical distinction is between root and bass. The root is always the pitch on which the triad is built (determined by stacking in thirds). The bass is the lowest-sounding pitch. In root position they coincide; in inversions they differ. To find the root of an inverted triad, rearrange the pitches into thirds.
