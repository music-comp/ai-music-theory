# Quick Definition
Minuet-trio form is a large ternary structure in which a minuet (A) is followed by a contrasting trio (B) of lighter texture, after which a da capo marking indicates the minuet is to be repeated.

# Formal Definition
The minuet, a Baroque dance type, remained popular in the Classical period as a standard inner movement of symphonies and chamber works. It is followed without pause by a trio (a companion piece of lighter texture), after which the minuet returns via da capo. The result is a large ternary form. As a compound ternary, both the minuet and trio typically contain nested binary forms (often rounded binary). The scherzo, developed by Haydn and Beethoven, is a more spirited transformation of the minuet that follows the same formal structure.

# Musical Context
The minuet-trio is one of the most standardized formal types in Classical music, appearing as the third movement of symphonies, string quartets, and sonatas. The trio typically contrasts with the minuet in key (often the parallel major or subdominant), texture, dynamics, and character, while sometimes maintaining subtle motivic connections.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 1 in F minor, op. 2, no. 1 (Example 31.9): A full sectional ternary form. Both minuet and trio are rounded continuous binary forms. The trio is in the parallel major (F major) and uses invertible counterpoint.
- Haydn, String Quartet in C major, "Emperor," op. 76, no. 3 (Example 31.12): The trio in A minor contrasts with the C-major minuet, but a hidden motivic connection links the two sections through a restatement of the cello figure.

# Common Confusions
The term "trio" does not mean the section is scored for three instruments. It originates from the Baroque practice of scoring the middle section for three instruments, but in the Classical period the full ensemble typically plays both sections.
