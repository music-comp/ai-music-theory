# Quick Definition
The five principal seventh chord types are: major seventh (MM7), dominant/major-minor seventh (Mm7), minor seventh (mm7), half-diminished seventh (dm7), and fully diminished seventh (dd7), each with a distinct sound and expressive character.

# Formal Definition
The five types are classified by triad quality and seventh quality: (1) MM7 (major seventh): major triad + major seventh -- strident, more dissonant than Mm7, common in jazz. (2) Mm7 (major-minor/dominant seventh): major triad + minor seventh -- the most common in common-practice music. (3) mm7 (minor seventh): minor triad + minor seventh -- soft, less dissonant. (4) dm7 (half-diminished): diminished triad + minor seventh -- mixed effect, associated with anguish in Romantic music. (5) dd7 (fully diminished): diminished triad + diminished seventh -- the most dissonant, containing three minor thirds and two diminished fifths, common in late Baroque through Romantic periods.

# Musical Context
Each seventh chord type has a distinctive expressive character. The Mm7 dominates common-practice harmony. The MM7 and mm7 are more characteristic of twentieth-century popular music and jazz. The dm7 is associated with emotional intensity in Romantic vocal music. The dd7 is dramatic and tension-filled, associated in silent film scores with danger.

# Examples
- Example 6.2: all five types built on C (C-E-G-B for MM7; C-E-G-Bb for Mm7; C-Eb-G-Bb for mm7; C-Eb-Gb-Bb for dm7; C-Eb-Gb-Bbb for dd7).
- The dd7 is unique in being built entirely of minor thirds and diminished fifths, making it symmetrical.

# Common Confusions
The nomenclature is often confusing. "Major seventh" (MM7) and "major-minor seventh" (Mm7) are easily conflated. Remember: the first word describes the triad, the second describes the seventh. A "half-diminished" seventh has a diminished triad but only a minor (not diminished) seventh -- hence "half" diminished. A "fully diminished" seventh has both a diminished triad and a diminished seventh.
