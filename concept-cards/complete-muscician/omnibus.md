# Quick Definition
The omnibus is an extended chromatic contrary-motion progression that prolongs a single harmony (typically the dominant) through an entire octave, with root-position dominant seventh chords appearing every third sonority, partitioning the octave into minor thirds.

# Formal Definition
The omnibus is an extension of chromatic contrary motion that traverses an entire octave (ascending or descending) while prolonging a single underlying harmony. Different root-position dominant seventh chords appear on every third sonority, sometimes spelled enharmonically as Ger6/5 chords. At each dominant seventh chord, an enharmonic respelling allows the contrary motion to continue with a new pair of voices. The bass ascent partitions the octave into minor thirds. First described by Viennese music theorists around 1800, the omnibus was used throughout the nineteenth century by Beethoven, Schubert, Chopin, and Liszt. In practice, composers generally used only portions of the complete omnibus.

# Musical Context
The omnibus represents one of the most extended applications of symmetrical harmony within tonal music. It demonstrates how chromatic contrary motion can completely traverse the octave while maintaining a single harmonic function. The technique shows how symmetrical divisions of the octave (minor thirds) can be embedded within a tonal context.

# Common Confusions
The omnibus is not a sequence, even though it involves regular patterns. It is a prolongation of a single harmony through chromatic contrary motion. The dominant seventh chords that appear within the omnibus are all part of the same prolongation, not independent harmonic events.
