# Quick Definition
The German Lied (pl. Lieder) is a genre of art song for voice and piano prominent in the nineteenth century, in which modal mixture and chromatic harmony serve as musical analogues to project poetic meaning.

# Formal Definition
The Lied became an important laboratory for harmonic experimentation in nineteenth-century Germany and Austria. Composers developed musically expressive techniques capable of communicating the emotionally rich poetry of the time. Modal mixture, particularly the juxtaposition of raised and lowered forms of scale degrees (especially ^6 and ^3), became central to these innovations. The conflict between diatonic and mixture tones almost always signals emotional tensions, contradictions, or dramatic turns in the poetic text. Composers including Schubert, Schumann, Brahms, and Wolf used chromatic harmony to create musical analogues that highlight, clarify, or expand on a text's meaning.

# Musical Context
The Lied tradition flourished from the early nineteenth century, with Schubert's over 600 songs establishing the genre. Song cycles such as Schubert's Die schöne Müllerin and Winterreise, and Schumann's Dichterliebe and Liederkreis demonstrate how modal mixture and chromatic modulation serve dramatic purposes at both local and structural levels. The piano accompaniment is not merely supportive but participates actively in projecting the poetry's meaning.

# Examples
- Schubert, "Lachen und Weinen" (Example 28.12): The raised and lowered forms of ^6 represent the contrasting emotions of joy and pain. b^6 underscores "weeping" while diatonic ^6 accompanies "laughter."
- Schumann, "Waldesgesprach" (Example 28.14): The chromatic modulation to bVI (C major from E major) characterizes the Lorelei's supernatural otherness, while the modal shift to E minor at the moment of recognition reflects terror.

# Common Confusions
The Lied is not simply any German song. It refers specifically to the art-song tradition of the nineteenth century, characterized by sophisticated text-music relationships, through-composed or modified strophic forms, and elaborate piano parts that are integral to the musical narrative.
