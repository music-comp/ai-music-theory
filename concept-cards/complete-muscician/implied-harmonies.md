# Quick Definition
Harmonies that are not fully voiced but can be inferred from compound melodies and voice-leading context, requiring the analyst to deduce missing chord members.

# Formal Definition
In works using compound melody, harmonies are sometimes incomplete even after all voice-leading strands are identified. Missing chord members must then be inferred from context. Knowledge of typical harmonic progressions, contrapuntal expansions, and voice-leading patterns guides these inferences. For example, a chord containing ^2, ^4, and ^5 in a dominant context implies a missing ^7 (the third of V).

# Musical Context
Implied harmonies are essential for analyzing works written for one or two voices without explicit chordal accompaniment, including much Baroque solo instrumental music. Understanding implied harmonies is crucial for performers, who must project the underlying harmonic structure through their interpretation of dynamics, color, and rubato.

# Examples
- Schubert, Piano Sonata no. 16 in A minor, D. 845, Rondo: three-voice texture with many incomplete chords; the key (A minor) and the tonic-expansion context help identify harmonies such as V4/3 with a missing third (G#)
- Bach, Partita for Solo Violin in B minor, BWV 1002: solo violin implies complete harmonic progressions through compound melody, with strict voice leading between harmonies

# Common Confusions
- Do not force a roman numeral onto every vertical sonority; many are incomplete and must be inferred from context
- Use knowledge of typical progressions (T-PD-D-T, common contrapuntal expansions) to guide inferences
- The harmonic rhythm of implied harmonies is usually slower than the surface rhythm of the compound melody
