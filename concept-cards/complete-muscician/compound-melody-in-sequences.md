# Quick Definition
A single melodic line within a sequence that gives the impression of two or more independent voices, creating implied seventh-chord sequences.

# Formal Definition
Compound melody in sequential passages occurs when a single melodic line splits into two registrally distinct voices, creating an implied multi-voice texture. This technique can produce implied seventh-chord sequences: when the two implied voices combine, they create alternating triads and sixth-five chords (or other seventh-chord patterns). The technique requires three conditions: (1) the two implied voices occupy separate registers, (2) each voice maintains independence through stepwise motion, and (3) the voices combine to make good counterpoint.

# Musical Context
Compound melody is common in solo instrumental works featuring sequences, particularly in Baroque sonatas for violin, flute, or cello with continuo. It allows a single melody instrument to imply a rich harmonic texture.

# Examples
- Handel, Sonata for Flute and Continuo in A minor, op. 1, no. 4, Adagio: treble voice splits into two implied voices, creating a D2 (-5/+4) sequence with alternating triads and 6/5 chords

# Common Confusions
- The implied voices are not actually present as separate parts; they emerge from the registral separation within a single line
- Students must be able to reduce compound melodies to their implied voice-leading patterns
