# Quick Definition
Second-species (2:1) counterpoint places two notes in the contrapuntal voice against each note of the cantus firmus, introducing weak-beat dissonance through passing tones while maintaining consonant strong beats.

# Formal Definition
In second-species counterpoint, the contrapuntal voice writes two half notes against each whole note of the CF, creating oblique motion and a metric hierarchy of strong and weak beats. Rules: (1) Strong beats must be consonant. (2) No parallel perfect intervals on successive strong beats or from weak to strong beat. (3) No direct motion to perfect intervals from weak to strong beats. (4) The only permitted dissonance is the unaccented passing tone on the weak beat, filling the space of a melodic third by step. (5) Begin on ^1 (or ^1, ^3, ^5 if above CF), optionally with a half rest. (6) In minor, raise ^7 only at the cadence. Guidelines: incorporate many passing tones; balance with consonant skips; place leaps within rather than between measures.

# Musical Context
Second-species counterpoint introduces the crucial concept of controlled dissonance. The unaccented passing tone is the first dissonant element students encounter, and its strict regulation (approached and left by step in the same direction) establishes the principle that dissonance in tonal music must be carefully prepared and resolved.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes think the weak-beat note between two downbeats "hides" parallel perfect intervals. It does not -- parallel P5s or P8s on successive downbeats are audible despite the intervening note (Example 4.14). Also, the 5-6 technique using consonant notes is not strong enough to hide downbeat fifths, but 6-5 motion is permissible since the ear hears parallel sixths on downbeats.
