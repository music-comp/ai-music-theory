# Quick Definition
An imperfect authentic cadence (IAC) is a V-I cadence where the soprano closes on ^3 or ^5 instead of ^1, creating weaker closure than a PAC.

# Formal Definition
An imperfect authentic cadence (IAC) occurs when V resolves to I in root position but the soprano closes on ^5 or ^3 rather than ^1. Any bass motion other than ^5-^1 (such as ^7-^1 or ^2-^1) also creates a special type of IAC called a contrapuntal cadence. Contrapuntal cadences are harmonically weaker than the standard IAC because they lack the strong bass leap of a fifth or fourth.

# Musical Context
IACs provide a sense of arrival that is less conclusive than a PAC, often used at the ends of interior phrases to maintain forward momentum. They can function as intermediate resting points within larger formal structures.

# Examples
When the soprano closes on ^5 or ^3 over a V-I bass motion, the result is an IAC. A contrapuntal cadence occurs when the bass moves ^7-^1 (stepwise) or ^2-^1 instead of the stronger ^5-^1 leap. In Beethoven's "Tempest" Sonata, the IAC in m. 8 arrives with the soprano on F5 (^3) rather than ^1.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes confuse an IAC with a PAC by focusing only on the bass motion (V-I) without checking the soprano. The soprano scale degree is the critical distinguishing factor. Also, a contrapuntal cadence is a subtype of IAC, not a separate cadence category.
