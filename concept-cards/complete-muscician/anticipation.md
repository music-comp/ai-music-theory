# Quick Definition
An unaccented nonchord tone that arrives prematurely, sounding a member of an upcoming chord before that chord actually sounds.

# Formal Definition
The anticipation (ANT) is an unaccented nonchord tone that appears before the chord to which it belongs, usually creating a dissonance with the currently sounding chord. It functions as the mirror opposite of the suspension: while a suspension is an accented tone that delays a chord tone's resolution, an anticipation is an unaccented tone that prematurely introduces a chord tone. Anticipations are most effective at cadences, when the final chord is strongly expected.

# Musical Context
Anticipations occur most frequently at cadential moments where the resolution chord is highly expected. Double anticipations (two voices anticipating simultaneously) intensify the cadential effect. They can appear in conjunction with other dissonance types such as suspensions.

# Examples
- Corelli, Violin Sonata no. 11 in D minor, op. 1: double anticipation at a phrygian cadence (iv6-V), where C# and A (members of V) enter prematurely while iv6 is still sounding

# Common Confusions
- Anticipations are unaccented (weak beat); suspensions are accented (strong beat) -- they are functional opposites
- An anticipation is not the same as an early attack of the next chord; it is a single voice entering prematurely while other voices sustain the current chord
- Do not confuse with an upbeat figure; anticipations create a brief dissonance against the current harmony
