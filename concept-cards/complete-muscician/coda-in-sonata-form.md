# Quick Definition
A coda is optional material following the recapitulation that confirms the tonic key, often incorporating earlier themes over a pedal point and frequently emphasizing the subdominant for a large plagal motion.

# Formal Definition
In sonata form, a coda occurs after the recapitulation and serves to confirm the closing key. It may incorporate material from the FTA or STA, often stated over a tonic pedal point that creates a strong cadential feeling. Codas frequently emphasize the subdominant, providing a large plagal motion that extends the tonic. A codetta is a shorter version that may appear at the end of the exposition (before the repeat signs), where it reinforces the contrasting key rather than the tonic. Both coda and codetta are optional, as the names imply ("tail" or "appendage"). In rondo form, the coda follows the final refrain and confirms the home key.

# Musical Context
Codas range from brief cadential confirmations to extended sections that constitute a significant portion of the movement. Beethoven, in particular, expanded the coda into a "second development" in some works. The coda provides a sense of finality beyond what the recapitulation alone achieves.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 19, op. 49, no. 1 (Example 33.2): The coda uses cadential material from the STA after m. 97.
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C major, Hob. XVI.35 (Example 33.9): A dramatic diminished seventh chord in m. 141 heralds an extended coda that closes the piece.

# Common Confusions
A coda is not the same as a closing section. The closing section is part of the exposition (reinforcing the contrasting key) and recapitulation (reinforcing the tonic). The coda comes after the recapitulation's closing section and provides additional confirmation. Also, a codetta at the end of the exposition is different from a coda at the end of the movement.
