# Quick Definition
Enharmonic equivalence is the principle that two differently named pitches can sound identical, such as C# and Db, because they share the same frequency.

# Formal Definition
Enharmonic pitches are pitches that sound the same but are spelled differently. In equal temperament, C# and Db, F# and Gb, and other such pairs are acoustically identical. The choice of spelling depends entirely on the musical context -- the key, the harmonic function, and the voice-leading implications. Enharmonic equivalence extends to intervals (e.g., an augmented second and a minor third sound the same) and to keys (e.g., F# major and Gb major).

# Musical Context
Enharmonic spelling is not arbitrary; it reflects a pitch's function within a tonal context. A composer writes C# when the pitch functions as a leading tone in D, but Db when it serves as a member of the Db major scale. Enharmonic reinterpretation is also a compositional technique used for modulation between distant keys.

# Examples
- C# and Db are enharmonic equivalents, as are E# and F, and Cb and B.
- The keys of F# major (6 sharps) and Gb major (6 flats) are enharmonic keys.
- Double sharps and double flats create additional enharmonic equivalences: C double-sharp is enharmonic with D.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes think enharmonic pitches are "the same note." While they sound identical, they function differently within tonal contexts. Spelling matters because it conveys harmonic meaning: G# in A major is ^7, while Ab in Bb major is ^7. Same sound, different function.
