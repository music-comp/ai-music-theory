# Quick Definition
Syncopation occurs when a musical accent falls on a metrically weak beat or weak part of a beat, creating tension against the established meter.

# Formal Definition
Syncopation is the displacement of the expected metric accent, placing emphasis on beats or parts of beats that are normally unaccented. While the underlying meter continues, a conflicting accent challenges it through durational accents (long notes on weak beats), dynamic accents (sforzandi on weak beats), or other accentual means. Syncopation creates rhythmic tension and energy without destroying the sense of meter.

# Musical Context
Syncopation is pervasive in popular music, jazz, and common-practice classical music. It adds vitality and rhythmic interest by creating tension between the expected metric accent and the actual surface accent. Syncopation is particularly effective when a stable, regular accompaniment provides the metric grid against which the syncopated line plays.

# Examples
- Mozart, Symphony in G minor, K. 183 (Example 2.36A): recurring accents off the beat in the string parts.
- Mozart, Piano Concerto in D minor, K. 466 (Example 2.36B): syncopated melodic line.
- Arlen, "It's Only a Paper Moon" (Example 2.37): vocal line syncopates against a regular left-hand waltz pattern, with "Say" entering on a weak part of the beat.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes confuse syncopation with hemiola. Syncopation displaces individual accents within the prevailing meter; hemiola temporarily imposes a different meter (usually duple over triple). Syncopation works against the meter; hemiola replaces it briefly.
