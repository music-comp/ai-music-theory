# Quick Definition
Harmonic rhythm is the rate at which harmonies change in a piece, ranging from rapid chord changes (one or more per beat) to slow changes (one chord lasting several measures).

# Formal Definition
Harmonic rhythm refers to the duration of each harmony and the pattern of harmonic change over time. It is represented in analysis by note values beneath the roman numerals that correspond to the duration of each chord. Harmonic rhythm may be fast (chords changing every beat or faster), moderate (one chord per measure), or slow (a single chord spanning multiple measures). Harmonic rhythm is closely tied to meter: chord changes typically create accents that should align with metric accents, moving from weak to strong beats. Syncopated harmonic rhythm (changing on a strong beat and sustaining through a weak beat) should generally be avoided.

# Musical Context
Harmonic rhythm is a powerful compositional tool. Slow harmonic rhythm creates stability and spaciousness; fast harmonic rhythm creates urgency and drive. Composers often accelerate harmonic rhythm near cadences to create a sense of arrival. The harmonic rhythm may be quite different from the rhythmic activity on the surface.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 545 (Example 6.8C): only seven chords in four measures, creating slow harmonic rhythm despite rapid Alberti bass figuration.
- Schubert, Impromptu in Gb (Example 6.8D): one chord per measure.
- Chopin, Etude in Ab (Example 6.8E): a single A-major triad for both measures.
- In Beethoven's Fifth Symphony finale, chords change every quarter note from m. 2 to m. 4 (fast harmonic rhythm).

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes confuse harmonic rhythm with surface rhythm. A passage with fast sixteenth notes may have slow harmonic rhythm if the same chord persists. Conversely, sustained whole notes may have fast harmonic rhythm if other voices change chords frequently. Harmonic rhythm measures how often the harmony changes, not how fast the notes move.
