# Quick Definition
Semitonal voice leading connects distantly related chords by moving two or more voices by half step while usually sustaining one common tone, creating smooth connections that suspend tonal function.

# Formal Definition
Semitonal voice leading is a nineteenth-century technique that transforms one chord chromatically into the next by moving individual voices by half step. It typically maintains at least one common tone while other voices shift by semitone. The resulting chord-to-chord connections sound smooth due to minimal voice movement, yet tonal stability evaporates because the motion bypasses functional harmonic progressions. The technique creates harmonic successions (rather than functional progressions) in which there is no clear sense of tonic, pre-dominant, or dominant.

# Musical Context
Semitonal voice leading is a key technique for creating tonal ambiguity in the nineteenth century. It appears prominently in slow introductions (Mozart's "Dissonant" Quartet, Beethoven's Fourth Symphony), in modulatory passages, and in through-composed songs where tonal wandering reflects poetic imagery.

# Examples
- Mozart, String Quartet in C major, K. 465, Adagio (Example 34.4): The sinuous motion of all voices through semitones clouds the tonic for the entire slow introduction, with cross relations and displaced pitches preventing tonal clarity.
- Example 34.5A: A harmonic succession of major-third-related chords (C-Ab minor-E-C) connected by consistent half-step motion, creating smooth voice leading but no sense of tonal function.

# Common Confusions
Semitonal voice leading is different from simple chromatic passing tones. It is a structural technique where half-step motion governs chord-to-chord connections at the harmonic level, not merely the melodic surface. The resulting chords are independent harmonies, not embellishments of an underlying diatonic progression.
