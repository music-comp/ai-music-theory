# Quick Definition
A cadence in which either the dominant or the tonic (or both) are inverted, producing a weaker close than a standard structural cadence.

# Formal Definition
Contrapuntal cadences close phrases using inverted dominant and/or tonic harmonies, rather than the root-position chords required for structural cadences. Composers use them when they want to save the powerful root-position V until a more dramatic and final-sounding cadence is required. Contrapuntal cadences often involve the same harmonies used in EPMs (e.g., I-IV6-V6/5-I) and can include vii-diminished-6 substituting for V in the authentic motion.

# Musical Context
Contrapuntal cadences create phrase endings that are perceptibly weaker than standard authentic or half cadences. They are useful for phrases early in a movement where the composer wants to maintain forward momentum rather than creating a strong point of arrival.

# Examples
- Schubert, "Die Krahe," from Winterreise: four-measure phrase closes with vii-diminished-6 to i, a very weak contrapuntal cadence; this weak close can be debated -- the passage might be heard as a tonic expansion rather than a phrase with a cadence

# Common Confusions
- A contrapuntal cadence is a real cadence (ending a phrase), not just an EPM; the distinction depends on context
- Whether a weak close constitutes a phrase ending or merely a tonic expansion is often a matter of interpretation
- Contrapuntal cadences still follow the T-PD-D-T model but with weaker chord positions
