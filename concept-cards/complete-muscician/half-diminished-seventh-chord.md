# Quick Definition
The half-diminished seventh chord (viio7) is the leading-tone seventh chord in major keys. Less dissonant than the fully diminished viio7, it occurs less frequently and primarily in root position.

# Formal Definition
The leading-tone seventh chord in major is a half-diminished seventh chord (e.g., B-D-F-A in C major). The minor seventh between ^7 and ^6 is much less dissonant than the diminished seventh found in the fully diminished seventh chord. Because of its less goal-oriented tendencies, viio7 occurs much less often than its minor-mode counterpart. It appears most often in root position, expanding tonic as a neighbor chord. Composers often import the fully diminished seventh from minor into major-mode works by borrowing b6 from the parallel minor (a technique called modal mixture, explored in Chapter 27).

# Musical Context
The half-diminished seventh is the default leading-tone seventh in major keys but is used sparingly due to its weaker drive toward resolution compared to the fully diminished seventh. When composers want a more intense leading-tone seventh in major, they borrow b6 from minor to create the fully diminished version.

# Examples
In Mozart's Symphony K. 97 Trio (Example 11.14), viio7 expands tonic as part of a neighboring process: the line D-E-D is transposed up a third to harmonize with viio7 because no V7 inversion contains ^6. Mozart uses viio7 specifically because it provides the needed ^6. Note the unusual rising seventh in the root-position V7 in m. 28, which passes up to D in m. 29.

# Common Confusions
Students often confuse viio7 (half-diminished, major keys) with viio7 (fully diminished, minor keys). The half-diminished has a minor seventh on top; the fully diminished has a diminished seventh. The half-diminished is less dissonant and less common. Follow the same voice-leading rules as for viio7.
