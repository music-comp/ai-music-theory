# Quick Definition
A slow introduction precedes the exposition in some sonata-form movements, typically beginning on a weakly established tonic and moving through chromatic harmonies to close on a dominant that prepares the allegro.

# Formal Definition
Some sonata-form movements, particularly in symphonies, include slow introductions that explore foreign harmonic territory, chromatic key areas, and modal mixture before arriving at a dominant that functions as a hugely extended upbeat resolving to the tonic "downbeat" at the allegro FTA. Slow introductions usually begin on the tonic (though not well established) and eventually close on a half cadence. The dominant is often extended with its added seventh, hovering in anticipation of tonic resolution. The tonal wandering of the slow introduction creates ambiguity and dramatic tension.

# Musical Context
Slow introductions are particularly common in large orchestral works such as symphonies and overtures. They provide a dramatic opening that establishes the work's serious character and creates anticipation for the allegro exposition.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Symphony no. 1 in C major (Example 33.4): The 12-measure Adagio introduction begins with a V7/IV that moves deceptively, thwarting tonal expectations multiple times before finally resolving V7 to tonic at m. 13.
- Beethoven, Symphony no. 4 in Bb major, op. 60 (Example 34.6): An extended introduction uses b^6 to displace ^5, creating 39 measures of tonal ambiguity before Bb major is established.

# Common Confusions
A slow introduction is part of the sonata-form movement but is not part of the exposition. The exposition begins with the allegro FTA. Also, not every slow first movement has a slow introduction; the term refers specifically to a contrasting tempo section that precedes the exposition.
