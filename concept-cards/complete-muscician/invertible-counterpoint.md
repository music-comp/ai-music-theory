# Quick Definition
A compositional technique in which two melodic voices swap registers -- what was on top moves to the bottom and vice versa -- while retaining their original contour.

# Formal Definition
Invertible counterpoint (also called double counterpoint) is a compositional procedure in which melodic material is swapped between voices: the soprano melody becomes the bass and vice versa. The most common type is invertible counterpoint at the octave, where the rearrangement is accomplished solely by octave leaps in one or both voices. This technique is economical (yielding twice as much music from a single idea) and unifying (exposing the listener to material in different registral contexts). When intervals are inverted at the octave, they maintain their consonant or dissonant character, except that a fifth becomes a fourth (dissonant). Therefore, composers must treat all perfect fifths as potentially dissonant when writing invertible counterpoint.

# Musical Context
Invertible counterpoint appears in all genres from piano sonatas to symphonies. It can be literal (obvious registral exchange) or subtle (buried within homophonic textures where it governs the structural bass-soprano counterpoint). The technique was a central activity of the "counterpoint club" formed by Brahms, Clara Schumann, and Joseph Joachim.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in G major, K. 283: outer-voice melodies swap places between two sections
- Haydn, Symphony no. 91 in F major: invertible counterpoint in the developmental section
- Brahms, Intermezzo in A minor, op. 76, no. 7: invertible counterpoint extending C major harmony

# Common Confusions
- Perfect fifths must be treated carefully in invertible counterpoint because they become dissonant fourths when inverted
- Invertible counterpoint is not limited to literal, obvious swaps; it can operate at deeper structural levels beneath a homophonic surface
- Inversion at the twelfth is also possible but less common; at the octave is the standard type
