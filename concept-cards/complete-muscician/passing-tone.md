# Quick Definition
A passing tone is a nonchord tone that fills the interval of a third between two chord tones by stepwise motion, creating a more fluid melodic line.

# Formal Definition
A passing tone (abbreviated P or PT) is a tone of figuration that connects two consonant pitches a third apart by moving stepwise in a single direction. Passing tones are more dynamic than neighbor tones because they move to a new pitch rather than returning to the original pitch. They may be dissonant (not a member of the underlying chord) or consonant (a member of the chord). Passing tones typically occur on unaccented beats or beat subdivisions, though accented and supermetrical passing tones also exist.

# Musical Context
Passing tones are among the most common melodic embellishments in tonal music. They appear in all voices and textures, from simple melodies to complex polyphonic scores. They can be harmonized (rendered consonant by a supporting chord) or left as bare dissonances.

# Examples
In a descending C-major scale over a sustained C bass, B (^7) passes between C (^8) and A (^6), and F (^4) passes between G (^5) and E (^3). In Vivaldi's Sonata in C minor, the oboe's D on beat 3 of m. 1 is a passing tone filling the third between Eb5 and C5.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes confuse passing tones with neighbor tones. The key distinction: passing tones connect two different pitches a third apart by stepwise motion in one direction, while neighbor tones depart from and return to the same pitch. Also, passing tones can be consonant -- they need not always be dissonant.
