# Quick Definition
A harmonic progression in which roots descend by thirds (I-vi-IV), with the submediant acting as a bridge connecting tonic to pre-dominant through what is called a harmonic arpeggiation.

# Formal Definition
In the descending-thirds progression I-vi-IV, the submediant provides a way station (bridge) connecting tonic to the pre-dominant. The three roots ^1-^6-^4 arpeggiate a triad, hence the name harmonic arpeggiation. This progression is also called a descending harmonic arpeggiation. Voice leading is smooth: I and vi share two common tones (^1 and ^3); vi and IV share two common tones (^6 and ^1). Keep common tones and move any moving voice up by step.

# Musical Context
The I-vi-IV progression characterizes much 1950s and early 1960s rock and roll music, though it appears throughout the common-practice period. In common-practice voice leading, parallel perfect intervals are avoided; in rock-style voice leading, they are freely used.

# Examples
- Classic 1950s rock and roll chord progression: I-vi-IV-V
- Schubert, "Fruhlingstraum": vi-ii6 variant combining descending-fifths motion with melodic bass arpeggiation

# Common Confusions
- The descending-thirds progression is distinct from descending-fifths progressions, though vi participates in both
- In the descending-thirds progression, vi bridges T and PD; it does not function as PD itself
- The bass motion ^1-^6-^4 is a melodic arpeggiation, but the roots of the chords trace the harmonic arpeggiation
