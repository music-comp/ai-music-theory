# Quick Definition
The sensation of briefly experiencing a key other than the tonic, accomplished through applied dominant chords that make a diatonic harmony sound momentarily like a tonic.

# Formal Definition
Tonicization is the process by which a non-tonic diatonic harmony is momentarily treated as a tonic through the use of applied chords. It stretches out a diatonic harmony by introducing chromatic chords that function as dominants of that harmony. The essential feature is that the tonicized harmony still participates in the underlying structural harmonic progression (T-PD-D-T). Tonicizations are fleeting, occurring within phrases, and do not disrupt the feeling of the home key. They lack strong cadences in the temporary key. Extended tonicizations employ multiple applied chords to expand a single diatonic harmony over several beats or measures.

# Musical Context
Tonicization is one of the most important chromatic techniques in tonal music, providing harmonic color and intensifying motion toward structural harmonies. It is distinguished from modulation by its brevity and lack of cadential confirmation in the new key.

# Examples
- In D minor: an A-major chord (V/ii) followed by the ii chord creates a brief tonicization of ii
- Extended tonicization: using V7/ii, vii-6/ii, and vii-7/ii to expand ii over nearly two measures while ii retains its pre-dominant function

# Common Confusions
- Tonicization is not the same as modulation; tonicizations are brief and do not establish a new key with cadential confirmation
- The tonicized chord retains its original function in the home key (e.g., ii remains pre-dominant even when tonicized)
