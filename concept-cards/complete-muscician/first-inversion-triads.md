# Quick Definition
First-inversion triads (six-three chords) place the chordal third in the bass. Less stable than root position, they create forward motion and make the bass more melodic.

# Formal Definition
A first-inversion triad places the chordal third in the bass, creating a six-three (6/3) sonority. First-inversion triads are less stable than root-position triads, which gives them the instability needed to push music forward. They render the bass line more melodic by replacing large leaps (such as the fifth between I and V) with smaller, stepwise motions. While root-position and first-inversion triads can often be interchanged, second-inversion triads cannot substitute freely because their dissonant fourth above the bass creates a fundamentally different function. First-inversion chords are best used within phrases rather than at beginnings or endings, where root-position stability is desired.

# Musical Context
First-inversion triads are the primary tool for contrapuntal expansion. I6 extends tonic, V6 extends dominant (as a neighbor), and viio6 serves as a passing chord. The intervals created between bass and soprano in first inversion are predominantly imperfect consonances (thirds, sixths, tenths), which provide fluid motion compared to the perfect consonances of root position.

# Examples
In Brahms's "Vom verwundeten Knaben," the use of I6 and V6 (alongside root-position chords) transforms a static harmonization into a buoyant, directed phrase. The bass creates a melodic arch, and the downbeat intervals become active sixths and tenths rather than static octaves and fifths. Schubert's Impromptu in Bb major opens with a I-I6 expansion via parallel tenths.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes treat first-inversion chords as independent harmonies equal in weight to root-position chords. In most cases, first-inversion chords are subordinate, serving to extend a root-position harmony through contrapuntal motion. Also, second-inversion triads are NOT interchangeable with first-inversion or root-position triads -- they function very differently.
