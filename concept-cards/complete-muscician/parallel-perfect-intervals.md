# Quick Definition
Parallel perfect intervals (parallel unisons, fifths, and octaves) occur when two voices move to the same type of perfect interval by parallel motion, and they are strictly forbidden in tonal counterpoint because they destroy voice independence.

# Formal Definition
Parallel perfect intervals occur when two voices move from one perfect consonance to another of the same generic size in the same direction (e.g., P5 to P5, P8 to P8, or P1 to P1). These are forbidden in all species of counterpoint and in tonal voice leading. Parallel octaves create the effect of one voice dropping out of the texture, and parallel fifths produce a hollow, medieval sound inappropriate to common-practice style. Parallel motion is permitted only with imperfect consonances (thirds and sixths).

# Musical Context
The prohibition against parallel perfect intervals is one of the most fundamental rules of tonal voice leading, dating back centuries. It applies not only to species counterpoint but to all four-voice writing in the common-practice period. Parallel fifths and octaves between any pair of voices must be avoided.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes think that inserting a note between two parallel perfect intervals "hides" them. This is not the case -- in second-species counterpoint, the metric accent of successive downbeats is too strong to hide perfect intervals, regardless of what occurs on the weak beat (Example 4.14). Also, note that two different perfect intervals in succession (e.g., P5 to P8) are not "parallel" -- they are just two consecutive perfect consonances, which is a separate guideline.
