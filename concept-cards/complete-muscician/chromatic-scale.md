# Quick Definition
The chromatic scale includes all twelve pitch classes, each separated by a half step, encompassing every available pitch within an octave in the Western tuning system.

# Formal Definition
The chromatic scale divides the octave into twelve equal half steps. Unlike diatonic scales, which select seven pitches from this twelve-note collection, the chromatic scale includes all available pitches. Chromatic pitches are those that fall outside the prevailing diatonic scale; the term "chromatic" (from Greek "chroma," meaning color) refers to pitches that add color or tension to a diatonic context. Half steps come in two varieties: diatonic half steps (between adjacent letter names, e.g., E-F) and chromatic half steps (between the same letter name, e.g., F-F#).

# Musical Context
The chromatic scale provides the complete pitch vocabulary from which diatonic scales are drawn. Chromatic pitches are used for embellishment, intensification, and modulation. The distinction between diatonic and chromatic half steps matters for correct enharmonic spelling.

# Examples
- An ascending chromatic scale from C: C-C#-D-D#-E-F-F#-G-G#-A-A#-B-C.
- Diatonic half step: E to F (different letter names).
- Chromatic half step: F to F# (same letter name).

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes confuse "chromatic" (meaning outside the prevailing diatonic scale) with "the chromatic scale" (all twelve pitches). A chromatic passing tone in C major might be F#, which is chromatic relative to C major but diatonic in G major. Chromaticism is always context-dependent.
