# Quick Definition
A chromatic pivot-chord modulation uses a chord that functions diatonically in one key and chromatically (through mixture) in the new key, or vice versa, to connect two chromatically related keys.

# Formal Definition
In a chromatic pivot-chord modulation, the pivot chord has a diatonic function in one key and a chromatic function (typically a mixture chord) in the other key. For instance, when modulating from C major to Ab major (bVI), the chord Ab major could function as bVI in C major and as I in Ab major. The pivot is analyzed with dual roman numerals showing its function in both keys. This technique requires at least one of the chord's functions to involve modal mixture, distinguishing it from diatonic pivot-chord modulations where both functions are diatonic.

# Musical Context
Chromatic pivot-chord modulations are the primary means of accessing chromatically related key areas in nineteenth-century music. They provide a smooth, logical connection between distant keys that would be impossible to reach through diatonic pivot chords alone. The technique is essential for modulations to bVI, bIII, VI, and III.

# Examples
- Schubert, Impromptu in Ab, D. 935 (Example 28.9): The shift from Db major to Db minor serves as a pivot linking Db major to its bVI (A major/Bbb major). The parallel minor is diatonic to both the original key and the new key area.
- Schubert, "Lachen und Weinen" (Example 28.12): Modal mixture facilitates the tonal shift that projects the emotional contrast between laughter and tears.

# Common Confusions
Not every chromatic chord can serve as a chromatic pivot. The pivot must have a clear functional interpretation in both keys. Students should verify that the chord makes harmonic sense as part of a progression in each key, not just that it contains the right pitches.
