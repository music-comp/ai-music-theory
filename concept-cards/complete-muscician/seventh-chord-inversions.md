# Quick Definition
Seventh chords have three inversions beyond root position, each identified by figured bass numbers that steadily decrease: 7 (root position), 6/5 (first inversion), 4/3 (second inversion), and 4/2 or just 2 (third inversion).

# Formal Definition
Seventh chords can appear in root position or three inversions: Root position (7): root in bass. First inversion (6/5): third in bass. Second inversion (4/3): fifth in bass. Third inversion (4/2 or 2): seventh in bass. The figured bass numbers decrease systematically: 7-6-5-4-3-2. To identify the root of an inverted seventh chord, look for the seventh (the defining interval) or its inversion, the second. The lower note of the seventh or the upper note of the second is the root. Inverted seventh chords almost always use complete harmonies with no doubled pitches.

# Musical Context
Inverted seventh chords are common in tonal music and provide smoother bass lines than root-position seventh chords. Each inversion creates a different bass note, allowing the composer to choose the most melodic bass line. Third inversion (4/2) is particularly important because the seventh appears in the bass, creating a strong downward pull.

# Common Confusions
Students often struggle to memorize the figured bass labels for seventh chord inversions. The mnemonic: the numbers decrease from root position through the inversions (7, 6/5, 4/3, 4/2). Also, finding the root of an inverted seventh chord is easier than it seems: look for the interval of a second between two adjacent chord members; the upper note of that second is always the root.
