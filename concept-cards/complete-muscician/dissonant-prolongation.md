# Quick Definition
A dissonant prolongation extends a dissonant harmony (most often V7) through contrary-motion chromatic voice leading, rather than the consonant triads typically prolonged in earlier tonal practice.

# Formal Definition
Dissonant prolongation extends a dissonant harmony through contrary motion in the outer voices while inner voices remain relatively stationary. This technique can span a third (e.g., V6/5 expanding to V4/3) or a tritone (e.g., V7 expanding to V6/5). The extension of dissonant rather than consonant harmonies represents a significant expansion of contrapuntal prolongation techniques beyond traditional consonant voice exchanges and passing chords. Depending on which portion of the contrary-motion pattern is used, different structural harmonies (tonic, dominant, or pre-dominant) can be prolonged.

# Musical Context
Dissonant prolongation is a characteristic technique of nineteenth-century harmony, reflecting the increasing acceptance of dissonance as a structural rather than merely ornamental element. It bridges the gap between traditional consonant prolongation and the more radical harmonic techniques of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

# Common Confusions
Students accustomed to consonant prolongation may not recognize that a dissonant chord is being extended. Look for stationary inner voices as a clue that an underlying harmony is being prolonged despite surface chromaticism.
