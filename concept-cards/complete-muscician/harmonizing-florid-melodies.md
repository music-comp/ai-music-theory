# Quick Definition
Harmonizing florid melodies involves choosing appropriate harmonies (typically one per measure) by analyzing accented pitches, cadence locations, and harmonic rhythm to accompany folk songs and other tunes.

# Formal Definition
To harmonize a florid melody: (1) Determine the key. (2) Sing or play the tune. (3) Locate cadences and phrase endings. (4) Determine the harmonic rhythm -- how often chords change. (5) Identify controlling harmonies by examining accented pitches: ^1-^3-^5 imply I, while ^5-^7-^2 and often ^4 imply V(7). Arpeggiations and chordal skips imply a single harmony. Most tones of figuration occur on weak beats. A pattern of cadences (HC, PAC) helps confirm phrase structure. The harmonic rhythm may be one chord per measure or even slower.

# Musical Context
Folk songs and many other melodies employ just a few basic harmonies (I, V, V7) with slow harmonic rhythm. This harmonization method applies broadly to any melody that needs harmonic support, from folk tunes to orchestral themes.

# Examples
"Simple Gifts" in F major has four-measure phrases with cadences in mm. 4 (HC), 8 (PAC), 12 (HC), and 16 (PAC). Harmonic rhythm is one chord per measure or slower. Accented pitches F and A in m. 1 imply tonic. In Haydn's String Quartet op. 76/6 Trio, the melody strongly implies harmonies through chordal leaps, but Haydn surprises by treating a metrically accented pitch as a dissonant figuration.

# Common Confusions
Students often try to change harmonies too frequently, harmonizing every note. In florid melodies, many notes are tones of figuration within a single harmony. Focus on accented, metrically prominent pitches to determine the controlling harmony for each measure or beat group.
