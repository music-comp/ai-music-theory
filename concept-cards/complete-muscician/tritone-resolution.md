# Quick Definition
The tritone (diminished fifth or augmented fourth) between ^7 and ^4 in the V7 chord resolves by contrary motion: the diminished fifth contracts to a third, and the augmented fourth expands to a sixth.

# Formal Definition
In the V7 chord, ^7 (the chordal third) and ^4 (the chordal seventh) form a tritone -- either a diminished fifth or an augmented fourth, depending on voicing. This tritone resolves to I by contrary motion: the diminished fifth contracts to a third (^7 ascends to ^1, ^4 descends to ^3), and the augmented fourth expands to a sixth (^4 descends to ^3, ^7 ascends to ^1). This contrary-motion resolution is a fundamental voice-leading principle for dominant-to-tonic progressions.

# Musical Context
The tritone is the most tension-producing interval in the V7 chord and its resolution is the primary engine of tonal motion. Understanding tritone resolution is essential for correctly part-writing V7-I progressions in all voicings and inversions.

# Examples
In C major, the V7 chord (G-B-D-F) contains the tritone B-F (diminished fifth). B resolves up to C while F resolves down to E, contracting from a d5 to a third (C-E). If the tritone is voiced as F-B (augmented fourth), F resolves down to E and B up to C, expanding from an A4 to a sixth (E-C).

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes resolve both tritone members in the same direction, creating parallel motion to a dissonant interval. The tritone must always resolve by contrary motion. Also, the resolution is consistent regardless of inversion -- ^7 always rises (in outer voices) and ^4 always falls.
