# Quick Definition
A common-tone diminished seventh chord (c.t.°7) is a contrapuntal diminished seventh chord that shares a common tone with the harmony it prolongs, functioning as a neighbor or passing chord rather than as an applied dominant.

# Formal Definition
The common-tone diminished seventh chord (c.t.°7) arises from contrapuntal motion (neighboring or passing) and sustains the root of the harmony it extends as a common tone. This distinguishes it from the tonicizing function of vii°7 or applied vii°7, which share no common tones with their chord of resolution. Two types of diminished seventh chords exist: (1) Tonicizing (vii°7 or applied vii°7): no common tones with the resolution chord. (2) Contrapuntal (c.t.°7 or applied c.t.°7): shares a common tone with the resolution chord. The c.t.°7 typically extends either tonic or dominant harmony.

# Musical Context
Common-tone diminished seventh chords are a hallmark of nineteenth-century harmonic style, providing chromatic coloring within prolongations of structural harmonies. They appear in the music of Schubert, Brahms, and many Romantic composers as a means of enriching the harmonic palette without disrupting tonal function.

# Examples
- Schubert, Waltz in D major, D. 779, no. 12 (Example 35.16): Lower neighbors and a 5-6 motion create a diminished seventh chord over a sustained D (the root of the tonic), prolonging the tonic through neighboring motion.
- Brahms, "Heimweh III," op. 64, no. 9 (Example 35.18): A diminished seventh chord connects I and V4/3 as a chromatic passing chord, with E sustained as the common tone of the dominant.

# Common Confusions
Beware of the progression vii°7/V to cadential 6/4: the vii°7 shares common tones with the cadential 6/4, but these are suspensions that resolve to the 5/3, which shares no pitches with the vii°7. This is a tonicizing chord, not a common-tone chord. The true diagnostic is whether common tones exist with the chord of resolution (not with intervening passing chords).
