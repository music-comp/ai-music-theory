# Quick Definition
A pattern of chords in which a two-chord model is transposed and repeated at successive pitch levels, creating seamless, goal-directed musical motion within a phrase.

# Formal Definition
A harmonic sequence consists of a model (a basic contrapuntal/harmonic pattern, usually two chords) and its copies (which replicate the model at different pitch levels). Sequences typically have two or three copies. They are classified as descending or ascending based on the overall direction of transposition, and labeled according to three criteria: (1) the root distance from the first chord of the model to the first chord of the copy (e.g., D2, D3, A2), (2) the root motion between the two chords within the model (e.g., -5/+4), and (3) whether inversions are used (e.g., +6/3s). Sequences form goal-directed musical units that prolong harmonic functions or transition between them.

# Musical Context
Sequences first appeared during the Renaissance (c. 1450-1600) and played a much larger role in Baroque music (c. 1600-1750) and later periods, including popular and commercial music today. They paradoxically create an impression of intense motion through rapid harmonic rhythm while actually slowing harmonic progress through repetition and predictability.

# Examples
- Vivaldi, Concerto Grosso in C minor: D2 (-5/+4) sequence interpolated between tonic and pre-dominant to prolong tonic function
- The complete diatonic circle-of-fifths pattern: I-IV-vii-iii-vi-ii-V-I

# Common Confusions
- Within sequences, normally forbidden voice-leading situations (parallel fifths, doubled leading tones, diminished triads in root position) are acceptable because of the overall repetitive pattern
- Sequences are not independent harmonic progressions; they are subordinate to the phrase model's harmonic functions
- Only the first and last chords of a sequence receive roman numeral analysis
