# Quick Definition
A single melodic line that implies two, three, or even four voices through registral leaps, allowing a solo instrument to project harmonies and counterpoint.

# Formal Definition
A compound melody occurs when a single melodic line implies multiple voices through registral leaps that split the line into separate voice strands delineated by register. Each implied voice follows standard voice-leading rules to connect harmonies. The Alberti bass is a common type of compound melody in Classical-period music. When performing such music, understanding the implied harmonic structure allows the performer to control and shape musical energy more effectively.

# Musical Context
Compound melody is especially prominent in music for solo instruments (violin, cello) and in keyboard accompaniment figures. Bach's solo violin and cello works are among the finest examples, where a single instrument projects complete harmonic progressions through compound melody. The technique reveals that harmonies unfold temporally rather than spatially.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D major, K. 284: Alberti bass in left hand unfolds three-voice harmony temporally
- Bach, Partita for Solo Violin in B minor, BWV 1002, Corrente: solo violin plays arpeggiated pattern implying full chord progressions with voice leading between harmonies
- Handel, Suite no. 3 in D minor, HWV 428: right-hand compound melody implies two voices moving primarily in parallel sixths

# Common Confusions
- Do not analyze each note of a compound melody as a separate chord; group notes by register to find the implied voices
- The implied voices must follow standard voice-leading rules from harmony to harmony
- Compound melodies can include nonchord tones within the arpeggiated patterns; these must be identified to determine the true harmonies
