# Quick Definition
The sonata principle states that material presented in the contrasting key during the exposition must return in the tonic key during the recapitulation.

# Formal Definition
The sonata principle is the fundamental tonal requirement of sonata form, derived from balanced binary form. Material from the STA, originally presented in the contrasting key (V in major, III in minor) at the end of the exposition, must return in the tonic at the end of the recapitulation. This creates the large-scale tonal resolution that gives sonata form its dramatic arc. The principle applies to all material presented in the contrasting key, including the closing section and codetta, not just the STA theme.

# Musical Context
The sonata principle is what transforms a simple ABA form into a dynamic process. It creates the expectation that tonal "wrongs" will be "righted" and that material heard away from home will eventually return home. This principle remained operative even as nineteenth-century composers expanded the range of contrasting keys beyond V and III.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 19 (Example 33.2): The STA theme (2a), originally in Bb major (III), returns in G minor (i) in the recapitulation, fulfilling the sonata principle.
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C major, Hob. XVI.35 (Example 33.9): The STA and closing section, originally in G major (V), return in C major (I) in the recapitulation.

# Common Confusions
The sonata principle requires tonal resolution, not thematic identity. Even if themes are reordered, compressed, or varied in the recapitulation, the principle is fulfilled as long as material from the contrasting key returns in the tonic. Conversely, a piece that presents STA material in the tonic in the recapitulation fulfills the principle even if the theme has been significantly altered.
