# Quick Definition
Tonal music is paradoxically built on asymmetrical structures (major/minor triads, unequal interval patterns) that create goal-directed motion, while the rise of symmetrical structures in the nineteenth century undermined this directionality and contributed to the dissolution of tonality.

# Formal Definition
The tonal system is predicated on asymmetry: major and minor triads contain unequal thirds, root motions divide the octave unequally (I-V vs. V-I), and diatonic sequences contain a tritone that breaks the pattern of perfect fifths and creates goal-directed motion. Without this asymmetry, sequences would cycle through all 12 chromatic steps before returning to their starting point, and tonal gravity would be lost. Symmetrical structures (diminished seventh chords, augmented triads, whole-tone scales) produce the opposite effect: equal intervals create tonal ambiguity because every component is as stable (or unstable) as every other. The historical trajectory from the eighteenth to the late nineteenth century shows a gradual shift from asymmetrical to symmetrical harmonic thinking.

# Musical Context
The tension between asymmetry and symmetry is the central dynamic of the transition from tonal to post-tonal music. Eighteenth-century tonal paths used asymmetrical fifths. Late-eighteenth/early-nineteenth-century paths incorporated mixture but maintained asymmetry. Late-nineteenth-century paths increasingly used symmetrical divisions that bypassed both dominant and subdominant.

# Common Confusions
Symmetry is often assumed to be a positive quality in music (and it is, in formal structures). But in the harmonic domain, symmetry undermines the very asymmetry that makes tonality work. Understanding this paradox is key to understanding the dissolution of tonality in the late nineteenth century.
