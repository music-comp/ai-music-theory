# Quick Definition
Tones of figuration are grouped into two categories based on rhythmic placement: unaccented (metrically weak, lighter) and accented (metrically stressed, more dramatic and emotive).

# Formal Definition
Unaccented tones of figuration occur in metrically unstressed positions, creating a light and buoyant sound. They include chordal leaps (consonant), passing tones, neighbor tones (complete and incomplete), and anticipations. Accented tones of figuration occur in metrically stressed contexts, creating more emotive and dramatic effects. They include the accented passing tone, accented neighbor tone, suspension, appoggiatura, and pedal. A third dimension, chromaticism, can be applied to either category, creating chromatic passing tones and chromatic neighbors that intensify the dissonance further.

# Musical Context
Baroque and Classical composers expected performers to add tones of figuration to published scores. Recognizing the type and placement of these figures influences performance decisions about dynamics, articulation, and expression. The distinction between accented and unaccented dissonance is crucial for stylistic interpretation and analysis.

# Examples
- Corelli, Sonata for Two Violins in Eb major, op. 2, no. 11, Giga: all dissonances metrically unstressed, creating light, buoyant sound
- Corelli, Sonata for Two Violins in E minor, op. 2, no. 4, Preludio: dissonances on accented beats, creating more striking effect

# Common Confusions
- The same pitch pattern (e.g., a passing tone) has vastly different expressive effects depending on whether it is accented or unaccented
- Consonant tones of figuration (chordal leaps) may appear without preparation or resolution; dissonant ones must be controlled
- Chromatic dissonances can be either accented or unaccented; the two dimensions (metric placement and diatonic/chromatic) are independent
