# Quick Definition
Enharmonic reinterpretation is the technique of respelling a chord enharmonically so that it functions in a different key, enabling chromatic modulations between distantly related keys.

# Formal Definition
Enharmonic reinterpretation exploits the sonic equivalence of enharmonically spelled chords to pivot between remote keys. The two principal applications are: (1) The Ger6/5 and V7 are enharmonically equivalent (differing by one respelled pitch, e.g., F#/Gb), allowing a Ger6/5 in one key to become V7 in another key (typically modulating to bII). (2) The Ger7 and V4/2 are similarly equivalent. (3) The diminished seventh chord, being symmetrical, can be respelled in four different ways, each resolving to a different key (Chapter 34). These reinterpretations function like harmonic puns, leading the listener's expectations in one direction while resolving in another.

# Musical Context
Enharmonic reinterpretation is a cornerstone of chromatic modulation in the nineteenth century. It permits certain chromatic modulations that would be impossible through diatonic means. The technique is particularly associated with Beethoven, Schubert, and Chopin, who used it to achieve dramatic tonal surprises.

# Examples
- Beethoven, "Rage Over a Lost Penny," op. 129 (Example 30.18B): A Ger6/5 in G minor is reinterpreted as V7 of Ab major (bII), enabling an effortless tonicization of the Neapolitan key area.
- Chopin, Mazurka in B major, op. 56, no. 1 (Example 30.22): The B-major tonic, with an added seventh, is reinterpreted as a Ger6/5 leading to D# major (III#, enharmonically Eb).

# Common Confusions
Composers do not always notate both spellings of the enharmonically reinterpreted chord. Sometimes a single notation serves as a pivot between the two keys. When analyzing, show the dual function using pivot-chord notation: the chord's function in the old key above and in the new key below.
