# Quick Definition
A phrase comprising three or more subphrases, usually eight or more measures long, giving the impression of being built in stages rather than as a single sweeping motion.

# Formal Definition
Composite phrases contain three or more subphrases and, like all phrases, have only one structural harmonic progression and one cadence. They are usually eight or more measures long and give the aural impression of being composed in an additive, stage-by-stage process. Two common types are distinguished: (1) the harmonic progression unfolds gradually, with each subphrase supported by a new harmonic function; (2) the harmonic progression stays on the initial tonic through most of the subphrases, changing to PD, D, and T only at the very end.

# Musical Context
Composite phrases allow composers to create longer, more complex phrase structures while maintaining a single overall harmonic trajectory. Projecting this structure in performance -- recognizing that closure is deferred until the final subphrase -- enhances the musical drama considerably.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in B major, op. 64, no. 3, Menuetto: 14-measure phrase with three subphrases, single I-ii6-V-I progression underlying the entire structure
- Haydn, Piano Sonata no. 30 in D major: eight-measure phrase where first two subphrases close on tonic and the third brings the structure to a final close

# Common Confusions
- Composite phrases have only one cadence, despite containing multiple subphrases
- The two types of composite phrase differ in how they distribute harmonic function across subphrases
- Identification of composite phrases versus multi-phrase groupings is often interpretive
