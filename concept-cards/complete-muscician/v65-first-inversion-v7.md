# Quick Definition
V65 places the leading tone (^7) in the bass and functions primarily as a neighboring chord to root-position tonic, similar to V6 but with the added dissonant seventh (^4).

# Formal Definition
V65 (first inversion of V7) places the chordal third (^7) in the bass. It functions like its triadic counterpart V6, primarily as a neighboring chord to I. The single difference between V6 and V65 is the presence of the chordal seventh (^4), which often appears in the soprano. The seventh can enter as an unaccented passing tone, as a metrically accented passing tone, or within a neighboring configuration where it mirrors the neighboring bass in contrary motion. V65 can also follow V or V6 because adding the seventh intensifies the triad.

# Musical Context
V65 is the most common inversion of V7, appearing frequently in tonic prolongations as a neighbor chord. Its leading-tone bass creates strong resolution to ^1. When the seventh appears in the soprano, it often passes between ^5 and ^3 (8-7 motion above the bass). Composers often use V65 in combination with other V7 inversions for extended tonic prolongations.

# Examples
In Mozart's Piano Sonata K. 282 (Example 11.2D), tonic opens with Bb (^5) prominently stated, then a neighboring V6 prepares the weak-beat passing seventh (Ab) leading to tonic in m. 3. In Chopin's Etude op. 10/8, an eight-measure phrase consists entirely of I-V65-I (tonic prolonged by a neighboring V65). Beethoven's Symphony no. 7 Allegretto uses V65 with the seventh as a neighbor tone mirroring the bass in contrary motion.

# Common Confusions
Students may treat V65 as a structural dominant arrival. In most contexts, V65 is a contrapuntal chord extending tonic, not a progressional harmony. Its leading-tone bass (^7) resolves to ^1, confirming its neighboring function. Also, V65 should appear as a complete chord (no omitted members).
