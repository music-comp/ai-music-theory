# Quick Definition
Applied chords typically appear on metrically weak beats because they contain leading tones that precipitate motion toward structural goals on stronger beats.

# Formal Definition
When incorporating applied chords within phrases, their metric placement is crucial for creating balanced and pleasing structures. Applied chords are embellishing harmonies and commonly appear on metrically weak beats, resolving to their target chords on metrically strong beats (such as downbeats). This weak-to-strong placement mirrors the leading-tone-to-tonic resolution pattern. The curved arrow notation is used as analytical shorthand when complete labels would be too crowded, though complete roman numerals are needed for deceptive resolutions of applied chords.

# Musical Context
Proper metric placement of applied chords ensures that structural harmonies receive appropriate metric emphasis while chromatic embellishments serve their subordinate role.

# Examples
- Beethoven, String Quartet no. 9, op. 59, no. 3, Menuetto: every applied chord appears on a metrically weak beat in a parallel interrupted period

# Common Confusions
- Applied chords can resolve deceptively (to what would be vi in the temporary key); in such cases, the complete roman numeral must be written since the arrow shorthand would be ambiguous
- Not every applied chord must be on a weak beat, but this is the default and most natural placement
