# Quick Definition
A generic interval is the numerical distance between two pitches (e.g., "a third"); a specific interval adds the quality modifier (e.g., "a major third"), precisely identifying the number of half steps.

# Formal Definition
The generic interval is determined by counting the number of letter names spanned by two pitches, inclusive. The specific interval adds a quality designation determined by scale membership. Intervals above ^1 in the major scale are either perfect (P1, P4, P5, P8) or major (M2, M3, M6, M7). Intervals above ^1 in the natural minor scale are either perfect (same as major) or minor (m2 from ^7 to ^1, m3, m6, m7). Perfect intervals retain their quality in both modes. To find a specific interval: (1) determine the generic size, (2) check whether the upper pitch belongs to the major or minor scale of the lower pitch.

# Musical Context
Specific interval identification is essential for harmonic analysis, voice leading, chord construction, and compositional technique. The quality of an interval (perfect, major, minor, augmented, diminished) directly determines its consonance or dissonance and its functional behavior.

# Examples
- D up to G: generic fourth; G is in both D major and D minor, so it is a perfect fourth (P4) (Example 3.8A).
- Ab up to C: generic third; C is in Ab major, so it is a major third (M3) (Example 3.8B).
- E up to G: generic third; G is in E minor, so it is a minor third (m3) (Example 3.8C).
- B up to G#: generic sixth; G# is in B major, so it is a major sixth (M6) (Example 3.8D).

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes assume that all intervals built on the same generic size sound the same. A major third (4 half steps) and a minor third (3 half steps) are dramatically different in sound despite sharing the generic name "third." Always specify both generic size and quality.
