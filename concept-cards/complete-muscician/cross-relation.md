# Quick Definition
A voice-leading situation where a chromatic tone appears in a different voice from its diatonic predecessor, creating a harsh aural effect.

# Formal Definition
A cross relation occurs when a chromatic pitch is prepared by a different voice rather than the same voice. For example, if G-natural appears in the soprano and G-sharp appears in the tenor on the next beat, the chromatic alteration crosses between voices rather than being prepared smoothly in a single voice. Cross relations are generally avoided, especially between the two most prominent voices (bass and soprano). When unavoidable, cross relations should be placed between inner voices or between one outer voice and an inner voice. A common acceptable cross relation occurs when the bass leaps to a chromatically altered note (e.g., a diminished fourth leap to the leading tone of an applied chord).

# Musical Context
Cross relations arise naturally when writing applied chords, particularly when voice-leading constraints prevent a chromatic tone from following its diatonic form in the same voice. In Baroque music, the diminished-fourth bass leap (a common cross relation) often accompanies words expressing sorrow or pain.

# Examples
- Undesirable: soprano has G-natural while tenor leaps to G-sharp (V6/vi), creating cross relation between outer and inner voice with awkward leaps
- Acceptable: bass leaps from C down to G-sharp (diminished fourth) in a V6/vi chord; expressive and common in Baroque music

# Common Confusions
- Not all cross relations are equally problematic; those between bass and soprano are worst
- The diminished-fourth bass leap, while technically a cross relation, is an accepted and expressive device
- Cross relations are a voice-leading issue, not a harmonic one
