# Quick Definition
A phrase is a musical unit defined by harmonic motion from one tonal area to a cadential arrival, typically four measures long, analogous to a sentence or clause in language.

# Formal Definition
A phrase is a fundamental unit of musical structure, typically containing a complete harmonic motion that leads to a cadence. A phrase requires both harmonic and melodic motion -- two-measure spans that do not give the impression of tonal motion leading to a goal do not constitute phrases. Phrases are defined by cadential arrivals, not merely by length. The standard phrase length is four measures, though phrases of other lengths are common. Changes in harmonic rhythm often help articulate phrase boundaries.

# Musical Context
Phrases are the building blocks of larger formal structures (periods, sentences, phrase groups). They combine to create the large-scale form of tonal compositions. The cadence type at the end of each phrase determines its relationship to surrounding phrases. Phrases typically begin on tonic and end on either tonic (authentic cadence) or dominant (half cadence).

# Examples
In "Clementine," the lengthy pause on G4 in m. 4 divides the melody into two four-measure phrases. The first phrase ends with an unsettled feeling (on ^2), while the second phrase provides closure. In Mozart's Rondo K. 144, two parallel four-measure phrases are defined by their cadences: HC (m. 4) and PAC (m. 8).

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes equate phrase length with a fixed number of measures. While four-measure phrases are common, phrase length is defined by harmonic content and cadential arrival, not by counting measures. A two-measure span without harmonic motion to a cadence is not a phrase.
