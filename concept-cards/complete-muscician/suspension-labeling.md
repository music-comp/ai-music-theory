# Quick Definition
Suspensions are labeled with figured bass numbers showing the intervallic motion of the suspension and resolution stages, using a dash to distinguish them from chord inversions.

# Formal Definition
Suspension labeling uses figured bass numbers that describe the interval between the suspending voice and the bass at the point of suspension and resolution. The four common labels are: 9-8 (suspension forms a ninth, resolves to an octave -- retains compound label by convention), 7-6 (suspension forms a seventh, resolves to a sixth), 4-3 (suspension forms a fourth, resolves to a third), and 2-3 (bass suspension, where numbers increase because the bass descends while upper voices hold). The dash between numbers distinguishes suspensions from chord inversions: "7" alone indicates a seventh chord member, but "7-6" indicates a suspension displacing a sixth. Compound intervals are reduced to their simple equivalents (11-10 becomes 4-3), except the 9-8 which keeps its compound label.

# Musical Context
Correct labeling is essential for distinguishing nonchord tones from chord members. Suspension labels appear immediately below the moving pitches in the score. The 7-6 works best with first-inversion chords; 4-3 and 9-8 with root-position chords; 2-3 with the progression I-V6.

# Examples
- A 7-6 suspension displaces the sixth of a first-inversion chord: the second chord is NOT a seventh chord, but a six-three chord with a suspended seventh
- V7 in m. 5 (no dash) indicates the dominant includes a chordal seventh, distinct from 7-6 (with dash) indicating a suspension

# Common Confusions
- "7" alone = chord member; "7-6" with dash = suspension -- the dash is critical
- The 2-1 suspension is uncommon because the resolution sits immediately adjacent to the dissonance
- In bass suspensions (2-3), numbers increase because the upper voices remain while the bass descends
