# Quick Definition
In first-inversion triads, double the root when possible; follow the general guideline of preferring scale degrees ^1, ^4, ^5, ^2 for doubling. Never double the leading tone (^7), which is the third of V6.

# Formal Definition
Voice-leading and doubling rules for first-inversion triads: Keep common tones (unless using a voice exchange). When possible, double the root of the chord. You may double the third or fifth if it smoothes the voice leading. As a general guideline, prefer doubling scale degrees ^1, ^4, ^5, ^2 in first-inversion chords. Never double the leading tone ^7 (the third of a dominant chord). For V6, doubling the bass (^7) is prohibited since it is the leading tone. Two techniques for playing first-inversion triads: (1) double unisons -- one upper voice doubles the bass pitch in the same octave, and (2) "neutral position" -- the three upper voices are spaced to avoid doubling the bass.

# Musical Context
Proper doubling in first-inversion chords is essential for maintaining smooth voice leading while respecting tendency-tone treatment. The prohibition against doubling ^7 in V6 is particularly important since the leading tone's resolution would create parallel octaves if doubled.

# Common Confusions
Students often assume "double the bass" applies to all chords. In first inversion, the bass is the chordal third, and doubling the bass note in V6 would mean doubling the leading tone -- which is forbidden. The rule is "double the root" (not "double the bass"), and the root of a first-inversion chord is not in the bass.
