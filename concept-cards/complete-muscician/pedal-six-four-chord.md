# Quick Definition
An unaccented six-four chord that arises from neighbor-note motion in two upper voices above a sustained bass, prolonging either tonic or dominant.

# Formal Definition
The pedal six-four chord (Ped 6/4) occurs when the bass sustains a pitch while two upper voices move by step (as neighbors) to create an apparent second-inversion triad. The six-four chord is not an independent harmony but an embellishment of the sustained chord. It is labeled at the first level as IV6/4 (when prolonging tonic) or I6/4 (when prolonging dominant), and at the second level either as I-Ped6/4-I or with figured bass showing the 5/3 to 6/4 to 5/3 motion. Pedal six-fours can arise from both neighboring and passing motion in the upper voices.

# Musical Context
Pedal six-four chords commonly prolong tonic (with an apparent IV6/4) and dominant (with an apparent I6/4). They appear frequently in hymns, folk songs, and art music. Recognition that these are embellishing chords rather than independent harmonies is crucial for accurate analysis.

# Examples
- Gruber, "Stille Nacht" ("Silent Night"): IV6/4 chords elaborate tonic through neighbor motion in inner voices
- Schubert, Minuet in D major, D. 41: apparent I6/4 in m. 3 arises from two upper neighbors moving in parallel thirds, prolonging dominant

# Common Confusions
- A pedal six-four is NOT an independent chord; it is the byproduct of neighbor motion over a sustained bass
- An apparent I6/4 prolonging V is not a tonic harmony, despite containing tonic pitches
- Do not confuse with the cadential six-four, which is accented and has a very different function
