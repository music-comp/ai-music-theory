# Quick Definition
Metrical figurations are tones of figuration whose duration occupies one full metrical unit (beat); they are typically harmonized to render them consonant.

# Formal Definition
Figurations are classified by their duration relative to the meter: metrical figurations occupy one full metrical unit (beat) and are usually harmonized (rendered consonant by supporting chords); submetrical figurations occupy less than one metrical unit and may or may not be harmonized; supermetrical figurations extend beyond the value of the beat, spanning multiple beats or measures, and are always made consonant by supporting bass notes. A metrical passing tone, for instance, fills an entire beat and is harmonized, while a submetrical passing tone is shorter than a beat and often left unharmonized.

# Musical Context
These distinctions help analysts determine the hierarchical importance of tones of figuration. Supermetrical figurations operate at the deepest levels of structure, connecting pitches across measures. Metrical figurations operate at the beat level. Submetrical figurations are the most surface-level embellishments.

# Examples
In Example 9.6A, the soprano D5 in m. 1 is a metrical passing tone -- it occupies one beat and is harmonized by V, passing between E5 and C5. The soprano C5 in m. 2 is similarly a metrical passing tone harmonized by I, passing between B4 and D5. The eighth-note A4 in the same passage is a submetrical passing tone. In Beethoven's "Tempest" Sonata, the large-scale passing motion D-E-F (^1-^2-^3) is a supermetrical figuration spanning eight measures.

# Common Confusions
Students may assume that all passing tones and neighbors are submetrical (shorter than a beat). Metrical and supermetrical figurations are equally valid -- the key distinction is that longer figurations must be rendered consonant by harmonization. The hierarchy of figurations mirrors the hierarchy of the music itself.
