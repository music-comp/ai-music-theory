# Quick Definition
The French augmented sixth chord (Fr4/3) contains an augmented fourth (^2) above the bass, creating a distinctive sonority with two tritones: b^6, ^1, ^2, and #^4.

# Formal Definition
The French augmented sixth chord (Fr4/3) contains b^6, ^1, ^2, and #^4. Its figured bass is 4/3 (reflecting the augmented fourth above the bass). The chord is distinctive because it contains two tritones (b^6-^2 and ^1-#^4), giving it an exotic, tense quality. When resolving to V, ^2 is held as a common tone (becoming the fifth of V). The Fr4/3 is the easiest augmented sixth chord to identify aurally because of its two-tritone sonority and the major-second clash often heard between its inner pitches.

# Musical Context
The French augmented sixth is less common than the German sixth but has a distinctive sound that makes it immediately recognizable. It appears in chromatically descending sequences involving augmented sixth and dominant seventh chords (Chapter 36). Its relationship to the altered dominant seventh chord (FrV4/3) creates an important reciprocal process in nineteenth-century music (Chapter 35).

# Common Confusions
The French sixth is sometimes confused with an altered dominant seventh chord (FrV4/3), which has the same intervallic structure but functions as a dominant rather than a pre-dominant. The French sixth resolves to V (PD function); the altered V4/3 resolves to I (D function). This enharmonic ambiguity creates the reciprocal process discussed in Chapter 35.
