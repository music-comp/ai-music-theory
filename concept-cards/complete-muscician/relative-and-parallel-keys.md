# Quick Definition
Relative keys share the same key signature but have different tonics (e.g., C major and A minor); parallel keys share the same tonic but have different key signatures and modes (e.g., C major and C minor).

# Formal Definition
Relative major and minor keys share identical key signatures and therefore the same pitch-class content (in the natural minor form). The relative minor tonic lies three half steps below the major tonic (e.g., A minor is the relative minor of C major). Parallel major and minor keys share the same tonic but differ in mode and key signature. Parallel keys differ by three accidentals (e.g., D major has 2 sharps while D minor has 1 flat).

# Musical Context
Relative and parallel key relationships are among the most common tonal relationships in music. Composers frequently move between relative keys within a piece (e.g., a minor-key piece may have its second theme in the relative major). Parallel key relationships (major to minor and vice versa) create dramatic shifts in mood while maintaining the same tonal center.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata op. 28, Andante (Example 1.19A): literal restatement of material from D minor to its relative major, F major.
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata op. 10, no. 3 (Example 1.19B): contrasting material moves from D major to its relative minor, B minor.
- Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 545 (Example 1.21): consecutive passages in the parallel keys of G major and G minor.
- Schubert, "Gute Nacht" (Example 1.10): shift between parallel keys D minor and D major.

# Common Confusions
Students often confuse relative and parallel keys. Relative keys share key signatures but have different tonics; parallel keys share tonics but have different key signatures. A useful mnemonic: relative = same signature, parallel = same tonic.
