# Quick Definition
Chromatic modulation is the process of moving from one key to another through chromatic harmonies, most commonly tonicizing bVI, bIII, or other chromatically related keys that arise from modal mixture.

# Formal Definition
Chromatic modulation extends modal mixture beyond single-chord colorings to establish new, chromatically related key areas. The most commonly tonicized chromatic harmonies in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century art music are bVI and bIII (e.g., C major to Ab major, C major to Eb major). Less commonly, composers tonicize VI (e.g., C major to A major) and III (e.g., C major to E major). Chromatic modulations employ pivot chords, common tones, or unprepared shifts to navigate between diatonic and chromatic tonal areas.

# Musical Context
Chromatic modulation became increasingly important from the late eighteenth century onward as composers explored the expressive possibilities of chromatically related keys. The technique is central to nineteenth-century art song (Lieder), where tonal shifts frequently project poetic meaning, and to large-scale instrumental works where mixture-derived key relationships create dramatic tonal narratives.

# Examples
- Schubert, Impromptu in Ab, D. 935 (Example 28.9): The trio modulates from Db major to A major (enharmonic bVI), using the parallel minor as a pivot. Schubert writes in A major rather than Bbb major for practical reasons.
- Schumann, "Waldesgesprach" (Example 28.14): The abrupt chromatic motion from E major to C major (bVI) characterizes the mysterious Lorelei figure.

# Common Confusions
Students may mistake chromatic modulations for sequences of applied chords. The distinction is that a chromatic modulation establishes a new key area (even if briefly), with its own functional harmonic progressions, while applied chords merely point to a chord without establishing a new tonal center.
