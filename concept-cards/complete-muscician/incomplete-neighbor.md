# Quick Definition
An incomplete neighbor departs from or arrives at a pitch by step but does not complete the return -- it either approaches the main note by step without departing by step, or departs by step without returning.

# Formal Definition
An incomplete neighbor is a neighbor tone that does not complete the full neighbor-note figure (departure and return). It either approaches a structural pitch by step from above or below without having departed from that pitch by step, or it departs by step from a structural pitch but does not return to it. The most common type occurs when the seventh of V7 enters by leap and then resolves by step (as in Example 9.9B). The incomplete neighboring chord is the harmonic analogue, as when I6-V6-I presents V6 as an incomplete neighbor approached by leap from ^3.

# Musical Context
Incomplete neighbors create a less symmetrical, more dynamic effect than complete neighbors. They appear frequently in both melody and bass lines. In harmonic contexts, incomplete neighboring chords (such as V6 following I6) expand structural harmonies.

# Examples
In "God Save the King," G4 in m. 1 is an incomplete neighbor to F4 -- it neighbors F but does not return to F. In V7 voice leading, the seventh sometimes enters by leap (incomplete neighbor) rather than by step -- this unprepared entrance is uncommon and should be avoided. In Example 10.8, I6-V6-I presents V6 as an incomplete neighboring chord.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes fail to recognize incomplete neighbors because they do not exhibit the symmetrical departure-return pattern. The key is that one end of the neighbor figure is connected by step while the other is not. An incomplete neighbor is still functionally a neighbor -- it embellishes a single structural pitch.
