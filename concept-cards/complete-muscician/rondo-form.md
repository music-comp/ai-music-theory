# Quick Definition
Rondo form alternates sections of recurring material (refrains: A1, A2, A3) with sections of contrasting material (episodes: B, C), creating either a five-part (ABACA) or seven-part (ABACABA) structure.

# Formal Definition
Rondo form is a composite form that extends the principle of ternary form by incorporating additional episodes and refrains. The refrain (A) always occurs in the home key and is the first thing heard. Episodes provide tonal and thematic contrast. Two main types exist: the five-part rondo (A1-B-A2-C-A3) and the seven-part rondo (A1-B1-A2-C-A3-B2-A4). Rondo sections may include transitions (connecting refrain to episode), retransitions (returning from episode to refrain), and codas (extra material after the final refrain). As a compound form, individual sections often contain nested binary forms.

# Musical Context
Rondos originated in French Baroque instrumental music (rondeau) and became a common final movement form in Classical sonatas, chamber pieces, and symphonies. Their themes are often folk- or popular-like, providing a light finish and contrast to more complex first movements and serious slow movements. The form demonstrates the balance between variety (episodes) and return (refrains) fundamental to human musical experience.

# Examples
- Francois Couperin, Les Gouts-reunis, Concert no. 8, "Air tendre" (Example 32.1): A five-part rondeau (A1-B-A2-C-A3) with episodes in III and v.
- Clementi, Piano Sonatina (Example 32.2): A five-part rondo with episodes in V and i, retransitions, and a coda. Each section contains nested rounded binary forms.

# Common Confusions
The seven-part rondo can be confused with a large ternary form (A1-B1-A2 = "A"; C = "B"; A3-B2-A4 = "A"). Context helps distinguish them: rondos favor the eighteenth century, faster tempos, and nested smaller forms. Also, rondos can be shorter than expected because seven-part rondos often lack the lengthy transitions and codas of five-part rondos.
