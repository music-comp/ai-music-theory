# Quick Definition
Chromatic sequences are sequential patterns whose main chords contain altered scale degrees, maintaining consistent chord quality and exact intervallic distances between repetitions, unlike diatonic sequences which conform to the asymmetry of the diatonic scale.

# Formal Definition
Chromatic sequences differ from diatonic sequences in that their main chords contain altered (chromatic) scale degrees, and they maintain strictly both chord quality and intervallic distance between repetitions. Diatonic sequences conform to the diatonic key, resulting in varying chord qualities and interval sizes. Because chromatic sequences divide the octave into same-sized intervals, they avoid the shifting whole steps and half steps that create goal-directed motion in diatonic sequences. Three main types correspond to diatonic counterparts: D2 (-P4/+m3), based on the 5-6 motion descending by whole step; D2 (-P5/+P4), descending by whole step via perfect fifths; and A2 (-M3/+P4), ascending by half step. The designation "chromatic" refers to the alteration of main structural chords, not merely the presence of accidentals.

# Musical Context
Chromatic sequences became increasingly important in the nineteenth century as they provided a means of traversing chromatically related tonal areas symmetrically. They create temporary tonal ambiguity at the surface while prolonging an underlying harmony at a deeper level. They occur more often in major-mode pieces.

# Common Confusions
Applied-chord sequences contain accidentals but are still diatonic sequences because their main structural chords are diatonic. Chromatic sequences are distinguished by having chromatically altered main chords. The label includes specific interval motions (e.g., -P4/+m3) to distinguish from diatonic counterparts (e.g., -4/+2).
