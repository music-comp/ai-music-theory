# Quick Definition
Each scale degree supports a triad of a specific quality determined by the key: in major, I, IV, V are major; ii, iii, vi are minor; vii is diminished. Minor keys have different patterns due to variable ^6 and ^7.

# Formal Definition
In major keys: triads on ^1 (I), ^4 (IV), and ^5 (V) are major; triads on ^2 (ii), ^3 (iii), and ^6 (vi) are minor; the triad on ^7 (vii) is diminished. In minor keys (using the most common scale forms): triads on ^1 (i) and ^4 (iv) are minor; triads on ^3 (III), ^6 (VI), and the subtonic ^7 (VII) are major; the triad on ^2 (ii) is diminished; the triad on raised ^7 (vii) is also diminished. The dominant (^5) appears most commonly as major (V) using the leading tone, though the minor form (v) exists using the subtonic.

# Musical Context
Knowing which triad qualities occur on which scale degrees is essential for harmonic analysis and composition. In most cases, you do not need to calculate the quality of a diatonic chord; knowing the scale degree and mode is sufficient. The primary triads (I, IV, V in major; i, iv, V in minor) carry the most harmonic weight.

# Common Confusions
Minor keys are more complex because the variable ^6 and ^7 create multiple possible triads on ^4, ^5, and ^7. Students should memorize that V (with leading tone) and vii (diminished, with leading tone) are the most common forms in minor. The natural minor forms (v and VII) exist but are less frequent in common-practice harmony.
