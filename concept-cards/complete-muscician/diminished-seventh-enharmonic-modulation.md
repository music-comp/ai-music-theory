# Quick Definition
The diminished seventh chord, being symmetrical (dividing the octave into four equal minor thirds), can be enharmonically respelled to function as vii°7 in four different keys, enabling modulation to keys a minor third or tritone apart.

# Formal Definition
The diminished seventh chord is a symmetrically constructed harmony whose inversions produce no new intervals (all contain minor thirds). Through enharmonic reinterpretation, each of its four pitches can function as a leading tone, giving access to four different keys (each a minor third apart). For example, B-D-F-Ab can resolve to C (B as leading tone), Eb (D as leading tone), F# (E#=F as leading tone), or A (G#=Ab as leading tone). There are only three distinct diminished seventh chords (since transposition by three semitones reproduces the same pitch classes), so together they provide access to all 12 keys.

# Musical Context
Enharmonic modulation via the diminished seventh chord was used from before Bach through the Romantic period. It serves as a vehicle for accessing distant key areas with remarkable ease, including keys a minor third or tritone away that would be extremely difficult to reach through diatonic means.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Sonata in C minor, op. 13 ("Pathetique") (Example 34.13A): At the transition from exposition to development, a vii°7 in G minor is enharmonically respelled to become vii°7 in E minor, connecting two keys six accidentals apart.
- Schubert, String Quartet in A minor, D. 804 (Example 34.13B): A vii°7 in A minor is enharmonically reinterpreted (invisibly, without even respelling) to access Eb major, a tritone-related key.

# Common Confusions
Composers do not always respell the diminished seventh chord when reinterpreting it enharmonically. Sometimes the same notation serves both functions, and only the resolution reveals the chord's new identity. Analyze based on function (where the chord resolves), not on spelling alone.
