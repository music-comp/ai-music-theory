# Quick Definition
Hierarchy in tonal music refers to the principle that not all pitches, intervals, or chords are equally important -- some are structural while others are subordinate embellishments.

# Formal Definition
Musical hierarchy establishes that certain pitches and harmonies are more important than others. Subordinate pitches embellish more important ones through passing tones, neighbor tones, and other figurations. The stable pitches of the tonic triad (^1, ^3, ^5) generally form the skeletal framework, while other scale degrees serve as embellishments. This hierarchy extends to intervals (perfect consonances are more stable than imperfect consonances, which are more stable than dissonances) and to harmonies (structural chords vs. ornamental chords). Analysis reveals these hierarchical relationships by "reducing" surface embellishments to reveal deeper structural connections.

# Musical Context
Hierarchy pervades every level of tonal music. At the note-to-note level, some pitches connect to others despite intervening pitches. At the phrase level, some harmonies control large spans while others merely embellish. At the formal level, some sections are structural while others are transitional. Understanding hierarchy is essential for both analysis and performance.

# Examples
In a descending C-major scale over a sustained C bass, the dissonant seventh (B over C) is only relatively resolved by the sixth (A over C), which is itself an imperfect consonance. True stability arrives with the perfect fifth (G over C). This creates a hierarchy: perfect consonance > imperfect consonance > dissonance. In "Clementine" and "God Save the King," surface differences conceal similar underlying hierarchical structures.

# Common Confusions
Students often treat all pitches as equally important, simply labeling surface events without considering deeper relationships. Analysis must go beyond labeling to reveal how subordinate events embellish structural ones. The deeper relationships actually control the surface events.
