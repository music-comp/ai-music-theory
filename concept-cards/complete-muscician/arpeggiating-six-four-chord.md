# Quick Definition
A consonant six-four chord that emerges from figurated textures and accompanimental patterns, especially in waltzes and marches, as part of a broken-chord pattern over a sustained harmony.

# Formal Definition
The arpeggiating six-four chord (Arp 6/4), also called a waltz six-four chord, is consonant and arises as part of an arpeggiation of the harmony that controls its measure. In pieces like waltzes, the first (low) bass note can be imagined as sustained through the entire measure, with the higher apparent six-four chords completing a root-position harmony. When analyzing, it is sufficient to draw a dash after the initial roman numeral, effectively ignoring the "filler" six-four chords.

# Musical Context
Common in marches, waltzes, folk tunes, and any music with broken-chord accompaniment patterns. The arpeggiating six-four fills chords whose root lies a fifth lower. Unlike pedal and passing six-fours, the arpeggiating type is consonant because it simply arpeggiates the prevailing harmony.

# Examples
- Schubert, Waltz in A minor, from 12 Grazer Walzer, D. 924: arpeggiating six-four on weak beats of mm. 1 and 3
- Beethoven, Symphony no. 3, in Eb major, op. 55, III, Trio: arpeggiating six-four in accompaniment

# Common Confusions
- Unlike pedal and passing six-fours, the arpeggiating six-four is consonant
- It does not need a separate roman numeral label; a dash after the controlling harmony suffices
- Do not confuse with a cadential six-four: the arpeggiating type is unaccented and part of a broken-chord figure
