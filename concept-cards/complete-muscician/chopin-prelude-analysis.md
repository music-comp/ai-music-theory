# Quick Definition
Chopin's Prelude in A minor, op. 28, no. 2 demonstrates how a three-note intervallic cell (fourth + third, containing a second) can generate melodic, harmonic, and structural events across an entire piece.

# Formal Definition
This prelude illustrates the generative potential of the intervallic cell. The opening right-hand melody consists of a three-note cell (E-B-D: a falling fourth and rising third, with a major second between first and third pitches). The cell appears throughout the piece in multiple transformations: in the melody (transposed down a second), in the left-hand ostinato (B-A#-B-G: small step + larger interval), in the harmonic bass (E-D-G: spanning a fourth with a second within), and at the deepest structural level (E-F#-A: the structural pitches spanning the entire piece). The prelude also demonstrates the relationship between the prelude set's key plan (pairs of relative major/minor ascending by fifth) and the piece's internal structure.

# Musical Context
The analysis reveals how a short, abstract pitch collection can influence musical events at every level of structure, from surface melody to deep harmonic planning. This approach transcends roman numeral analysis and points toward the intervallic thinking that would dominate twentieth-century composition. The prelude also contains a hidden quotation of the "Dies irae" chant in the left-hand ostinato, connecting to Chopin's illness with tuberculosis.

# Examples
- The three-note cell (fourth + third, containing a second) appears in: the right-hand melody (mm. 3-6), the left-hand ostinato, the structural bass notes (mm. 1-6), and the deepest-level melodic structure spanning the entire piece.
- The large-scale key plan (E minor-G major then B minor-D major) mirrors both the cell's intervallic content and the prelude set's organizing principle of relative-key pairs ascending by fifth.

# Common Confusions
The analysis demonstrates that traditional roman numeral analysis, while useful, may not capture the most important organizing principles of some late-Romantic works. The intervallic cell provides a complementary analytical perspective that explains features (such as deep-level parallel octaves) that seem anomalous from a purely harmonic viewpoint.
