# Quick Definition
A backward motion in the phrase model where a chord from a later function moves to a chord from an earlier function (e.g., V moving to ii, or D moving to PD).

# Formal Definition
A retrogression is a harmonic motion that moves backward through the phrase model -- from dominant to pre-dominant, or from pre-dominant to tonic -- violating the standard T-PD-D-T order. Retrogressions usually sound awkward and weak. However, apparent retrogressions can often be explained through contextual analysis: the back-relating dominant interpretation shows that V-ii is not a true retrogression because V relates backward to the preceding tonic rather than forward to the following ii.

# Musical Context
True retrogressions are rare in common-practice music because they weaken harmonic direction. Most apparent retrogressions in well-composed music can be reinterpreted through contextual analysis (e.g., as back-relating dominants or voice-leading chords).

# Examples
- Bach, Prelude in Eb Major, WTC Book 2: apparent retrogression V-ii is better understood as a back-relating dominant followed by a fresh pre-dominant

# Common Confusions
- Not every V followed by ii is a retrogression; contextual analysis often reveals a BRD interpretation
- The T-PD-D-T order is normative, not absolute; apparent violations require contextual explanation
- A true retrogression weakens the harmonic direction and is avoided by skilled composers
