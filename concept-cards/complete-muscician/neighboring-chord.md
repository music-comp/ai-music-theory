# Quick Definition
A neighboring chord is a harmony created by harmonizing a neighbor tone in the bass, extending the surrounding structural harmony. V6 is the most common neighboring chord, extending tonic with ^7 as a bass neighbor between two ^1s.

# Formal Definition
A neighboring chord arises when a bass neighbor tone is harmonized with consonant upper voices, creating a new chord that is subordinate to the surrounding structural harmony. The V6 chord commonly functions as a neighbor chord to I: the bass ^7 neighbors ^1 (I-V6-I), with the upper voices changed to be consonant with the bass. An incomplete neighboring chord occurs when the neighbor is approached by leap rather than step, as when I6-V6-I (where the bass leaps from ^3 to ^7). Neighboring chords usually fall on metrically weak beats.

# Musical Context
Neighboring chords are one of the three primary means of contrapuntal expansion (along with passing chords and chordal leaps). They create harmonic color and motion while maintaining the control of a single structural harmony. The V6 neighboring chord often features ^5 prominently in the soprano, since this common tone secures the kinship between I and V6.

# Examples
In Example 10.6, a bass neighbor B (^7) is first presented as a bare dissonant neighbor (10.6A), then harmonized as V6 (10.6B). Beethoven's Symphony no. 7 Allegretto opens with V6 as a neighbor to I, with the outer voices moving from a perfect fifth (^1-^5) to a sixth (^7-^5). Schubert's Piano Sonata D. 959 opens with V6 neighboring I, with parallel tenths in the outer voices.

# Common Confusions
Students may treat every V6 as a structural dominant. When V6 appears between two tonic chords (I-V6-I), it functions as a neighbor, not as a progressional dominant. Its function is melodic (neighbor), not harmonic (dominant-to-tonic progression). In minor, the bass leap from ^3 to ^7 (in I6-V6-I) creates a diminished fourth, which is permitted because the leading tone resolves immediately.
