# Quick Definition
A neighbor tone (also called auxiliary tone) is a nonchord tone that steps away from a chord tone and returns to it, embellishing a single pitch.

# Formal Definition
A neighbor tone (abbreviated N) is a tone of figuration that modifies a single stable pitch by moving a step above or below it and returning. Upper neighbors (UN) lie above the stable pitch; lower neighbors (LN) lie below. A double neighbor combines both upper and lower neighbors around a single pitch. Neighbors may be consonant or dissonant: a dissonant neighbor forms an interval of a second, fourth, or seventh above the bass, while a consonant neighbor forms a consonant interval (the 5-6 motion being particularly important). An incomplete neighbor departs from or arrives at a pitch by step but does not complete the return.

# Musical Context
Neighbor tones create a more static embellishment than passing tones, decorating a single pitch rather than bridging two different pitches. They appear in all voices and textures throughout common-practice music.

# Examples
In a C-major melody over a sustained C bass: F (a fourth above the bass) is a dissonant neighbor between two Es (a third), while A (a sixth above the bass) is a consonant neighbor between two Gs (a fifth). In "God Save the King," G4 in m. 1 is an incomplete neighbor to F4 -- it neighbors F but does not return to F.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes forget that neighbor tones can be consonant as well as dissonant. The 5-6 relationship (the sixth and the fifth being the only adjacent consonant intervals above a bass) is a privileged relationship with major compositional ramifications. Also, an incomplete neighbor departs by step but does not return to the original pitch.
