# Quick Definition
The most important accented tone of figuration, in which a consonant pitch is sustained past a chord change to become dissonant, then resolves downward by step.

# Formal Definition
A suspension is composed of two pitches governed by a three-stage process: (1) Preparation (P): the pitch sounds as a consonant chord tone on a weak beat; (2) Suspension (S): the prepared pitch is held (by tie or dot) into a strong-beat chord change, where it becomes dissonant; (3) Resolution (R): the dissonant suspended pitch falls by step on a weak beat to a chord tone. The three common upper-voice suspension types are 9-8, 7-6, and 4-3, labeled by the intervals formed between the suspended voice and the bass. The common bass suspension is 2-3. The dash in notation (e.g., "7-6") distinguishes suspensions from chord inversions.

# Musical Context
Suspensions are found consistently from fifteenth-century music through today's commercial and popular music. They are among the most expressive nonchord tones, creating controlled tension and release. The 7-6 suspension works best with first-inversion chords; the 4-3 and 9-8 work best with root-position chords; the bass 2-3 works best with a 6/3 chord (especially I-V6).

# Examples
- Corelli, Sonata for Two Violins in E minor, op. 2, no. 4: multiple suspensions creating expressive accented dissonances
- Haydn, Piano Sonata no. 37 in E major: 7-6 suspension chain over parallel six-three chords

# Common Confusions
- The resolution pitch should not be doubled in another voice (except for 9-8 suspensions, where the octave distance mitigates this)
- The 2-1 suspension is uncommon because the dissonance sits immediately adjacent to its resolution
- A "7" by itself indicates a root-position seventh chord; "7-6" with a dash indicates a suspension
- The suspended note's duration should be at least as long as the preparation and resolution
