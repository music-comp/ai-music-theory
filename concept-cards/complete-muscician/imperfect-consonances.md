# Quick Definition
Imperfect consonances (major and minor thirds and sixths) are moderately stable intervals that combine warmth with a sense of forward motion, making them the preferred intervals in counterpoint.

# Formal Definition
Imperfect consonances include M3, m3, M6, and m6. They are more stable than dissonances but less stable than perfect consonances, creating a fluid quality that is central to moving music forward. In counterpoint, imperfect consonances are preferred over perfect consonances for vertical intervals within the exercise because they provide both stability and momentum. Parallel motion is permitted only with imperfect consonances (limited to three consecutive uses to avoid monotony). Imperfect consonances carry the bulk of the harmonic content in tonal music.

# Musical Context
Imperfect consonances are the workhorses of tonal harmony and counterpoint. Thirds define chord quality (major vs. minor); sixths are common in first-inversion chords and parallel voice-leading. The interplay between perfect and imperfect consonances creates the balance of stability and motion that characterizes tonal music.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes wonder why parallel thirds and sixths are allowed while parallel fifths and octaves are forbidden. The reason is that imperfect consonances maintain a sense of two distinct voices even when moving in parallel, while parallel perfect consonances reduce the texture to one perceived voice (or create a hollow, archaic sound). Three consecutive parallel imperfect consonances is the recommended maximum.
