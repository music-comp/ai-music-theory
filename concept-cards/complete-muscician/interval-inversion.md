# Quick Definition
Interval inversion moves the lower pitch of a simple interval up an octave (or the upper pitch down an octave), producing a complementary interval whose generic sizes sum to 9 and whose half steps sum to 12.

# Formal Definition
Inverting an interval places the lower pitch above the upper pitch (or vice versa) by an octave displacement. Three rules govern inversion: (1) Generic sizes of inversionally related intervals always sum to 9 (e.g., a third inverts to a sixth: 3 + 6 = 9). (2) The number of half steps sums to 12 (e.g., m3 = 3 half steps inverts to M6 = 9 half steps). (3) Quality is swapped: perfect remains perfect; major becomes minor (and vice versa); augmented becomes diminished (and vice versa).

# Musical Context
Interval inversion is a practical tool for identifying large intervals (invert to a smaller, easier-to-identify interval, then reverse the quality) and for constructing intervals below a given pitch. It also reveals why inversionally related intervals share similar stability characteristics.

# Examples
- Unison inverts to octave (1 + 8 = 9); second to seventh; third to sixth; fourth to fifth (Example 3.13A).
- P inverts to P; A inverts to d; M inverts to m (Example 3.13B).
- To find a M7 below C: invert M7 to m2, find m2 above C (Db), then place Db an octave lower (Example 3.15).
- The tritone pair (A4/d5) is unique: both contain 6 half steps.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes forget that quality swaps during inversion. A major interval does not invert to another major interval; it inverts to minor. Perfect is the only quality that remains unchanged. The mnemonic: Perfect stays Perfect; Major becomes minor; Augmented becomes diminished.
