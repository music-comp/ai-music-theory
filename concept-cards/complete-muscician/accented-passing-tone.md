# Quick Definition
A passing tone that fills in a melodic third but occurs on the beat rather than between beats, reversing the usual alignment of consonance and metrical stress.

# Formal Definition
An accented passing tone (APT) fills in a melodic third like an ordinary passing tone, but occurs on a metrically stressed beat or part of a beat. This creates heightened tension because consonance and metrical accent do not align: the dissonance sounds on the strong beat while the consonance occurs on the weak offbeat. APTs most often occur in descending lines and usually participate in either a 7-6 or 4-3 contrapuntal motion against the bass voice.

# Musical Context
APTs impart a new level of tension compared to unaccented passing tones. They are common throughout the common-practice period and provide expressive emphasis. Composers use them strategically to draw the listener's attention to specific harmonic moments, including half cadences.

# Examples
- Schumann, "Am leuchtenden Sommermorgen," from Dichterliebe: accented passing tone A at downbeat of m. 4 forms a dissonant fourth with E harmony before resolving to chordal third
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in Bb, K. 570: strong double APTs at end of m. 4 postpone and draw attention to the half cadence

# Common Confusions
- APTs are metrically accented; regular passing tones are not -- the metric placement is the only difference
- APTs can be mistaken for chord tones when they fall on strong beats; context determines their nonchord-tone status
- APTs most commonly create 7-6 or 4-3 motions, not other intervallic patterns
