# Quick Definition
An unaccented six-four chord that arises from stepwise passing motion in the bass, filling the interval of a third between a root-position chord and its first inversion.

# Formal Definition
The passing six-four chord (P 6/4) derives its name from bass passing motion that fills the interval of a third. It connects a root-position chord with its first-inversion form through a stepwise bass line (e.g., ^1-^2-^3 connecting I to I6). The passing six-four functions identically to a passing vii6 chord -- both fill the space between tonic and its first inversion. It occurs on a metrically weak beat (or weak measure in fast-moving four-bar groups, or weak parts of beats in very slow tempos). At the second level, it is labeled P6/4 to reflect its passing function.

# Musical Context
Passing six-four chords may extend any five-three chord by connecting it to its six-three inversion. They are found throughout common-practice music in contexts ranging from slow movements to fast allegros.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in D minor, op. 31, no. 2: P6/4 chords connect i to i6 and iv to iv6 with restruck suspensions

# Common Confusions
- The passing six-four is unaccented; do not place it on a strong beat
- It connects any chord to its first inversion, not just tonic
- It is interchangeable in function with a passing vii6 chord between I and I6
