# Quick Definition
A variation set in which the theme is relatively short and designed to flow seamlessly into successive variations through cadential overlapping.

# Formal Definition
In a continuous variation set, the theme is usually a single phrase that is intentionally incomplete, permitting each variation to flow into the next without clear sectional breaks. Continuity is often achieved through overlap: the ending tonic of one variation simultaneously acts as the beginning of the next. Continuous variations are typically constructed over a repeated idea called an ostinato, which may take the form of a ground bass (repeating bass pattern), chaconne (repeating harmonic pattern), or passacaglia (both repeating bass and harmonic pattern). Changes in register, texture, and motivic design occur over the stable ostinato foundation.

# Musical Context
Continuous variations are characteristic of Baroque music (chaconnes, passacaglias, grounds) and appear in later periods as well. They create an impression of unbroken musical flow.

# Examples
- Monteverdi, "Lamento della ninfa": continuous variations built on a repeating descending bass pattern (ground bass)
- Purcell, Ground in G: eight-measure ground bass harmonized with a consistent harmonic pattern
- Bach, Chaconne from Partita no. 2 in D minor: repeating harmonic ostinato with overlapping variations
- Beethoven, Thirty-two Variations in C minor: harmonic ostinato with continuous figurations

# Common Confusions
- The three terms (ground bass, chaconne, passacaglia) are often used interchangeably; the distinctions are frequently artificial
- Continuity is achieved through tonal means: the theme tends toward V, whose resolution coincides with the next variation's tonic
- Even though variations overlap, the underlying pattern repeats at fixed intervals
