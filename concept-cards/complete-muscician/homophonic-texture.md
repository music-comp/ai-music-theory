# Quick Definition
Homophonic texture features a clear melody accompanied by supporting voices or chords, ranging from simple block-chord chorale style to elaborate broken-chord accompaniment patterns.

# Formal Definition
Homophonic texture combines a primary melody (usually in the highest voice) with subordinate accompanying voices. It is the most widespread texture type in common-practice music. Varieties include: chorale texture (block chords aligned rhythmically with the melody), Alberti bass (broken-chord pattern in the accompaniment), arpeggiated accompaniment, and various other accompanimental figures. In homophonic textures, single harmonies may be spread over one or more beats or measures, with chordal members distributed between different registers and instruments.

# Musical Context
Homophonic texture dominates Classical and Romantic music and all popular music. The accompaniment distributes chord tones in time, allowing the listener to collect individual pitches into a single harmony. Understanding how to identify the underlying harmonies within varied homophonic textures is a crucial analytical skill.

# Examples
- Chorale texture: Hassler, "O Haupt voll Blut und Wunden" (Example 6.8A) -- all voices move in the same rhythm.
- Alberti bass: Mozart, Piano Sonata K. 545 (Example 6.8C) -- broken-chord pattern in the left hand with melody in the right.
- Arpeggiated: Schubert, Impromptu in Gb (Example 6.8D); Chopin, Etude in Ab (Example 6.8E).
- Mozart's K. 467 slow movement (Example 6.8B): a pulsing F-major triad sustains for three measures beneath the melody.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes struggle to identify harmonies in elaborate homophonic textures where chord tones are spread across registers and beats. The key is to "collect" all pitches within a harmonic unit (often determined by bass note changes) and stack them in thirds to identify the chord. Reducing complex textures to simple block chords is an essential analytical skill.
