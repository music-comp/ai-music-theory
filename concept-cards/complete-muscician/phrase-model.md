# Quick Definition
The phrase model (T-PD-D-T) is the ordered harmonic progression of tonic, pre-dominant, dominant, and return to tonic that guides a phrase from beginning to cadence.

# Formal Definition
The phrase model describes the goal-directed harmonic motion T-PD-D-T that underlies tonal phrases. Although phrases can rely exclusively on T and D, most incorporate PD to create a richer progression. The model can occupy any number of measures, though four measures (or multiples of four) is common. The tonic function usually occupies at least as much time as PD and D combined, often considerably more. The PD typically occupies less time than the dominant. Four common configurations exist: (1) T-PD-D-T closing with an authentic cadence; (2) T extended, then PD-D-T; (3) T-PD-D closing with a half cadence; (4) T extended, then PD-D half cadence.

# Musical Context
The phrase model generates unlimited compositional possibilities across four centuries. Composers from the Baroque through the late nineteenth century employed these models consistently despite very different surface styles. Two excerpts sharing the same harmonic structure can sound completely different due to different surface figurations.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in D major ("Der Frosch"), op. 50, no. 6, Menuetto: balanced four-measure phrase with one harmony per measure
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in A major, K. 331: tonic extended for three and a third measures, PD and D occupying barely more than one beat
- Schubert, Violin Sonata no. 2 in G minor, D. 408: five and three-quarter beats of tonic, two beats of dominant, half a beat of pre-dominant

# Common Confusions
- The T-PD-D-T order is not reversible
- PD is not required (phrases can use only T and D), but most phrases include it
- The model describes harmonic function, not specific chords -- many different chord choices can fulfill each function
- Tonic typically dominates the phrase in duration; a very short PD is normal
