# Quick Definition
A longer tonicization that occupies an entire section of a piece, includes a strong cadence in the new key, and gives the feeling that the new key has usurped the home key.

# Formal Definition
A modulation is distinguished from a tonicization by three criteria: (1) it includes a strong cadence in the new key, (2) the new key continues after the cadence, and (3) it gives the feeling that a new key has replaced the home key (at least temporarily). Modulations participate in the overall harmonic motion of a piece; very few compositions end in a different key from the one in which they begin. Thus, modulations create large-scale progressions (e.g., i-III-iv-V-i). When analyzing modulations, three events are identified: the location of the new key (by roman numeral), its function in the overall progression, and the point where the original and new keys are fused (the pivot chord).

# Musical Context
Modulations are fundamental to the large-scale tonal structure of common-practice music. They appear in binary forms (typically modulating to V or III in the first section), sonata expositions, and transitions between formal sections.

# Examples
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E minor, Hob. XVI.34: modulation from E minor to G major (III) with convincing PAC in mm. 7-8 confirming the new key
- Contrast with Beethoven, Violin Sonata in A major, op. 12, no. 2: extended tonicizations of ii and IV that do not disrupt the overall A-major tonality

# Common Confusions
- The line between tonicization and modulation is not absolute; it is partly a matter of interpretation
- A repeat sign can change perception: what sounds like a modulation the first time may sound like a tonicization when the music immediately returns to the home key at the repeat
- Modulations always participate in a larger-scale harmonic progression in the home key
