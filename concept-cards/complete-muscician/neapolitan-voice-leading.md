# Quick Definition
The Neapolitan sixth chord follows specific voice-leading conventions: b^2 descends to ^7 (or through passing ^1), b^6 descends to ^5, and the bass ^4 moves to ^5, typically through a cadential 6/4 or applied diminished seventh chord.

# Formal Definition
The bII6-V progression typically places b^2-^7 in the soprano, creating a characteristic diminished third. This interval is acceptable, though it can be filled with a passing ^1. Two common ways to harmonize this passing tone are: (1) a cadential six-four chord (bII6-cad 6/4-V), or (2) an applied diminished-seventh chord (bII6-vii°7/V-V). The bass ^4 descends by step to ^5. Double ^4 (the bass) preferentially; ^6 may be doubled if needed, but never double b^2. In major mode, both ^2 and ^6 must be lowered; avoid the augmented second between ^3 and b^2 by using soprano ^1-b^2.

# Musical Context
These voice-leading conventions ensure smooth connections between the Neapolitan and the dominant. The diminished third (b^2-^7) is a distinctive interval that gives the Neapolitan its characteristic expressive quality. Filling the diminished third with a passing tone through a cadential 6/4 or vii°7/V adds additional harmonic richness.

# Common Confusions
Students often double b^2 (the root of the Neapolitan), which creates voice-leading difficulties because b^2 is a strong tendency tone that must descend. Always double the bass (^4) instead. Also, avoid resolving b^2 upward; it must descend to ^7 (possibly through ^1).
