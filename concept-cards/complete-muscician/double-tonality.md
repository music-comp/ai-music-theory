# Quick Definition
Double tonality occurs when two keys vie for supremacy simultaneously throughout a piece, creating an ambiguity that may never be fully resolved.

# Formal Definition
Double tonality describes pieces in which two keys are juxtaposed throughout, with the analyst finding it difficult to determine a single controlling tonic. Some analysts conclude that the piece is controlled by both keys simultaneously. Examples include songs by Schubert, scenes from Wagner operas, and orchestral works by Liszt, Mahler, and Strauss. Most of the time, however, double-tonal pieces are better interpreted as being in a single key: the key in which the piece closes. The concept represents an extreme form of tonal ambiguity.

# Musical Context
Double tonality became a theoretical consideration in the late nineteenth century as composers pushed tonal ambiguity to its limits. It is most commonly encountered in through-composed vocal music where the dramatic content motivates sustained tonal conflict, and in large-scale instrumental works where competing key centers create structural tension.

# Examples
- Brahms, "Es hing der Reif," op. 106, no. 3 (Exercise 34.1C): C major and A minor compete throughout, with interior cadences in C major but the closing key suggesting A minor.
- Scriabin, Prelude, op. 39, no. 2 (Example 37.12): D major is only confirmed in the final two measures, while competing tonal centers create sustained ambiguity throughout.

# Common Confusions
Double tonality is not the same as modulation. In modulation, one key clearly gives way to another. In double tonality, both keys are simultaneously present and competing. Most apparent cases of double tonality can be resolved by analyzing the piece in the key in which it closes.
