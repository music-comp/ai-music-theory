# Quick Definition
Tonal transposition maintains generic interval sizes while staying within the key; real transposition maintains exact interval qualities but may leave the key.

# Formal Definition
Tonal transposition maintains the generic (numerical) size of the intervals but alters their quality in order to remain within the key. For example, the motive E-F-G in C major transposed up a third tonally yields G-A-B (staying in C major). Real transposition maintains the specific intervals (both generic and quality) between each member of the motive and the transposed version. The same motive transposed up a third in real transposition yields G-Ab-Bb (maintaining m2 + M2), which departs from C major.

# Musical Context
Most motivic transposition in tonal music is tonal, maintaining the key while adjusting interval qualities. Real transposition is used when a composer wants to maintain the exact intervallic profile of a motive, often in chromatic or modulatory passages. When a motive is transposed two or more times successively at a consistent interval, the process is called sequence.

# Examples
- Beethoven, "Eroica" Symphony: real transposition of opening motive up a major second
- Mozart, Symphony no. 40, Trio: tonal transposition of motive up a second
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in D major, K. 576: tonal transposition of motive

# Common Confusions
- Tonal transposition stays diatonic; real transposition preserves exact intervals and may introduce accidentals
- In tonal transposition, a minor second may become a major second (or vice versa) to stay in the key
- Sequence is a specific application of transposition where the motive is restated multiple times at consistent intervals
