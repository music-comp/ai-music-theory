# Quick Definition
IV6 can expand the tonic by filling in a bass arpeggiation from ^1 down to ^3 (I-IV6-I6), with ^6 as an intermediate step. It also expands the dominant as a passing chord between V and V6.

# Formal Definition
IV6 serves two contrapuntal functions: (1) In tonic expansion, when the bass arpeggiates from ^1 down to ^3 (I to I6), the large leap of a sixth can be split by adding IV6 on ^6, creating the arpeggiation ^1-^6-^3. The soprano typically ascends ^3-^4-^5, with ^4 ascending to ^5 (contrasting its usual tendency to descend). Common doubling: double ^1 (the third above the bass). (2) In dominant expansion, IV6 passes between V and V6, harmonizing bass ^6 between ^5 and ^7. In minor keys, ^6 must be raised to avoid an augmented second between ^6 and ^7, resulting in a major IV6 chord.

# Musical Context
IV6 adds a subdominant color to contrapuntal expansions. In tonic expansion, it fills in an otherwise large bass leap. In dominant expansion, it creates smooth stepwise bass motion. Both functions are subordinate and contrapuntal rather than structural.

# Examples
In Example 10.14, IV6 splits the bass sixth from ^1 to ^3 into two smaller intervals (^1-^6-^3). In Example 10.15, IV6 passes between V and V6 in both major and minor keys. In Corelli's Violin Sonata op. 5/4, the I-IV6-I6 progression is the result of transferring a violin tune to the harpsichord.

# Common Confusions
Students may confuse IV6 as a contrapuntal chord with IV as a pre-dominant (which has a structural harmonic function). In the contrapuntal context discussed here, IV6 is subordinate and embellishing. Also, in minor keys using IV6 between V and V6, ^6 must be raised to avoid the augmented second.
