# Quick Definition
Structural harmonies are progressional -- they define the underlying harmonic motion of a passage. Embellishing harmonies are prolongational -- they extend structural harmonies through contrapuntal means.

# Formal Definition
Structural harmonies are progressional: they indicate movement from one harmonic function to another (e.g., tonic to dominant) and keep their Roman numerals in second-level analysis. They are usually in root position, on strong beats, and part of a harmonic progression. Subordinate (embellishing) harmonies are prolongational: they extend a single structural harmony through chordal skips, neighbors, or passing motion. They have melodic function, are usually in inversion, on weak beats, and part of a contrapuntal progression. In second-level analysis, they are labeled by their ornamental function (N, CS, P) rather than retaining Roman numerals.

# Musical Context
This distinction is the central interpretive tool of tonal analysis. Merely labeling every chord with a Roman numeral (descriptive analysis) misses the deeper structure. True analysis requires determining which harmonies are structural and which are embellishing -- a creative, interpretive activity with direct performance implications.

# Examples
In a passage labeled i-V6-i-viio6-i6, the root-position i chords are structural, while V6 (neighbor) and viio6 (passing) are embellishing. In second-level analysis, these would be labeled: i (N) i (P) i6, showing that tonic controls the entire span. Corelli's violin sonatas demonstrate how harmonies are often byproducts of melodic figures transferred between instruments.

# Common Confusions
The most common error is treating all Roman-numeral-labeled chords as equally structural. An analysis that simply labels every chord misses the point -- the harmonies are often byproducts of contrapuntal processes, not independent harmonic events. The distinction between structural and embellishing is the heart of musical analysis.
