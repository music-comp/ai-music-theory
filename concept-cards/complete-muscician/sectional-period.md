# Quick Definition
A period in which both phrases close on the tonic, but the second cadence is stronger or more conclusive than the first, completing something left melodically open.

# Formal Definition
In a sectional period, the antecedent phrase typically ends with an IAC (or a weaker PAC), and the consequent phrase ends with a PAC. Both phrases are tonally closed, meaning each section is harmonically self-contained. The incompleteness that drives the period forward is primarily melodic rather than harmonic. Can be parallel (PSP) or contrasting (CSP).

# Musical Context
Sectional periods are somewhat common, especially in pieces where both phrases cadence on tonic but the melody of the first phrase is left incomplete or the cadence is weaker (IAC vs. PAC). They appear in Classical-era keyboard works and vocal music.

# Examples
- Mozart, Piano Sonata in B-flat major, K. 281, Allegro: both phrases close on tonic, but the melodic incompleteness at the midpoint creates the need for continuation

# Common Confusions
- Students may question whether two phrases closing on tonic can form a period; the key is that the first cadence must be weaker than the second
- Sectional periods rely more on melodic incompleteness than harmonic incompleteness
