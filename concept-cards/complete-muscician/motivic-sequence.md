# Quick Definition
A pattern created when a motive is transposed two or more times successively at a consistent interval, combining motivic unity with forward melodic motion.

# Formal Definition
A motivic sequence occurs when a motive is transposed two or more times in succession at a consistent interval (e.g., each repetition a tonal step higher or lower). Most motivic transposition is by step, creating melodic fluency that, combined with the restatement of the unifying motive, produces powerful musical unity and forward motion. Sequences can employ tonal transposition (remaining diatonic) or real transposition (maintaining exact intervals). Composers often combine sequential treatment with other transformations such as fragmentation.

# Musical Context
Motivic sequences are a primary means of extending musical material and creating momentum in development sections, transitional passages, and thematic areas. They allow a compact motive to generate large-scale melodic trajectories.

# Examples
- Mozart, Symphony no. 29 in A major, K. 201: opening theme built from sequential repetition of a two-measure motive, rising stepwise from ^1 to ^4, followed by rapid return to ^1 to close the eight-measure theme

# Common Confusions
- A sequence requires at least two successive transpositions (three total statements) at a consistent interval
- Motivic sequence differs from harmonic sequence: motivic sequences concern melodic patterns, harmonic sequences concern chord progressions (though they often coincide)
- Most tonal sequences adjust interval quality to remain within the key
