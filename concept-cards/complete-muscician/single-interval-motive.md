# Quick Definition
A motive composed of only one interval type (such as a third, fourth, or half step) that generates both melodic and harmonic material throughout a piece.

# Formal Definition
A single-interval motive consists of a single interval that is highly versatile and can be employed in multiple musical environments to generate both melodic and harmonic content. Because single intervals (e.g., a perfect fourth, a falling third) are ubiquitous in tonal music, they risk being nonmotivic -- merely byproducts of ordinary melodic and harmonic motion. To establish a single interval as truly motivic, composers often combine it with a more striking element (such as a half-step neighbor) or subject it to transformations including inversion, augmentation, diminution, and octave displacement.

# Musical Context
Single-interval motives became increasingly important from the late eighteenth century onward, as composers like Beethoven and Brahms explored their potential to unify entire multi-movement works. The ^5-^6-^5 upper-neighbor motive appears as a generating interval in numerous Chopin works.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata in Ab major, op. 110: perfect fourth Ab-Db generates the fugue theme, subjected to inversion, augmentation, and diminution
- Brahms, Symphony no. 4: falling third generates the first movement theme and is summarized in the closing measures as a long chain of thirds (E-C-A-F#-D#-B-G-E-C-A-F#-D#)
- Chopin, multiple Preludes and Nocturnes: ^5-^6-^5 upper-neighbor motive as a generating interval

# Common Confusions
- A single interval is not automatically motivic; it must be treated in a way that distinguishes it from ordinary harmonic and melodic motion
- Octave displacement can transform a single-interval motive beyond recognition (e.g., a third becoming a sixth)
- Single-interval motives often generate intermovement connections in multi-movement works
