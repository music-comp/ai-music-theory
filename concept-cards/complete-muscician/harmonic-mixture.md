# Quick Definition
Harmonic mixture occurs when b^6 or b^3 appears as the root of a chord, shifting the root down from its diatonic position to produce bVI or bIII.

# Formal Definition
Harmonic mixture is a type of modal mixture in which the root of a chord is chromatically lowered to produce a major triad borrowed from the parallel minor. The most important harmonic mixture chords are bVI (built on b^6, requiring b^3 as well to form a consonant major triad) and bIII (built on b^3, requiring b^7 for consonance). The flat sign before the roman numeral indicates the lowered root. Despite their chromatic inflection, bVI and bIII continue to function in their normal submediant and mediant roles within the harmonic progression.

# Musical Context
bVI participates in descending arpeggiations, descending-fifth motions, precedes the dominant as a PD chord, substitutes for tonic in deceptive motions (V-bVI), and serves as a dramatic upper neighbor extending V (since bVI lies a half step above the dominant). bIII divides the fifth between I and V into two smaller thirds, bridges T and PD functions, participates in descending-fifths motion, and can be preceded by its own dominant (V/bIII).

# Examples
- Brahms, Symphony no. 3, Andante (Example 27.12): bVI and minor iv resolve to the tonic in a plagal cadence with no dominant harmony, even at the final cadence. The conflict between A-natural and Ab creates a motive that spans multiple movements.
- The major tonic has access to three sets of third relations: diatonic (iii, vi), raised chromatic (III, VI), and lowered chromatic from mixture (bIII, bVI).

# Common Confusions
Do not confuse bVI (harmonic mixture, with b^6 as root) with VI (a major triad on the diatonic ^6, which is a different chromatic chord entirely). Also, bVI in A major is built on F-natural, not F-flat; the generic "b" refers to the lowering from the diatonic position regardless of the specific accidental used.
