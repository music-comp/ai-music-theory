# Quick Definition
A false recapitulation occurs when the FTA theme appears in the wrong key during the development, creating the illusion that the recapitulation has begun before the real recapitulation arrives in the tonic.

# Formal Definition
A false recapitulation is a harmonic anomaly in which the theme from the FTA appears during the development section but in a key other than the tonic. Because the theme sounds familiar, the listener initially believes the recapitulation has begun, only to discover that the tonal "address" is wrong. The real recapitulation, in the correct tonic key, usually follows soon thereafter. False recapitulations are actually part of the development section and are a deliberate compositional strategy to create surprise and extend the development's dramatic tension.

# Musical Context
False recapitulations are especially characteristic of Haydn, who delighted in surprising his listeners with tonal misdirection. The technique creates a moment of recognition followed by disorientation, heightening the dramatic impact of the eventual true recapitulation.

# Examples
- Haydn, String Quartet in B minor, op. 33, no. 1 (Example 33.5): After what seems to be a retransition preparing V of B minor, the FTA theme enters boldly in A major rather than the expected tonic. Only after twists through an augmented sixth chord does the true recapitulation arrive in m. 59.

# Common Confusions
A false recapitulation is not an error in analysis; it is a deliberate compositional device. The key diagnostic is: Does the FTA theme return in the tonic? If not, the apparent recapitulation is false and belongs analytically to the development.
