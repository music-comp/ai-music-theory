# Quick Definition
Simple intervals span an octave or less; compound intervals span more than an octave and can be described as an octave plus their simple equivalent.

# Formal Definition
Intervals no larger than the octave are simple intervals (unison through octave). Intervals larger than the octave are compound intervals. Compound intervals up to a twelfth are named directly (ninth, tenth, eleventh, twelfth). Intervals larger than a twelfth are reduced to their simple forms with the prefix "compound" (e.g., "compound sixth" or "octave plus a sixth"). The quality of a compound interval is the same as its simple equivalent.

# Musical Context
In figured bass notation and harmonic analysis, compound intervals are typically reduced to their simple equivalents (e.g., a tenth is labeled as a third). This reflects the principle that compound intervals share the consonance/dissonance characteristics of their simple forms. However, in practice, the wider spacing of compound intervals affects their sonic character.

# Examples
- C4 to E5 is a tenth (compound third) (Example 3.4).
- C2 to C4 is a compound octave.
- D4 to B5 is a "compound sixth" or "octave plus a sixth."

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes struggle to reduce compound intervals to their simple forms. The quick method: subtract 7 from the compound interval number to get the simple form (e.g., a tenth minus 7 equals a third; a ninth minus 7 equals a second). The quality remains the same.
