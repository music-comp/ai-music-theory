# Quick Definition
A transition bridges from the original key to a new key (A to B in ternary), while a retransition bridges from a contrasting key back to the original key (B back to A).

# Formal Definition
In ternary form, transitions and retransitions are bridging sections that create continuity between independent formal sections. A transition modulates from the tonic to the key of the B section. A retransition (returning transition) leads from the contrasting key back to the tonic for the return of A. A ternary form may have both, one, or neither. Retransitions occur much more frequently than transitions. These passages may be as simple as a dominant preparation of the upcoming key or as involved as elaborate passages that foreshadow motives and harmonies of the next section. The terms also apply in rondo and sonata forms.

# Musical Context
Transitions and retransitions make for some of the most exciting music in a piece because their content is unpredictable. They serve a crucial function in creating directed motion between stable key areas and can range from a single chord to extended modulatory passages.

# Examples
- Brahms, Romanze in F, op. 118, no. 5 (Examples 31.3-31.4): A brief transition in the final measure of A (m. 16) prepares D major through a bass arpeggiation of D minor. The retransition (mm. 45-48) shifts from D major to D minor (vi in F major) and proceeds through a cadential 6/4 to the tonic.

# Common Confusions
It can be hard to distinguish between a continuous ternary form and a sectional ternary with a transition. If there is a conclusive end to a section followed by modulation with melodic fragmentation, this is likely a transitional area. If a section closes in another key but the closure is integral to its tonal motion, there is likely no transition.
