# Quick Definition
Consonance refers to stable intervals (unisons, octaves, fifths, thirds, sixths), while dissonance refers to unstable intervals (seconds, sevenths, perfect fourths above the bass, augmented/diminished intervals) that require resolution.

# Formal Definition
Perfect consonances (unison, octave, fifth) are the most stable intervals. Imperfect consonances (major and minor thirds and sixths) are less stable but more fluid. Dissonant intervals include seconds, sevenths, the perfect fourth (when formed with the bass), and all augmented and diminished intervals. The distinction between consonant and dissonant is not absolute but depends on musical context -- the same interval can be consonant or dissonant depending on which pitches surround it.

# Musical Context
Consonance and dissonance create the fundamental tension-release dynamic of tonal music. Dissonant intervals provide musical tension and energy that is discharged through resolution to consonance. The hierarchy of stability among intervals (perfect consonances > imperfect consonances > dissonances) governs melodic and harmonic behavior throughout common-practice music.

# Examples
The minor sixth C-Ab can be consonant (when F or Ab appears below it, creating an F minor or Ab major triad) or dissonant (when C is in the bass, creating a yearning for resolution to a perfect fifth). The perfect fourth F above C is dissonant when C is the bass but consonant when another pitch (like A or Ab) appears below, creating a first-inversion triad.

# Common Confusions
Students often assume consonance and dissonance are fixed properties of intervals. In reality, the same interval can be consonant or dissonant depending on context -- particularly which pitch is in the bass. The perfect fourth is the most commonly misunderstood: it is dissonant above the bass but consonant in upper voices.
