# Quick Definition
The second chord of each two-chord unit in a sequence, which serves to break up potential parallel motion that would result from moving directly by step or third.

# Formal Definition
In sequences, the second chord of the model (and of each copy) functions as a voice-leading chord (also called a helping chord). Without these intervening chords, moving all voices directly downward by step or third would create parallel fifths and octaves. The voice-leading chord provides the necessary contrary or oblique motion to maintain good counterpoint. These chords are not harmonically functional in the traditional sense but are essential contrapuntal components of the sequential pattern.

# Musical Context
Voice-leading chords are present in all four basic sequence types. They explain why sequences use alternating root motions (e.g., -5/+4 rather than simply -2) and why the second chord of each pair often appears in inversion.

# Examples
- In D2 (-5/+4): the ascending fourth motion (+4) provides the voice-leading chord that prevents parallels from the overall descending-second motion
- In D3 (-4/+2): the ascending second (+2) serves the same function for the overall descending-third motion

# Common Confusions
- Voice-leading chords are not independent harmonic entities; they exist to serve the contrapuntal needs of the sequence
- Root-position diminished triads (vii in major, ii in minor) are permitted as voice-leading chords within sequences
