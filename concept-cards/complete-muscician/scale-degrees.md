# Quick Definition
Scale degrees are the numbered positions of pitches within a scale, each with a specific name reflecting its function in the tonal hierarchy.

# Formal Definition
Each of the seven pitches in a major or minor scale occupies a numbered position (^1 through ^7) called a scale degree. Each degree has both a number and a functional name: ^1 = tonic, ^2 = supertonic, ^3 = mediant, ^4 = subdominant, ^5 = dominant, ^6 = submediant, ^7 = leading tone (when a half step below ^1) or subtonic (when a whole step below ^1). These names reflect each pitch's relationship to the tonic and its role within the tonal hierarchy.

# Musical Context
Scale degree numbers and names are fundamental to tonal analysis because they describe pitch function independent of key. The statement "the melody moves from ^5 to ^1" applies in any major or minor key. The dominant (^5) and subdominant (^4) are named for their position a fifth above and a fifth below the tonic. The mediant (^3) and submediant (^6) lie midway between tonic and dominant, and between tonic and subdominant (below), respectively.

# Examples
- In C major: C = ^1 (tonic), D = ^2 (supertonic), E = ^3 (mediant), F = ^4 (subdominant), G = ^5 (dominant), A = ^6 (submediant), B = ^7 (leading tone).
- In D minor: D = ^1, E = ^2, F = ^3, G = ^4, A = ^5, Bb = ^6, C# = ^7 (leading tone when raised).

# Common Confusions
The term "dominant" refers both to the fifth scale degree (^5) and to the chord built on that degree (V). Similarly, "subdominant" refers to both ^4 and the IV chord. Context usually clarifies which meaning is intended. Also, the leading tone (^7 raised, a half step below ^1) must be distinguished from the subtonic (^7 lowered, a whole step below ^1).
