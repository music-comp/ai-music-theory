# Quick Definition
Sequences that employ seventh chords in first inversion (6/5) or third inversion (4/2), creating smoother voice leading and bass suspension effects.

# Formal Definition
Just as first-inversion triads smooth the bass of D2 sequences, first-inversion seventh chords (6/5) and third-inversion seventh chords (4/2) are common in sequences. Third-inversion seventh chords resolve to first-inversion triads or seventh chords, and the seventh in the bass creates the impression of a bass suspension (prepared by common tone, resolved down by step). When determining the sequence type with inverted seventh chords, locate the root of each chord to identify the underlying root motion. Figures reveal the interlocking inverted seventh chord patterns (e.g., 4/2 to 6).

# Musical Context
Inverted seventh chords in sequences add harmonic richness while maintaining smooth bass lines. They are common in Baroque trio sonatas and orchestral works.

# Examples
- Leclair, Trio Sonata in D major, op. 2, no. 8, Allegro: D2 (-5/+4) sequence with interlocking 4/2 to 6 chords, creating a bass suspension effect
- Mozart, Symphony in G major, K. 74: D2 sequence with first-inversion triads followed by repetition with added oboe sixths including the chordal seventh

# Common Confusions
- When seventh chords appear in inversion, make sure each sonority is complete (unlike root-position interlocking sevenths)
- Locate the root of each chord to determine the underlying sequence type, regardless of inversions
- The bass seventh in 4/2 chords functions like a suspension: prepared and resolved down by step
