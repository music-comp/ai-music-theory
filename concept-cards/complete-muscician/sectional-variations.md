# Quick Definition
A variation set in which the theme and each variation are in binary form, tonally closed, and separated from one another by clear formal boundaries.

# Formal Definition
In a sectional variation set, the theme is usually in binary form (often rounded continuous binary) and is tonally closed, creating clear separations between variations. More substantive changes among variations are possible than in continuous variations, including reharmonization, tonicization of new keys, and changes between major and minor modes. The form and proportions of the theme are typically preserved across variations. Composers use two strategies for creating coherence: (1) rhythmic crescendo or expanding textures leading to climax, and (2) grouping variations into larger units (e.g., a set of 12 grouped as 5+2+5, with the central group in the parallel minor).

# Musical Context
Sectional variations are common in Classical-era piano music and chamber works. They are the basis for theme-and-variations movements in sonatas, string quartets, and symphonies.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Six Variations on "Nel cor piu non mi sento," WoO 70: theme in balanced sectional binary form; six variations with progressive intensification, mode change in Variation IV (parallel minor), and concluding coda

# Common Confusions
- Even in sectional variations with clear boundaries, composers strive for larger-scale coherence through progressive intensification
- The form and proportions of the theme are usually preserved even when melody, harmony, and texture change substantially
- Mode change (major to minor or vice versa) is a common strategy for creating contrast within a variation set
