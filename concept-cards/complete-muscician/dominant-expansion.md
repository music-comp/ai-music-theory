# Quick Definition
Dominant expansion extends the duration of dominant harmony through subordinate chords, though it is less common than tonic expansion. V6 and IV6 can expand V through chordal leaps and passing motion.

# Formal Definition
Just as tonic can be expanded through contrapuntal chords, the dominant can also be extended, though less commonly. V can move to V6 through a chordal leap, and IV6 can pass between V and V6 (harmonizing bass ^6 between ^5 and ^7). In minor keys, ^6 must be raised when using IV6 between V and V6 to avoid an augmented second between ^6 and ^7, resulting in a major IV6 chord. Parallel fifths and octaves in IV6-V6 should be avoided by moving upper voices in contrary motion to the bass.

# Musical Context
Dominant expansion typically occurs in the cadential area of a phrase, extending the dominant's control before final resolution to tonic. It is less common than tonic expansion because the dominant's instability and tendency to resolve limits how long it can be meaningfully sustained.

# Common Confusions
Students may confuse dominant expansion with tonic expansion. The context determines which harmony is being extended. When V6 or IV6 appears between root-position V chords, the dominant is being expanded. When V6 appears between tonic chords (I-V6-I), the tonic is being expanded. Also, in minor: raise ^6 for IV6 between V and V6.
