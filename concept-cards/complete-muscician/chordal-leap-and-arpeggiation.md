# Quick Definition
A chordal leap (or consonant leap) is a melodic leap between two members of the same underlying harmony. An arpeggiation occurs when three or more tones from the same harmony appear in succession.

# Formal Definition
Chordal leaps (CL), also called consonant leaps, are nonstepwise melodic motions between pitches that are members of the underlying harmony. When three or more chord tones appear in succession, they form an arpeggiation (ARP). Both are consonant embellishments because the pitches involved are members of the prevailing chord. Triadic skips and leaps are permitted as long as they are members of the underlying harmony.

# Musical Context
Chordal leaps and arpeggiations appear throughout tonal music as a means of melodically unfolding a harmony over time. They are especially prominent in bass lines (defining harmonic function), accompaniment patterns, and opening gestures of melodies.

# Examples
In "Clementine" (F major), the melody arpeggiates the tonic triad F4-A4-C5 on the strong beats of mm. 1-3. In Bach's Presto in G minor, the opening five measures consist of a cascading G-minor broken-chord figure (arpeggiation).

# Common Confusions
Unlike passing tones and neighbor tones, chordal leaps and arpeggiations are consonant embellishments -- they are members of the underlying harmony. Students sometimes label all nonstepwise motion as "leaps" without distinguishing whether the pitches belong to the chord (consonant) or not (dissonant, which would be unusual and require special treatment).
