# Quick Definition
A common-tone augmented sixth chord (c.t.+6) sounds like a German augmented sixth but sustains the tonic root as a common tone in the bass, functioning as a neighbor chord that extends the tonic rather than driving toward the dominant.

# Formal Definition
The common-tone augmented sixth chord (c.t.+6) contains an augmented sixth interval (like a Ger6/5) but has the tonic pitch sustained in the bass as a common tone. Instead of the expected b^6-^5 bass motion of a standard augmented sixth chord, the bass remains on the tonic. This creates a neighbor figure above and below the fifth of the tonic harmony, extending the tonic rather than leading to the dominant. The bass may skip down a major third to the usual bass position of an augmented sixth chord, but the chord still returns to the tonic, confirming its common-tone function.

# Musical Context
The c.t.+6 is characteristic of late-nineteenth-century harmonic style, providing chromatic color within tonic prolongations. It represents the extension of the common-tone principle (seen in the c.t.°7) to augmented sixth sonorities, reflecting the nineteenth-century trend of using chromatic harmonies for coloristic rather than functional purposes.

# Examples
- Schubert, "Am Meer," Schwanengesang, D. 957 (Example 35.19): A c.t.+6 on beat 2 of m. 1 creates a neighbor figure above and below the fifth of the C-major tonic, with C sustained in the bass.
- Wolf, "Man sagt mir," Italienisches Liederbuch, no. 21 (Example 35.21): A c.t.+6 appears in m. 1, after which an apparent altered V7 of G actually functions as an augmented sixth chord leading to F# major, a key six accidentals away from A minor.

# Common Confusions
Do not confuse the c.t.+6 with a standard Ger6/5. The c.t.+6 sustains the tonic in the bass and resolves back to the tonic; the standard Ger6/5 has b^6 in the bass and resolves to V. The presence or absence of the tonic pedal is the key diagnostic.
