# Quick Definition
A compositional technique where an expected strong cadence is thwarted by converting the dominant to V4/2, which must resolve to the weaker I6 instead of root-position tonic.

# Formal Definition
An evaded cadence occurs when a composer circumvents an expected cadence, most often by adding a passing seventh in the bass of the dominant (creating V4/2), which necessarily resolves to I6 rather than root-position tonic. This technique is used for two main purposes: (1) elision, where the seams between two independent phrases are hidden to create one large phrase, and (2) cadential extension, where elements of the cadence (including PD and the dissonant part of cad 6/4) are repeated before the authentic cadence finally arrives, creating a harmonic crescendo.

# Musical Context
Evaded cadences dramatically intensify the listener's desire for closure. They are especially powerful when the cadential six-four chord is used as the dominant, since the passing seventh in the bass effectively dissolves the strongest possible cadential approach. Multiple evasions before a final cadence create mounting dramatic tension.

# Examples
- Mozart, Symphony in A major, K. 114: cad 6/4 converted to V4/2 creates a single eight-measure phrase instead of two four-measure phrases
- Mozart, "Hm! Hm! Hm!" from The Magic Flute, Act I: three attempts at cadential closure, with V4/2 dissolving the first two, before the cad 6/4 finally resolves to V7-I

# Common Confusions
- Evaded cadence is different from deceptive motion (V-vi); the evaded cadence uses V4/2-I6
- Elision hides phrase seams; cadential extension repeats cadential material -- both use evasion but for different purposes
- The evasion heightens expectation; the eventual cadence sounds more powerful by contrast
