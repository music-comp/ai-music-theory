# Quick Definition
Equal divisions of the octave are symmetrical progressions that segment the octave into intervals of the same size (1, 2, 3, 4, or 6 half steps), creating inherently nonfunctional, ambiguous harmonic motion.

# Formal Definition
Equal divisions of the octave incorporate two or more identical intervals into progressions that partition the octave symmetrically. The possible divisions are: by semitone (12 divisions, chromatic scale), by whole tone (6 divisions, whole-tone scale), by minor third (4 divisions, diminished seventh chord), by major third (3 divisions, augmented triad), and by tritone (2 divisions). These divisions can operate at the level of individual chords (as in symmetrically constructed harmonies) or at the level of key relationships (as in sequential progressions that divide the octave into equal-sized transpositions). Because all steps are identical, these progressions lack the asymmetry that creates tonal direction.

# Musical Context
Equal divisions of the octave became increasingly important from the late eighteenth century through the early twentieth century. They represent the ultimate extension of symmetrical thinking in tonal music, pushing harmony to the edge of tonality. Nineteenth-century composers used them for dramatic and coloristic effects, while early-twentieth-century composers (Debussy, Scriabin) used them as alternatives to traditional functional harmony.

# Common Confusions
Not every chromatic progression constitutes an equal division of the octave. True equal divisions require consistent, identical intervals between all steps. A progression that moves by two major thirds and one minor third, for example, is an asymmetrical (not equal) division.
