# Quick Definition
An additional closing section appended to the final variation that extends beyond the theme's proportions, providing a fitting conclusion to the entire set.

# Formal Definition
A coda in a variation set abandons the fixed length of the theme and variations to provide a conclusive ending for the entire work. It typically restates crucial features of the theme, summarizes motivic ideas developed across the variations, and often touches on the subdominant to create a plagal effect signaling closure. The coda may feature imitative treatment of thematic motives, extreme register exploitation, and intensified harmonic activity. It serves to break the potentially mechanical repetition of theme-length sections and provide a climactic or summarizing conclusion.

# Musical Context
Codas are common in longer sectional variation sets where the fixed proportions of the theme would make a sudden ending feel anticlimactic.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Variations on "Nel cor piu non mi sento," WoO 70: 24-measure coda (vs. 20-measure theme) presents the opening scale-degree gesture (^5-^3-^2-^1) tossed between hands in imitative fashion; brief tonicization of IV creates plagal effect; summarizes events from earlier variations

# Common Confusions
- The coda is not simply another variation; it breaks free of the theme's proportional framework
- Subdominant tonicization in the coda signals closure (plagal effect), not a new modulation
