# Quick Definition
The tonic (I) and dominant (V) chords form the harmonic axis of tonal music. Together they can define a key, creating a sense of arrival and departure that underpins virtually every tonal phrase.

# Formal Definition
Tonic and dominant chords provide the harmonic foundation of tonal music. By themselves they can define a key. Compositions and phrases generally begin with the tonic and progress through nontonic chords, eventually reaching the dominant and returning to the concluding tonic. The tonic stated at the opening need not progress to any particular chord, but the dominant inevitably leads back to tonic. The V chord contains active tendency tones: ^7 wants to ascend to ^1, ^2 can move to ^1 or ^3, and ^5 (as the root of V) tends to return to ^1.

# Musical Context
The I-V-I progression, though simple, underlies much great and imaginative music. Extended passages of a single harmony (either I or V) create very different psychological effects: tonic is stable and self-sufficient, while dominant creates powerful expectations of resolution. Most tonal phrases can be understood as elaborations of the I-V-I axis.

# Examples
Wagner's Das Rheingold opens with a single Eb-major tonic triad sustained for an extended introduction, creating stability without forward harmonic motion. Beethoven's Eroica Symphony finale features an extended dominant passage (mm. 328ff.) that sounds unstable and expectant. Beethoven's Fifth Symphony finale opens with simple alternation of I and V7.

# Common Confusions
Students may assume that I and V always alternate in equal importance. In fact, one often controls extended spans while the other is subordinate. Also, the addition of the seventh to V (creating V7) intensifies the drive to tonic but does not change the fundamental I-V axis.
