# Quick Definition
Counterpoint is the art of combining two or more independent melodies simultaneously, governed by rules of consonance and dissonance that control the vertical intervals between voices.

# Formal Definition
Counterpoint (from Latin "punctus contra punctum," point against point, or "melody against melody"), also called polyphony ("multiple sounds"), is the relationship between and movement of two or more voices. It depends on two elements: (1) the behavior of consonant and dissonant intervals between voices, and (2) the harmonies implied by their interaction. In four-voice textures, voices are labeled soprano, alto, tenor, and bass (SATB). The outer voices (soprano and bass) are the most aurally prominent and provide the structural skeleton of tonal music.

# Musical Context
Counterpoint has been central to Western music study since the tenth century. Johann Fux's Gradus ad Parnassum codified a species-based pedagogy that was studied by Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, and Brahms. Contrapuntal thinking underlies all tonal music, even textures that appear predominantly harmonic, because harmony emerges from the interaction of melodic voices.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes think counterpoint and harmony are separate subjects. In fact, harmony arises from counterpoint: chords are the vertical result of combining melodic lines. Understanding this relationship is essential for writing effective voice leading.
