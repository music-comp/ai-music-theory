# Quick Definition
Tonicizations are brief, occur within phrases, and do not disrupt the home key; modulations include strong cadences in the new key and give the feeling that the new key has replaced the home key.

# Formal Definition
The distinction between tonicization and modulation is based on three criteria. Tonicizations: (1) usually occur within phrases, (2) do not disrupt the feeling of the home key, (3) lack strong cadences in the new key, and (4) are fleeting. Modulations: (1) include a strong cadence in the new key, (2) the new key continues after the cadence, (3) give the feeling that a new key has usurped the home key. The line between the two is not absolute and often depends on interpretation. A repeat sign can change the perception: a passage that sounds like a modulation the first time through may seem like a tonicization when the music returns immediately to the home key.

# Musical Context
This distinction is one of the most important analytical judgments in tonal analysis. It affects how large-scale tonal structure is understood and how formal sections are delineated.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Violin Sonata in A major, op. 12, no. 2: extended tonicizations of ii and IV that do not displace A major (tonicization)
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in E minor, Hob. XVI.34: PAC in G major at mm. 7-8 with G major continuing afterward (modulation)

# Common Confusions
- There is no firm line between tonicization and modulation; it is often a matter of interpretation
- Cadential strength and duration in the new key are the primary distinguishing factors
- Repeat signs complicate the analysis by providing different contexts for the same music
