# Quick Definition
Enharmonic intervals are intervals that sound identical but are spelled differently, reflecting different musical contexts and functions.

# Formal Definition
Just as enharmonic pitches are differently named pitches that sound the same, enharmonic intervals are differently named intervals that contain the same number of half steps but are spelled differently. For example, the minor third C-Eb (3 half steps) sounds identical to the augmented second C-D# (also 3 half steps). The choice of spelling depends entirely on the musical context -- the key, the scale degrees involved, and the harmonic function.

# Musical Context
Correct enharmonic spelling is essential for communicating harmonic function. A minor third occurs diatonically in many keys, while an augmented second occurs only in specific contexts (such as between ^6 and raised ^7 in harmonic minor). Enharmonic reinterpretation of intervals is also a technique used in chromatic modulation.

# Examples
- m3 from C to Eb is enharmonic with A2 from C to D# (Example 3.17A).
- C-Eb is diatonic in several flat keys; C-D# occurs only in contexts involving E harmonic minor.

# Common Confusions
Students sometimes think enharmonic intervals are interchangeable. While they sound the same, they convey different theoretical information. Writing an augmented second when a minor third is intended (or vice versa) misrepresents the harmonic context. Always spell intervals according to their function in the prevailing key.
