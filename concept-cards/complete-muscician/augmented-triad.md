# Quick Definition
The augmented triad is a symmetrical chord consisting of two major thirds, spanning an augmented fifth, that divides the octave into three equal parts, creating inherent tonal ambiguity.

# Formal Definition
The augmented triad (e.g., C-E-G#) is composed of two major thirds and spans an augmented fifth. Like the diminished seventh chord, it partitions the octave equally (into three major thirds). Its inversions retain the same intervallic content (major thirds and minor sixths or their enharmonic equivalents), making it impossible to distinguish inversions aurally. Only four distinct augmented triads exist (since transposition by four semitones reproduces the same pitch classes). The augmented triad evolved from a byproduct of melodic motion (passing tone, appoggiatura) to an independent sonority in late-nineteenth-century music.

# Musical Context
The augmented triad appears in three evolutionary stages: (1) as a metrically weak byproduct of voice leading (Beethoven, Example 35.8); (2) as a more prominent contrapuntal sonority within sequential progressions (Example 35.10); (3) as an independent, harmonically ambiguous chord (Liszt, "Nuages gris," Example 35.12, where descending augmented triads obscure all sense of tonality).

# Examples
- Beethoven, Bagatelle in C major, op. 119 (Example 35.10): The chromatic passing tone G# creates an augmented triad that could be heard as I with raised fifth (C-E-G#) or as an E-major chord with raised fifth (E-G#-B#).
- Liszt, "Nuages gris" (Example 35.12): Descending augmented triads become independent sonorities, obscuring tonality to evoke the image of amorphous gray clouds.
- Grieg, "Illusion," Lyric Pieces VI, op. 57 (Example 35.11): Augmented triads create tonal ambiguity reflecting the song's title.

# Common Confusions
Not every augmented triad is an independent harmony. In many contexts, it arises from voice leading (passing tones, appoggiaturas) and should be analyzed as a contrapuntal sonority, not a root-position chord. Determine independence by checking whether the chord receives metric emphasis and whether it participates in a harmonic progression.
