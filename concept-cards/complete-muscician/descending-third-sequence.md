# Quick Definition
A sequence in which each copy descends by a third, with root motion alternating between descending fourths and ascending seconds.

# Formal Definition
The D3 (-4/+2) sequence features chord pairs whose roots fall a fourth and rise a second, creating a deeper-level descent by third. It incorporates a miniature deceptive motion (V-vi pattern) and typically has a stepwise descending soprano line. The first-inversion variant, D3 (-4/+2) + 6/3, also known as the descending 5-6 sequence, creates a stepwise bass descent moving in parallel tenths with the soprano. In this variant, one voice (often tenor) remains stationary against the falling bass, forming alternating fifths and sixths. The D3 with 6/3 is much more common than its root-position counterpart.

# Musical Context
The D3 sequence often serves a dual function: it can prolong tonic (when I is both the first and last chord) and simultaneously lead to the pre-dominant. It is one of the most recognizable sequences in tonal music.

# Examples
- Pachelbel, Canon in D major: the famous repeating pattern is a D3 (-4/+2) sequence (I-V-vi-iii-IV-I-IV-V), serving both to prolong tonic and lead to PD
- The descending 5-6 variant features in many Baroque and Classical works

# Common Confusions
- In minor mode, the minor v chord may be necessary to avoid the augmented second between ^6 and ^7
- The D3 often sounds like a series of deceptive motions; students should recognize this as a sequential pattern rather than functional deceptive cadences
- The 6/3 variant (descending 5-6 sequence) is far more common than root position
