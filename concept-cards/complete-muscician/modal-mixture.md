# Quick Definition
Modal mixture is the technique of borrowing harmonies from the parallel mode, most commonly importing elements of the minor mode into a major-mode piece.

# Formal Definition
Modal mixture (sometimes called simply "mixture") is a chromatic procedure in which pitches or harmonies from the parallel mode are incorporated into a passage. Although borrowing can occur in either direction, it is far more common for elements of the minor mode (particularly b^3, b^6, and b^7) to be imported into the major mode. The resulting chromatic harmonies typically carry pre-dominant function and create expressive coloring that ranges from subtle shading to dramatic tonal conflict.

# Musical Context
Modal mixture became increasingly prominent from the late Classical through the Romantic era. Composers such as Schubert saturated their major-mode pieces with minor-mode elements to such a degree that the 24 major and minor keys could be said to fuse into 12 "major-minor" keys. Mixture chords usually function as pre-dominants, distinguishing them from applied chords, which function as dominants.

# Examples
- Mascagni, "A casa amici," Cavalleria Rusticana (Example 27.1): A passage in F major borrows extensively from F minor, creating a chromatic progression that briefly tonicizes bIII before moving through PD and D to resolve to the major tonic.
- Schubert, "Schwanengesang" (Example 27.7): The key of F major is pervaded with elements of F minor, including a tonicization of bIII.

# Common Confusions
Students often confuse modal mixture with tonicization/applied chords. The key distinction: mixture harmonies are independent chords that participate in the harmonic progression and usually carry pre-dominant function; applied harmonies function as dominants pointing to tonicized chords. Additionally, modal mixture alters the quality of a chord without changing its root or function.
