# Quick Definition
A five-part rondo follows the plan A1-B-A2-C-A3, alternating three statements of the refrain with two contrasting episodes, typically with the B episode in V (or III) and the C episode in a contrasting key such as i, iv, or vi.

# Formal Definition
The five-part rondo (A1-B-A2-C-A3) is the more common of the two Classical rondo types. The refrain (A) always appears in the tonic. Episode B typically moves to V (in major) or III (in minor); episode C moves to a more remote key such as i (parallel minor), iv, or vi. The form may include transitions (connecting refrain to episode), retransitions (returning from episode to refrain), and a coda (after the final refrain). As a compound form, individual sections often contain nested binary forms (commonly rounded binary). The overall tonal plan creates an arch: tonic-dominant-tonic-contrasting key-tonic.

# Musical Context
The five-part rondo was a favorite movement type for Classical sonatas and symphonies, particularly as finales. Its combination of predictable refrains and contrasting episodes provides both structural clarity and variety, making it ideal for spirited, crowd-pleasing closing movements.

# Examples
- Clementi, Piano Sonatina (Example 32.2): A1 in I, B in V with retransition, A2 in I, C in i with retransition, A3 in I, followed by a coda. Each section contains nested rounded binary forms.
- Mozart, Viennese Sonatina in C, K. Anh. 229 (Example 32.3): Each section contains two eight-measure units forming binary forms.

# Common Confusions
The five-part rondo is often confused with ternary form. The key difference is that the rondo has five sections (ABACA), not three (ABA). Also, the C episode in a five-part rondo is typically in a more remote key than the B episode, creating greater contrast and making the final refrain return more satisfying.
