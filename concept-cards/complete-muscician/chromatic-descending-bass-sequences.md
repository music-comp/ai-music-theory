# Quick Definition
Chromatic descending-bass sequences use a one-chord repeating pattern (six-three chords, diminished seventh chords, or augmented sixth chords) to create a chromatically descending bass line, though they are not true sequences in the strict two-chord-model sense.

# Formal Definition
In addition to standard two-chord sequential models, it is possible to descend chromatically using a one-chord repeating pattern. Three common sonority types serve this function: (1) Six-three chords with 7-6 suspensions, where dissonant sevenths align with chromatic bass notes and consonant resolutions align with diatonic notes. (2) Diminished seventh chords, which create a stream of apparently root-position diminished sevenths (since inversions are aurally indistinguishable due to the chord's symmetry). (3) Augmented sixth and dominant seventh chords, which exploit the ambiguity between Ger6/5 and V7, often clarified by transforming the German sixth into a French sixth to avoid parallel fifths.

# Musical Context
These chromatic descending patterns are particularly expressive in slow-tempo pieces with emotional texts. The 7-6 suspension pattern over a chromatic bass creates a succession of various seventh-chord types that is characteristic of the Romantic style. The augmented sixth/dominant seventh pattern provides a smooth descent from tonic to dominant.

# Examples
- Chopin, Impromptu in Ab major (Example 36.12D): A chromatic six-three motion extends tonic before leading to the pre-dominant.
- Chopin, Prelude in Ab major, op. 28 (Example 36.14B): A descending series of augmented sixth and dominant seventh chords, with French sixths clarifying the ambiguity at each step.

# Common Confusions
These one-chord descending patterns are not true sequences (which require a two-chord model and copy). They are better described as chromatic descending-bass patterns or chromatic parallelism. However, they share with sequences the property of suspending goal-directed motion while prolonging an underlying harmony.
