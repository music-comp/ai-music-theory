# Quick Definition
The transition (Tr) in sonata form is a modulatory passage connecting the FTA to the STA, ending either on the new tonic or the new dominant, and classified as dependent (DTr) or independent (ITr) based on whether it reuses FTA material.

# Formal Definition
The transition connects the FTA to the STA in the exposition by modulating to the contrasting key. Two types exist: (1) A dependent transition (DTr) begins with a restatement of the FTA's initial theme, which is then altered to effect the modulation. (2) An independent transition (ITr) uses entirely new thematic material. Both types end on the new tonic or new dominant, at which point the medial caesura occurs before the STA entrance. In the recapitulation, the transition reappears but is typically altered since it no longer needs to modulate; it may be compressed or harmonically varied to maintain a sense of motion without leaving the tonic.

# Musical Context
Transitions are crucial structural components that manage the tonal conflict between FTA and STA. In major-mode sonatas, the presence of accidentals often signals the beginning of the transition. In minor-mode sonatas, the opposite occurs: the transition reinstates the lowered ^7 (which served as the raised leading tone for the minor tonic) since it now functions as ^5 in the new relative major key.

# Examples
- Beethoven, Piano Sonata no. 19, op. 49, no. 1 (Example 33.2): A DTr (mm. 9-15) begins with the FTA theme but alters it in m. 13 to modulate to III, ending on a half cadence.
- Haydn, Piano Sonata in C major, Hob. XVI.35 (Example 33.9): An ITr (mm. 20-35) uses new material with a stepwise ascent (C-D-E, E-F#-G) to modulate to V.

# Common Confusions
In the recapitulation, the "transition" is altered since it must stay in the tonic. Some analysts refer to this as a pseudo-transition. Also, the transition is defined by its modulatory function, not by its thematic content; even if the theme is familiar (DTr), its harmonic redirection distinguishes it from the FTA.
