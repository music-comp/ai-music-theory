# Quick Definition
A procedure in which a motive stated in one voice is restated in one or more different voices, intensifying musical drama through registral and timbral variety.

# Formal Definition
Imitation is the restatement of motivic material in different voices. It intensifies pacing, density, and overall musical drama when the imitation occurs in distinct registers and different instruments. Imitation can occur at any interval, with the fifth being the most common (after the octave). In fugues, the imitated motive is called a "subject." Composers often combine imitation with other techniques such as sequence and stretto.

# Musical Context
Imitation is a fundamental contrapuntal technique found throughout tonal music, from two-part inventions to orchestral symphonies. It is the basis of fugal writing and appears in virtually all genres of the common-practice period.

# Examples
- Bach, Invention in D minor, BWV 775: motive stated in upper voice, imitated in lower voice
- Bach, G minor Fugue, WTC Book 1: subject transposed up a fifth in imitation

# Common Confusions
- Imitation at the fifth often involves tonal rather than real transposition to maintain the key
- Imitation is not limited to strict (exact) repetition; modified and developmental imitation are common
- Stretto is a specific type of overlapping imitation where a new entry begins before the previous one finishes
