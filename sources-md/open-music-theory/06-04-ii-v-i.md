# ii–V–I

Key Takeaways

  * ii7–V7–Ima7 in major, or ii∅7–V7–i7 in minor, is a fundamentally important progression in traditional jazz.
  * The [pb_glossary id="2845"]ii–V–I[/pb_glossary] progression can be identified through a combination of [pb_glossary id="1883"]root motion[/pb_glossary] by fifths plus its distinctive sequence of [pb_glossary id="2846"]chord qualities[/pb_glossary] ([pb_glossary id="2847"]mi7[/pb_glossary]–[pb_glossary id="2848"]7[/pb_glossary]–[pb_glossary id="7305"]ma7[/pb_glossary] in major, or [pb_glossary id="2850"]∅7[/pb_glossary]–[pb_glossary id="2848"]7[/pb_glossary]–[pb_glossary id="2847"]mi7[/pb_glossary] in minor).
  * Because this progression is so important to jazz, the concept of [applied chords](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/tonicization) can expand to include applied subdominant chords—i.e., the ii chord.
  * Incomplete ii–V–Is, i.e., ii–Vs, can also be identified because the combination of root motion and quality is so distinctive.

All the examples end in [pb_glossary id="2904"]perfect authentic cadences (PACs)[/pb_glossary]. But the similarities don't end there: each PAC is preceded by the ii chord. So we have three chords, each related to the next by fifth. This ii–V–I progression is one of the most important progressions in jazz music. You can find it reliably at cadences, but also as a building block that occurs throughout a tune. When the progression occurs in a major key, as in the snippets in Example 1, the chord qualities of these chords are mi7–7–ma7. When the tune is in minor, the shift in mode changes the quality of the harmonies to ∅7–7–mi7 (the V chord is major whether you are in a major or minor key). Both of these progressions and a typical voice-leading pattern are summarized in Example 2.

# ii–V–I as Schema

[pb_glossary id="970"]Schema[/pb_glossary] is a useful concept in music theory, used in many ways within this book ([pop harmony](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/pop-rock-schemas/), for one). Put simply, schemas are common patterns our brains can recognize, even when variations are altering a specific presentation of that schema. The ii–V–I progression is an example of a schema. It happens so frequently that informed listeners can recognize the schema in many formats. Some examples of alterations are given in Example 3. In "[Misty](https://open.spotify.com/track/4uxsv9PjV3Yeyn51RdWvGJ?si=QzRm5WCVR-KJocw2MY5CIA)," the ma7 chord is replaced with a 6 chord (this occurred in "[My Funny Valentine](https://open.spotify.com/track/4l9hml2UCnxoNI3yCdL1BW?si=6gPNDx3cQ9qS3RtRL0wZFQ)" in Example 2 also). In "[Prelude to a Kiss](https://open.spotify.com/track/48z5YXpqifAmKoLzaFOjHo?si=3w_PGbrYRhi_p9YGBw1qog)," the typically dominant-quality V chord is replaced with an augmented chord (the minor seventh is preserved). The V chord is altered in "[A Night in Tunisia](https://open.spotify.com/track/5fxJzPPvuvdhHypgme9cVX?si=2wu8PHxSRkKD3p-_th-eBQ)," but this time, the fifth is lowered instead of raised. 

Example 3. The ii–V–I is still recognizable, even if alterations occur.

# Applied ii–Vs

An important marker of [pb_glossary id="2006"]dominant-function chords[/pb_glossary] is the chord's [pb_glossary id="2846"]quality[/pb_glossary]. This is most obvious in the case of the dominant seventh quality, since it has “dominant” in the name. Fully diminished chords also have dominant function. The compositional technique of [pb_glossary id="2458"]applied chords[/pb_glossary] capitalizes on the relationship between quality and function by taking dominant chords out of their key and dropping them into a new key. The applied dominant chords retain their function as dominant chords even when applied to a chord other than I (this concept is fully explained in the [Tonicization chapter](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/tonicization/)). The omnipresence of ii–V–I as a schema in jazz means that in this style, we can have not only applied dominants, but applied ii chords as well. In other words, the entire ii–V–I progression can be used in keys other than the tonic key to [pb_glossary id="2121"]tonicize[/pb_glossary] another chord. The association between these chord qualities and root motions is so strong that a ii–V progression need not even resolve to its I chord to create the effect of a ii–V. Take the rest of the A section of "[Afternoon in Paris](https://open.spotify.com/track/1WKNhhhwZkfqJVmi5cuD2z?si=OWXs3gb-TwGICSb0fKEQ7A)" as an example (Example 4). Not only does it end with a ii–V–I progression, but it begins with two other ii–V–Is: one tonicizing B♭ major, which is ♭VII in the key of C, immediately followed by another in A♭ major, which is ♭VI in the key of C. ## ii–V space

By relating all possible ii–V–I motions together, we can come up with a space in which these progressions operate, and visualize how the ii–V–Is in "Afternoon in Paris" are related. This idea comes from [Michael McClimon (2017)](http://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.1/mto.17.23.1.mcclimon.html), and his "ii–V space" is reproduced in Example 5. The space is arranged as the circle of fifths (note the letter names at the end of each progression), with each chord in the circle preceded by a ii–V. [caption id="attachment_2918" align="aligncenter" width="800"][![visual diagram described in-text](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/08/mcclimon_fig06.png)](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/08/mcclimon_fig06.png) Example 5. McClimon's ii–V space relates ii–V progressions of keys related by fifth.[/caption] There are four different variations of arrows in Example 5, and each signifies a different transformation from the first chord to the chord at the other end of the arrow. The two solid arrows have to do with root motion: the black arrows connect chords within a ii–V–I schema, while the gray arrows show the circle-of-fifths relationships. The dashed arrows show changes to chord quality. The larger dashes indicate that the chord is the same except that the seventh has been lowered, so each larger-dashed arrow connects a ma7 chord to a (dom)7 chord with the same root. The smaller dashes show that the chord is the same, but the third has been lowered: each smaller-dashed arrow connects a 7 chord to a mi7 chord with the same root. [caption id="attachment_2919" align="alignright" width="150"][![diagram](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/08/music-61-2-150x150.jpg)](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/08/music-61-2.jpg) Example 6. "Afternoon in Paris" transforms Ima7 chords into minor seventh chords, thus changing their function: they become ii7 chords.[/caption] Example 6 traces the progressions in "Afternoon in Paris" that were annotated in Example 4. Notice how the space helps to illustrate the logic of the progression: by transforming the preceding Ima7 chords into ii7 chords, it forces a modulation down by whole step.

# Turnarounds

A particularly common version of applied ii–Vs comes in what is called the [pb_glossary id="974"]turnaround[/pb_glossary]. In the broadest sense, a turnaround is a progression that serves to loop back to the original tonic chord, and the typical progression that achieves this is I–vi–ii–V–I. Using the concept of applied chords, we can substitute a V7/ii for the vi chord, since they share the same root. But we can also precede that V7 with its ii chord—effectively, a ii/ii (two of two). Thus, the ii chord of the turnaround is tonicized with its own ii–V–(I) progression. This tonicized version of the turnaround is a very common variant (Example 8).
