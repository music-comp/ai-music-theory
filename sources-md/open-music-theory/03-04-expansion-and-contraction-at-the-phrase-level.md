# Expansion and Contraction at the Phrase Level

Key Takeaways

  * [pb_glossary id="2295"]Expansions[/pb_glossary] make a phrase last longer than expected. They can be internal (between the beginning and ending of the phrase) or external (before the beginning or after the end). 
    * Internal expansion techniques include [pb_glossary id="2297"]repetition[/pb_glossary], [pb_glossary id="2298"]stretching[/pb_glossary], [pb_glossary id="2299"]one-more-time technique[/pb_glossary], and [pb_glossary id="2300"]alternative path[/pb_glossary].
    * External expansions fall into two categories: 
      * [pb_glossary id="2423"]Prefixes[/pb_glossary] occur before the beginning, such as an introduction.
      * [pb_glossary id="2424"]Suffixes[/pb_glossary] occur after the cadence, such as [pb_glossary id="2488"]post-cadential extensions[/pb_glossary], [pb_glossary id="2489"]codettas[/pb_glossary], and [pb_glossary id="2334"]codas[/pb_glossary].
  * [pb_glossary id="2296"]Contractions[/pb_glossary] make a phrase shorter than expected.

The terms [pb_glossary id="2295"]expansion[/pb_glossary] and [pb_glossary id="2296"]contraction[/pb_glossary] refer to ways composers play with the expected length of a phrase. What does "expected length" mean? 

  * When a [pb_glossary id="1220"]phrase[/pb_glossary] invokes one of the [pb_glossary id="2107"]archetypes[/pb_glossary] without following it exactly, we can often identify how long a closer representation of that archetype would have been.
  * Sometimes the cadential motion of the phrase allows us to anticipate where a cadence might occur, but the phrase [pb_glossary id="7975"]evades[/pb_glossary] the expected cadence.
  * Sometimes a piece states two versions of a phrase—one unexpanded and the other expanded—giving us a model to which we can compare the expanded version.

Expansion refers to the process of making a phrase longer than we expect. This lengthening might occur within the phrase ("[pb_glossary id="2302"]internal expansion[/pb_glossary]") or outside of the phrase ("[pb_glossary id="2303"]external expansion[/pb_glossary]"). Contraction refers to the process of making a phrase shorter than we expect, and it always occurs within a phrase. In this chapter, we offer an overview of several techniques for phrase expansion and contraction. This isn't an exhaustive list, but it covers several of the more common techniques. The terminology concerning expansion and contraction techniques can quickly become overwhelming. What's most important is to be able to recognize when a phrase is longer or shorter than expected and to be able to describe what's creating the difference in length. 

# Internal Expansions

The four techniques we discuss below—[pb_glossary id="2297"]repetition[/pb_glossary], [pb_glossary id="2298"]stretching[/pb_glossary], [pb_glossary id="2299"]one-more-time[/pb_glossary], and [pb_glossary id="2300"]alternative path[/pb_glossary]—all occur _within_ a phrase: after the phrase's beginning, but before its cadence. Often, composers use these techniques in combination; in fact, it's relatively rare to find a phrase that's expanded using only one technique. 

## Repetition

[caption id="attachment_2305" align="alignright" width="150"][![notation](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/07/Example_001_Repetition_Sheldon_Bright_Lights-150x150.png)](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/07/Example_001_Repetition_Sheldon_Bright_Lights.png) Example 1. Internal expansion via exact repetition in Robert Sheldon's _Bright Lights!_ ([0:49–1:03](https://youtu.be/rQCGWof1_us?t=48)).[/caption] Sometimes when a composer repeats material, the [pb_glossary id="2297"]repetition[/pb_glossary] creates extra length. Example 1 shows an exact repetition of a motive _within a unit_ that creates extra length: a four-measure [pb_glossary id="1853"]antecedent[/pb_glossary] is followed by an expanded five-measure [pb_glossary id="1855"]consequent[/pb_glossary]. The extra length results from a repeated [pb_glossary id="1211"]motive[/pb_glossary] within a unit. [caption id="attachment_7904" align="alignright" width="150"][![notation](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2021/06/combined-Example_002b_Repetition_Mozart_447-3-150x150.png)](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2021/06/combined-Example_002b_Repetition_Mozart_447-3.png) Example 2. Internal expansion via varied repetition in Mozart's Horn Concerto, K. 447, I, mm. 1–9 ([0:00–0:18](https://open.spotify.com/track/29lQckJgUUlqkiGFDIcNfU?si=nJK2FsKrRU-ScM9xR270kg)).[/caption] Example 2 shows a varied repetition in which _a whole unit_ is repeated to add extra length: a four-measure [pb_glossary id="2273"]compound basic idea (c.b.i.)[/pb_glossary] is followed by a five-measure [pb_glossary id="1832"]continuation[/pb_glossary]. The continuation begins with a [pb_glossary id="1845"]sequence[/pb_glossary] that expands it from the expected four measures to five. Not all repetitions create expansion! Be careful to differentiate expansion-creating repetitions, which are unexpected, from more predictable repetitions, such as when the basic idea is repeated in a presentation. 

## Stretching

[caption id="attachment_2383" align="alignright" width="150"][![notation](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/07/Example_003a_Stretching_Schubert_459-150x150.png)](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/07/Example_003a_Stretching_Schubert_459.png) Example 3. Internal expansion via stretching in Schubert's Piano Sonata D. 459, I, mm. 1–9 ([0:00–0:17](https://open.spotify.com/track/2w7acEUx1nzuh5ADgTvikr?si=7vpt2nHNQDGd8r-tqSj1kQ)).[/caption] Sometimes composers will lengthen a harmony or melody by increasing its duration so that it lasts longer than expected. When that happens, we say that the unit that contains the harmony or melody has been [pb_glossary id="2298"]stretched[/pb_glossary]. In Example 3, a four-measure [pb_glossary id="1831"]presentation[/pb_glossary] is followed by a five-measure [pb_glossary id="1832"]continuation[/pb_glossary]. The continuation's [pb_glossary id="2284"]cadential idea[/pb_glossary] is stretched when a [latex]\mathrm{I}^6[/latex] chord and the cadential dominant (embellished by a [latex]\mathrm{Ger}^6[/latex] and a cadential [latex]^6_4[/latex]) each last for a full measure as opposed to both occurring within a single measure.  

## One-More-Time Technique (o.m.t.)

Coined by Janet Schmalfeldt (1989), the one-more-time technique involves three steps: 1) the music tries to cadence, 2) the attempted cadence is evaded, and 3) the music retries the cadence. The re-tried cadence often uses the same material, such that it feels like the music is "backing up," but it may also retry the cadence with different cadential material. [caption id="attachment_8157" align="alignright" width="150"][![](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/07/Slide5-6-150x150.png)](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/07/Slide5-6.png) Example 4. Internal expansion via the one-more-time technique in Mendelssohn, Op. 8, no. 5, "Pilgerspruch" ([0:00–0:37](https://open.spotify.com/track/31CjWqgQhH2wMHumjyAcnF?si=PelVogCOSdez3BER7aMk0g)).[/caption] In Example 4, a cadence is proposed in m. 10, but it's evaded ([latex]\mathrm{cad^6_4 – V^4_2 – I^6}[/latex]). A one-more-time repetition of the [pb_glossary id="2284"]cadential idea[/pb_glossary] ensues. The one-more-time unit is [pb_glossary id="2298"]stretched[/pb_glossary]: notice that it takes two measures (compared to one originally) for the melody to descend C–B♭–A–G, and it takes a full measure (compared to a half measure originally) for the cadential [latex]\mathrm{^6_4}[/latex] to resolve to [latex]\mathrm{V}^7[/latex]. 

## Alternative Paths

An [pb_glossary id="2300"]alternative path[/pb_glossary] occurs when unexpected new material either temporarily or permanently causes a phrase to deviate from its expected trajectory toward a cadence. A temporary deviation is called a [pb_glossary id="2414"]detour[/pb_glossary]. Detours return to previous material from within the phrase before the phrase achieves a cadence. A permanent deviation is called a [pb_glossary id="2415"]reroute[/pb_glossary]. Reroutes achieve cadences without returning to previous material from within the phrase. [caption id="attachment_8171" align="alignright" width="150"][![](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/07/Slide8-10-150x150.png)](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/07/Slide8-10.png) Example 5. Internal expansion via alternative path (detour) in Mendelssohn, _Hebrides_ Overture, mm. 9–26 ([0:20–0:58](https://open.spotify.com/track/268QFsnVawnmGdlRhMum3T?si=2KI7fqQiRUyWKeGMsx0N3g)).[/caption] Example 5 shows a detour: the sentence's continuation is repeated. We realize the repetition is getting "off track" when the motive in the cadential idea (m. 19) is repeated (m. 20), which did not happen in the analogous place before (m. 15). A highly contrasting two-measure passage emerges (mm. 21–22) that leads to a return of material (mm. 23–26) similar to the cadential idea in mm. 19–20, now stretched. [caption id="attachment_8178" align="alignright" width="150"][![](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/07/Slide10-11-150x150.png)](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/07/Slide10-11.png) Example 6. Internal expansion via alternative path (reroute) in Maria Theresia von Paradis, Sicilienne, mm. 1–10 ([_0:00–0:42_](https://open.spotify.com/track/55dSAv52AK0DnVj5ZUIDBh?si=1G2p8eSASG-y2QyHp2xhRw)).[/caption] Example 6 shows a reroute: a four-measure [pb_glossary id="1853"]antecedent[/pb_glossary] is followed by a six-measure expanded [pb_glossary id="1855"]consequent[/pb_glossary]. The consequent begins with the same [pb_glossary id="1827"]basic idea[/pb_glossary] as the antecedent, but its [pb_glossary id="1854"]contrasting idea[/pb_glossary] moves in a markedly different direction, one that doesn't lead to a cadence. A new cadential idea is needed to bring the phrase to a [pb_glossary id="2869"]perfect authentic cadence (PAC)[/pb_glossary]. Although [pb_glossary id="2299"]one-more-time technique[/pb_glossary] and alternative paths both involve cadences, they are very different techniques, as explained below:  **One-more-time technique** | **Alternative path**  
---|---  
  
  * Passage has tried and failed to achieve a cadence
  * Feels as though the phrase backs up to try the failed cadence again

| 

  * Passage has not yet tried to cadence
  * Feels as though the phrase has gone off course

  
  
# External Expansions

When composers add length outside of a phrase, they may add measures before the beginning ([pb_glossary id="2423"]prefixes[/pb_glossary]) or after the cadence ([pb_glossary id="2424"]suffixes[/pb_glossary]). In diagrams, we indicate external expansions using dashed arcs or brackets. 

## The Prefix

[caption id="attachment_8183" align="alignright" width="150"][![](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/07/Slide12-13-150x150.png)](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/07/Slide12-13.png) Example 7. External expansion via small prefix in Cécile Chaminade, "L'amour captif," mm. 1–10 ([0:00–0:13](https://open.spotify.com/track/243TaFRW3lZHhNNMIJqacw?si=Hnd3vfj3SFCniqoz2iiiPw)).[/caption] A [pb_glossary id="2423"]prefix[/pb_glossary] occurs before the beginning of a phrase, usually taking the form of an introduction. A prefix can be small, but it may also be large, such as when a composer begins a symphony with a slow introduction. Example 7 shows a small prefix. As is very common with songs, this [pb_glossary id="2607"]mélodie[/pb_glossary] opens with an instrumental introduction before the singer enters—that is, before the phrase beginningg. Note also that this phrase includes a variant on the typical [pb_glossary id="2279"]core bass cadential pattern[/pb_glossary]. Instead of _mi-fa-sol-do_ ([latex]\hat{3}-\hat{4}-\hat{5}-\hat{1}[/latex]), Chaminade writes _di-re-sol-do_ ([latex]\uparrow\hat{1}-\hat{2}-\hat{5}-\hat{1}[/latex]) (mm. 8–11). 

## The Suffix

A suffix occurs after a phrase has cadenced. The terms in Example 8 are flexibly used to describe different types of suffix. While Example 8 provides guidelines for their use, what one person calls a [pb_glossary id="2488"]post-cadential extension[/pb_glossary], another might choose to call a [pb_glossary id="2489"]codetta[/pb_glossary]. It's more important to recognize that a given suffix adds length outside the bounds of a phrase than to worry about which term is most appropriate.

# Contraction

[caption id="attachment_2486" align="alignright" width="150"][![Notation](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/07/Example_013_Contraction_Elmas_PC_1_III-150x150.png)](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/12/2019/07/Example_013_Contraction_Elmas_PC_1_III.png) Example 12. Contraction in Stéphan Elmas, Piano Concerto No. 1, III, mm. 15–21 ([32:21–32:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O-iFgieitho&amp;t=231s)).[/caption] Contractions occur less frequently than expansions. A [pb_glossary id="2296"]contraction[/pb_glossary] occurs when a phrase is shorter than we might expect it to be. In Example 12, a four-measure [pb_glossary id="2273"]compound basic idea[/pb_glossary] is followed by a three-measure continuation. Given the length of the compound basic idea, we might have expected the continuation to be four measures long to create proportional balance. As you listen, notice how the cadence feels abrupt, a feeling that usually accompanies contraction.
