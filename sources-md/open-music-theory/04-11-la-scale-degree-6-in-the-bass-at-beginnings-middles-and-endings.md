# La (Scale Degree 6) in the Bass at Beginnings, Middles, and Endings

Key Takeaways

This chapter discusses the various ways in which composers harmonize _la_ [latex](\hat6)[/latex] in the bass depending on where it's found within a phrase: beginning, middle, or ending. 

  * At phrase beginnings, _la_ [latex](\hat6)[/latex] is often used to prolong tonic in two ways: 
    * harmonized with IV6 in the progression [latex]\mathrm{I-IV^6-V^6_5-I}[/latex] (**Examples 1** and**3**)
    * harmonized with plagal (IV6) in the progression [latex]\mathrm{I-IV^6-I^6}[/latex] (**Examples 2** and**4**)
  * At phrase middles, _la_ [latex](\hat6)[/latex] is often used: 
    * in a deceptive motion (V(7)–vi) (**Examples 5** and**7**)
    * to connect the tonic (T) and strong predominant (PD) areas, harmonized with vi (**Examples 6** and**8**)
  * At phrase endings, _la_ [latex](\hat6)[/latex] is often used: 
    * to create a [pb_glossary id="5988"]phrygian half cadence[/pb_glossary] (iv6–V in minor) (**Examples 7** and**9**)
    * as a "stand-in" for the expected strong PD note _fa_ [latex](\hat4)[/latex] (Example 10)

# Overview: uses of _la_ (scale degree 6)

So far, we've seen every scale degree appear in the bass except for _la_ [latex](\hat6)[/latex]. In this chapter, we see that _la_ [latex](\hat6)[/latex] is commonly harmonized by two chords: vi or IV6. It may show up in beginnings, middles, or endings of phrases, and each location is associated with particular progressions that involve _la_ [latex](\hat6)[/latex] in the bass. 

# At phrase beginnings

When _la_ [latex](\hat6)[/latex] appears in the bass at the beginning of a phrase, it typically prolongs tonic in one of two ways: (1) using IV6 as a predominant in the progression [latex]\mathrm{I-IV^6-V^6_5-I}[/latex] (Example 1), or (2) using plagal (IV6) in the progression [latex]\mathrm{I-IV^6-I^6}[/latex] (Example 2).

Example 2. Tonic prolongation via [latex]\mathit{I-IV^6-I^6}[/latex] in Josephine Lang, Lied.

## Using IV6 as a predominant

Writing [latex]\mathrm{I-IV^6-V^6_5-I}[/latex] can be tricky, particularly when [latex]\mathrm{IV^6}[/latex] goes to [latex]\mathrm{V^6}[/latex], since there is a danger both of parallels and of a doubled leading tone. No single piece of advice can guarantee that you’ll avoid problems. When you write [latex]\mathrm{I-IV^6-V^6_5-I}[/latex], the soprano lines _do–do–re–do_ [latex](\hat1-\hat1-\hat2-\hat1)[/latex] and _mi–mi–fa–mi_ [latex](\hat3-\hat3-\hat4-\hat3)[/latex] will help, but you'll need to check carefully for parallels. Using [latex]\mathrm{V^6_5}[/latex] instead of [latex]\mathrm{V^6}[/latex] will also mitigate some of the danger of parallels (Example 3). Note that this progression doesn't work well in minor, where the bass would create an augmented second from _le_ to _ti_ [latex](\downarrow\hat6-\uparrow\hat7)[/latex]. The rare occurrences of this progression in minor raise _le_ to  _la_ [latex](\downarrow\hat6[/latex] to [latex]\uparrow\hat6)[/latex] to avoid the augmented second (**Examples 3c** and**3d**). 

Example 3. Writing with [latex]\mathit{I-IV^6-V^6_5-I}[/latex].

## Using plagal (IV6)

Writing [latex]\mathrm{I-IV^6-I^6}[/latex] is relatively easy (Example 4). Here, remember three things: 

  1. The bass always arpeggiates down.
  2. The most common soprano is _mi_ – _fa_ – _sol_ [latex](\hat3-\hat4-\hat5)[/latex].
  3. The other voices should move by step or common tone.



Example 4. Writing with plagal (IV6).

# At phrase middles

In the middle of a phrase, _la_ [latex](\hat6)[/latex] shows up in the bass in one of two ways: 

  1. To avoid a cadence as part of a deceptive motion (Example 5)
  2. To connect the tonic area to the strong predominant area by arpeggiating _do_ – _la_ – _fa_ [latex](\hat1-\hat6-\hat4)[/latex], harmonized by vi or IV6 (Example 6)

Example 6. vi connecting **T** and strong **PD** areas in Bernhard Henrik Crussell, Clarinet Quartet Op. 7, II, mm. 1–4 ([0:00-0:18](https://open.spotify.com/track/44eNIUR22CLtueQNBC3Mw5?si=16677c7349af434a)).

## Deceptive motion

When a V chord sets up the expectation for a cadence, but moves instead to an unexpected, non-tonic harmony, this is called [pb_glossary id="11618"]deceptive motion[/pb_glossary]. Deceptive motion most commonly occurs when V(7) moves to vi rather than I, with the bass moving _sol_ – _la_ [latex](\hat5-\hat6)[/latex]. Less commonly, _la_ [latex](\hat6)[/latex] may be harmonized with IV6 rather than vi in a deceptive motion. Writing deceptive motion with V(7)–vi carries an inherent danger of parallels that can be avoided by doing the following two things (Example 7): (1) resolve _ti_ – _do_ [latex](\hat7-\hat1)[/latex] as you would do normally, and (2) move all upper voices in contrary motion to the bass (downward). 

Example 7. Writing deceptive motion.

Deceptive motion vs. Deceptive cadence

Some people use the term “deceptive cadence” to describe what we refer to as “deceptive motion.” Since the progression V(7)–vi avoids a cadence rather than creating one, we find that the term “deceptive cadence” inaccurately describes the progression’s purpose, so we prefer the more neutral description “deceptive motion.” 

## vi as a weak predominant

Using vi to connect the tonic and strong predominant areas is quite easy to write (Example 8). As long as your upper voices move by step or common tone and you follow [pb_glossary id="5895"]typical writing procedures[/pb_glossary], you should not run into writing issues. Notice that for the phrase model analysis (**T/PD/D**) labels, the **PD** label goes on the first strong **PD** before the cadence, as in Example 6.

Example 8. Writing with _la_ [latex]\mathit{(\hat6)}[/latex] connecting **T** to strong **PD** area.

# At phrase endings

_La_ [latex](\hat6)[/latex] may appear at the end of a phrase in one of two ways: 

  1. As part of a phrygian half cadence (Example 9)
  2. Harmonized with a predominant chord as part of a push to a cadence



Example 9. Phrygian half cadence in Franz Schubert, "Die Mainacht," mm. 1–10 ([0:00-0:19](https://open.spotify.com/track/0cMXaclO8JfrGK9uwmIfLc?si=ce4f962e04f6417b)).

## The phrygian half cadence (PHC)

The [pb_glossary id="5988"]phrygian half cadence (PHC)[/pb_glossary] is a special kind of cadential phrase ending that occurs only in minor and involves the progression iv6–V. It's called “phrygian” because of the half step that occurs when _le_ moves to _sol_ [latex](\downarrow\hat6-\hat5)[/latex] in the bass, a sound that’s similar to when _ra_ moves to  _do_ [latex](\downarrow\hat2-\hat1)[/latex] in the [pb_glossary id="4236"]phrygian mode[/pb_glossary]. The progression carries a danger of parallels and of writing an augmented second between _le_ and _ti_ [latex](\downarrow\hat6-\uparrow\hat7)[/latex]. These can be avoided if you choose to double _do_ [latex](\hat1)[/latex] in the iv6 chord (Example 10). Very often, PHCs are approached from i, and are accompanied by the soprano _me–fa–sol_ [latex](\hat3-\hat4-\hat5)[/latex], as in Example 10.

Example 10. Writing a PHC.

## _La_ [latex](\hat6)[/latex] harmonized with a predominant at a cadence

It's possible to see _la_ [latex](\hat6)[/latex] harmonized with a predominant (usually vi or IV6) at a cadence without the presence of _fa_ [latex](\hat4)[/latex] in the bass before the cadential dominant (Example 11). This is much less common, however, than seeing _fa_ [latex](\hat4)[/latex] in the bass harmonized with a strong predominant before the cadence. 

Example 11. _La_ [latex]\mathit{(\hat6)}[/latex] as "stand-in" for strong **PD** in Joseph Haydn, String Quartet Op. 76, no. 2, I, mm. 1–4 ([0:00-0:08](https://open.spotify.com/track/6OqC7YMlzKxJTsmhqqrqcD?si=bd9d3992c77c4794)).
