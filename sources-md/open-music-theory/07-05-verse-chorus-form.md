# Verse-Chorus Form

Key Takeaways

  * [pb_glossary id="1013"]Verse-chorus form[/pb_glossary] is a versatile song form that rapidly took over rock-and-roll in the 1960s and has dominated the genre ever since.
  * In verse-chorus form, the title lyrics, the most memorable music, and the main narrative are split between two [pb_glossary id="1468"]core sections[/pb_glossary]: the [pb_glossary id="1489"]verse[/pb_glossary] and the [pb_glossary id="1488"]chorus[/pb_glossary].



Verse-chorus form is so named because the two most important sections are the [pb_glossary id="1489"]verse[/pb_glossary] and the [pb_glossary id="1488"]chorus[/pb_glossary]. Other possible sections in verse-chorus form are [pb_glossary id="1517"]prechorus[/pb_glossary], [pb_glossary id="1012"]bridge[/pb_glossary], and [pb_glossary id="1518"]postchorus[/pb_glossary]. As an example, look at the form of Bon Jovi’s song "Livin’ on a Prayer," given in Example 1. https://open.spotify.com/track/0J6mQxEZnlRt9ymzFntA6z [table id=4 /] "Livin' on a Prayer" follows a typical verse-chorus form. It also illustrates common usage of five [pb_glossary id="1468"]core sections[/pb_glossary] in verse-chorus forms (and a bonus [truck driver's modulation](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TruckDriversGearChange)!). Cycles Notice that the sections used in "Livin' on a Prayer" recur and seem to follow a pattern. Sections within a verse-chorus form have certain prototypical orderings and groupings. The verse, prechorus, chorus, and postchorus sections, for example, always progress in this order (though they don't all need to be present). These groupings are referred to as [pb_glossary id="1519"]cycles[/pb_glossary]. In "Livin' on a Prayer": 

  * After an extended intro, the first cycle begins with a [pb_glossary id="1489"]verse[/pb_glossary] at 0:47.
  * Then at 1:18, a [pb_glossary id="1517"]prechorus[/pb_glossary] increases energy and tension…
  * …into the [pb_glossary id="1488"]chorus[/pb_glossary] at 1:34.
  * After a brief interlude, this cycle is repeated beginning at 1:54, with the addition of a [pb_glossary id="1518"]postchorus[/pb_glossary] at 2:56.
  * A final cycle at 3:00 is atypical and abbreviated, and it's followed by a repetition of its final chorus multiple times, during which a fadeout ends the song.

# Sections within Verse-Chorus Form

Terms, concepts, definitions, and notational guidelines are taken from common convention and a combination of the resources listed below under Further Reading. 

## Verse (**V**)

  * Verse sections are [pb_glossary id="987"]lyric-variant[/pb_glossary] and often contain lyrics that advance the narrative.
  * Until the 1960s, verse sections tended to be [pb_glossary id="998"]harmonically closed[/pb_glossary].
  * Beginning in the 1960s, verse sections became more and more likely to be [pb_glossary id="999"]harmonically open[/pb_glossary] (Summach 2012, 114).
  * Verses (like [pb_glossary id="877"]strophes[/pb_glossary]) tend to begin on-tonic.



## Prechorus (**P**)

  * Prechorus sections can be recognized most easily by [pb_glossary id="1567"]energy gain[/pb_glossary].
  * They bear many of the functional characteristics of the  _d_ phrase in [pb_glossary id="12010"]_srdc_[/pb_glossary]—[pb_glossary id="1844"]fragmentation[/pb_glossary], acceleration of [pb_glossary id="1846"]harmonic rhythm[/pb_glossary], movement away from tonic harmony, and [pb_glossary id="999"]harmonic openness[/pb_glossary].[footnote] This is because prechorus sections originate historically in the _d_ section of an _srdc_ pattern. Think of an _srdc_ strophe becoming longer until _sr_ forms its own two-part verse section, _d_ forms its own prechorus section, and _c_ forms its own chorus section.[/footnote]



## Chorus (**C**)

  * Chorus sections are [pb_glossary id="988"]lyric-invariant[/pb_glossary] and contain the primary lyrical material of the song (the title lyrics and/or lyrical hook).
  * Chorus function is also typified by heightened musical intensity relative to the verse, including features like "a more dense or active instrumental texture; prominent background vocals; and/or a higher register melody" (Summach 2012, 106).
  * Choruses most frequently (but not exclusively) begin on-tonic.
  * Although the terms [pb_glossary id="1488"]chorus[/pb_glossary] and [pb_glossary id="1019"]refrain[/pb_glossary] are often used interchangeably when speaking colloquially, take care to keep them separate when discussing music theory. Chorus sections are distinct from refrains because choruses constitute an entire section by themselves, whereas refrains are _contained within_ a section (as described below).



## Postchorus (**Z**)

  * A postchorus is a short section that follows a chorus and serves only to close the [pb_glossary id="1519"]cycle [/pb_glossary](not to introduce or transition to the beginning of the next cycle) (Spicer 2011, para. 9).
  * A clear postchorus can be heard in ["Independent Women, Pt. 1" by Destiny's Child](https://open.spotify.com/track/69XUpOpjzDKcfdxqZebGiI?si=st4MyPsDSsmie1KS4u7gvA) at 1:18, beginning with the lyric "Girl, I didn't know you could get down like that."



## Bridge (**B**)

[pb_glossary id="1012"]Bridge[/pb_glossary] sections are a flexible section type in verse-chorus form. 

  * In verse-chorus form songs, the bridge tends to appear once, followed by the last chorus (or the last prechorus and chorus) of the song.
  * Within a cycle, bridges will replace the verse and/or prechorus sections instead of being added in as an extra element. Thus, you will not usually see all five [pb_glossary id="1468"]core section[/pb_glossary] types in a single cycle.
  * A verse-chorus song may not have a bridge at all.

Each of these points contrasts with [the way bridges are used in **AABA** form](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/aaba-form#bridge). 

# Standout Lyrics within Sections

## Refrain

While [pb_glossary id="1019"]refrains[/pb_glossary] are primarily associated with [pb_glossary id="1008"]**AABA** form[/pb_glossary] and [pb_glossary id="876"]strophic form[/pb_glossary], they can occasionally be used within sections of a verse-chorus form song. However, take note that refrains are distinct from choruses—refrains are a lyric _within_ a section, whereas a chorus is an entire standalone section. 

## Climb

A climb is a phrase with prechorus function. Like the refrain, because it is only one phrase long, a climb is too short to be its own section. The climb is always the last phrase of a [pb_glossary id="877"]strophe[/pb_glossary] or [pb_glossary id="1489"]verse[/pb_glossary]. An example of a climb is in [“Come On Eileen”](https://open.spotify.com/track/3MrWxJaD2AT0W9DjWF64Vm?si=f2ec99d7c30245bf) by Dexys Midnight Runners (1982): listen for the one-phrase climb at the end of its verses and bridge (“Tu-ra-lu-ra…”), as heard at 1:13.
